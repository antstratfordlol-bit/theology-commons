## Chapter 10: The Doctrine on God in the History of Theology {#mateo-seco-trino.10}

The Middle Ages inherited the great patristic legacy also with regard to the theology of the mystery of God. In the West, the thought of Saint Augustine was the universal point of reference. This thought was taken up, however, with a new mentality, enthusiastic for speculative achievements, and exercising rational discourse with a new coherence and rigour. Faith was taken as given and a purely analogical knowledge of the mystery was admitted; yet at the same time, *ratio* was brought forward with a far more pronounced claim to penetrate and systematise the truth. A fine illustration of this is Saint Anselm of Canterbury († 1109), who reasoned upon the trinity of Persons on the basis of God's perfect spirituality, necessarily directed towards the infinite and eternal act of intratrinitarian knowledge and love.[^1]

From this thrilling period in which the speculative formulation of the Trinitarian mystery reached its zenith, we shall study five authors who are among the most representative: Saint Anselm of Canterbury († 1109), Richard of Saint Victor († c. 1173), Alexander of Hales († 1245), Saint Bonaventure († 1274), and Saint Thomas Aquinas († 1274).

### Saint Anselm of Canterbury

Anselmian thought reveals its rational tendency and unmistakable personality in a paradigmatic way in the best-known of his arguments to demonstrate the existence of God: the ontological argument. With this argument, Saint Anselm intended to deduce the existence of God from the fact that within the idea of God the affirmation of his existence is also included.

The fundamental issue is this: God is supremely simple, and in Him all perfections are identified to such a degree that God does not *have* existence, but *is* his very existence. This means that if we had an intuitive knowledge of the divine essence, we would see with complete clarity that this essence necessarily exists, and therefore that the proposition "God exists" is self-evident. However, while we journey on this earth we do not contemplate the divine essence intuitively. Consequently, although the proposition "God exists" is self-evident in itself, it is not self-evident to our knowledge as wayfarers.[^2] To assert the contrary would be to lapse into ontologism.

The various tendencies grouped under the name of ontologism share the common idea that the human intellect, even on this earth, is capable of a direct intuition of the divine essence, and therefore that God's existence is self-evident to man to such a degree that the atheist, in order to be an atheist, would find himself forced to deny the obvious.[^3]

Saint Anselm's ontological argument has a very different meaning. This argument does not proceed from the intuition of the divinity, but from the content of the idea of God; it does not claim that man has immediate knowledge of God, but only that man possesses the idea that God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived (*aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari potest*). Here is Saint Anselm's course of argument: All men express by the concept "God" the being than which nothing greater can be thought or imagined. Now, if this being lacked existence, it would not be the greatest being that can be thought, for it would lack the perfection of existence; therefore, within the very concept of God his existence is already implied. Consequently, the existence of God is evident to all men who understand what the word "God" signifies.[^4]

The customary critique of this argument is that it leaps from the mental order to the real order. It is obvious that whoever thinks of God as infinitely perfect must think of Him as possessing existence. But can one deduce from this that this being, conceived as infinitely perfect, also really exists? Is there not in this reasoning an illicit transition from the world of ideas to the real world? Saint Thomas inclines towards a negative solution: this argument does not demonstrate the real existence of God, but only existence as a perfection indissolubly joined in the mind to the concept of God.[^5]

Saint Anselm was by no means unaware of such a difficulty. Indeed, he only employs this discourse when speaking of God; moreover, he explicitly affirms that this argument can only be applied in the case of the idea of God. According to Saint Anselm, only the idea of God could justify an argument of this kind. And this because, for Saint Anselm, the concept of the being than which nothing greater can be conceived possesses such singular characteristics as to permit that line of argument. "The objection that Anselm, in the ontological argument, deduces existence from a mere concept constitutes a caricature of his mental process. Such criticisms ignore the structure of the ontological demonstration. The Anselmian argument does not rest upon just any concept, but upon the supreme idea, which is necessary to thought and in which thought transcends itself."[^6]

The Anselmian argument finds its proper perspective if it is considered as embedded within the tradition of Augustinian thought, and if one bears in mind, moreover, that the concept "God" is a limit-concept. Indeed, God cannot be thought of as non-existent without internal contradiction, since the concept "God" implies his existence; therefore one must either deem it absurd, or judge that He exists. For this reason, anyone would misunderstand the Anselmian argument who failed to take into account the following observation: "It is true that, in this initial moment of reflection, the Most Perfect Being exists only in the intellect. But since the intellect is the mirror and image of God, this is a guarantee of objectivity: it could not happen that a thing were possible in the intellect and yet not possible in reality. Here lies the utmost subtlety, since Saint Anselm does not pass from what is thought to the real directly, but rather—moved by his complete trust that the mind is the mirror and image of the divine mind, the source of Truth—passes from the ideal possibility of the all-perfect being (a possibility that obtains in the intellect) to the real possibility of that being (possibility on the level of the real). A Being so perfect that nothing greater than it can be conceived, I cannot consider as possible unless it is so in reality. Now then: *Si ergo cogitari potest esse, ex necessitate est*. If thought can admit its existence, it exists really and necessarily. Such a being cannot exist only in the intellect, but exists both in thought and in reality."[^7]

From the very beginning, the Anselmian argument encountered vigorous opposition, starting with the monk Gaunilo. The objection raised against it is ever the same: the leap from the ideal order of the conceived to the real order of existence. Peter Lombard did not explicitly reject it; Alexander of Hales regarded it as close to the Augustinian argument from supreme truth; Saint Thomas, as has been seen, rejected it unequivocally. In subsequent history the same divergences regarding the validity of the argument have persisted. Saint Anselm himself replied to Gaunilo's criticisms, and in this reply he provided the surest keys for grasping the meaning he attached to his argument. And that meaning is none other than the singularity entailed by the idea of God as the most perfect being possible.[^8]

As regards the Trinitarian mystery, Saint Anselm develops his thought especially in *De fide Trinitatis et incarnatione Verbi* and in *De processione Spiritus Sancti contra Graecos*. Although dialectical argumentation and recourse to necessary reasons carry great weight in Saint Anselm, it would be unjust to exaggerate this trait of the Bishop of Canterbury to the point of forgetting that he is, first and foremost, a man of faith. In his theology, faith occupies the preponderant place proper to it. Not without reason did Saint Anselm define the theological task as *fides quaerens intellectum*:[^9] faith seeking understanding. Anselmian theology is rooted in faith, even though the rational effort—precisely because of the great role he assigns to dialectic—at times seems to take the forefront.

*De fide Trinitatis* was written against Roscelin († c. 1120),[^10] who, by rationalising the faith, fell back practically into the error of Sabellius: if there is only one God—he argued—one must conclude that the three Persons are only one thing (*res*), and therefore *Pater et Spiritus Sanctus cum Filio est incarnatus* ("the Father and the Holy Spirit became incarnate with the Son").[^11] Saint Anselm begins the refutation of Roscelin by drawing attention, in the first place, to the importance of faith—that is, the importance of receiving sincerely all that Revelation teaches about God.[^12] Next he shows Roscelin that he is not only a heretic, but even falls into contradictions. To this end he employs the now traditional distinctions between nature and person, and between the absolute and the relative,[^13] which allows him to make clear that there is no repugnance between the mystery of the Trinity and human reason, precisely because it is not asserted that in God there are at once one person and three persons, but one nature and three persons; it is not said that there are one absolute and three absolutes, but one absolute and three relations.[^14]

Once again, therefore, we encounter the centrality of the concept of *relation* in the speculative exposition of the Trinitarian mystery. With Saint Anselm the theological axiom that the Council of Florence would solemnly consecrate reaches its clearest formulation:[^15] *omnia sunt idem ubi non obviat relationis oppositio*—all things are one and the same where there is no opposition of relation. Following Saint Augustine, Saint Anselm attaches great importance to the operations of the human soul—knowledge and love—as an analogy of the Trinity.

Perhaps what is most outstanding in Saint Anselm regarding Trinitarian doctrine is his pneumatological doctrine, developed in *De processione Spiritus Sancti* in an attempt to show the meaning and rationale of the *Filioque*.[^16] Saint Anselm records in this book his intervention at the Council of Bari (1098) in an attempt to prevent the rupture between Eastern and Western Christians from becoming definitive.

Saint Anselm first enumerates the points on which Greeks and Latins are in agreement: a) That the Holy Spirit is God; b) That the Holy Spirit is one same God with the Father and the Son; c) That the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father; d) That the Holy Spirit is the Spirit of the Son; e) That the Holy Spirit is distinct from the Father, because He proceeds from Him, and from the Son, because He proceeds from the Father by way of procession (*ekporeusis*) and not by way of generation.

In his argumentation in favour of the *Filioque*, Saint Anselm adduces fundamentally the underlying reason upon which Westerners rely: in virtue of the unity of the divine essence, whatever is said of one divine Person must be said of the other two, excepting solely what belongs to the opposition of relation. Thus, according to Saint Anselm, in order to distinguish the Holy Spirit from the Son, one would have to say either that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son as He proceeds from the Father, or else that the Son is begotten by the Holy Spirit as He is begotten by the Father. Scripture tells us that the Holy Spirit is the Spirit of the Son, whereas nowhere do we read that the Son is also the Son of the Spirit. Therefore, in virtue of the unity of nature existing between the Father and the Son, one must admit—Saint Anselm concludes—that the Holy Spirit proceeds jointly from the Father and the Son.[^17]

After adducing the texts of Sacred Scripture that indicate a relation of origin of the Holy Spirit with respect to the Son, especially John 14:16 and John 15:26, Saint Anselm attempts to resolve the difficulties presented by the Greeks. The principal difficulty is that the *Filioque* would allegedly introduce a double principle into the divinity—the Father and the Son—thus endangering its unity. This is not so, Saint Anselm replies: the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son inasmuch as the two are one single being with the divinity, and inasmuch as the Son receives from the Father the power to spirate the Holy Spirit together with Him.[^18]

The solution proposed by Saint Anselm rests precisely upon the consideration of the Father and the Son as a single principle of the Holy Spirit. We—says Saint Anselm, evoking the *Quicumque* Creed—call the three Persons one single Creator, not three creators, for although they are three, they create inasmuch as they are one; in like manner it is with regard to the procession of the Holy Spirit: Father and Son spirate the Holy Spirit inasmuch as they are one single principle, for the Father and the Son differ between themselves solely by the relation of paternity-filiation. Consequently, the Holy Spirit proceeds from both as from a single principle (*tamquam ab uno principio*).[^19]

### Richard of Saint Victor

Richard of Saint Victor occupies an outstanding place in the development of Trinitarian theology precisely in the line that runs from Saint Augustine to Saint Thomas. Richard belongs to the School of Saint Victor, founded by William of Champeaux in 1108 in an ancient abbey on the outskirts of Paris. In the milieu of this abbey, where Hugh of Saint Victor had already distinguished himself, an effort was made to unite dialectical learning and science with mysticism, faith with reason, science with wisdom. At the same time, an attempt was made to ensure that the reasons sought to "comprehend" the faith were within the reach of all.[^20]

Richard lived in a truly enthralling epoch: these were the years in which, alongside the theology of the monasteries, the theology of the cathedral schools and that of the religious orders began to flourish. There was a common language throughout Europe and common ideals; above all, there was an immense vitality. There was "a youthful, perhaps overly absolute confidence in dialectic, that discipline of disciplines. And at the same time, in the very same thinkers, an incomparable impulse of piety that must be described as the fervour of intelligence or as mystical intellectualism."[^21]

As points of reference for the period in which Richard writes, it suffices to recall the following: Abelard died in 1142, having enraptured university youth with his dialectic and eloquence; Gilbert of Poitiers (de la Porrée, † 1154) occupied the see of Poitiers—the very see of Saint Hilary—and taught a clear tritheism;[^22] Peter Lombard was teaching in Paris, and in more than one question Richard seems to have him in mind; Saint Bernard died in 1153. The Abbey of Saint Victor was still in its splendour, with the memory of Hugh of Saint Victor still vivid.

Richard is, above all, a mystical author who, like the entire School of Saint Victor, stands under the influence of Saint Augustine. He is also influenced by Saint Anselm, particularly in his conception of the theological task as *fides quaerens intellectum*—that is, as an effort to "understand". We shall expound his Trinitarian thought according to the order he himself follows in his treatise *De Trinitate*.[^23]

This order is itself an entire lesson in theological method, precisely because it presents together the questions concerning the treatise on God as One and on God as Triune. Unmistakable is his deeply personal development of Trinitarian theology, centring his reflection upon the nature of love: *Ecce tria sunt ergo amans et quod amatur et amor*,[^24] a thought he grounds upon the axiom that alterity is indispensable for the existence of true love.[^25] Here is a summary of his intellectual itinerary:

**Prologue:** We must ascend from faith to the understanding of faith, arriving even at penetrating with thought into the very intimacy of God. Indeed, the Christian is called to behold God in the life to come, and must prepare for this vision by working here on earth to attain through reason what he already possesses through faith.

**Book I: The Existence of a Divine Substance, Unique and Absolutely Perfect.** The existence of God is proved beginning from the existence of contingent beings and the existence of various degrees of perfection. In contemplating the existence of a being that does not exist of itself—the visible world—we must conclude to the existence of a being that exists of itself and is therefore eternal. If this were not so, nothing would exist. Therefore, above all known reality, in order to explain it, one must posit a being that is not only the best and greatest, but exists of itself, being the source of all wisdom and all power. This primordial substance is necessarily unique and perfect.

**Book II: The Divine Attributes.** God is uncreated and immense. Being uncreated, He is immutable, and therefore all increase or decrease must be excluded from Him. He is infinite in greatness and duration. The divine attributes are identical with the essence of God, and all of them lead to the uniqueness of one single Lord, one single Almighty, one single Eternal. The divine essence is supersubstantial, since it is entirely essence and has no accidents.[^26]

**Book III: Plurality and Trinity in God.** Richard attempts to show by arguments intelligible to reason that within the divine unity there must exist a plurality of Persons. Perfect and sovereign charity exists in God. Now, perfect charity—which is not love of oneself, but of another—requires alterity. But sovereign divine charity, in order to be perfect and perfectly ordered, cannot be directed towards anyone other than a person of equal dignity. Therefore it must be directed towards a divine Person (ch. 2).

In God there is fullness of beatitude. But there is no love more delightful than mutual love, for love cannot be gratifying unless it is mutual. Therefore, in that true and supreme beatitude mutual love cannot be lacking, just as joyful love cannot be lacking. Consequently, in supreme beatitude a plurality of persons cannot be lacking (ch. 3).

In God there is fullness of glory. Now true glory consists in communicating generously to another what one possesses, which presupposes an associate in glory (*consors gloriae*). Therefore there is in God a plurality of Persons (ch. 4).

These, Richard concludes, are arguments that are irresistible (ch. 5), and they lead to the following considerations:

a) *Equality and unity in plurality:* The second Person is co-eternal with the first, for God is immutable. He is completely equal, for otherwise He would not merit being loved with absolute love. The two Persons, therefore, possess the same perfection, the same unique substance, and are nothing other than a single God (chs. 6–8).

b) *Trinity of Persons:* Supreme and perfect charity requires the desire to communicate the happiness experienced in love: each of the two Persons must, therefore, desire to have a common object of love (*condilectus*). This desire must be harmonious and equal in both; otherwise, there would be a deficiency in love. Thus, without a duality of Persons there would be neither true love nor communication of glory; without a Trinity, there would be no communication of the delights of love, which would be contrary to the perfection of love in each of the Persons (chs. 14–15). Equality among the three divine Persons consists in the full possession of sovereign being, whereas in creatures equality presupposes many substances and is therefore always imperfect (chs. 22–25).

**Book IV: The Persons.** Having affirmed the unity of nature and the plurality of Persons existing in God, it is necessary to elucidate the meaning of the term *persona*. The word *persona* refers to substance, but is not synonymous with substance: it designates a substance endowed with reason and signifies also a singular, incommunicable property. By the name "substance" is designated *what* (*quid*) a thing is; by that of "person", *who* (*quis*) that individual is[^27] (chs. 6–7). Three persons signify three individuals of rational nature. This does not imply diversity of three substances, but requires alterity in the three who possess it (chs. 8–9).

In the person one must consider, on the one hand, its nature; on the other, the manner in which it receives it (ch. 11). The term *existentia* corresponds to this twofold point of view: *sistentia* indicates that a being subsists; *ex* indicates from whom it has received this subsistence (ch. 12). In God, existences are distinguished by diversity of origin; the plurality of Persons does not stem from a diversity of nature in being, but from a diversity in how they receive it (chs. 14–15). In God there is a common existence, since the three Persons hold the divine substance in common. And there is in each Person an incommunicable existence, that which confers upon it its personal property (ch. 17). In God there is a plurality of Persons without there being a plurality of substances, for each possesses, with its own distinguishing characteristic, the supersubstantial Being without distinction (chs. 18–19).[^28] To speak, then, of three Persons in God means speaking of three "someones" who possess being endowed with reason, each according to his incommunicable personal property (ch. 20).

The definition of person formulated by Boethius—"an individual substance of a rational nature" (*rationalis naturae individua substantia*)—cannot be applied to the Trinity, for the Trinity is an individual substance of a rational nature, yet is not a person (ch. 21). On the other hand, in order to apply it to the Trinity, person can be defined as *naturae divinae incommunicabilis existentia*, that is, as an incommunicable existence of the divine nature (ch. 23). Indeed, to every person this definition can be applied: an existing being of itself alone according to a certain singular mode of rational existence (chs. 24–25).[^29]

**Book V: The Processions.** Since there are in God distinct Persons by reason of their distinction of origin, it is necessary to study this origin. One Person must have existence from Himself and from Himself alone, for possessing power in fullness, He is the source and principle of every other essence, existence, and person. The Person who exists of Himself requires another who is equal to Him in order to have the fullness of charity.[^30] The third Person does not proceed from the first alone, for the second has equal power with the first.

**Book VI: The Names of the Persons.** Despite the dissimilarities that the generation of the Son involves in comparison with human generation, it is appropriate to speak of Father and Son in God. The processions of the Son and of the Holy Spirit are distinct: the procession of the Son is the communication of majesty; the procession of the Holy Spirit is the communication of love (ch. 7). The Holy Spirit, equal in all things to the Father, is called neither Image nor Word. Only the Word is Word, because He alone, like the human word, is born of the heart and manifests its thought (ch. 13). Power is attributed to the Father, wisdom to the Son, and goodness to the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit is called the Gift of God, since He is bestowed upon man as a gift of love, which inflames the soul to assimilate it to his proper characteristic, which is love (ch. 14). In thinking of the Trinity it is lawful to attribute power to the Father, wisdom to the Son, and goodness to the Holy Spirit (ch. 15). The Son alone is begotten and He alone can properly be called the Image of the Father; the Holy Spirit, by contrast, cannot be called the Image of the Son, for although He receives everything just as the Son does, in the Holy Spirit there is no active spiration (ch. 19).

Richard presents in *De Trinitate* a personal synthesis of great intellectual vigour, which is unmistakable. It is not by chance that he chose love as the key to his presentation of the Trinitarian mystery. Richard is linked to mysticism and carries out his work with an ardent desire to know God better in order to love Him more. Charity is an absolute value, as is evident from Saint John's definition (cf. 1 Jn 4:8): "There is nothing better, nothing more delightful, nothing more magnificent than true, authentic, and sovereign charity. It could not exist without a plurality of persons."[^31] It is charity, which is identical with the infinite divine Wisdom, that requires personal alterity and perfect communication. Here in synthesis is the mystery of the Trinity.

The schema followed by Richard is paradigmatic, and permits him a linear itinerary entirely in keeping with his fundamental intuition. He begins with the demonstration of the existence of a supreme substance, and proceeds by describing the perfection of that Substance, in order to meditate thence upon the multi-personal mystery in God. As has been written, *De Trinitate*, which unfolds around the *Quicumque* Creed, is first and foremost a vigorous theological treatise on God that attempts to deduce the three Persons from the infinite perfection of God.[^32]

### Alexander of Hales

With Alexander of Hales begins the Golden Age of the Scholastic conception of theology—that is, of the structuring of all theological thought into a coherent and compact *Summa*, where each question finds its place in a perfectly ordered whole. Alexander, who was born between 1186 and 1190, became a Master of Theology in Paris in 1221. In 1236, when nearly fifty years of age, he entered the Order of Friars Minor. Among his pupils was Saint Bonaventure. He died in Paris in 1245.

The written work of Alexander of Hales is typical of Scholasticism: alongside sermons, glosses, and biblical commentaries are found his *Commentary on the Sentences*, his *Quaestiones disputatae*, and the *Summa Theologica* or *Summa Halensis*, which represents a kind of collaborative work of his school.[^33] With Alexander begins the Franciscan current that we shall see flourish in Saint Bonaventure, which pays greater attention to the distinctiveness—to the proper notes—of each divine Person.

Alexander essentially follows the approach of Richard of Saint Victor: he considers God first in his unity, passing thereafter to the contemplation of the Trinitarian mystery. His starting point is the consideration of God as supreme goodness (*summa bonitas*). In speaking of the first procession, he employs the argument that the good is diffusive of itself (*bonum diffusivum sui*), and that there exists no greater self-diffusion than substantial generation. He also attempts to show that the spiration of the Holy Spirit springs from that same goodness.

Alexander adopts the concept of person employed by Richard: person appears as *quis habens* ("the who who possesses"), whereas substance appears as *quid habitum* ("the what possessed"). In God—says Alexander in a manner very similar to Richard—there are several Persons and not several substances, because there are several who have one and the same being, but with diverse properties.[^34]

Alexander points out that it is not the divine substance that begets the Son, but the Person of the Father who begets Him as *principium quod*. The clarification is important. The Trinity cannot be conceived as a substance that begets itself; rather, it is the Father who, from his substance, begets the Son.[^35] Thus the Son proceeds from the divine substance understood not in an absolute sense, but in a relative sense—that is to say, inasmuch as this substance is possessed by the Father.

In speaking of the processions in God, Alexander does not rely upon the Augustinian distinction between concept and love, and consequently encounters difficulties in distinguishing the processions as to their origin, since he cannot assign the first to intellect and the second to love; he distinguishes them by pointing to "a twofold manner of proceeding from the first Person. This twofold manner is action by nature and action by will."[^36] Like Saint Bonaventure, Alexander understands that the generation of the Word is by nature (*per naturam*), and the spiration of the Holy Spirit by will (*per voluntatem*).

Theology, especially from Saint Thomas Aquinas onwards, would affirm that it is opposite relations that distinguish the divine Persons and formally constitute them as persons. For Alexander, by contrast, what constitutes the Persons is their properties of origin; relations do nothing other than manifest this personal distinction. The question had already crossed our path in the fourth century when dealing with Eunomius—though not formally posed—and it would reappear with Saint Bonaventure when answering the question of what constitutes the divine Persons. The characteristic notes of the Persons are these: the Father is principle, innascibility, and paternity; the Son is wisdom and Word; the Holy Spirit is communion, charity, gift.

### Saint Bonaventure

Saint Bonaventure was born in 1221 in Bagnoregio, and at an early age took the Franciscan habit. He studied theology in Paris under the direction of Alexander of Hales, whom he acknowledged as master and father. His life was closely bound up with the University of Paris. He was not only a great speculative author, but a great mystic and a great ruler as Minister General of the Franciscan Order. Created a cardinal in 1273, he died in 1274, the same year as Saint Thomas Aquinas.

Saint Bonaventure devoted great attention to Trinitarian questions.[^37] Perhaps nothing summarizes his thought better than this twofold aphorism: "That God exists is an indubitable truth; that God is triune is a credible truth [a truth of faith]".[^38] Saint Bonaventure thus gathers up the whole tradition of thought we have been studying. It is rightly said of him that he is situated within the Augustinian line, depending upon Saint Anselm and Alexander of Hales alongside Pseudo-Dionysius and Saint John Damascene.[^39] Saint Bonaventure finds the orientation of Saint Anselm's "ontological" argument convincing, centring it on his part upon the fact that God is supreme Truth.[^40]

Saint Bonaventure places great confidence in the possibility of discovering God through created things, especially from the human soul, which is the most perfect creature and in which the mysterious divine intimacy is reflected as in a vestige. Like Saint Augustine, Saint Bonaventure finds in the three powers of the soul the vestige of the Trinity: memory is the vestige of the Father, intellect is the vestige of the Son, and will is the vestige of the Holy Spirit. Saint Bonaventure finds an even greater vestige in these realities because he does not distinguish between the soul and its faculties: thus the three faculties—which are one and the same soul—show themselves as three and, at the same time, as one single being.

In structuring the questions, Saint Bonaventure follows the Western itinerary: studying first the unity of God, and passing thence to the Trinity of Persons. In speaking of the Trinity he takes as his starting point the axiom of Pseudo-Dionysius: *bonum est diffusivum sui*. Saint Bonaventure's argumentation may be summarized thus: we cannot think of the divine being as good without at the same time seeing Him as diffusive of Himself. Moreover, since God is the supreme Good, his communicability must likewise be supreme. A "small" diffusion such as creation or even the work of sanctification does not suffice; rather, a supreme diffusion is necessary: the total and full communication of the Person of the Father in the generation of the Son and in the spiration of the Holy Spirit. This is a diffusion that must be "supreme and eternal, actual and intrinsic, natural and voluntary, free and necessary, indefectible and perfect."[^41]

Saint Bonaventure, like Alexander of Hales, understands that the divine Persons are not constituted by relations, but by their origin. Between person, relation, and notion (property) there exists, according to this view, a distinction of reason. Saint Thomas Aquinas would say the contrary: between person, relation, and personal property there is total identity, without it being possible to conceive the person as prior to his relation. And the reason is that, whereas for Saint Thomas relations are constitutive of the Persons, for Saint Bonaventure relations are merely characteristic of the Persons, who are already distinct by reason of their properties of origin. Relations merely manifest these properties.[^42]

### Saint Thomas Aquinas

The life of Saint Thomas Aquinas runs parallel to that of Saint Bonaventure, especially in their mature years. He was born around the year 1224 in Roccasecca. He was a university student in Naples (1239–1243). In 1244 he entered the Order of Preachers. Between 1248 and 1251 he attended the lectures of Saint Albert the Great in Cologne. After being ordained a priest and following a brief period of teaching in Cologne (1251–1252), Saint Thomas went to teach in Paris, first as Bachelor (1252–1255), and then as Master and Regent of the chair for foreigners (1256–1259). From 1259 to 1268 Saint Thomas resided in Italy and was named Preacher General of his Order. He returned once again to Paris to continue teaching (1269–1272), and in that year returned to the University of Naples. On 6 December 1273 he ceased writing. At the end of January 1274 he set out to attend the Council of Lyon. He died on 7 March 1274.[^43]

In our exposition we shall follow fundamentally the questions of the *Summa Theologiae*. In this respect, the place in which Saint Thomas situates the questions on God is itself a decisive stance. Peter Lombard began his *Sentences* precisely with the treatment of Trinitarian questions; Saint Thomas begins the *Summa Theologiae* with the questions concerning God considered in his unity, passing next to Trinitarian questions, and thence to creation: "Our consideration of God has three parts: in the first, we shall consider what belongs to the divine essence; in the second, what pertains to the distinction of Persons; and in the third, what concerns the procession of creatures from God."[^44] Following Saint Augustine, Saint Thomas places himself within the typical Western approach: starting from the unity of God and the consideration of the divine attributes in order to analyse subsequently the truths concerning the trinity of Persons.

The questions concerning divine unity find in Saint Thomas an accomplished logical order, entirely dependent upon the term reached in the Five Ways. Here is a summary of the schema he follows:[^45]

- I. The Existence of God (qq. 1–2).
- II. The Nature of God (qq. 3–13).
- III. The Operations in God (qq. 14–25).
- IV. The Beatitude of God (q. 26).

Saint Thomas begins by rejecting the possibility of proving the existence of God *a priori*. He then develops the Five Ways, all of which rest exclusively upon the principle of causality. Within each proof, the order followed is likewise rigorous: a universally observable starting point, such as motion, the existence of caused beings, the contingency of beings, the various degrees of perfection in beings, or the fact that things tend towards an end; to this fact the principle of causality is applied according to the mode proper to each case; the possibility of an infinite regress in causes subordinated *per se*—that is, in essentially and currently subordinated causes—is denied; and one arrives at the existence of God, whose nature appears described under the aspect proper to each way: as unmoved mover, first uncaused cause, necessary being, supreme perfection, ultimate end.[^46]

The manner in which Saint Thomas treats the questions proper to the divine essence is of immense importance, causing the Five Ways to converge united towards it: the prime mover must be his very activity, and therefore must be Being itself; the first cause, to be uncaused, must have within itself the rationale of its existence; the necessary Being must have its own existence as an essential attribute; supreme perfection cannot be a participated perfection; the supreme final cause cannot be ordered to anything distinct from itself.[^47] Thus divine transcendence and the distinction between God and the world are highlighted, and the whole subsequent treatment of the divine attributes and operations is protected from the temptation of pantheism.

This profound perception of divine transcendence led Saint Thomas to take negative theology very much into account: of God we can say what He is not, not what He is;[^48] and at the same time to reinforce the unity of God by identifying the divine nature with its act. He thus understands in the most radical manner that God is an eternal and supremely simple act of intellect and love. God is pure being: his existence is his essence. He is *ipsum esse subsistens*.[^49] This steadfast perception of the intimate unity of God will find expression when speaking of the divine life—knowledge and love—and, above all, will exert a decisive influence upon his treatment of the Trinitarian mystery.

Here is the schema followed in the questions concerning the Trinity:[^50]

**The Treatise on the Trinity (*Summa Theologiae*, I, qq. 27–43)**

- **I. The Processions in God** (q. 27)
- **II. The Relations in God** (q. 28)
- **III. The Divine Persons**
  - **1. Considered absolutely (in themselves)**
    - A) In common:
      - a) The concept of person in God (q. 29)
      - b) The number of divine Persons (q. 30)
      - c) What pertains to the unity or plurality in God (q. 31)
      - d) How we can know the divine Persons (q. 32)
    - B) In particular:
      - a) The Person of the Father (q. 33)
      - b) The Person of the Word and the Image (qq. 34–35)
      - c) The Person of the Holy Spirit (qq. 36–38)
  - **2. Considered relatively (by comparison)**
    - 1) To the divine essence (q. 39)
    - 2) To the divine relations (q. 40)
    - 3) To the acts expressing the origins of the Persons (q. 41)
    - 4) To one another:
      - a) As to equality and likeness (q. 42)
      - b) As to their mission to the souls of the just (q. 43)

Thus the entire treatise is divided into three major blocks of questions following a rigorous logical order: I. The processions, understood and explained by means of the analogy of the operations of knowledge and love immanent to every intellectual being (q. 27); II. The relations that necessarily follow upon these divine processions and constitute the Persons (q. 28); III. The divine Persons considered absolutely, that is, in themselves (qq. 29–38), or relatively, that is, in their relation to the divine essence, to the acts expressing their origins, or in their relation to one another (qq. 39–43). It is here that the mission of the divine Persons is studied—that is, their relation to the history of salvation.

#### Analogical Thought

The analogy of the immanent operations proper to every intellectual being lies at the foundation of Saint Thomas's structuring of his treatment of the Holy Trinity. However, in order to understand the scope and limits that Saint Thomas attributes to this analogy, it is necessary to keep in mind what he says regarding the "demonstrability" of the mystery of the Trinity. His position here is far more nuanced than that which we found in Saint Anselm, Richard of Saint Victor, or Saint Bonaventure:

"That God is triune—he writes in his Commentary on Boethius's *De Trinitate*—is solely an object of faith and cannot be proved by any argument. Some non-necessitating reasons can be offered that possess probability only for the believer. In our present state as wayfarers, we cannot know God except through his effects in the world. Now the Trinity of Persons cannot be perceived through his causality, since this causality is common to the whole Trinity."[^51]

Later, in the *Summa Theologiae*, he would add that to seek to demonstrate the Trinity is to offend faith doubly: in the first place, by lowering the fascinating mystery of the divine intimacy to the pettiness of our mind; in the second place, by exposing the mystery to the ridicule of unbelievers:

"We have shown that man, by his own powers, cannot attain to the knowledge of God except through creatures. But creatures lead to the knowledge of God as the effect to its cause (...) Now the creative power of God is common to the whole Trinity, and therefore belongs to the unity of the essence and not to the distinct Persons. Hence through natural reason that which belongs to the unity of the essence can be known of God, but not what pertains to the distinction of Persons."[^52]

Nevertheless, we do find vestiges of the Trinity in creatures, especially in the human soul and its operations:

"The reasons alleged to manifest the mystery of the Trinity (...) are congruent, but not such that by them the trinity of Persons is demonstrated. And this is verified by examining each of the demonstrations cited. The infinite goodness of God is manifested also in the production of creatures, because to draw something from nothing requires infinite power, and for Him to communicate Himself with infinite goodness it is not necessary that something infinite proceed from God, but that each being receive the divine goodness according as its mode or capacity requires. Likewise, the maxim that without companionship the joyful enjoyment of any good is not possible holds good when it is a question of a person who does not possess the fullness of good, and therefore, to attain the fullness of the good of joy, needs the good of another to be joined to his own. Finally, the likeness of our intellect does not sufficiently prove anything concerning God, because intelligence is not in God and in us univocally."[^53]

Saint Thomas's allusions to the arguments of Richard of Saint Victor are evident, as is the fact that he reduces their probative force to mere congruences—that is, to the fact that, presupposing the revelation of the foundational truth (the existence of the mystery), they show its congruence. In this regard he is far more prudent than Richard of Saint Victor or Saint Bonaventure himself.

#### Overview of His Doctrine

In his biblical commentaries, Saint Thomas analysed all the New Testament texts concerning the Trinity, from the baptismal mandate (Mt 28:19) to the texts of Jesus' farewell discourses (Jn 14–15) and the principal Pauline Trinitarian formulas (1 Cor 2:10; 6:11, 19; 12:3–6; 2 Cor 13:13; Rom 8:26). He analysed with special care each of the verses of the Prologue of Saint John's Gospel. He was also well versed—insofar as the instruments of his time permitted—in patristic doctrine, especially that of Saint Augustine. In this respect, the precision with which he grasps the positions of Arius, Sabellius, or Macedonius is truly eloquent, as is his knowledge of the principal writings of Saint Athanasius, Saint Hilary, and the theological tradition that preceded him.

Saint Thomas grasps with Saint Augustine the theological importance of the fact that in John 1:18 it is said that the *Filius unigenitus* proceeds from the Father as Word. The Only-begotten proceeds from the Father as an interior word. He is spoken as a mental word from all eternity by the Father as the expression of his essence, of his infinite spiritual nature, perfectly known from all eternity. The Father begets his Son in the eternal "today" of eternity by an intellectual act in a manner similar to how our spirit conceives its mental word; but whereas our word is nothing more than an accidental modification of our intellect, the Word is substantial to the divine thought. In this perspective, the intimate mode of the generation of the Word is illuminated in large measure. Consequently, the intimate mode of spiration is likewise illuminated, especially if one attends to the analogy with the operations of the human soul, endowed with intellect and the capacity to love. If it is said that the Son proceeds from the Father as an intellectual word, one can conceive that the Holy Spirit proceeds from them according to a procession of love, and that He is the term of this procession in the same manner as the Word is the term of the intellectual procession.[^54]

#### The Processions

Faith teaches us that there exist in God two internal, immanent processions, and that it is necessary to understand these processions as spiritual, not material processions. These are two real processions. Now in every procession three elements are found: a) the one who proceeds (*qui*); b) the one from whom he proceeds (*a quo*); c) the relation or order of proceeding. For the procession to be real it is required that these three elements be real. Saint Thomas defines them in God as an *ordo originis*,[^55] that is, as a "pure order of origin between two really distinct terms existing in God, without motion, priority or posteriority, diversity of nature, composition of act and potency, transmutation of any kind, succession, or time; without distinction of cause and effect, or any admixture of imperfection."[^56] In applying these characteristics to the divine processions, one must also bear in mind, as Saint Thomas points out, that every procession is dependent upon an action.

The Greek Fathers conceived the processions as donations of the divine nature that the Father makes to the others: the Father gives his nature to the Son and to the Holy Spirit; Richard of Saint Victor explained them as a requirement of perfect charity. Saint Thomas, following Saint Augustine, starts from the reality of our soul as the image of the Trinity:

"Since every procession presupposes an action, in the same way that from an action terminating in exterior matter an external procession follows, so to an action remaining within the agent itself there corresponds an internal procession; and this is observed better than anywhere else in the intellect, whose action—understanding—remains within the one who understands. In everyone who understands, by the very fact of understanding, there proceeds within him something, which is the conception of the thing understood, arising from intellectual power and proceeding from the knowledge of the thing. This conception is what the word articulates, and it is called the word or verbum of the heart."[^57]

The first procession is that of the Word, who proceeds from the Father *secundum emanationem intelligibilem Verbi intelligibilis a dicente, quod manet in ipso*: according to the spiritual procession of the intelligible Word from the one speaking, which remains in Him.[^58] And for this reason, the Word, conceived with complete perfection from all eternity by the Father, is not distinct from Him. It is not an accidental word like ours, but a substantial Word, because intellection in God is not an accident, but is identified with his subsistent being.

Saint Thomas also shows that this intellectual procession merits the name of generation, for a living being is born of a living being and receives a nature like that of the begetter:

"...generation signifies the origin of a living being proceeding from a living principle with which it is united (...) Thus the procession of the Word in God has the nature of generation, because it takes place by intellectual operation, which is a vital operation; and it proceeds from a principle united to it, as we have said; and in it is found the note of likeness, because the conception of the intellect is a likeness of the thing known; and finally, it is of the same nature, because in God to be and to understand are one single thing."[^59]

The second procession is not by way of generation, but of spiration. Just as in us the love of the good proceeds from the knowledge of the good, so there is in God a second procession, which is not a generation, because love, unlike knowledge, does not assimilate its object to itself, but tends towards it:

"In God there are two processions: that of the Word and another (...) In God there is no procession except by reason of operations that do not tend to what is exterior, but remain within the agent. Now in an intellectual nature, actions of this kind are those of the intellect and the will. By the action of the intellect the Word is produced, and by the operation of the will there is also in us another procession, which is the procession of love, whereby the beloved is in the lover, just as by the conception of the word the thing spoken or understood is in the one who understands."[^60]

"The procession of love in God ought not to be called generation. To understand this, let it be noted that the intellect is actuated because the object understood is in it by way of likeness. On the other hand, the will is actuated, not because there is any likeness of the thing willed, but because in the will there is a certain inclination towards what it wills (...) consequently, that which in God proceeds by way of love does not proceed as begotten nor as son, but rather as spirit in the sense of a certain motion or vital impulse."[^61]

#### The Divine Relations

In philosophy, relation is one of the nine categories of accidents. Whereas the other accidents affect the substance by modifying it, relation is not considered in order to the subject in which it inheres, but in order to a term. Relation is that accident *cuius totum esse est ad aliud se habere*. The entire being of relation consists in its reference to another. For this reason, in every relation three things obtain: a) a subject that has an order or reference to another thing; b) a term to which the subject refers; c) a foundation upon which this reference is based.[^62] Since relation does not modify the substance, the concept of relation does not include in itself perfection or imperfection, and therefore can obtain in God.

In God there are real processions; there are also real relations. In a manner analogous to how among human beings the fact of generation grounds the father-son relationship, so in God the generation of the Word grounds the relations of paternity and filiation; the procession of love grounds the relations between active spiration and passive spiration. These relations are not distinct from the divine essence, for in God there are no accidents, and therefore the *esse in* of relation—which is an accident in creatures—is in God of a substantial, not an accidental order:

"These processions are only two, as we have seen: one that follows upon the operation of the intellect, which is the procession of the Word, and another upon the act of the will, which is the procession of love; and for each of these processions we find two opposite relations: one of that which proceeds to its principle, and another of the principle to that which proceeds."[^63]

The divine relations that are opposed to one another are really distinct by the very virtue of this opposition: the Father is not the Son, because no one begets himself; and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son. Thus is made explicit the axiom already familiar to us and which would be reaffirmed at the Council of Florence: *In Deo omnia sunt unum et idem, ubi non obviat relationis oppositio*.[^64] There are, then, in God four real relations: paternity, filiation, active spiration, and passive spiration. Since active spiration is not opposed either to paternity or to filiation, it is not really distinct from them. Four real relations exist in God, and yet only three of these relations are really distinct from one another.

Yet how is it possible that relations identified with the divine essence can nevertheless be really distinct from one another? Is it not true that two things equal to a third are equal to each other? Saint Thomas meets this difficulty by arguing that certain divine relations are really opposed to one another, such as paternity and filiation, with a purely relative opposition. This opposition entails in them a relative distinction. Consequently, the distinction of reason that all the divine relations have with the essence of God, combined with their real opposition to each other, leads us to admit their real identity with the divine essence alongside their real distinction from one another.[^65] Recourse is often had to the example of an equilateral triangle, whose angles are equal and are distinguished solely by the relation of opposition in which they stand to one another.[^66]

#### The Divine Persons

Three relations exist in God that are really opposed to one another, and consequently three Persons exist in God. Saint Thomas takes as the basis of his study of the divine Persons the Boethian definition of person: *rationalis naturae individua substantia* ("an individual substance of a rational nature").[^67] This was the definition with which Richard of Saint Victor felt uncomfortable. Saint Thomas uses it because he sees that it is possible, by means of the corrective measures proper to analogy, to avoid the misunderstandings that troubled Richard. It is necessary to affirm that in God the concept of person is realised with complete perfection. Since person signifies the most perfect mode of being of substance, the concept of person is not repugnant to God, provided that this concept is purified of the imperfections with which we find it realised in created persons.[^68]

In God, person signifies nothing other than relation inasmuch as it is subsistent. Indeed, "relation in God is not like an accident inhering in a subject, but is the divine essence itself, wherefore it is subsistent, since the divine essence subsists. Therefore, just as the Godhead is God, so divine paternity is God the Father, who is a divine Person."[^69] Thus the name "person" designates in God the relation of origin, but considered as subsistent. In other words, person in God is subsistent, incommunicable relation. At the same time, it is solely by their relative opposition that the three divine Persons are distinguished from one another. Saint Thomas's position here is clearly distinguished from that of Saint Bonaventure: whereas Saint Bonaventure considers that the divine Persons are constituted not by relations, but by the notes proper to them, and therefore that in God there exists a distinction of reason between person and relation, for Saint Thomas it is the relations themselves that constitute the Persons, so that if, by an impossible hypothesis, the idea of relation were removed from the Persons, nothing would remain but the idea of a single essence.[^70]

Consequently, the divine notions (properties) and relations are also identical: the notions do nothing other than indicate in abstract form the reason distinguishing one relation from another.[^71] The Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God, but the Father is not the Son, and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son; and yet Father, Son, and Spirit are identical with the divine essence, conceived not only as one single substance, one single intellect, and one single will, but also as one single act of existing (*unum esse*). In their turn, paternity, filiation, and passive spiration are incommunicable relative personalities.[^72]

Here is the schema followed by Saint Thomas in developing these questions:[^73]

**The Notions of the Divine Persons**

- **I. The Father**
  1. Inasmuch as He does not proceed from another Person: **Innascibility**
  2. Inasmuch as from Him proceed:
     - The Son by generation: **Paternity**
     - The Holy Spirit by active spiration: **Common Spiration**
- **II. The Son**
  1. Inasmuch as He proceeds from the Father by generation: **Filiation**
  2. Inasmuch as from Him proceeds the Holy Spirit by active spiration: **Common Spiration**
- **III. The Holy Spirit**
  1. Inasmuch as He proceeds from the Father and the Son by active spiration: **Procession** (passive spiration)
  2. Inasmuch as from Him proceeds no other divine Person (this is not a notion or personal property)

The distinction of the Persons is manifested to us in the names we give to each of them: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. We also distinguish these Persons by means of the notions or distinctive marks that we apply to the divine Persons, such as paternity, filiation, spiration. The notions by which we are accustomed to speak of the divine Persons are five: innascibility and paternity referring to the Father; active spiration referring to the Father and the Son; filiation referring to the Son; procession or passive spiration referring to the Holy Spirit. Of these five, only four are real relations, for innascibility is not a real relation; four are properties of a Person, for active spiration is common to the Father and the Son; three are personal or constitutive properties of the Persons.

The proper names of the first Person are Father and unbegotten (*ingeneratus*) or innascible, or principle without principle.[^74] The first name corresponds to the real relation of paternity; the second corresponds to the fact that it is proper to the Father not to proceed from any Person. In constructing the theology of the Father, Saint Thomas departs from the position of Saint Bonaventure, who conceived the innascibility of the Father as something positive and as the reason for the fruitfulness of his paternity.[^75]

The proper names of the second Person are Son, Word, and Image. Saint Thomas pays special attention to the designation "Word", following the Augustinian thought that the meanings of Son and Word are identified—that is, that the Son is Son inasmuch as He is Word, and is Word inasmuch as He is Son. Here is how he describes the rationale behind these various names: "In the name Word the same property is included as in that of Son, and hence the aphorism of Saint Augustine: *He is called Word for the same reason that He is called Son*. Indeed, the very birth of the Son, which is his personal property, is designated by various names attributed to the Son to express his perfection in different ways. And so, to designate Him as connatural with the Father, He is called Son; as co-eternal, Splendour; as entirely like, He is called Image; to signify that He is immaterially begotten, He is called Word. And this because it has not been possible to find a single suitable name to designate all these things."[^76]

Among the proper names of the third Person, Saint Thomas dwells upon the study of these three: Holy Spirit, Love, and Gift.[^77] The Holy Spirit is of the same nature as the Father and the Son, but cannot be called begotten by them. Richard of Saint Victor judged that this was so because the nature that the Spirit received from the Father and the Son was not fruitful. Saint Bonaventure judged that this was so because the Holy Spirit proceeds not according to nature, but according to the will. Saint Thomas provides another reason: this is so—he argues—because love does not tend to reproduce the lover in the beloved, but tends to create in the lover an inclination towards the beloved.[^78] Saint Thomas's position on the question of the *Filioque*—a question in which he was called upon to take part in his day—is clear and coherent with his entire Trinitarian doctrine: the axiom that in God everything is identical except where an opposition of relation obtains leads him to affirm that the Holy Spirit would not be distinguished from the Son if He did not proceed from Him, and therefore that, since He is a Person distinct from the Son, He proceeds not only from the Father, but also from the Son:

"It is necessary to say that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son, because if He did not proceed from the Son, He would not be distinguished from Him. And this is understood from what has already been explained. It is not possible to say that the divine Persons are distinguished by anything absolute (...) The divine Persons are distinguished from one another solely by relations. Now relations cannot distinguish Persons from one another except insofar as they are opposed (...) If, then, in the Son and in the Holy Spirit there were found only the two relations whereby each refers to the Father, these would not be opposed relations, just as the two relations whereby the Father refers to them are not opposed; whence it follows that, just as the Person of the Father is one, so also the Son and the Holy Spirit would be a single Person with two relations opposed to the two of the Father (...) Therefore no other recourse remains than to say either that the Son proceeds from the Holy Spirit, which no one says, or that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son, which is what we confess."[^79]

This is the argument that had been employed since Saint Augustine. After analysing what is specific to each Person, Saint Thomas returns to the treatment of equality and unity among them. The three Persons are equal because they have the same nature, numerically one, and a single *esse*, a single act of existing.[^80] For this reason, in God begetting is no more perfect than being begotten, because eternal generation does not cause the divine nature of the Son, but merely communicates it to Him. That is to say, the nature of the Son is no less uncreated than that of the Father or that of the Holy Spirit. It can thus be seen that the relations of the three Persons constitute the expression of the highest intellectual life and the highest life of love. Goodness is essentially communicative, and the higher its order, the more abundantly it communicates itself. The Father communicates his whole infinite and indivisible nature to his Son without multiplying it, and through his Son to the Holy Spirit. Consequently, the three Persons comprehend one another with total and perfect intimacy, since they know and love one another with the same act of understanding and of loving. The entire personality of the Father is his reference to the Son and to the Spirit; the entire personality of the Son is his reference to the Father and to the Spirit; the entire personality of the Spirit is his reference to the Father and to the Son. Each of the Persons is distinguished from the others solely by his relation of opposition, so that the very thing that differentiates them and constitutes them as Persons is the very thing that unites them, referring them to one another in the unity of their nature and of their act of being.[^81]

#### The Historical Importance of the Thomistic Trinitarian Synthesis

With Saint Thomas Aquinas the structuring of the treatise *De Trinitate* attains a great formal perfection that makes it the supreme exponent of Scholastic organisation and a universal point of reference. His influence was therefore of the first order in subsequent theological endeavour. This influence forms part, moreover, of the global influence acquired by the whole of Thomistic thought in subsequent centuries, above all because of its capacity to structure the broad tradition that preceded it with a clear and rigorous order. It was also strengthened by the profound commentators who followed him.

It would be unjust if the perfect logical structuring of the Thomistic treatise on God caused one to forget the profound biblical and patristic spirit that permeates it. It is characteristic of the method employed by Thomas—and herein once again the influence of Augustine is evident—that the Aquinas accentuates more than his predecessors the revealed character of the Trinitarian affirmation and distinguishes it unequivocally from the rational concept of God.[^82]

To this characteristic must be added another of supreme importance in the theological task: the connection Saint Thomas establishes between Trinitarian faith and the message of salvation. The entire structuring of the Trinity in its immanent aspect is at the service of the theology of the divine missions. The doctrine of the missions constitutes the goal and key of his structuring of Trinitarian theology. From the very outset, his Trinitarian doctrine is at the service of the consideration of the salvation of the human race, "which is fulfilled in the Son made man and in the gift of the Holy Spirit."[^83] Saint Thomas concludes his treatise on the Trinity with the question devoted to the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul, that is, with the study of the relation of the Trinitarian mystery to the salvation of man. This question forms an essential part of the structuring of the entire treatise, to such an extent that without it the treatise as a whole is misunderstood.[^84]

Considering the contributions belonging to the strict Trinitarian domain, two points should be highlighted in which the thought of Saint Thomas decisively influenced even those authors who do not accept the whole of his thought:

a) The affirmation that the Word proceeds *secundum intellectum*, as opposed to Saint Bonaventure's view that the Word proceeds *secundum naturam*. Here Thomas remains far more faithful to Saint Augustine than to Saint Bonaventure, and at the same time is more in conformity with the revealed data pointing to the operation of knowledge—the Word and the Speech—as the proper mode of proceeding of the Son;

b) The affirmation that the divine Persons are not constituted and distinguished by the notes proper to them, but solely by relations of origin. This allows him to establish more firmly the equality among the Persons and, above all, to show how the two terms of the relation of opposition characterise one another mutually. Thus, for example, the Father is Father in begetting the Son, to the extent that He begets the Son, and in this sense the Son forms part—so to speak—of the paternity of the Father. We shall see that the same holds true for the procession of the Holy Spirit.

[^16]: Here are the historical antecedents of the question and of Saint Anselm's book. From the earliest times of the Church, faith in the Holy Spirit as a divine Person has been professed. The Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed solemnly professes the divinity of the Holy Spirit and his proceeding from the Father. In the West, from the *Quicumque* Creed onwards, not only is the divinity of the Holy Spirit confessed, but also his proceeding from the Father and the Son. At the Third Council of Toledo (589), the addition of the *Filioque* (*qui ex Patre Filioque procedit*) was introduced into the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed, thereby consecrating this doctrine of the proceeding of the Holy Spirit also from the Son. This formula passed from Spain to Italy and France. Pope Leo III († 816), although in agreement with the doctrine, opposed the introduction of the *Filioque* into the Church of Rome for fear of the reaction of the Greeks; nevertheless, this introduction of the *Filioque* eventually became universal throughout the Western Church. Patriarch Photius in 867 revived the question of the *Filioque*. According to him, Westerners had no right to augment the Creed on their own; moreover, the doctrine that makes the Holy Spirit proceed from the Father and the Son introduces a double principle into the divinity. In 1054, Patriarch Michael Cerularius renewed the accusations against the Latin Church, and from that point the schism was consummated. Naturally, before the rupture became definitive, various attempts at rapprochement were made. One of these was the Council of Bari held in 1098, convened by Pope Urban II. Saint Anselm's intervention at the council was of immense brilliance. The arguments employed there are gathered in *De processione Spiritus Sancti*.
