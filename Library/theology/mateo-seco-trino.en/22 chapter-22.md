## Chapter 22: The Father, Principle Without Principle {#mateo-seco-trino.22}

There exist only two immanent processions in God: that of the Son and that of the Holy Spirit. The Father proceeds from no Person. Nor does He proceed from the divine essence. For this reason He is called *principium sine principio* (principle without principle). He is the principle of all things in the most absolute and universal of senses: He is the principle of the Trinitarian mystery; He is likewise the principle of creation and of the history of salvation. All things proceed from Him and all things are called back to Him. From Him every other fatherhood in heaven and on earth receives its reality (cf. Eph 3:15).

The fatherhood of God extends to all men; yet, in Jesus Christ, this fatherhood is revealed in a radically new manner and with an unfathomable depth. God is the *Abba* of Jesus to such a degree that the Church understands Jesus' filiation to imply that, in the bosom of the Godhead, the relations of paternity and filiation exist in their most absolute and profound form.

The filial relationship of Jesus to the Father is so absolute that the entire being of Jesus—in His divinity and in His humanity—is nothing other than filiation. His personal mode of being consists exclusively in this filiation. In union with Jesus Christ, Christian life is completely oriented towards the Father, since it has our divine filiation as its foundation.[^1] Christians are baptised in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit (cf. Mt 28:19); Christian prayer begins "in the name of the Father" and is addressed to Him through our Lord Jesus Christ.

The history of salvation has its origin in the Father. The mystery of redemption is likewise the initiative of the Father: it is the Father who sends the Son and who indicates to Him the path to follow. During His earthly journey, the Son, obedient unto death, even death on a cross (Phil 2:8), has no other food than to accomplish the will of the Father (cf. Jn 4:34).[^2]

The person of the Father appears mysterious to us, just as the unfathomable abyss of the first principle is mysterious. Yet in the Son, the Father has definitively revealed Himself to men. When Philip asks Jesus to show them the Father, the Lord responds to the request by saying: *Philip, he who has seen me has seen the Father* (Jn 14:9). Whoever wishes to know the Father finds His living and perfect Image in Jesus. In Him—especially in the mystery of His death and resurrection—the face of God is ineffably revealed.[^3]

We have already considered the teaching of Sacred Scripture concerning the Father[^4] and the development this teaching received in the tradition of the Church. Some of the questions we have already considered in the historical context in which they arose, such as the name *agennetos*.[^5] In this chapter we confine ourselves to presenting a summary of the principal theological assertions concerning the Person of the Father.

### The Father, Fount and Origin of the Whole Trinity

The first assertion is that the Father is the fount and origin of the whole Trinity (*fons et origo totius Trinitatis*). The Father does not proceed from another divine person. Nor does He proceed from the divine essence, as though this were an impersonal principle that, upon generating, multiplied itself by dividing into Father and Son. The Father is a principle without principle (*principium sine principio*). The texts of the Magisterium are sufficiently explicit in this regard, and in enumerating them we already find sketched the principal traits of the theology of the Father:

In the Athanasian Creed it is confessed that the Father is "neither made, nor created, nor begotten."[^6] The Eleventh Council of Toledo adds that the Father receives His origin from no one, and is therefore the fount and origin of the whole Trinity. The Father begets the Son from His own substance, which is ineffable; He begets one who is God equal to Himself. He is Father before all ages, so that there is no moment in which the Father is without the Son; nor is there any moment in which the Son did not exist. The Father is God, but receives His divinity from no one; the Son is God, but receives His divinity from the Father.[^7] The Fourth Lateran Council specifies that it is not the divine essence that begets, but the Father.[^8] The Council of Florence adds that "whatever the Father is or has, He does not receive from another, but has from Himself; and He is principle without principle."[^9]

Thus, the Father is the fount of the whole Trinity and the foundation and principle of its unity. This unity is the consequence of the Father communicating to the Son and to the Holy Spirit His own substance totally—without any division—so that the Son and the Spirit have numerically the same substance as the Father. The Father is the fontal fullness (*plenitudo fontalis*), which communicates itself so intimately to those who proceed from Him that they constitute with Him one and the same God. The Father is identified with the divine essence; yet it is not the essence that begets the Son, but the Father, who begets by communicating His essence to the Son.[^10] The person of the Father is the one who begets the Son (*principium quod*), handing over to Him His own substance (*principium quo*), that is, the divine essence.

In the infinity of God, His paternity is infinite. He is Father infinitely. This means that the being of the Father is wholly committed to the generation of the Son. In turn, the Son exists only in this begetting. The paternity of the Father is absolute, and the filiation of the Son is likewise total. In the mystery of the divine fatherhood, the Son is essential to the life of the Father, to His eternal subsistence as Father—that is, as person. The relationship of the Father with Christ, His incarnate Son, is in turn the expression within the world of this eternal mystery, for the Word communicates to His humanity His own personal mode of existing within the Trinity.[^11] The same must be said of the procession of the Spirit. In this ineffable procession, the Father gives His being indivisibly to the Holy Spirit, who for this very reason is also God.

### The Names of the First Person

Christian faith emphatically affirms the unity of the Persons, since they are identified with the divine essence; it affirms with equal vigour the personal distinction existing among them. There exists only one God in three really distinct persons. This real distinction entails that we too can distinguish the Persons by employing names that, in their notional sense, belong properly to only one divine person. With respect to the Father, these names are Principle, Father, and Unbegotten (*Ingenitus*).

#### a) Principle

The term "principle" can be understood in an essential sense or in a notional sense—that is, it may be understood of the divine essence or of the person of the Father.[^12] God is indeed the principle and cause of the entire extra-divine world. The three Persons are a single principle of the creature.[^13] Taken in a notional sense, the term "principle" applies properly only to the Father, as being the unprincipled principle (*principium non principiatum*). The Son, by contrast, is a principled principle (*principium principiatum*). It should be emphasised that when it is said that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from a single principle,[^14] it is considered that the Spirit proceeds from the Son inasmuch as He is Son—that is, inasmuch as He is principled, for the Son is *principium de principio* (principle from principle).[^15]

Sacred Scripture does not apply the term "principle" to the Father. Nevertheless, it clearly teaches that the other two Persons proceed from Him. Hence theology, both Greek and Latin, has used the name "principle" to refer to what is proper to the Father. To the Father belongs in a unique manner the name "principle".

This name must be understood as stripped of all imperfection, both with respect to the Father and with respect to the other two Persons. In applying it to the Father, it signifies only that from Him proceed the Son and the Holy Spirit. It does not signify, however, that there is in God priority and posteriority, superiority or inferiority; it signifies solely that among the Persons there exists an eternal relation of origin.[^16]

We are already familiar with the difference between Greeks and Latins when it comes to the use of the names "cause" and "principle". The Greeks use the names "principle" and "cause" almost synonymously, while making it clear that in God there is neither before nor after, and that the generation of the Word and the procession of the Spirit are eternal. "Cause" thus appears as synonymous with "principle". By this usage, they highlight the radicality with which the Father is said to be the principle of the whole Trinity. The Latins almost always avoid applying the name "cause" to the Father, because among them this term usually designates that which proceeds as an effect and which, consequently, is really distinct from its cause with respect to its essence and its being.

The Greeks not only call the Father principle or cause, but also call the Son and the Spirit principled and caused. Yet they point out that both the Son and the Holy Spirit lack a principle of creation: "To be without principle of origin belongs only to the Father; but to be without principle of creation, without principle of time, belongs also to the Son, who has not been made, nor created, and has no beginning."[^17] Here lies the core of the question, beyond mere matters of language. The Son proceeds from the Father in an origin without temporal beginning, in a genuine and uncreated procession. It is a real procession, which transcends the limits within which we conceive the relationship between a principle and that which is principled by it. It is a procession that requires that we take in all its force the Augustinian assertion that "the Father is the principle of the whole divinity,"[^18] that is, He is the principle of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. But He is an eternal principle, since the Son and the Spirit are likewise eternal.

#### b) Father

The name "Father" can likewise be taken in an essential sense or in a notional sense. God is the Father of men, especially of the chosen people, of the king, and of the Messiah. We have seen it frequently used in this way in Sacred Scripture. It also appears used on a different level by Jesus Christ. In using the expression "Father" when addressing God, Jesus signifies the intratrinitarian paternity of the first Person, to the point that this name can be applied properly only to Him.

The first Person is called Father exclusively with respect to the Son. Although He is also the principle of the Holy Spirit, He cannot be called the father of the Holy Spirit. The name "Father" signifies not only that the Father is the principle of the Son, but also the manner in which the first Person is the principle of the second: by means of an authentic generation. The Father truly begets the Son in a vital act analogous to human generation. What is more, He begets Him in supreme perfection.

When speaking of paternity and filiation in God, it is necessary to divest these concepts of all imperfection—for example, of any notion of priority or posteriority, superiority and inferiority. Consequently, only in God do paternity and filiation exist in their infinite perfection. The Father eternally begets the Son not only in equality of nature, but gives Him His own substance, to the point that the Son possesses the same and indivisible substance of the Father. He is so equal to the Father that He is distinguished from Him solely by the relation of filiation:

> "The fact that in divine generation the form of the begetter and of the begotten is numerically the same, whereas in creatures it is not the same in number, but only in species, shows that generation—and consequently paternity—is more perfect in God than in creatures. Therefore, the very fact that in God the begetter and the begotten are distinguished exclusively by relation proves how true divine generation and paternity are."[^19]

The Father begets the Son with infinite perfection not only because this generation is eternal and without any change, but because this generation has as its term a Son so perfectly equal to the Father that He is one and the same God with Him.

It has repeatedly been said that the Father was never without the Son, nor the Son without the Father. This statement entails the fact that paternity cannot be conceived as something that accrues to a Father already constituted in His personal being; rather, the Father exists as person insofar as He begets the Son. The Person of the Father is constituted by His relation to the Son, in the same way that the person of the Son is constituted by His relation to the Father. The Father thus appears to us turned in all His majesty towards the Son. He is and lives through and for the Other.

In eternal generation, the Father communicates the whole divinity to the Son, so that the Son in turn possesses the totality of the divinity; the Son does not "participate" in the divinity of the Father, but possesses fully—by donation—the same and unique divinity of the Father.[^20] The perfect equality between Father and Son springs not only from the infinite simplicity of the divine substance that the Father gives to the Son in begetting Him, but also from the infinite perfection of the paternal act of begetting. The Father gives Himself totally to the Son, so that the Son has all that the Father has. When Jesus affirms *he who has seen me has seen the Father* (Jn 14:9), He is affirming that His being as Son reflects the Father most faithfully, and that this filiation is also expressed in His human life.

It was already pointed out in the preceding chapter that the Word proceeds from the Father by way of intellect, whereas the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son by way of love.[^21] Indeed, the names used by Sacred Scripture to designate the procession of the Son invite us to understand this procession by analogy with the procession of the mental word, and the procession of the Holy Spirit with the procession of love. It is not uncommon in contemporary theology, while maintaining this analogy in its entirety, to note that love is also present in the generation of the Son. The intention behind this observation is to avoid a narrow interpretation of the psychological analogy according to which the Son would proceed exclusively from the intellect, whilst the Holy Spirit would proceed exclusively from love.[^22] It is adduced, among other reasons, that God is essentially Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8), and that generation is an act of generosity and self-giving—of love—whereby the Father communicates to the Son the entire divine substance, that is, all the infinite essential Love.[^23]

This does not mean that the procession of the Son can be understood as a procession that takes place through the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit is not the love by which the Father begets the Son; rather, He is the love that proceeds from the Father and the Son.[^24] The Son does not proceed from the Holy Spirit, because it is the Holy Spirit who proceeds from Him.[^25] When it is remarked that Love is present in the generation of the Son, something very different is being said: namely, that love is present in the reason why the Father begets the Son, and in the very act with which He begets Him, which consists in communicating to the Son the divine substance, which is essentially love.

The doctrine of the Holy Spirit finds a more adequate framework in the light of this consideration. Here is a suggestive formulation:

> "The love of the Father that unfolds in generation elicits as a response the love of the Son: the Son adheres to the Father as completely as the Father adheres to Him. This love that unites the Father and the Son constitutes the third divine Person, the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit arises as a person-communion, a person in whom the most perfect love is consummated. The Holy Spirit likewise possesses all the divine perfection of the Father. He receives it from the Father and the Son, for the Son is in Himself the gift of the Father. For this reason it can be said that in Him love is consummated: all the power of divine love, that of the Father and of the Son, finds its term in Him. The person of the Spirit results from the gift that the Father makes of Himself through His Son."[^26]

Saint Thomas already observes that the Father begets the Son "with will"—that is, "neither compelled, nor forced by natural necessity."[^27] Love therefore accompanies the generation of the Word.[^28] Hence Saint Bonaventure considers that charity should be appropriated to the Father.[^29] Love likewise accompanies the generation of Jesus by Mary. In Mary, this love manifests itself in a special voluntariness and in the privilege of virginity.[^30] Contemporary theology regards the mode of Jesus' birth as related to His eternal generation:

> "No man was ever conceived and begotten with so much love as the Son of Man. His earthly generation is a pale reflection of His mysterious and eternal generation, but it is its closest reflection. Patristic tradition regards the virginal birth as the only one fitting for Him who is spiritually born of the Father in eternity. The more spiritual this birth in the flesh of Jesus is, the more it will resemble the eternal birth of the Son of God. Divine maternity is the created participation closest to the Paternity of the heavenly Father with respect to Jesus Christ, for its term is identical."[^31]

The word "Father" is exclusively relational: the Father is Father with respect to the Son. Active generation is proper and exclusive to the first Person; hence the name "Father" is proper to Him. "That whereby the Person of the Father is distinguished from all the others is paternity. Therefore, the proper name of the Father is this—Father—which signifies paternity."[^32]

This entails underlining paternity as constitutive of the Person of the Father, and indeed in its aspect of total and perfect self-giving, just as the filiation of the Son is perfect in every respect. The Father is essentially and infinitely Father. It cannot be admitted that there was ever a moment when the Father had no Son. Father and Son are co-eternal; without the Son, the Father would not be a person. The position of Arius and Eunomius in asserting that there was a time when the Son did not exist is entirely devoid of meaning, not only from the standpoint of filiation, but even from the standpoint of the divine paternity. The Father did not begin to be Father; He is Father throughout all eternity.[^33]

The analogical character of all that we have been saying thus becomes evident. In the human realm, fatherhood accrues to a man when the person has already attained his perfection; in the divine realm, the person of the Father is constituted by His paternity—that is, by the generation of the Son.[^34] Paternity is not something that accrues to the Father, but constitutes Him. He is essentially and absolutely paternity. Paternity in God is so sublime that it surpasses all knowledge. The Father is He who exists totally and absolutely for the Other. It may be said that the Father is Father to the extent that He gives Himself, for it is in the generation of the Son that the Person of the Father is constituted. For this reason Saint Gregory of Nazianzus writes:

> "The Father is more truly Father than those whom we know as such. He is Father in a unique and singular manner, not corporally. He alone is Father without any union. He is Father of One alone, the Only-Begotten. Without having previously been a son, He is solely Father. He is Father in everything and totally, something that cannot be affirmed of us. He is Father from the beginning to the end."[^35]

As essential paternity, the Father is loving self-giving to the Son, to whom He communicates His love. The Father is love that gives itself; the Son is love received. In their mutual union they spirate the Spirit.[^36]

#### c) Unbegotten

The Father is also called *agennetos*, unbegotten or ungenerated, because He proceeds from no one. We have already treated this name, especially in connection with the Eunomian controversy.[^37] In itself it signifies that which is not begotten. In principle, it could also be applied to the Holy Spirit, who proceeds from the Father, but is not begotten. Nevertheless, in customary theological language it is applied exclusively to the Father, signifying by this term not only that He is not begotten, but that He does not proceed from any other Person either. From this perspective, only the Father is unbegotten.

In this regard, the distinction made by Saint Basil between absolute names and relative names is essential: absolute names designate the divine essence; relative names designate what is proper to each Person. In this context, *agennetos* is a relative name—that is, a name referring to the Person of the Father, and not to the divine essence.

Eunomius applied this term equally to the divine essence and to the person of the Father. Moreover, he asserted that unbegottenness (*agennesia*) adequately defined the divine essence. In fact, he made the concepts of *agennesia* and aseity synonymous, identifying the Father's innascibility with the fact that in God there is nothing caused, and that He exists of Himself. Following this identification, the conclusion was logical: the Son is begotten, therefore He cannot be God. Only the innascible is God; the Begotten, consequently, can only be the first creature of the Father. Eunomius denied not only the filiation of the Son, but also the paternity of the Father. The Greek Fathers, especially the Cappadocians, showed that *agennesia* is not equivalent to aseity, and that innascibility is therefore a characteristic property of the Father, without it being necessary on that account to say that the Son is a second-rate God. In begetting Him, the Father does not communicate His innascibility to the Son, but He does communicate His aseity to Him.[^38]

After the Eunomian controversy, the term *agennetos* was reserved for the person of the Father as a proper name. But in the Eunomian controversy, upon applying *agennetos* to the divine essence, another question arose: is *agennesia* to be understood in a negative sense—as the mere signification that the Father has no origin—or in a positive sense, that is, as pointing, within its negative phrasing, to a positive property of the Father? The different answers given to this question mark one of the most typical divergences of approach in Trinitarian theology between Greek theologians and Latin theologians.

Broadly speaking, Greek theologians understand the innascibility of the Father in a positive sense: they do not understand it negatively, as the simple denial that the first Person has an origin, but consider that innascibility of the Father as the ground of the vital activities of generation and spiration. According to this conception, the Father would be Father by virtue of being innascible—that is, He would not be constituted by His paternity, but by a distinct property. This approach was taken up by Richard of Saint Victor († 1173), Alexander of Hales († between 1231 and 1237), and Saint Bonaventure († 1274), who understand the term "unbegotten" as the underlying reason for the paternity of the Father. Saint Bonaventure understands the *innascibilitas* of the Father as the original fullness of His intratrinitarian fruitfulness. For this reason he prefers to speak of the primacy of the Father (*primitas*) rather than of His innascibility.[^39]

Saint Augustine, on the other hand, interprets innascibility in a negative sense: it would signify nothing other than the mere negation that the Father proceeds from another. According to this, the Father is Father because He begets, not because He is innascible. Innascibility would indicate nothing more than the simple fact of not being begotten.[^40] This is also the sense in which Thomas Aquinas understands it. According to him, "unbegotten" means solely that the Father proceeds from no person.

And yet, Saint Augustine and Saint Thomas likewise teach that Unbegotten is a proper name of the Father, since we understand simple things in a negative manner:[^41] "The Father, with respect to the Persons proceeding from Him, is known through paternity and common spiration; but inasmuch as He is principle without principle, He is known because He does not proceed from another; and this is what belongs to the property of innascibility, signified by the word unbegotten."[^42]

The Father is the principle without principle, the principle of the Life that God has in Himself. This life—that is, the very divinity—the Father possesses in communion with the Son and with the Spirit, who are consubstantial with Him, because He has bestowed upon them His own substance.[^43]
