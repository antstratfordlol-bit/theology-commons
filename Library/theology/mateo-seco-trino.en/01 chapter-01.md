## Chapter 1: The One and Transcendent God in the Old Testament {#mateo-seco-trino.1}

Christian doctrine concerning God has a long history, for its roots are deeply planted in the teachings of the Old Testament. Indeed, when Jesus speaks of his God or of his Father (cf. Mt 18:35; Jn 17:3), he is referring to the God of his people, that is to say, to the God in whom Israel believes and whom she adores: the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob (cf. Mt 22:32). He is the very same God of whom the Epistle to the Hebrews affirms that he spoke of old to men in many and various ways by the prophets, and now at last has spoken to us by his Son (cf. Heb 1:1). In turn, the God spoken of in the Old Testament is, first and foremost, the God in whom the fathers trusted (Ps 22:5), the one who intervened time and again in the history of the people of Israel. He is a personal being who, properly speaking, is not a human "discovery", but Someone who has stepped forth to encounter man and has spoken to Israel, revealing his will and manifesting his designs (cf. Hos 1–2). Christian doctrine on God—like the teaching of the Old Testament—is not presented, therefore, as the fruit of a human conquest, but as the outcome of a self-revelation that God has made to mankind, and of the reception that men have accorded to that revelation. God himself is the primary author of Scripture, and in this sense he is likewise the one who furnishes the data for theological reflection upon God.

According to the testimony of the Bible, the concept of God contained within it has been forged through successive encounters between God and men—that is, in the conjunction between divine self-manifestation and human reception throughout the course of history. Given the present state of biblical studies, it is at times no easy task to offer an exhaustive reading of these divine self-manifestations, for the chronological sequence of the events narrated does not always coincide with the order in which they were committed to writing.

In any case, however interesting historical investigations into the religious phenomenon of Israel and even into the religious phenomenon of primitive Christianity may be, the attention of the theologian reflecting upon the mystery of God is not centred primarily upon how the understanding of that mystery developed within the people of Israel, but rather upon what fundamental features God has manifested of himself as they have been crystallised in the sacred writings.[^1]

Within this perspective, one must affirm that it is indeed possible to present a synthesis of sacred teaching concerning this one God who has manifested himself to men—first partially, and then in all fullness in Christ. From Abraham to Saint Paul, by way of Moses and the Prophets, the God of whom the Bible speaks is ever the One who called the Patriarchs in order to constitute for himself a chosen people, and who, precisely to realise this election, created heaven and earth in the beginning (Gen 1:1), tirelessly pursuing his design of drawing near to mankind, a design already contained in the blessing bestowed upon Abraham, in whom all the nations of the earth were blessed. Christ, indeed, redeemed us (...) "so that in Christ Jesus the blessing of Abraham might come upon the Gentiles, that we might receive the promise of the Spirit through faith" (Gal 3:13–14).

### Uniqueness of Old Testament Doctrine on God

Among all the peoples of the earth, Israel presents herself with a unique and singular personality regarding the question of God, to the extent that one may affirm that what gives strength and unity to the Old Testament is the affirmation of the sovereignty of God. God is always above all things and all events, and is, moreover, the foundation of the whole of creation. Whatever exists outside of him subsists solely by the will of God.[^2]

According to the Old Testament, the people of Israel owes its existence to free divine election; it is the bearer of the messianic Promise. As has already been noted, in this dialogue between God and his people, it is not the people, even in its most eminent figures, that takes the initiative; rather, it is God who has resolved to establish his Covenant with it and has brought it to pass. For this reason, the Old Testament presents itself throughout as a *revelatio in fieri*, as a progressive revelation that God makes of his will, of his designs, and, in making it, reveals certain features of what He himself is.[^3]

This makes God a being who is universally near. Nevertheless, this divine closeness implies no loss of his transcendence. From the opening paragraphs of Genesis, the distance existing between God and the world is thrown into relief: the world was created by God in a beginning, "in the beginning" (Gen 1:1); God, by contrast, who is its creator, has neither beginning nor end. God neither stands on the same level as the world, nor can he be confused with it. Yet at the same time, this distinction does not signify remoteness or absence. Quite the contrary: the God who reveals himself to Israel is intimately related to the world, to man, and to his history. He is near and, at the same time, sovereignly free: he is a God who can never be manipulated or grasped by man. Furthermore, despite being the God of the fathers, he is never identified with the clan or the nation, but is presented as the God of all men and of all creation.[^4]

The Covenant constitutes the central fact of Old Testament reflection.[^5] If Scripture speaks of the creation of the world or the primeval history, it is not out of speculative curiosity regarding the origin of the cosmos, but because both creation and divine providence are bound up with the Covenant. Even unto inanimate beings extends the blessing of messianic times (Gen 8:22; Jer 33:20–25), whose ultimate purpose is the salvation of humanity, beginning with Israel. When Saint Paul speaks of the salvation bestowed in Christ, taking up this theology, he likewise refers to material creation, which groans in travail, awaiting the manifestation of the children of God (Rom 8:20–22). Indeed, salvation reaches even material creation, for human salvation entails not only the redemption of our body, but also the liberation of the material creature, which will be brought to its final perfection (Rom 8:23). Rather, this was the salvific plan established from the beginning in Christ, for all things were created through him and for him; he is before all things, and in him all things hold together (Col 1:16–17).

The Bible opens with what might be called an eleven-chapter prologue (Gen 1–11), which narrates the creation of the world and the dawn of history. From these first pages it is evident that the principal dilemma of history is man's acceptance or rejection of God; that is to say, it is clear that the centre of biblical history is man's relationship with God. On God's part, this relationship is described as an approach, a call, an election. This is seen, for instance, in Noah (Gen 9:9–17) and in Abraham (Gen 15:17). It is manifested in a pre-eminent way in the covenant established at Sinai (Ex 19–24).

From the theology of the covenant, one can better understand a fundamental element of the entire Old Testament experience of God: the effective character of divine revelation. God reveals himself by acting, saving, liberating. His word is omnipotent and creative (Gen 1:3); it is efficacious and does not return to him void (Isa 55:11).

This efficacy of the divine word is accompanied by another essential feature in the biblical concept of God: Yahweh is not a capricious and arbitrary being, but Someone in whom one can always place one's trust. He is the eternally faithful One. The covenant with Yahweh is, above all, a promise, which entails clear ethical demands. There is no uncertainty whatever in Sacred Scripture on this point. This also brings with it the fact that the Old Testament image of the God of the Covenant maintains an immediate relation with history: one does not merely believe in God as a theoretical truth, but, first and foremost, trusts in him as a provident power and Lord of history; one believes—one is certain—that He will save the people and redeem history.

Biblical teaching regarding God furthermore excludes the notion, widely prevalent among Israel's neighbouring peoples, that there exists a natural kinship between the national deity and his worshippers, whether a kind of blood relationship or a territorial bond tying the god to the land and its inhabitants. The religion of Israel is a religion of election, understood in the sense that this divine election distinguishes Israel's religion from natural religion.[^6]

### Progressive Self-Revelation of God

Biblical doctrine concerning God unfolds in step with the narratives of divine action. Rather than describing him—God is above all description and imagery—what the Bible does is narrate his intervention in history, and in doing so, it points out the divine attributes that are revealed in those actions.

Hence biblical statements display an extraordinary variety, according to the diversity of situations in which God intervenes, and also according to the diversity of epochs and persons to whom God addresses himself. It must not be forgotten, indeed, that these writings record a religious experience spanning more than eight hundred years.

It is by no means easy, therefore, to present a ready-made, fully articulated image of God as offered to us in the Bible. This would prove even more difficult were one to attempt to present, not simply biblical teaching on God, but the evolution that the idea of God underwent within the consciousness of the people of Israel.[^7] That task, moreover, belongs to another branch of theological studies, and is customarily dealt with when studying the history of the religion of Israel.[^8]

As far as speculative theology is concerned, however, it is entirely possible—and indeed obligatory—to indicate the principal and definitive features with which the image of God stands out in the Old Testament. It is the very same image of God to which Christian faith appeals from the outset, for Jesus, when speaking of his Father, constantly points back to the Old Testament. His God—whose image he delineates—is the God believed in, known, and venerated in the Old Testament. For Jesus as well, God is the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob (cf. Mt 22:32).

The revelation that God makes of himself possesses a progressive character, just like salvation history itself. The Old Testament writings offer a notion of God that underwent a long and complex enrichment. Following the order in which biblical history is narrated to us, five stages may broadly be distinguished in the development of biblical doctrine on God.[^9]

A first stage corresponds to the nomadic tribes, where God accompanies the patriarchs as their protector and the defender of the clan. He is not presented as a local divinity, for he is not tied to a sanctuary or to any special place. Later he will always be spoken of as the God whom the fathers worshipped. God is called the God of "my father", of "your father", of "his father" (Gen 31:5, 29; 43:23; 46:3; 50:17; Ex 3:6; 15:2; 18:4). Perhaps for these patriarchs God had no proper name, but was the God who had revealed himself to the ancestor—that is to say, he was the God of their father. The divine promises themselves are in keeping with the fundamental desires of semi-nomadic herdsmen: offspring ensuring the continuity of the clan, and a land where they may permanently settle.[^10]

The period prior to Israel's settlement in Canaan may be considered a second stage. It is the period in which Israel's religious tradition situates Yahweh's Covenant with the people. Yahweh manifests himself to Moses as the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, the God of Jacob, the God of your fathers, El Shaddai (Ex 3:6, 13, 15; 6:3). However, whereas the God of the Fathers had no proper name, the God who establishes the Covenant at Sinai does have a name: He is "I am who I am" (Ex 3:14). Here, too, God is not bound to any geographical location. He guides the people everywhere. Exodus 3:9–15 underlines, at one and the same time, continuity with the faith of the fathers and the novelty expressed by the name that God has given to himself.

In a third stage, the action of Yahweh, God of Israel, appears closely bound to a political institution: the kingdom of Judah and the Davidic dynasty. The messianic prophecies bear eloquent witness to this union. However, this is also the era of a vast movement animated by the prophets, who react and strive to liberate Yahwist faith from political and national boundaries, bringing to light its moral demands and deepening its monolatric character towards the clearest affirmation of monotheism.[^11]

Bound up with this situation is what may be termed a fourth stage. It is known to us above all through the prophetic oracles, which increasingly accentuate faith in one single, transcendent God, mighty and full of mercy. He is a God who, being the unique Creator of all, loves all men. The whole earth is full of his glory. His presence is universal (Isa 49:6, 26). These are the oracles contained in the books from Amos to Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Isaiah.

In the Persian period, what may be regarded as a fifth stage opens. Prominent within it is the sapiential reflection on Yahweh, on his existence, and on his providence. The books of Job and Ecclesiastes, and a large part of the Psalter—so rich in reflections on God and his providence, on his being and his attributes—are clear instances of this. One might say that in this period the theology of God attains its full development. Alongside these thinkers, the voice of apocalyptic literature also rises, preoccupied with reconciling faith in divine action with the reality of the people's hardships, striving to unite God's providence with the existence of evil suffered even by the innocent. Through its voice, it is stressed that this present age is transitory, that the afflictions besetting the people are not final, and that the kingdom of God lies beyond history.[^12]

### The Names of God

God transcends all human knowledge and, consequently, transcends every word that can be spoken concerning Him. At the same time, however, if no name could be attributed to him, God would appear to us as an anonymous and impersonal force, incapable of being addressed and invoked by a concrete name. The question of the name of God is therefore inseparable from the consideration of God as a personal being. In the revelation of the name of God his personal character is manifested to such an extent that it has been possible to describe God's revelation of his name as "an attenuated kind of incarnation of God".[^13] Indeed, by revealing his name, God enters decisively into human history as a personal being who can be invoked.[^14]

The Bible accords paramount importance to the name of God. This is seen, for instance, in the solemnity with which the revelation of God's name on Mount Horeb is presented (Ex 3:13–15); it is seen in the Decalogue, where the taking of the Lord's name in vain is forbidden (Ex 20:7; Sir 23:9; Mt 5:34); and in the Lord's Prayer, where Our Lord Jesus Christ sets down as the first petition that the name of the Father who is in heaven be hallowed (Mt 6:9).

The attributes that accompany the name of God demonstrate the extent to which, in biblical thought, the name is identified with God himself. The name of God is holy (Ps 111:9), great and terrible (Mal 1:11, 14), and incommunicable—that is, it belongs to no other, but solely to God (Wis 14:21). The name of God is mighty. It is in the name of God that the prophets speak, for they speak as envoys of God to men (Deut 18:19; Josh 9:9; Mt 7:22; Lk 24:47). Even Our Lord Jesus Christ speaks only in the name of his Father (Jn 5:43; 10:25).[^15]

The proper name of the God of Israel is Yahweh. But there are other complementary ways of designating God. Among these are *El Shaddai*, God Almighty (Gen 17:1; 28:3; Ex 6:3); *El Olam*, the Everlasting God (Gen 21:33); *El Elyon*, God Most High (Gen 14:18–22); and *Adonai*, Lord (Jdt 16:16).[^16]

*El* is the generic name for any god in the Bible. It is the common Semitic word used to designate the divinity, understood as supernatural power, and serves to designate the godhead in its vaguest and most primitive sense among the Semites. It is perhaps a term irreducible to anterior components, to be understood as an intelligent energy belonging to a mysterious higher sphere, capable of acting upon things and men. Among polytheists, *El* was the term employed to designate the divine being in general or the chief deity of their pantheon; among monotheists it was the name used to designate the one God.[^17]

The Old Testament also uses the term *Elohim* to designate God. It is a name that can be found applied both to the true God and to false gods. It is a plural form, yet with it the one God is frequently designated in a manner customarily understood as a "plural of majesty" or "plural of greatness". *Elohim* almost always designates the true God, and in this case it is determined in various ways: with an article (Yahweh is the true *Elohim*, Deut 4:35); or with a genitive qualifier (the *Elohim* of Abraham, Gen 24:27).[^18]

Yahweh is the proper name of the God of the Israelites. It consists of four consonants: YHWH. The notions of holiness, transcendence, uniqueness, and salvation are indissolubly linked to this name as his proper and exclusive attributes. In Jewish circles, the designation "the Lord" (*Adonai*) became synonymous with Yahweh, since it is what is spoken aloud whenever the Tetragrammaton is to be read. Exodus recounts that Yahweh is the name God reveals to Moses in the theophany of the burning bush (cf. Ex 3:13–15).[^19] According to this passage, God calls to Moses from a bush that burns without being consumed and says to him: "I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Ex 3:6). There is an evident intent to emphasise that the God who now calls is the very same who called the fathers; he is the faithful and compassionate God who remembers them and his promises, and comes to deliver their descendants from bondage.

The narrative continues: "Moses said to God: 'If I come to the people of Israel and say to them, "The God of your fathers has sent me to you", and they ask me, "What is his name?", what shall I say to them?' God said to Moses: 'I AM WHO I AM.' And he said: 'Say this to the people of Israel, "I AM has sent me to you" ... This is my name for ever, and thus I am to be remembered throughout all generations'" (Ex 3:13–15).[^20]

The core of this account lies precisely in the name that God reveals to Moses—YHWH—and in the meaning it enshrines. It is a name related to the concept of existence, usually translated as "I am who is", or "I am who I am", or also "I am that I am", or "I will be what I will be". The Israelites use it in the third person: "He who is".[^21]

The words "I am who I am" are interpreted by some authors as an evasive answer on God's part—that is, as though instead of giving a name, God had refused to give one, in order to make clear, on the one hand, that He is above all naming, and on the other, to prevent the Israelites—who lived in an environment where it was commonplace to strive to learn the names of deities in order to employ them in magical practices—from falling into this temptation.[^22]

It is reasonable to understand the meaning of the words "I am who I am" in the light of analogous biblical formulas placed on God's lips: "I say what I say" (Ezek 12:25); "I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious" (Ex 33:19). These expressions insist upon the reality of God's sovereign existence, or rather, upon the efficacy of his word and grace. This is even clearer if one follows directly the reading of the Hebrew text, which affords greater latitude than the translation of the Septuagint or Saint Jerome, since it admits also of being read in the future: "I will be who I will be".

The formula "I am who I am" expresses, therefore, an existence that manifests itself actively—an efficacious being. According to the context of Exodus 3:14, the name that God gives of himself must vindicate the mission of Moses, tasked with the liberation of Israel; hence it is fitting that the name under which he presents himself—Yahweh—should imply in one way or another the efficacy of divine assistance: "I will be with you".[^23]

If the name of Yahweh is read, especially in the interpretation provided by the Septuagint translation, taking the verb "to be" as signifying being in an absolute sense, then Yahweh would mean "he who is of himself", in opposition to all contingent being—which does not exist of itself—and in opposition to the gods of the nations, which are nothing. Clearly, it is quite possible that the sacred writer did not understand the name of Yahweh in this profound metaphysical sense; yet it is equally certain that the great theological tradition understood it in the metaphysical sense of absolute Being.[^24] Thus too it appears to be understood by the Book of Wisdom, which calls God *ton onta*, "He who is" (Wis 13:1).

The name Yahweh manifests something belonging to the being of God, something proper to him that distinguishes him from creatures. Yahweh is not, in effect, an abstract, impersonal, or indefinite name. It is, nevertheless, a mysterious name, insofar as, while affirming its connection with being or presence, no specific trait is added to define that being or presence. Yet it is the being or presence proper to someone; that is why in the Septuagint he is designated in the masculine (*ho ōn*, "he who is"), and not in the neuter (*to on*, "that which is").[^25]

In Exodus 3:13–15 the identity of the God who speaks to Moses from the bush with the God of the fathers is underscored, giving it to be understood that the God who now speaks to Moses was not altogether unknown to the Patriarchs. From Moses onwards—"from the land of Egypt", as Hosea says (Hos 12:9; 13:4)—Yahweh is the God of Israel, the God of the covenant (Ex 24:3–8), who stands at the centre of the union of all the tribes of Israel.

It is impossible to establish a definitive etymology for this divine name.[^26] Nonetheless, it is clear that God manifests himself to Moses as a living reality standing above time. "The *I am* is the God of the past (the fathers), of the present (of his people), and of the future (he is the one who will manifest himself). The God who reveals himself to Moses is a God who enters into contact with men, and it is in that contact and in the vicissitudes of this human history that his being, his existence, is made manifest."[^27] The Patriarchs knew Yahweh under the name *El*, common to all Semites, and with the attribute of *Shaddai*, Almighty. By choosing his people now and delivering them with a mighty hand from the oppressors, God makes himself known as the God of the covenant; by selecting a name related to something as universal as existence itself, without binding it to any clan or territory, he presents himself as Someone; he is not a mere national deity.[^28]

### God as Personal Being

The personal character of the God of Israel—namely, that Yahweh is a being of a personal nature who cannot be confused with an anonymous cosmic force, but possesses his own individuality, endowed with intellect, will, and freedom—is thrown into sharp relief, as has already been seen, from the very first pages of the Bible. God creates by his word alone; he sees that everything he made is good; he gives a commandment to the first parents; he walks with them in paradise; he imposes a punishment upon them for their transgression; he promises them a redeemer (Gen 1–3). God manifests himself always as a wise, merciful, and provident power. God is the One who through the centuries remains faithful to the Covenant in which He himself took the initiative (cf. Ex 19:1–6).

For this reason it may be asserted that pantheism is utterly alien to the biblical concept of God. In Scripture, God is not on the level of nature, not even as its foundation; neither is he an abstract or diffuse idea, but an intimately personal being.[^29] In the Old Testament, this personal character of God is underscored by the copious use of anthropomorphisms. Indeed, speaking of God as Someone who is angered, who has compassion, who repents of having made man, and so on, necessarily implies conceiving him as a personal being. The abundant presence of anthropomorphisms in the Old Testament is not the product of so naive a conception of the godhead as might be supposed, but rather the fruit of the effort to express the individual, living character of God.

Before the transcendence and ineffability of God, anthropomorphic language is inevitably inadequate, like stammering; but philosophical language and theological language are likewise inadequate, for God is beyond all concepts and words. Yet all these modes of speech convey something of God. The use of anthropomorphisms in speaking of God does not imply an ignorance of absolute divine transcendence; rather, it reflects the impossibility of adequately expressing the majesty of God in human words. In the case of Sacred Scripture, these anthropomorphisms are clearly framed within affirmations and manifestations of God's transcendence and ineffability, of his absolute superiority over man and over all creation, and at the same time they are employed with extraordinary poetic power and vigour. Thus, for instance, God speaks (Gen 1:3), hears (Ex 16:12), sees (Gen 6:12), moulds from the clay, and breathes life (Gen 2:7), and so forth.

This concept of the personal nature of God is directly linked to the whole doctrine of the Covenant. In it, the divinity displays an expressly personal character, precisely because all the qualities proper to a decision-making being are accentuated. In revealing his own name, God shows himself to be determinate, a being distinguishable from others.[^30] As has been seen, in revealing his name God shows himself not only to be a concrete being, but at once transcendent and personal: first, he has no need whatever to keep his name secret lest men gain power over him (as was customary among the Egyptians and Romans); and second, he severely punishes any abuse made of his holy name.[^31]

In the Bible, God is not a philosophical abstraction devised to explain the schema of the universe as its first cause, nor is he confused with the cosmos. God controls and directs cosmic forces and the course of history, but always in the service of his designs for human salvation. The revealed character of Israel's religion is intrinsically bound up with the conception of God as a personal being.[^32]

The Old Testament highlights this personal character by designating God as "the living God"—that is, a God who does not remain inert, but sees, hears, and acts. God is essentially the Living One (cf. Deut 5:26; 1 Sam 17:26, 36; Jer 10:10; 23:36, etc.). Yahweh swears by his own life: "As I live!" (Num 14:21, 28; Deut 32:40; Jer 46:18, etc.). This expression signifies the vitality and power manifested by God in his intervention in history, in his defence of Israel against foreign nations (cf. Josh 3:10). The Bible frequently contrasts the living God—Yahweh—with the gods of the nations, which are nothing other than dead and powerless idols (cf. Isa 44:18; Ps 135:15–17; Wis 15:17).

### Israel's Monotheism

Polytheism is the belief that there exist multiple gods. These are deities generally interrelated with one another, forming an upper realm. These gods are, moreover, frequently arranged in a hierarchy, such that there is almost always a supreme god considered superior to all others, as in the case of Zeus in Greco-Roman paganism. Monolatry, on the other hand, is the worship of one single god, considered proper to the clan or country, without thereby excluding the existence of other gods: they are simply not worshipped. Monotheism represents a further step beyond monolatry: it is belief in one unique God; that is to say, only one God is worshipped because of the steadfast conviction that there is no God besides him. This monotheism is beautifully formulated in Isaiah: "I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god (...) Thus says the Lord, your Redeemer, who formed you from the womb: 'I am the Lord, who made all things, who alone stretched out the heavens, who by myself spread out the earth'" (Isa 44:6, 24).

Monotheism appears clearly in the Bible. Yahweh is the sole creator of all, the only God who establishes the covenant with the fathers, who reveals his name to Moses, and who insists that his divinity is exclusive. Despite the pressure of the surrounding culture and despite the people's repeated lapses into idolatry, the expression of monotheistic faith is present with vigour and consistency throughout the Old Testament. In turn, as is understandable, monotheism—so clearly articulated in the Bible—encountered great resistance in penetrating the consciousness of the people of Israel, given their propensity for idolatry. It is necessary, therefore, to distinguish between the popular religiosity of the ancient Israelites and the doctrine concerning God professed by the sacred books. The monotheism expressed in these books is the outcome of what is sometimes characterised as a long struggle on behalf of monotheism.[^33] The purity of biblical monotheism is striking, given that these texts were written in the midst of a people so prone to polytheism. The question arises how a people sprung from a nomadic clan, possessing no special cultural prominence, immersed in a clearly polytheistic environment, arrived at the idea of the one God, Lord of the universe, Sovereign of human history. One viable answer is that this one God is not an invention of that people, nor of its sages, but that He himself stepped forward to encounter the people.[^34]

The purity of the concept of God in biblical teaching is astonishing. At a remove of so many centuries and from within a deeply monotheistic culture, it is difficult for us to grasp the complex religious mentality of those Old Testament righteous who had to interact—as a tiny minority—with polytheistic peoples who, moreover, enjoyed a higher level of material culture.

The situation might be described as follows: the monotheism discernible in the patriarchal narratives is a monolatry, in which the theological affirmation of God's uniqueness had not yet been made explicit—that is, without the non-existence of local deities having been formally declared. Patriarchal religion consisted essentially in an exclusive personal bond with this God who had revealed himself, thus inaugurating the education of his people towards monotheism. This practical monotheism was profoundly reinforced by the revelation of the name of Yahweh and the absolute prohibition against worshipping other gods. God is a jealous God. It is worth noting that this exclusivism of Yahweh is unique in the history of religions. He is a God who demands all worship for himself alone (cf. Deut 18:13; Ex 20:3–5).

This practical monotheism does not imply that in the earliest period the prohibition against worshipping other gods was necessarily equivalent to the requirement of an explicitly monotheistic confession. What is stated in numerous texts is that Yahweh alone saves. The prophets had to wage a vigorous struggle against the people's temptation to worship the gods of neighbouring nations in an attempt to render them propitious. It is now that the indisputable formulations of an absolute theological monotheism emerge (cf. e.g. Isa 44–45).[^35] In these texts we are not confronted with a philosophical argument, as though their monotheism were a truth attained by intellectual effort; nor are they presented as a novel revelation, but above all as a definitive awakening to the full content of the revelation of the God of Israel.[^36]

Thus biblical monotheism is the outcome of the revelation of the living God within the historical experience of the people of Israel. There is no speculative deduction here based on the requirement that the infinitely perfect must be unique, so characteristic of the philosophical journey towards God. Israelite monotheism clears its path through formulations that respond simultaneously to God's self-manifestation and to the deepening perception that the sacred authors acquired of that self-manifestation at various moments in history.[^37]

### The Notion of Creation and its Implications for the Concept of God

In the teaching of Sacred Scripture, monotheism is expressed with even greater clarity because it is accompanied by the concept of creation *ex nihilo*: God made all things from nothing (cf. 2 Macc 7:26–28; Isa 44:24) and is therefore prior to them. By this very token, he is the sole and supreme Lord of the world, who can under no circumstances be confused with it or commingled with its elements. God is not part of the world, nor is the world divine in the sense of being an emanation from him. Nothing is more foreign to biblical thought than pantheism in any of its guises, for the cosmos is neither an emanation of God nor the product of an evolution of the Godhead, but the work of his hands, his creature.[^38]

The ethics entailed by Israelite monotheism—Yahweh alone is to be served—is grounded in the fact that He is the Supreme and absolute Lord, the only one, precisely because He is the sole author of all that exists. He is the first and the only (cf. e.g. Ps 90:2; 102:25–27; Prov 8:22; Isa 41:4; 44:6; 48:12). For this reason, God is not the prisoner of creation, but its Lord and Judge; his designs are inscrutable: no one can teach him anything, no one can call him to account for his deeds (cf. e.g. Job 38–40).

This standing above all things as Creator is what makes it possible for him, at the same time, to be intimately near. The theology of creation is vigorously present in the Old Testament, transcending mythical notions regarding the formation of the world. The Bible emphasizes that creation is the fruit of God's omnipotent speaking: "By the word of the Lord the heavens were made (...) For he spoke, and it came to be; he commanded, and it stood forth" (Ps 33:6, 9). This efficacious "speaking" of God constitutes not only the initial creation, but also what is customarily termed the conservation and governance of the universe (cf. Job 37:6). As has been written, "where nature and history—that is, the totality of the world—appear as the incarnate word of God speaking with omnipotence, the 'in' and 'above' of God's relation to the world is presented to us in a magnificent manner. We have the clearest example of the total immanence of his cosmic governance, whilst his transcendence manifests itself as the transcendence of his being in absolute self-possession. The universe depends entirely upon him in all things and is subject to his decrees. Everything that occurs is the outcome of his plan and purpose: 'My counsel shall stand, and I will accomplish all my purpose' (Isa 46:10). His sovereignty is absolute in all respects: 'I will be gracious to whom I will be gracious, and will show mercy on whom I will show mercy' (Ex 33:19)".[^39]

This one Creator God is the God of the Covenant. If the theology of creation finds a place in the Old Testament, it is precisely because it forms part of the Covenant as its prelude, to the point where one may affirm that creation, rather than being merely the stage upon which the Covenant unfolds, is already a prefiguration of this Covenant. Furthermore, one may say that the Covenant is the final goal of creation, while creation is an indispensable event for the Covenant.[^40]

### Divine Transcendence

The transcendence of God is manifested in the affirmations that He is the only one, the Creator, the Almighty. God is everywhere, in all places, without being bound to any one of them. This transcendence is displayed in an especially graphic manner in the narration of the theophanies of Yahweh, in the symbols of the cloud and the glory, and in the prohibition against depicting Yahweh in images. The transcendence of God over all created things is likewise manifested in two of his attributes constantly highlighted by Sacred Scripture: holiness and spirituality.

The concept of "holy" appears constantly in Sacred Scripture and is inseparable from the concept of God, to such a degree that one may assert that in the Bible, contrary to naturalist religions, holiness is predicated essentially of God; it is, as it were, the very essence of God: God is the Wholly Other, set apart from all that is not God, the transcendent Being. The realm of God is the realm of holiness; outside of it lies the realm of the profane, of the unholy. Between these two realms there is the possibility of communication. God, in effect, communicates himself to men. For this reason, what is holy is, at once, *mysterium tremendum*—something awe-inspiring and perilous, if approached profanely, without the requisite dispositions—and *mysterium fascinans*, a fascinating mystery that attracts and saves as a power of self-communication.[^41]

Holiness is so intimately bound up with Yahweh that it constantly accompanies all his works. Yahweh is holy (cf. Isa 40:25; Hab 3:3; Job 6:10); he is the Holy One of Israel (Isa 1:4; 10:17). Yahweh manifests his holiness by displaying his power in his wondrous deeds (Lev 10:3; Num 20:13), through the work of creation (Isa 41:20; 45:11–12), and by the election and deliverance of his people (Ezek 20:41; 28:22–25). It is God's holiness that inspires veneration in the believer—awe and trust at once—for in God's holiness his infinite majesty and infinite purity are made manifest (cf. Ex 15:11; Hos 11:9; Isa 8:13). God is utterly holy, since he is goodness itself; hence all his works are holy.[^42] Holy, too, is everything that bears a relation to Yahweh, from the place where he reveals himself (Gen 28:17; Ex 3:5) to the priestly vestments (Ex 29:37; 30:29). Holy must be everyone who approaches Yahweh (cf. Josh 3:5; 1 Sam 16:5): "You shall be holy, for I Yahweh your God am holy" (Lev 11:44; 19:2; 20:26).

Before the awesome holiness of Yahweh, man perceives his own nothingness and God's infinite transcendence. Thus Abraham is seized with terror before Yahweh (Gen 15:12) and perceives himself to be but dust and ashes (Gen 18:27); Job is struck dumb with trembling before the experience of God's supreme holiness (Job 40:3–5); Moses and Elijah veil their faces as the glory of Yahweh passes by (Ex 3:6; 33:22; 1 Kgs 19:13).

Divine holiness, by virtue of the intimate relation between God and his sanctity, is closely linked in Sacred Scripture with the glory of God. To give glory to God is to acknowledge him as holy and mighty. To sanctify the name of Yahweh is to acknowledge him as God; this entails blessing his name and, at the same time, behaving with the moral sanctity required of anyone in relation with God. Hence the sins of Israel are an affront to the glory of God (Isa 3:8). They are, in truth, a challenge to his infinite holiness. Yahweh cannot yield his glory to another (cf. Isa 42:8; 48:11). This conjunction of divine transcendence, God's holiness, divine glory, and man's sense of his own nothingness is strikingly evident in the account of Isaiah's vocation: "In the year that King Uzziah died I saw the Lord Yahweh sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up; and his train filled the temple. Above him stood the seraphim (...) And one called to another and said: 'Holy, holy, holy is the Lord of hosts; the whole earth is full of his glory' (...) And I said: 'Woe is me! For I am lost; for I am a man of unclean lips, and I dwell in the midst of a people of unclean lips; for my eyes have seen the King, the Lord of hosts!'" (Isa 6:1–5).

The Old Testament, while employing anthropomorphisms to underline God's personality, makes it unmistakably clear that God is not a man. Thus, for instance, he neither slumbers nor sleeps (Ps 121:4); he does not faint or grow weary (Isa 40:28); he does not have eyes of flesh like men (Job 10:4). God fills the earth (Jer 23:24), heaven is his throne and the earth his footstool (Isa 66:1), and he is too immense for heaven and the highest heaven to contain him (1 Kgs 8:27). No image of God may be fashioned, for God revealed himself under no form (Deut 4:15–16). All these descriptions point towards the spirituality of God—that is, towards the conception of a personal being transcending all materiality.

Naturally, in speaking of the spirituality of God as in speaking of so many other themes, the theological perspective of the Old Testament does not coincide entirely with the notions proper to Greek philosophy. For our Hellenic-shaped mindset, spirit is contrasted with matter, which of itself implies composition, heaviness, limitation, and mutability; spirit, therefore, evokes the idea of something simple, free of all composite nature. The Hebrews, on the other hand, with the noun *ruah*—meaning wind, breath of life—think first and foremost of something dynamic, like the breath that animates man. Thus *ruah* applied to Yahweh is not so much a description of his immateriality as an expression of his creative power, which imparts life to living beings and is communicated to men in a charismatic manner at decisive moments in the history of the chosen people.[^43]

### The Natural Knowledge of God

In the Bible, God is the supreme and total reality, the first and ultimate certainty to which the entire worldview is subordinated. God is the Infinite One of whom man knows that he exists, lives, and acts. For this reason, the sacred writers do not concern themselves with proving his existence, for they consider that the reality of God imposes itself, since it is manifested in God's intervention in historical events.[^44] For the sacred writers, this manifestation is the most certain of facts. Creation, too, was readily a place of encounter with Him, as though created things were transparent to display their ground—God—from whom they receive their being. Fire and hail, snow and mist, stormy wind, mountains and all hills: all creatures speak of God; all praise and extol him.[^45]

The existence of God is never called into question. Nor is the affirmation of God presented as the outcome of a philosophical demonstration. In Sacred Scripture, the existence of God appears with such clarity that it is presented not only as independent of human thought, but as the fundamental reality whose denial is scarcely even conceivable.[^46]

Throughout the Bible, the conviction is patent that the God of the Covenant, who has intervened so clearly in the events of the people of Israel, could neither have been invented by man nor fashioned by him. Idols, on the contrary, are indeed made to human measure. In Jewish history, the permanent temptation is not atheism, but idolatry. The temptation of the people is not to cease believing in God, but to live as if He did not exist.[^47]

When the Bible speaks of "knowing" God, it frequently refers to acknowledging him as the supreme rule of life, submitting to his demands (Deut 11:2–8; Isa 41:20; Hos 11:3) and, above all, acknowledging him as the only God, to the exclusion of all other deities (Isa 43:10; 44:8; 45:5–6; Wis 13:1–9). The prophet declares openly: "You know no God but me" (Hos 13:4; cf. Ps 9:10), just as is demanded in the Decalogue (Ex 20:1–3). In all these expressions, acknowledging God signifies in reality honouring him by a life of self-surrender to his will. The intellectual cognitive act and its moral consequence cannot be separated. Thus it is said of the sons of Eli, wicked in their conduct: "They did not know the Lord" (1 Sam 2:12)—that is to say, they did not acknowledge his inalienable rights in the sacrifices and offerings presented at the sanctuary of Shiloh. Jeremiah would later declare concerning unscrupulous priests: "Those who handle the law did not know me" (Jer 2:8). It is the unprincipled practical attitude of the prophet's contemporaries, who heed neither his warnings nor his oracles: "They have spoken falsely of the Lord, and have said: 'He will do nothing; no evil will come upon us'" (Jer 5:12).

Parallel to taking the existence of God for granted, Sacred Scripture draws attention to man's responsibility in accepting or rejecting Yahweh—that is, in acknowledging him. When Psalms 14:1 (13:1) and 53:1 (52:1) portray the fool saying that there is no God, what is properly being spoken of is corrupt men who do not seek God and act as if He did not exist, imagining that He does not concern himself with justice on earth. These are the atheists, for they act in a spirit of practical atheism.

### The Passage of Wisdom 13:1–9

It has already been remarked that the Old Testament speaks of the existence of God as a self-evident fact requiring no demonstration. Only the Greek Book of Wisdom (Wis 13:1–9), in polemicising against idolatry, points to a proof of the existence of God the Creator: man can and ought to recognise the Craftsman of the world precisely in the very structure of this world. The beauty and harmony of creation is a summons to acknowledge God addressed to all men, especially to the wisest, those who best know the world. It is easy to see that when Sacred Scripture describes the wonders wrought by God in nature or among men (e.g. Ps 19:1–6; 104:1–7; Job 38; Isa 40:25–31), it does so not so much to prove the existence of God as to praise him (Ps 8; 19:7–10; 104) and to exhort to trust in his power (e.g. Isa 40:27–31).

Here is the text of Wisdom:
"For all men who were ignorant of God were foolish by nature; and they were unable from the good things that are seen to know him who is, nor did they recognise the artisan while paying heed to his works; but they supposed that either fire or wind or swift air, or the circle of the stars, or turbulent water, or the luminaries of heaven were the gods that rule the world. If through delight in their beauty men assumed them to be gods, let them know how much better than these is their Lord, for the author of beauty created them. And if men were amazed at their power and working, let them perceive from them how much more powerful is he who formed them. For from the greatness and beauty of created things comes a corresponding perception of their Creator. Yet again, not even these are to be severely blamed, for perhaps they go astray while seeking God and desiring to find him. For as they live among his works they keep searching, and they trust in what they see, because the things that are seen are beautiful. Yet again, not even they are excusable; for if they had the power to know so much that they could investigate the world, how did they fail to find sooner the Lord of these things?" (Wis 13:1–9).

The author of the Book of Wisdom characterises as foolish those men who were incapable of recognising the true God through visible things, but instead called the forces of nature gods. It is quite possible that this reasoning, written in the polytheistic milieu of Alexandria, was addressed to idolaters or sceptics, not to Israelite society immersed in a Semitic environment that inherently saw divine presence in everything. It seems that in these paragraphs the author of Wisdom has philosophers especially in mind, and more specifically the Stoics, so attentive to the beauty and harmony of the universe.[^48]

To grasp our author's reflection, one must bear in mind the distinction, admitted by the Stoics and popularised above all by Cicero's *De natura deorum*, between the existence of the divine and its nature. The existence of the divine was proved, according to the philosophers, by the Aristotelian argument which might be summarised as follows: observing the world, its beauty, and the forces displayed within it, one comes to think that these marvels are the work of a divine architect. Just as a perfectly constructed mansion leads to the conviction that it was planned by an architect.

In this passage, genuine validity is accorded to the mental journey leading from admiration of creatures to the discovery of the existence of God. Within this perspective, the observation is justified that in this passage the author, who deliberately applies to God the title of architect of the universe, wishes to affirm that the God whom the philosophers attain is the very same God of the Bible.[^49] If he criticises the philosophers, it is not for their intellectual path, but because this path, which leads them to discover the existence of God, ought to have led them a little further: it ought to have led them to discover something of the true nature of God. Above all, it ought to have led them to discover a God distinct from the world, rather than confounding him with the elemental forces of the cosmos and deifying them.

Furthermore—and this is of paramount importance—this passage from the Book of Wisdom, in describing man's mental journey towards God, presents the analogical way as a certain and universal path of access to the divine. It is the first time this expression appears in Sacred Scripture, and it constitutes the key upon which the entire passage rests: had the philosophers pursued analogy to its ultimate consequences, they would have avoided going astray when speaking of the nature of God.[^50]

This passage, then, invites us to contemplate the world with sensitivity and wonder towards created beauty, confident that what is created, since it reflects the divine, serves as a visible point of reference for man, pointing clearly towards the Creator. In this sense, the reasoning employed here is linear and simple: foolish are those men who do not know how to read the book of Creation in all its depth, and for this reason do not know him. They are men who do not feel challenged by the harmony of the world, and who, enraptured by such beauty, feel no urgency to seek its Author. It is quite possible that the writer of these paragraphs was a Hellenist impressed, like the Greeks, by the beauty of the world.[^51]

The passage concludes with a moral judgment regarding this failure to recognise the Creator. Those who fail to read the book of Creation aright are more excusable than those who make no attempt to read it at all, though neither are they entirely blameless. They fail to read the world correctly precisely because, instead of treating it as a point of reference, they become so fascinated by the beauty and power of the world's elements that they renounce all further inquiry. Hence they too are not entirely excusable, since if they had the intellectual power to master the elements of the world, they ought not to have allowed themselves to be mastered by them to the point of failing to take new steps towards knowing their Maker. This judgment appears to transcend the purely speculative plane to encompass the entire existential domain of the philosophers.[^52] This thought constitutes the framework within which the Pauline affirmations concerning the natural knowledge of God are situated, especially the well-known passages of Romans 1:18–23 and Acts 17:22–29, which we shall examine below.
