## Chapter 18: The Oneness of God {#mateo-seco-trino.18}

We conclude the chapters devoted to the study of divine transcendence by considering the oneness of God, which is the supreme expression of his absolute transcendence. There is but one God, because He is above every other being. He receives being from no one, whereas all that exists outside Him belongs to the realm of the created and the contingent. Even the angels and demons of whom Sacred Scripture so frequently speaks are nothing more than his servants.

### The Contingency of Angelic Spirits

In Sacred Scripture angels and demons are frequently mentioned as superior beings who are not circumscribed by the material world. From the earliest strata of the biblical tradition, angels appear as messengers of God and benefactors of men; demons appear as tempters and accusers.[^1]

After the Fall, an angel intervenes to bar Adam from returning to Paradise (cf. Gen 3:24); angels visit Abraham to announce to him the birth of Isaac (cf. Gen 19:1); an angel appears to him to prevent the sacrifice of Isaac (cf. Gen 22:11). In the New Testament as well, angels are frequently spoken of; the texts are exceedingly abundant. From the angels who intervene in the Annunciation and the Nativity of Our Lord—the Archangel Gabriel appears to Zechariah (cf. Lk 1:11) and announces the Incarnation to the Virgin (cf. Lk 1:26); an angel appears in dreams to Joseph (cf. Mt 1:20)—to the angels who minister to the Lord after the temptations (cf. Mt 4:11), or accompany the glory of the Lord (cf. Mk 13:26; Mt 25:31). An angel comforts Jesus in the Garden (cf. Lk 22:43). The angels are depicted full of majesty and power. They are the most sublime creatures. They stand close to the throne of God (cf. Gen 28:12; Rev 7:11). They are endowed with strength and majesty (cf. Heb 1:7), and often appear enveloped in radiant light (cf. Mk 16:5; Acts 1:10).[^2]

Sacred Scripture speaks with great sobriety concerning demons. By their nature demons belong to the angelic realm, but they are enemies of God and of his Christ. Foremost among the demons is Satan (cf. Mt 12:26). He is also termed serpent, great dragon, devil (Gen 3:1; Rev 12:9; Jn 8:44), ruler of this world (cf. Jn 12:31). The devil is the tempter, as is made manifest in the temptations of the Lord (cf. Mt 4:1). He is the great persecutor of Christians (cf. Rev 12:17), but in the end he will be vanquished and cast into the lake of fire (cf. Rev 20:10).

The existence of angels and demons belongs to the faith of the Church, as could not be otherwise given the clarity and frequency with which both appear in Sacred Scripture. It likewise belongs to the faith of the Church that God created both angels and demons out of nothing. Consequently, demons are not the outcome of dualism—that is, they do not proceed from an evil principle opposed by nature to the principle of good; rather, their malice is due to a free choice they made over against God. Evil enters creation through the free decision of beings who were originally good.[^3]

This has always been the faith of the Church, and is proclaimed in confessing God as creator "of all things visible and invisible."[^4] The same is stated in the professions of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215), Lyons II (1274), Florence (1441), and Trent (1564).[^5] In the *Creed of the People of God* (1968) it is explicitly stated:

> "We believe in one only God, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, Creator of things visible—such as this world in which we spend our brief life—and of things invisible, such as the pure spirits which we also call angels."[^6]

Above the visible world there exists another world of invisible beings: creatures powerful and superior to man by their nature and activity. Yet God is infinitely superior even to that invisible world. Purely spiritual beings were likewise created by Him out of nothing. Those beings are neither aspects of God nor divine emanations. Nor are they intermediate beings between God and the world: they are not demiurges. There is an abyssal difference between the Uncreated One and all created beings, including those of that higher realm.

### Henotheism, Monotheism, and Polytheism

Faith in one only God is the first and fundamental affirmation of the Creed, to such an extent that the confession of the existence of God is inseparable from faith in his oneness.[^7] This oneness is entirely coherent with the discourse of reason. Nonetheless, its presence in Judaeo-Christian thought is due primarily to divine revelation, not to the effort of human reason. Jewish monotheism is first and foremost the outcome of an experience of faith, answering to the interventions of the God of the Covenant.[^8]

The spiritual journey of Israel is truly astonishing, especially regarding the purity of the concept of God that appears in the Bible. That purity is not the result of metaphysical speculation, but of the interventions of God.[^9] The God of Israel reveals Himself as a jealous God, incompatible with polytheism, a God who demands to be worshipped to the exclusion of other gods. This is the primordial experience of Israel. Later, the rationale for this demand was given: Yahweh alone must be worshipped, because outside of Him there is no other God. Monolatry thus becomes conscious monotheism.[^10]

The religious history of Israel is altogether singular, but it is not unique. The other peoples of the earth also have a religious journey, to the extent that no people is known without religious attitudes. Tertullian († 222/223) was right to affirm that there is no people without religion and without gods.[^11] This is a statement widely accepted at the present day:

> "One of the most momentous conclusions of modern studies in religious historiography has been the demonstration of the existence of belief in divinity among all peoples, both among the civilised and among those termed primitive—or, to speak more precisely, those lacking writing, since 'primitive' is a term and concept now abandoned in ethnological literature."[^12]

It is more difficult to verify whether, in their origin, these religions were monotheistic or polytheistic. D. Hume and J.-J. Rousseau put forward the hypothesis that polytheism was the earliest form of religion, which subsequently evolved towards monotheism. Voltaire, on the other hand, proposed monotheism as the first stage of religion. This is also the position maintained by the Vienna School of the history of religions. According to it, the original religious phenomenon was monotheism, whereas polytheism was a degradation of religion.[^13]

Both theories agree in conceiving the religious journey of peoples as an evolution forming part of the history of mankind. Current positions in describing the history of religions are far more cautious. Over against evolutionary theories concerning the origin of religion, more recent studies insist that religion is not a phenomenon that can be explained in evolutionary terms. Religion is a vital human reality that emerges from the very nature of man, whose heart will be restless until it rests in God.[^14] In this scholarly milieu, the general conviction is that polytheism does not constitute a historical phase prior to monotheism; rather, it appears characteristic of peoples who have reached a developed level of culture and social organisation, capable of divinising the various forces of nature and even their most illustrious ancestors.[^15]

As far as our historical knowledge reaches, in the beginning were tribes or human groups. These would worship a God as the sole God of the tribe or clan. Alongside this God, other gods gradually appeared. In developed peoples (Sumerians, Hittites, Babylonians, Assyrians, Egyptians, Greeks, Romans), we encounter a multitude of gods serving to divinise the forces of nature and sponsor various tasks.[^16] Generally, amidst this multitude of deities, a supreme God is found as the guarantor of the nation's identity or of the political system.

Henotheism appears, then, to be a frequent form of ancient religion. In henotheism the existence of other gods is not ruled out, but official worship is prescribed for one alone, who is the defender of the tribe or clan. Polytheism would be a rather later phenomenon. In any case, within polytheism one notes a tendency towards monarchy among the gods, as a desire for unity and a flight from crass polytheism, to such an extent that one must not lightly assume that a religion is polytheistic merely because many gods are spoken of within it.[^17]

In Israelite monotheism, adoration is rendered to one God alone, because He is deemed to be the only God. The paradigmatic text is Deuteronomy 6:4: *Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one*. Perhaps the chapters that most vigorously excoriate idols are found in Deutero-Isaiah: irony against other gods is reasoned not only from the uselessness of idols, but from the oneness of Yahweh: *I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god. Who is like me? [...] All who fashion idols are nothing [...] I am the Lord, who made all things, who alone stretched out the heavens, who spread out the earth by myself* (Isa 44:6, 7, 24). The journey of Israel towards monotheism presents itself to us as a long march from henotheism towards the confession of one God, while at the same time contending with numerous polytheistic outbursts that arose within its midst through contamination from neighbouring peoples.[^18]

This monotheism of Israel, as confirmed by Our Lord (cf. Mt 22:37; Mk 12:30; Lk 10:27), is not only opposed to all polytheism and to all confusion between the divine and the forces of nature, but also possesses features that make it unmistakable with any other monotheism. The fundamental difference lies in the clarity with which the doctrine of creation *ex nihilo* is affirmed and, consequently, the absolute qualitative diversity existing between the being of God and created being.

Here we touch upon a key question for the concept of God. The essential difference between monotheism and polytheism does not lie so much in the number of gods worshipped or believed in, but above all in the depth and authenticity with which the divine being is conceived. The polytheist not only worships false gods, but also holds a devalued concept of the deity. There are many gods because in reality none of them is truly infinite. Polytheism is possible only when the transcendence of God over the created world is ignored—that is, when there is no clear awareness of the authentic grandeur of God.

Hence in the affirmation of monotheism there is something far more important than the question of number. It is a matter of the very nature of God. The theology of creation is decisive here. An authentic understanding of monotheism occurs only when God is conceived as Creator and absolute master of all that exists—that is, as Lord of heaven and earth, transcendently surpassing all beings by an infinite abyss, because He alone is Being.

Only Being by essence can be the Creator. Only He who receives being neither from anything nor from anyone, but possesses it in fullness because He is Being, can cause contingent being to arise out of nothing. Only He who is a unity so perfect that it does not even admit of a composition of *essentia et esse* can cause the being of beings. This implies a quality of being infinitely superior to that of created entities. In turn, all created beings have a relation to God as the cause and ground of their being. This is a relation of total and radical dependence, of absolute belonging.[^19]

The revelation of monotheism reaches its fullness with the revelation of the Trinitarian mystery. This is not a corrective to monotheism, but a deepening of the truth concerning God. Indeed, this revelation shows the inner life of the one God. God is not only a personal being, but a unity in communion of persons. In God there is a perfect harmony between unity and communion. The Trinitarian mystery indicates, at the same time, the richness of the monotheistic affirmation and the depth of the affirmation that *God is love* (1 Jn 4:8).

The mystery of the Trinity finds a reasonable framework only in the radical affirmation of monotheism: only when one has become aware of the absolute transcendence of God over all things can one realise the possibility that this unity is not to be conceived as solitude but as communion, and grasp the absolute unity existing among the three Persons.[^20] So to speak, the affirmation of unity belongs to faith in the Trinitarian mystery with the very same force as the affirmation of the trinity of Persons. In unity divine transcendence is revealed, and in the Trinitarian affirmation it is revealed that this oneness of God is supremely perfect and, precisely on this account, of an infinite interpersonal richness.

It is well to keep in mind that the unity of God is that proper to simple beings and is therefore the unity of that which possesses itself in fullness. This full self-possession takes place only through self-consciousness and freedom. Divine unity leads us necessarily to the affirmation of God's personal being:

> "This unity is what we begin to glimpse in personal being, in that which we call an *I*. The *I* is never divided or separated from itself, and that strict unity possessed by it is also consciously lived or perceived; the *I* is one and knows that it is one: which constitutes what we call intimacy. And since it is a higher unity than any other given to us to know, it may well serve as a point of reference for thinking about the unity of God. The unity of God must be something like the intimacy of God, which compels us to think of God as Person. God, since He enjoys the greatest unity we can think of, must be Person; He must enjoy the supreme intimacy, that proper to the Being who is Person in the highest degree. It seems, however, that the person demands company, demands transcending itself towards other persons, and therefore accords poorly with solitude. Yet if God is absolutely unique [...] He would have to be solitary, and this can lead us to think that He is not a person, or that He is an imperfect person."[^21]

In the light of this argument, one better understands the force present in the patristic affirmation that God is one, but not a solitary being, or in the Trinitarian approach of Richard of Saint Victor when, in the affirmation that *God is love* (1 Jn 4:8), he saw contained the teaching that the one God must live in an ineffable interpersonal communion.

### The Uniqueness of God

In their struggle against polytheism, the Holy Fathers frequently resorted to common sense to demonstrate the uniqueness of God. Here is the line of argument followed by Saint John Damascene, which is of an enviable clarity:

> "The divine essence is perfect; it lacks nothing in goodness, wisdom, and power; it is without beginning and without end, eternally unlimited—in short, it is absolutely perfect. If we suppose that there are many gods, there will necessarily be a difference among them, since, if there were no difference, they would be one God and not many. Now, if there is a difference among them, where then is perfection? For one would fall below what is perfect in respect of goodness, power, wisdom, or place, and would not be God. But perfect identity would prove that there exists only one and not many. And how would it be possible for infinity to be preserved in the existence of many? [...] And furthermore, how could the world be governed by many? Would it not vanish and perish, since evidently a struggle would arise among those who governed it? For difference implies opposition. But if it is asserted that each rules his respective part, I ask: who is it that established order and distribution among them? This one would truly be God. One, then, is God: perfect, limitless, creator, preserver, and ruler of the universe, all-perfect and completely blessed. Moreover, it is a natural necessity that unity be the foundation of duality."[^22]

Damascene's argument synthesises centuries of intellectual effort to show that the oneness of God is a truth accessible to the light of natural reason, and that polytheism is absurd. The train of reasoning is quite simple: it takes as its point of departure the absolute perfection of God—something accepted by all who accept the existence of diverse gods—and shows how this absolute perfection must be unique, for it admits within itself of no division or degree. Unique, too, must be He who governs the world with such marvellous order. Damascene concludes by recalling that unity is the foundation of multiplicity.

A similar argument is found in Saint Thomas Aquinas. There can exist but one God, given his simplicity and all-perfection, and given also the unity and order of the world. In God the most perfect unity is found, not only because He is not divided in Himself, but because there can exist no other gods besides Him. Divine uniqueness is nothing other than the other side of the simplicity and infinity of *Ipsum esse subsistens*. Here are the three arguments:

a) God is his very nature. Therefore that very thing which makes Him to be God makes Him to be this God. It is, therefore, impossible that there be many gods, since that which makes an individual thing to be precisely this thing cannot be communicated to others.

b) God possesses all perfections in an infinite degree. In the case of there being many gods, they would have to be distinct, and therefore one would have to possess something that the other lacked. This truth—Saint Thomas recalls—is what compelled ancient philosophers to acknowledge a single infinite principle.

c) All existing things are ordered to one another. But things so diverse would not be coordinated into a single plan unless they were ordered by someone who is one; therefore there must be only one God.[^23]

The argument is simple, yet forceful. Pure Act cannot be limited by anything. Consequently, neither can He be divided, for every division or distinction implies a limit. God is absolute and most simple unity. For this reason, Saint Thomas concludes his inquiry into divine unity by noting that God, possessing being in the highest degree, is also unity in the highest degree and, consequently, supreme indivision.[^24]

### Divine Aseity and Creaturely Contingency

Transcendence over all that is created is based, then, upon what is deepest in the essence of God and in the essence of created beings. God is the Being who exists of Himself (*a se*), whereas the cosmos has been created by Him out of nothing. There exists, therefore, not merely a difference of degree between God and the world, but an essential difference, an abyss in a certain sense infinite: the difference that exists between aseity and contingency.

The depth of monotheism is understood only when it is considered in its ground, that is, in divine aseity. God has within Himself the reason for his existence; nothing of all that exists outside Him has the reason for its existence in itself. To be created means not only having received from God all that one has, but also all that one is. This means that everything exists to the extent that God thinks it, designs it—so to speak—and sustains it in being.

Everything that happens—in natural phenomena or in historical events—occurs because He wills it, or because, even while running counter to his will, He permits it. As we shall see later, the objection that evil poses to the existence of God is never resolved by diminishing the omnipotence of God. Nor can one deny God the possibility of intervening in history or in nature on the grounds that such an intervention would alter the order of the world, as though this order had to be closed in upon itself. A mechanistic conception of a closed world in which there is no room for God has proved false and is certainly incompatible with the Christian vision of God. It suffices to recall, for example, the person of Christ, the Son of God made man. It is God Himself who has revealed Himself to us in Christ and who, in Christ, intervenes decisively in history. This is demonstrated in the virginal motherhood of Saint Mary or in the resurrection of the Lord, which inaugurate a new era, breaking open the ordinary course of the world. The believer must bear in mind that the modern rejection of these mysteries does not stem primarily from the paradox they present to human reason, but from the rejection of the possibility of any divine intervention in history. That is to say, it stems from the denial of the transcendence of God and, ultimately, from the denial of the creatureliness of the world.[^25]

God alone is Uncreated; all that exists outside Him has been created by Him. Saint Anselm of Canterbury structured the exposition of his doctrine on God upon this distinction. Everything that exists proceeds from some being, not from nothing—Saint Anselm argues—because only being can bestow being. And God alone can bestow being, because He alone is subsistent being. The mystery of the divine essence lies precisely in the fact that it exists of itself and through itself—*ex se ipsa, et per se ipsam*—whereas all that is created exists from it and through it: *ex illa et per illam*. God is, then, being from Himself, in Himself, and for Himself, whereas the creature is neither from itself nor for itself, but is from God and exists for God.[^26] God alone is infinitely self-sufficient and blessed. And precisely because of this, He alone can be infinitely generous. Divine aseity is the ultimate reason for the gratuitousness of his self-giving to man.
