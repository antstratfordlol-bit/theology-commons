## Chapter 9: The End of the Patristic Era {#mateo-seco-trino.9}

In preceding chapters, we have studied the culminating point of theological thought in the East during the fourth and fifth centuries with the Cappadocians, and in the West during the same period with Saint Augustine. In the centuries that followed, until the close of the patristic era, the theological interest of the writings that have reached us is markedly lesser. With a few exceptions, which we shall examine presently, one notes an absence of great masters, and authors generally confine themselves to reiterating the arguments and questions that had been addressed previously. It is also true that intellectual assaults against Christianity diminished or ceased and, consequently, theologians no longer felt the same urgency toward a more profound intellectual elaboration of the questions concerning God. In this period, what stands out in the West is the meticulous care with which Trinitarian doctrine was formulated in various Creeds and Professions of Faith. Both in the East and in the West, the last great Fathers applied themselves to gathering and transmitting the entirety of the preceding tradition. In the West, the ascendance of Augustinian thought was already firmly established.

With regard to the questions concerning God, three names stand out in the East: Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite, Saint Maximus the Confessor († 662), and finally Saint John Damascene († 750), with whom the patristic period in the East is brought to a close. In the West, virtually the same situation obtains: one does not encounter the towering figures of the Golden Age; nevertheless, there are authors of genuine significance for the history of theology: Saint Leo the Great († 461), Severinus Boethius († c. 524), Saint Fulgentius of Ruspe († 533), Cassiodorus († c. 570–578), and Saint Gregory the Great († 604). The patristic era closes in the West with Saint Isidore of Seville († 636).

### Divine Ineffability in Pseudo-Dionysius

Pseudo-Dionysius is one of the most enigmatic figures in patristics. Neither his true identity nor the exact period in which he lived is known. We know that in 532, during a theological colloquy between moderate Monophysites and the orthodox held in Constantinople, the Monophysites adduced in their favour, as an authority, a collection of writings (*On the Divine Names*, *Mystical Theology*, *The Celestial Hierarchy*, *The Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*, and ten letters) which they attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, converted by Saint Paul in Athens (Acts 17:34). These writings are grouped under the title of the *Corpus Dionysiacum*.[^1]

Although the author of this important body of doctrine remains unknown, the conviction that he was none other than Dionysius the Areopagite conferred immense authority upon these writings, which exerted a profound influence upon all subsequent theological literature, including the entire Middle Ages. They were translated countless times into Latin and commented upon by a great multitude of authors. In reality, they are writings dependent upon Proclus and must be situated at the beginning of the sixth century. God is the central idea of Dionysian thought, especially in *De divinis nominibus*. God appears here, as in Neoplatonism, as super-being (*hyperousios*), super-goodness, the super-perfect, the super-pure.[^2]

Pseudo-Dionysius maintains that one must not inquire with reason into what the nature of God is, inasmuch as God is beyond everything that we can think of Him; He is beyond being and the good, beyond power and life, beyond reason and intellect.[^3] We find ourselves, therefore, before the most forceful formulation of negative theology, which was to exert so vast an influence on Christian thought and spirituality. Negative theology, as we have seen on other occasions, is the method of arriving at God by denying of Him those attributes found in created things: God is neither soul, nor intellect, nor matter, nor body. Sacred Scripture assigns many names to God: it says that God is life, light, truth; and this is so because God, although in Himself inaccessible and ineffable, is also the source of all that exists, of all being and all goodness in the world. For this reason, according to Dionysius, one can affirm that God has no name and that He has all names; consequently He also possesses opposite predicates: He is great because He exceeds all extension and every place; He is small because He is unextended and penetrates everywhere.[^4] In applying this thought, Pseudo-Dionysius delights in using paradox to set forth with clarity the infinite transcendence of God. Hence he says that God is great because He surpasses all extension, and at the same time that He is small because He is unextended and penetrates all things.

This radical defence of the transcendence of God beyond all knowledge has sometimes led to the negative theology of Pseudo-Dionysius being interpreted as a denial of all human knowledge of God. Nevertheless, Pseudo-Dionysius acknowledges and admits a natural knowledge of God which, proceeding from the contemplation of the world, develops in the manner customary in this form of argumentation through the threefold way of affirmation, negation, and eminence. It has been pointed out that Pseudo-Dionysius alludes in numerous passages to the Sapiential books—specifically Proverbs 8:30 and Wisdom 8:1; 9:1–9—passages affirming that we can know God by admiring the beauty of the world. The same occurs with the text of Romans 1:20.[^5] The fact that Pseudo-Dionysius cites this passage of Saint Paul demonstrates that, for him, the invisible God is not the totally other, the utterly unknowable, but can be known through the realities of this visible world.

According to Dionysius, there are three paths to reach God. The first is positive theology (*kataphatikē theologia*). This consists in approaching God on the basis of the sensible world. This positive theology must be corrected by a higher theology, which Pseudo-Dionysius calls negative theology (*apophatikē theologia*), which erases everything belonging to the created world in order to fix its gaze solely upon that which is above all created things. Finally—and with this we reach what is most characteristic of Pseudo-Dionysius—there is a third way, mystical theology: when man, as if closing the eyes of the soul, plunges into the silence of darkness, and enveloped in a super-essential light, without form, without figure, without sound, and without concept, in mystical immersion, becomes one with God.[^6] It is within this setting that Pseudo-Dionysius situates what he calls the theology of symbols, or symbolic theology. This involves approaching God in the darkness of mystical life, contemplating Him in the divine symbols found in Sacred Scripture—for example, the symbols contained in the Mosaic worship. The goal of this path is "union with the ineffable light, above and beyond all intellectual activity."[^7]

Alongside these perspectives—which make Pseudo-Dionysius simultaneously attractive and demanding, and which raised the question of whether his mystical or negative theology implied that God was utterly unknowable to the human mind—there arises another problem, likewise stemming from the Neoplatonic influence to which Pseudo-Dionysius was subject: the relation of origin between the world and God. God is the Good, and as such tends to diffuse Himself. In Him are contained the perfections of all things. All is contained in Him, who is the One and who confers unity upon the entire universe. Just as in the centre of a circle all the radii of the circle are contained, so a higher contemplation will perceive the confluence of all things in the cause of the whole.[^8]

This mode of speaking obviously harbours a problem: the centre of the circle lies within the same dimension as the entire circle. Hence the impression arises at times that, according to Pseudo-Dionysius, the world proceeds from God by emanation—that is, the creature's participation in the being of the One would be a participation received from Him because they emanate from Him. This pantheistic interpretation of Pseudo-Dionysius does not appear correct. Indeed, as has been seen, for Pseudo-Dionysius God exists before all things and is above all things. In a felicitous phrase, Pseudo-Dionysius states that things are similar to God, but that God is not similar to things.[^9] Pseudo-Dionysius thus makes clear that God is infinitely above all created realities.

It may be said that Pseudo-Dionysius "is above all a Christian, and the Neoplatonic verbal instrument, the vehicle of his thought, is not intended to convey the entire real content of its original sources, but is placed at the service of the Christian conception, which confers upon it a comprehensive meaning entirely different from what its expressions sometimes suggest."[^10] It should be noted, moreover, that the Areopagite clearly distinguishes between the communication of the simple and indivisible nature that occurs within the Trinity, and the analogous participation that takes place in the communication *ad extra*—that is, in created beings.[^11] The history of theology demonstrates how the vast majority of theologians over the nearly fifteen centuries that followed—among them Saint Thomas Aquinas and Saint John of the Cross—understood the theology of Pseudo-Dionysius as a thoroughly Christian body of thought and as one of the peaks of theological reflection, in which the transcendence of God and the mystical life of man are pre-eminently highlighted.

### Divine Transcendence in the West

In the West, too, the transcendence and incomprehensibility of God stand out as one of the most prominent themes.[^12] Severinus Boethius († c. 524) expresses this transcendence by stating that God is above all the categories (*praedicamenta*); for example, in Him substance is not truly substance, but surpasses the very notion of substance: "In Him, substance is not substance in the strict sense, but is beyond substance" (*ultra substantiam*).[^13] As is evident, to say that God is *ultra substantiam* is equivalent to what the Greek Fathers affirm when declaring of God that He is *hyperousios* (ὑπερούσιος)—that is, a suprasubstantial substance. The same holds true when applying to God the categorial notion of number.[^14] Although we say that the divine substance is one, this refers to a unity that is opposed to multiplication and identified with absolute simplicity; it is not a unity that can be numbered.

The traditional positions concerning the knowability of God and the proofs of His existence likewise continue to be maintained. Argumentation proceeds typically from the beauty and order of the world. Emphasis is placed, especially by Boethius, upon the existence of imperfect being as an indication pointing toward the existence of perfect being, since—in Boethius's thought—the imperfect is nothing other than a diminished participation in the perfect.[^15]

Boethius devotes only two short opuscula to the Trinity. In this domain he confines himself to following the path already traced by Saint Augustine, especially in the central importance he accords to relation. Only relation makes it possible to speak of three in the Trinity while simultaneously upholding their unity. This is so because relation does not express what the subject is in itself, but only its reference to another.[^16] Severinus Boethius formulated one of the most celebrated definitions of person (*individualis substantia rationalis naturae*). He did not, however, succeed in integrating this concept with that of relation; that was a task reserved for subsequent theology.[^17]

Saint Isidore of Seville is characterised by his endeavour to gather and synthesise the doctrine of his predecessors. He is fundamentally an encyclopaedist. He treats of God principally in the *Etymologies*[^18] and the *Sentences*.[^19] Proofs of the existence of God, divine transcendence and ineffability, God's omnipotence, divine goodness, and the existence of evil are topics treated in an orderly fashion by Saint Isidore. Here are some of his words: "God is the supreme Good, because He is immutable and cannot undergo any alteration. Creation, on the other hand, is a good, but not the supreme Good, because it is mutable; and although it is truly a good, nonetheless it cannot be the supreme Good (...) God does not fill heaven and earth because they contain Him, but rather the contrary, because they are contained in Him. Nor does He occupy the whole part by part, but, since He is unique, He is wholly present in every part. Thus, it cannot be thought that God is present in all things in such a way that each thing contains Him in proportion to the magnitude of its dimensions—that is, greater things in greater measure, and smaller things in lesser measure—but rather He is wholly in all things, or all things are in Him."[^20] As can be seen, there is no novelty in the argumentation. There is only a magnificent clarity of exposition and orderly arrangement.

### The Synthesis of Saint John Damascene

With Saint John Damascene, the same situation obtains as with Saint Isidore of Seville: we find ourselves before a magnificent compilation of preceding patristic thought. His teaching concerning the divine nature is expounded systematically in the first book of his *Exposition of the Orthodox Faith* (*De Fide Orthodoxa*). It represents a synthesis that serves as the finest epilogue to the patristic era and, at the same time, the portal to the medieval period.[^21]

The Damascene begins by underscoring the ineffability and incomprehensibility of the divine nature. This is the starting point of his considerations concerning God: the Godhead is ineffable and incomprehensible. Yet—he continues—we are not condemned to an absolute ignorance of God. Every man can know God by using his reason and regarding the world as a witness to its Creator: "God is incomprehensible. *No man hath seen God at any time: the only-begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he hath declared him* (Jn 1:18). Thus, God cannot be explained by word, nor can He be comprehended by any concept, for *no man knoweth the Father, but the Son, and he to whom it shall please the Son to reveal him* (Mt 11:27) (...) Nevertheless, God did not leave us in absolute ignorance concerning Himself. For there is no mortal in whom God has not naturally implanted lights whereby he may know that He exists. Moreover, created things, their conservation, and their governance proclaim the majesty of the divine nature (Wis 13:5). To this must be added the knowledge that God revealed of Himself in accordance with our capacity and manner of understanding: formerly through the Law and the Prophets;[^22] and later through His Only-Begotten Son, our Lord, God, and Saviour Jesus Christ."[^23]

In the journey toward God, the discoveries of human reason are not the sole source of knowledge. Above these discoveries, with greater firmness and greater light, stands the teaching that proceeds from the divine initiative through the Old Testament and, above all, through His Son. Human reason has its role; but revelation comes to its aid.[^24]

After observing, not without humor, that he has received neither the gift of working miracles nor the gift of teaching, the Damascene, having implored the grace of the Most Holy Trinity, begins his exposition of the reasons for affirming the existence of God. These are threefold:

The first reason that God exists is drawn from the mutability of things: "All that exists is either created or uncreated. If it is created, it is also mutable. For what began through a change must necessarily remain subject to change, whether it perishes or is transformed from one thing into another. But if it is uncreated, it follows necessarily that it cannot change. Things whose mode of being is contrary must also have a contrary mode of being, and their properties will likewise be contrary (...) Thus, since these things are mutable, it is also necessary that they have been created. But if they have been created, it is beyond doubt that they have been created by some creator. Now, it is necessary that the creator be uncreated; for if he himself were created, he would have to have been created by another, and we should therefore have to proceed in this manner until we reached someone uncreated. Now, since the creator of things is uncreated, it is necessary that he be immutable. What else is this but God?"[^25]

The second reason is drawn from the governance and conservation of the world: "Furthermore, the very conjunction of all created things, their conservation, and their governance teach us that there exists a God who has united the universe, who sustains it, conserves it, and governs it. For how could it come about that among so many natures in mutual conflict (I refer to fire, water, air, and earth), they should contribute to the constitution of a single world, and be coherent with one another, or be bound together with so strong a bond that it can never be loosed, unless an omnipotent power united them and perpetually preserved them from dissolution?"[^26]

The third reason is drawn from the order existing among things in the world. The Damascene has in mind principally the Manichaeans, against whom it is affirmed that the cosmos is united neither by chance nor spontaneously: "What is it that saw or appointed determined places for the things that are in heaven, that exist on earth, that wander through the air, or that dwell in the waters—indeed, for the things that are prior to these: heaven, earth, air, the nature of fire and of water? By whom were they united and separated? What is it that imparted movement to them and guides them in a perennial and unceasing course? Is it not their Artificer who imprinted on all things the reason and the manner by which this universe is guided and ruled? And who is their Artificer? Is it not He who created them and brought them forth into the nature of things? We must not attribute this either to an automatic force or to chance. For even if their existence were due to chance, who ordered them? And even if they wish to concede this: to whom does it belong to conserve them and guard them in accordance with those first laws whereby they were created? It is evident that this belongs to someone other than chance, and this can be none other than God."[^27]

Having arrived at this point, an important question arises: once the existence of God is accepted, how far can our knowledge of the divine nature extend? On this question, the Damascene reproduces the teaching of Saint Gregory of Nazianzus: God is neither wholly accessible to human knowledge, nor completely inaccessible. The distinction between divine attributes or names as affirmative or negative is thoroughly reasoned by the Damascene: affirmative names are grounded upon the principle of causality—that is, that God is the principle of all things, the being of beings, the life of the living, and therefore in the perfections of beings He has left His trace; negative names—and this is very important—are negative in their grammatical form, but not in their intention. Indeed, negative names are to be understood in the sense of eminence. Thus, for example, God is not a body, but He contains within Himself in an eminent manner whatever perfection belongs to bodily being, and so forth.[^28]

Among the divine names, two are especially emphasised by the Damascene: *Theos* and *He Who Is*. The Damascene understands *Theos* as deriving from the verb *theaomai* and assigns to it the meaning of "to see": God is He who always sees. The other name, contained in Exodus 3:14, is the most proper for speaking of God, because God is the being who comprehends within Himself the entire fullness of being, like an immense sea of essence. Here the Damascene harmonises once again with Saint Gregory of Nazianzus.[^29]

The Damascene links God's holiness, God's existence, and God's goodness. Following Pseudo-Dionysius, he views creation as an outcome of divine goodness, which tends to bestow itself, to give itself, to diffuse itself;[^30] but with greater clarity than Dionysius, he reaffirms divine independence and freedom in this self-giving. For the Damascene, the assertion that creation does not proceed from any internal necessity in God, but from His free will, is truly foundational.[^31]

After pointing out that there are many things concerning God which we can perceive obscurely yet find difficult to express, the Damascene divides his exposition of the divine attributes into two parts:

1) What things we can understand and affirm of God: "That God is without beginning and without end, that He is eternal, sempiternal, uncreated, immutable, free from all alteration, simple, uncomposed, incorporeal, invisible, intangible, incircumscript, infinite, exceeding all knowledge, greater than all thought, incomprehensible, good, just, creator of all creatures, omnipotent, all-containing, all-seeing, foreseeing all things, ruling all things with power, and, finally, that He is judge. We know and confess all these things. And also that He is the only God, that is, one sole substance."[^32]

2) What we can neither understand nor say of God: "Now, what the substance of God is, in what manner He is present in all things, how God is begotten or proceeds from God (...), these things we neither know nor are capable of explaining."[^33] As can be seen, the Damascene faithfully reproduces the great patristic tradition concerning God: God is knowable by natural reason, and at the same time transcends all knowledge; we know by natural reason what God is and we can use diverse designations to speak of Him, distinguishing even several of His various attributes; but we do not know—because it transcends us—what the divine substance is like and, therefore, what those attributes are truly like, all of them united in the infinite simplicity of God.

There are in God three perfect and subsistent Persons, who cannot be considered as "parts" of a divine substance; rather, each is the whole divine substance. For this reason one must not say that the divine substance belongs to three Persons, but that it is in three Persons.[^34] That is to say, each of the divine *hypostases* must be regarded as the whole of God, not as a part of God. One understands the force with which the Damascene accompanies this thought with the affirmation of *perichoresis*, in which the unity of substance is manifested: "These *hypostases* are in one another, not so as to be confused, but to contain one another mutually, in accordance with the word of the Lord: *I am in the Father, and the Father in me* (Jn 10:38; 14:10). We do not call the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit three gods. On the contrary, we affirm one sole God, the Holy Trinity, for the Son and the Spirit are referred to a single principle, without composition or confusion, contrary to the heresy of Sabellius. These Persons are united without being confused, yet containing one another mutually. There exists among them a circuminsession (one Person being in the other two) without mixture or confusion, by virtue of which they are neither separated nor divided in substance, contrary to the heresy of Arius. To express it in a word, the Godhead is undivided in each of the individuals."[^35]

This final expression is exceptionally felicitous and brings into relief the importance that must be accorded to circuminsession in a correct exposition of Trinitarian doctrine. Circuminsession is nothing other than the expression of the unity whereby the three Persons are the same and only God. The Godhead is undivided in each of the individuals. We have seen this thought appear almost from the earliest moment of patristics: given the infinite divine simplicity, the Father imparts to the Son, in begetting Him, the entire divine substance, since that substance is indivisible.

Adopting expressions already utilised by the Cappadocians, the Damascene conceives the Persons as distinct modes of subsisting in God (*tropoi tēs hyparxeōs*). These distinct modes are nothing other than the relations that distinguish the Persons among themselves.[^36] The Damascene gives them the names of paternity, filiation, and procession—or unbegottenness, birth, and procession—and confesses that little more can be said of the Trinitarian mystery, especially regarding the distinction between "generation" and *ekporeusis*.[^37] The Damascene insists that the Father is the sole fountain of the Godhead (*monarchia*), within an understanding that accommodates without difficulty the procession of the Spirit from the Father through the Son (*a Patre per Filium*).[^38] In this, too, he bears witness to the venerable Eastern tradition.[^39]

### Trinitarian Formulations in Western Professions of Faith

In questions concerning the mystery of the three Persons in God, the most significant texts—not only on account of their authority, but also because of the precision of their language—are found in the professions of faith that come to us from the West. Here are those of greatest prominence:

#### a) Fides Damasi

This Creed appears to have been composed in Gaul around the year 500. The matters concerning the Trinity appear deliberately defined by means of glosses:

"We believe in one God the Father Almighty, and in one Lord Jesus Christ, our Lord, the Son of God, and in [one] God the Holy Spirit. We do not worship and confess three gods, but confess the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit as one God. Not one God as if solitary, or the same being Father to Himself and the same being the Son, but that it is the Father who begot, and the Son who is begotten, and the Holy Spirit neither begotten nor unbegotten (*non genitum, nec ingenitum*), neither created nor made, but proceeding from the Father and the Son (*sed de Patre et Filio procedentem*), co-eternal and co-operator with the Father and the Son (...) Thus, in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit we confess one God, because God is a name of power, not of property. For Father is the proper name of the Father, and Son is the proper name of the Son, and Holy Spirit is the proper name of the Holy Spirit. And in this Trinity we believe in one God, because it proceeds from one Father, which signifies that it has the same nature as the Father, the same substance, and the same power. The Father begot the Son, not by His will, nor by necessity, but by nature."[^40]

Gathering formulas already familiar to us, the Creed insists upon God who is one, but not solitary. Nor can the Trinity of Persons be conceived in a modalist sense, as though the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit were the same. The procession of the Spirit is distinguished from that of the Son. Being the same God, each of the Persons has His proper name. The equality of the Persons among themselves is rooted in the fact that they proceed from the Father. Note that the Holy Spirit is said to proceed *de Patre et Filio*.

#### b) Clemens Trinitas

This formula is likewise situated in Gaul around the year 500. The Trinitarian affirmations are very similar:

"The merciful Trinity is one Godhead. Thus the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are one font, one substance, one power, one dominion. We say that the Father is God, that the Son is God, that the Holy Spirit is God; we do not confess that they are three gods, but most piously confess that they are one single God. For, proclaiming three Persons, we confess with a Catholic and apostolic voice that they are one substance. Thus, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and the three are one (*unum*) (cf. 1 Jn 5:7). Three, neither confused with one another nor divided, but distinct yet joined, and joined yet distinct (*sed et distincte coniuncti et coniuncti distincti*); united in substance, but distinct (*discreti*) in their names: united in nature, distinct in Persons; equal in Godhead, equal in majesty, concordant in trinity, partakers of splendour. They are in such wise one (*unum*), that we doubt not that they are at the same time three; they are in such wise three, that we confess they cannot be separated from one another."[^41]

The formula begins clearly with the affirmation of oneness in God, proceeding thence to speak of the trinity of Persons. The formulas *nec confusi nec divisi* approximate already the more detailed clauses of the *Quicumque* Creed.

#### c) The Creed Quicumque

This Creed, designated "Athanasian", was composed in Latin between the end of the fifth century and the end of the sixth. It is a detailed exposition of Trinitarian faith. The teaching of Saint Augustine is universally present here. The section dedicated to the mystery of the Trinity reads as follows:

"The Catholic faith is this: That we worship one God in Trinity, and Trinity in Unity; neither confounding the Persons, nor dividing the Substance. For there is one Person of the Father, another of the Son, and another of the Holy Ghost. But the Godhead of the Father, of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost is all one, the glory equal, the majesty co-eternal. Such as the Father is, such is the Son, and such is the Holy Ghost. The Father uncreated, the Son uncreated, and the Holy Ghost uncreated. The Father immense, the Son immense, and the Holy Ghost immense. The Father eternal, the Son eternal, and the Holy Ghost eternal. And yet they are not three eternals, but one eternal. As also there are not three uncreated, nor three immenses, but one uncreated, and one immense. So likewise the Father is Almighty, the Son Almighty, and the Holy Ghost Almighty; and yet they are not three Almighties, but one Almighty. So the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Ghost is God; and yet they are not three Gods, but one God. So likewise the Father is Lord, the Son Lord, and the Holy Ghost Lord; and yet not three Lords, but one Lord. For like as we are compelled by the Christian verity to acknowledge every Person by Himself to be God and Lord; so are we forbidden by the Catholic religion to say, there be three Gods, or three Lords. The Father is made of none, neither created, nor begotten. The Son is of the Father alone, not made, nor created, but begotten. The Holy Ghost is of the Father and of the Son, neither made, nor created, nor begotten, but proceeding. So there is one Father, not three Fathers; one Son, not three Sons; one Holy Ghost, not three Holy Ghosts. And in this Trinity none is before or after other, none is greater or less than another; but the whole three Persons are co-eternal together, and co-equal. So that in all things, as is aforesaid, the Unity in Trinity, and the Trinity in Unity is to be worshipped."[^42]

Language and concepts appear here fully articulated. Unity and trinity must be confessed, avoiding simultaneously Sabellianism and Arianism. The grammatical rules governing the use of singular and plural are observed with perfection when referring either to the substance or to the Persons. The distinction of origin between the Son and the Spirit is defined with precision. Deliberate emphasis is placed upon the procession of the Spirit from the Father and the Son. The Spirit is *non factus, nec creatus, nec genitus, sed procedens*, without saying more regarding the manner of this procession. There is one sole God, but three Persons, who are distinct and incommunicable: the Father is not the Son, the Son is not the Father, and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son. The Creed possesses great pedagogical force and constitutes a fine summary of Trinitarian doctrine. Its wide acceptance is readily understandable. From the perspective of the history of Trinitarian dogma, it must be observed that the viewpoint is highly conceptual and somewhat detached from the consideration of the Trinitarian mystery in the light of salvation history.[^43]

### Councils I, III, and XI of Toledo

The Councils of Toledo numbered eighteen. They were celebrated between the years 400 and 702—that is, up to nine years prior to the Arab conquest. The First Council convened to counter the Priscillianist heresy, connected with Gnostic errors. The Trinitarian formula of this first Council, supplemented by twelve anathemas, was expanded in the *Libellus in modum Symboli* of Pastor, Bishop of Palencia, a redaction which was in turn approved at the Council of Toledo of 447.[^44]

Here is a summary of the text:

"We believe in one true God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, maker of all things visible and invisible (...) That this God is one, and one is the Trinity of His divine substance. That the Father is not the same as the Son, but has a Son who is not the Father. That the Son is not the Father, but is the Son of God, of the nature of the Father. That there is also the Paraclete Spirit, who is neither the Father nor the Son, but proceeds from the Father and the Son. The Father is therefore unbegotten; the Son is begotten; the Holy Spirit is not begotten, but proceeds from the Father and the Son."[^45]

The Third Council of Toledo, celebrated in May 589, marked the end of Arianism in Spain. Eight Arian bishops, King Reccared, and his court subscribed to the Trinitarian profession of faith formulated by the Council. Here is a summary:

"We confess the Father, who begot of His substance the Son equal and co-eternal with Himself; not that the Father is at once born and father, but that in Person, one is the Father who begot, another the Son who was begotten; yet both subsist in the Godhead of the one substance: the Father, from whom the Son proceeds, proceeds from none; the Son, who has His origin from the Father, yet without beginning or diminution in the Godhead (...) Likewise we must confess and preach the Holy Spirit, who proceeds from the Father and the Son, and is of the same substance with the Father and the Son; that the Holy Spirit is the third Person in the Trinity, and possesses a common divine essence with the Father and with the Son."[^46]

The Eleventh Council of Toledo was held in the opening days of November 675. Its importance derives neither from the number of bishops in attendance nor from subsequent confirmation by Rome; rather, it lies in the expository clarity of its Creed, for whose composition earlier materials from other councils and from the great Doctors of the Church—such as Saint Ambrose, Saint Hilary, Saint Augustine, and Saint Fulgentius of Ruspe—were employed. This expository clarity has caused this Creed to be cited universally as a model of the Church's Trinitarian faith, and in this respect it enjoys immense authority. Here are several passages of the text:[^47]

#### a) The Trinity

"We believe and confess that the holy and ineffable Trinity, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, is one God by nature, of one substance, of one nature (...)"

#### b) The Three Persons

"And we confess that the Father is neither begotten nor created, but unbegotten. For He has His origin from none: from Him the Son received His birth and the Holy Spirit His procession. He is, therefore, the fountain and source of the entire Godhead (...)

"We confess also that the Son was born of the Father's substance without having had a beginning, before all ages; and yet He was not created. For neither did the Father ever exist without the Son, nor the Son without the Father.

"And nevertheless, the Father does not proceed from the Son as the Son from the Father; because it is not the Father who receives generation from the Son, but the Son from the Father (...) He is indeed Father of the Son, but is God not proceeding from the Son; the latter, on the other hand, is Son of the Father and God proceeding from the Father (...)

"We believe also that the Son is of one substance with the Father (*unius substantiae*) ... For it must be believed that the Son is begotten or born, not from nothing, nor from another distinct substance, but from the bosom of the Father, that is, of His own substance.

"And let it not be thought that, having been begotten by the Father, we say this Son of God is a severed portion of His nature; rather we affirm that the perfect Father has begotten a perfect Son, without diminution or division ...

"This Son is Son of God by nature and not by adoption (...) We must believe that God the Father begot Him neither by will nor by necessity, for in God there is no necessity, nor does will precede wisdom.

"We believe also that the Holy Spirit, who is the third Person of the Trinity, is one God and equal to God the Father and the Son, of their very substance and their very nature. Nevertheless, He was not created, nor begotten, but proceeding from both (*ab utrisque procedentem*), He is the Spirit of both.

"(...) He is not said to be only the Spirit of the Father, but the Spirit of the Father and of the Son together.

"For He does not proceed from the Father to the Son, nor proceed from the Son for the sanctification of creatures, but is shown as proceeding simultaneously from the one and the other, since He is recognised to be the charity of both (...)"

#### c) The Language Concerning the Trinity

"Here is how one must speak of the Trinity: it must be said and believed that it is not triple, but triune. Nor can it be affirmed that the Trinity is in one God, but rather that one God is triune.

"In the names of Persons that express a relation, the Father is referred to the Son, the Son to the Father, and the Holy Spirit to both; but when speaking of the three Persons considered in their relations, one believes in a single nature or substance."

#### d) One Single Nature

"...one single substance, but three Persons.

"In effect, the Father is Father not by reference to Himself, but by reference to the Son. The Son is Son, not by reference to Himself, but by reference to the Father. Likewise the Holy Spirit bears reference not to Himself, but to the Father and the Son, because He is called the Spirit of the Father and of the Son (...)

"If we are asked concerning each of the Persons, we must confess that each is God. The Father is said to be God, the Son is said to be God, the Holy Spirit is said to be God, each individually; yet there are not three gods, but one God (...)

"Thus we confess and believe that each Person individually is fully God; and the three, one God. Their unique, undivided, and equal Godhead, their majesty and power, is neither diminished in each nor increased in the three; for neither does it lack anything when each Person singly is called God, nor has it anything in addition when the three Persons are called one God.

"Thus this holy Trinity, which is one true God, is neither outside number nor enclosed in number. For number appears in the relation of the Persons; but in the substance of the Godhead, it is meaningless to speak of number."

#### e) Trinity without Separation or Confusion

"And not because we have said that these three Persons are one God may we say that He who is the Father is Son, or that He is Son who is Father, or that He is Father or Son who is Holy Spirit (...)

"For when we say that the Father is not the same as the Son, we refer to the distinction of Persons. On the other hand, when we say that the Father is the same as the Son (...), we express that this belongs to the nature or substance by which He is God (...)

"We recognise, then, the Trinity in the distinction of Persons; we profess unity by reason of nature or substance (...)

"Nevertheless, these three Persons must not be conceived as though they could be separated; for we believe that none of them has existed or operated before the other, nor after the other, nor without the other (...)

"Wherefore we confess and believe that this Trinity is inseparable and unconfused (...)

"Thus, these three being one single reality and one single reality three, each Person nevertheless preserves His property. The Father possesses eternity without birth; the Son, eternity with birth; the Holy Spirit, procession without birth, with eternity."

The Latin penchant for precision is manifested here in all its vigour. Of the Trinity one must affirm that it is triune (*trina*), but not triple (*triplex*), since the adjective "triple" connotes complete numerical distinction. Nor should one say that the one God is "in the Trinity", as though it were a question of something divided or shared among three. Each Person does not merely participate in the Godhead, but is the very Godhead itself. This is why it is specified that we do not affirm greater divinity when considering the three divine Persons together than when considering them separately. The unity of substance in God is not the sum of three substances. Since the Persons possess the identical numerical substance and each is identical with this sole substance, they differ in nothing other than the relation existing among them by reason of their origin of procession. In this, and in this alone. They are, therefore, inseparable and unconfused among themselves. Although they are the same reality, nonetheless each Person, by reason of origin of procession, possesses His own distinctive note, His *proprietas*: the Father has eternity without birth; the Son has it with birth; the Holy Spirit has it without birth, but as procession. The distinction of Persons is explained by the relation of opposition: number exists solely in the relation of the Persons. Both the distinction of Persons and *perichoresis* are vigorously underscored.[^48] Grounded upon this expository clarity, medieval theology would endeavour to articulate the Trinitarian mystery within a speculative framework and in dialectical form.
