# Foreword

For many years it has fallen to me to teach the treatise on God: first at the Major Seminary of Seville during the 1960s, and later in the Faculty of Theology at the University of Navarra. When preparing my lectures, it seemed indispensable to draft some lecture notes to be used in presenting the matters of greatest moment; subsequently, it became clear that it would facilitate my students' study—above all their immediate preparation for examinations—if I provided them with those very personal notes.

Thus were these pages born: as I was preparing classes, and with the desire to gather together, both for my own personal meditation and for my lectures, several nuclear themes concerning God and the mystery of His intimate life. Furthermore, these pages have been written with the intention of offering my students materials with which to delve deeper into the many excellent treatises and monographs on the Trinitarian mystery that exist today, and, above all, with the desire to prepare them to take delight in reading the great authorities of the theological tradition, such as—among others—Saint Athanasius, Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, Saint Gregory of Nyssa, Saint Augustine, Richard of Saint Victor, Saint Bonaventure, and Saint Thomas Aquinas.

These pages now seeing the light of day pursue, therefore, the same aim and have followed a similar path to that of the majority of theological manuals and treatises that come to be written. They have sprung forth slowly, in step with academic needs and in close contact with students. Hence their scholastic character, their didactic style, and their primary concern to provide the indispensable data for a first university-level encounter with the central mystery of Christianity: the Triune God, who reveals Himself definitively in Christ.

As the years pass, I give thanks to God ever more, among so many other things, for the magnificent students He has granted me. They can bear witness that frequently, when explaining any given theme and recalling the expressions of some of my former professors, I have proudly expressed my gratitude to God for having had them as masters and for having lived alongside them at the Pontifical University of Salamanca and at the Pontifical University of Saint Thomas Aquinas in Rome.

I now recall those theologians with intimate gratitude. I think, for example, of A. Colunga, J. A. de Aldama, M. Cuervo, L. Arias, A. Peinador, A. Huerga, and so many others, who wrote important pages in Spanish theology during the second half of the twentieth century, and whose memory remains indelible in those of us who had the grace of being their disciples. They gave us, above all else, the witness of their faith, of their love for the Church, and of their passion for theological work well done. To them, who knew how to impart to us so many invaluable things, this book is primarily dedicated.

I also dedicate this book to my students: they number in their hundreds, and are scattered across all continents. They assisted me in my work through their presence, their attentiveness, their questions, their enthusiasm, and even at times through their urgent pleading that I explain with greater clarity some question which I had not succeeded in expounding fully. Some of those students have also assisted me materially: first in the successive editions of the lecture notes, and later in the final drafting of this book and in the correction of the printer's proofs.

To Raymund, Pablo, Carlos, Ángel, Ricardo, Fernando, and Wilson Fredy goes, together with this dedication, all my heartfelt gratitude for their help and friendship. This gratitude is particularly warm towards Juan Luis Bastero, a student of mine many years ago and now a well-known professor among mariologists, whose help proved of great value in finalising this book.

Saint Josemaría, in an especially important text which the Church has chosen for her Office of Readings, when sketching the soul's journey towards God, describes the contemplation of the Trinitarian mystery in this way:

> "Do not forget, however, that to be with Jesus is, assuredly, to meet His Cross. When we abandon ourselves into the hands of God, it often happens that He allows us to taste sorrow, loneliness, contradictions, calumnies, slanders, mockery, both from within and from without; for He wishes to conform us to His image and likeness, and He also tolerates that we be called madmen and accounted fools. In truly admiring and loving the Most Sacred Humanity of Jesus, we shall discover His Wounds one by one. And in those seasons of passive purgation—painful and severe, of sweet and bitter tears which we strive to hide—we shall need to enter into each one of those Most Holy Wounds: in order to be purified, to rejoice in that redeeming Blood, and to be strengthened.
> 
> Then the heart feels the need to distinguish and adore each of the divine Persons. In some measure, it is like a discovery made by the soul in the supernatural life. And it converses lovingly with the Father, and with the Son, and with the Holy Spirit; and it submits readily to the action of the life-giving Paraclete, who is bestowed upon us unmerited. Words are no longer needed, for the tongue cannot express itself; the intellect is stilled. One does not reason; one gazes! And the soul breaks out afresh into song with a new song, because it feels and knows itself also to be lovingly gazed upon by God, at all hours."[^f1]

Contemplation of the Sacred Humanity of Christ and, through Him—in whose countenance the face of God is definitively revealed—entering into the contemplation of the divine intimacy.

This book will remind my students of those slow afternoons in Pamplona, peaceful and so often rainy, in which we were happy contemplating together, from our temporal vantage-point, the unfathomable mystery of the One and Triune God, who is eternally young and immensely happy, because He is an ineffable communion of Persons in an eternal act of Intellect and Love. In dedicating this book to them, I warmly desire that they may traverse, each according to how the Holy Spirit leads him, the path of contemplation described in this text of Saint Josemaría, knowing that their theological knowledge will not be authentic theology until it becomes an act of love and praise—that is to say, until it becomes authentic doxology. This is likewise what I wish for the reader.

# General Introduction

As its very name indicates, *Theo-logy* is the science of God just as He has revealed Himself to man and, therefore, just as He is known by faith. Our manual takes as the centre of its consideration the God who revealed Himself in creation, who spoke of old to our fathers by the prophets at many times and in various ways, and who in these last days has spoken to us by His Son (cf. Heb 1:1–2)—that is to say, the living God, the God manifested definitively in Christ, in the mystery of His oneness and of His Trinity.

Understood in all the breadth of its facets, the question concerning God is the most vital and radical question in the whole of theology. Within the theological enterprise, every other question finds its *raison d'être* precisely in its relationship to God, who is the centre of every theological enquiry. As J. H. Nicolas writes: "To say that the subject of theology is God is equivalent to saying that the mystery of the Holy Trinity is at the heart of theology: not merely that its study occupies a central place therein, but that everything studied in theology is in function of it and is carried out in its light."[^1]

The question of God is likewise the most radical question of human existence. Indeed, in the living God is to be found the ultimate reason for our existence; in Him lies the purpose of our earthly pilgrimage—of our *status viatoris*—and in Him is our final end. It may be said that the relationship between God and man is so intimate that God Himself enters into the theological definition of man. Philosophy, for its part, defined man as a *rational animal*, that is to say, as animated, sensible matter endowed with reason. Sacred Scripture, on the other hand, opens with the teaching that man is made in the image and likeness of God (Gen 1:26), reaches its culminating point with the death of the Redeemer on behalf of the whole human race (Rom 5:12–20) and with the affirmation that we are made children of God in the Son (Rom 8:14–17), and closes in the Apocalypse (Rev 21:2–4) with the vision of the New Jerusalem descending from heaven—that is, with the vision of the eternal triumph of man redeemed by Christ.

Consequently, to treat of God is to treat of a theme that decisively and profoundly affects man. At the same time, probing the structure of the human being inevitably raises the question of God. His Holiness John Paul II frequently exhorted theologians "to seek in the essential structures of human existence the transcendent dimensions that constitute man's radical capacity to be addressed by the Christian message so as to comprehend it as salvific, that is to say, as a gratuitous response of fullness to the fundamental questions of human life."[^2] Questions concerning God and questions concerning man can never be wholly separated.

The question concerning God is written into the very heart of man as a capacity, and even more as a desire for the infinite; hence man was created *capax Dei*, capable of God. As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states: "The desire for God is written in the human heart, because man is created by God and for God; and God never ceases to draw man to Himself, and only in God will man find the truth and happiness that he never stops looking for."[^3] For this reason, the question of man is so deeply implicated in the question of God that to omit or deny the question concerning God is tantamount to denying the transcendent dimension of man, reducing him to mere biology, to a bare product of chance, while disregarding the longings for the infinite within his heart.[^4]

Yet omniperfection, transcendence over the world, infinity, and ineffability are essential to the concept of God. God is above all speech and above all thought; how then shall we be able to speak of Him? As Saint Thomas wrote, referring precisely to divine ineffability: "Because we cannot know what God is, but rather what He is not, we cannot consider how He is, but rather how He is not."[^5] The reason for this trenchant assertion by Saint Thomas lies in the fact that we know through the images of things, whereas God transcends every image and every concept of Him that a created mind could ever construct. There is no intellect and no image capable of "containing" God.

For this reason, everything we say of God is always slight in comparison with what He is in reality. And, if one possesses a theological sense, the fear immediately assails us that what we say of Him may conceal Him rather than reveal Him, given the poverty of our words. Nor is this all. Unless the utmost exactitude in language and prudence in speaking of God are exercised, one runs the risk of speaking of an idol fashioned by our imagination, rather than of the true God, who is more intimate to us than we are to ourselves.[^6] And yet, despite this risk, it is necessary to speak of that God who, although He infinitely transcends us, is at the same time a God truly near, and the foundation of our very existence. It is both necessary and possible to speak of God precisely because of the transcendent calling inscribed in the heart of every man, because of his vocation to God, which renders him capable of God (*capax Dei*). And, above all, it is possible to speak of God because God Himself has spoken to man about Himself.

This is fundamentally what we propose to do in these lessons: to say something of what God has said of Himself. God has spoken and continues to speak to men "at many times and in various ways". He has spoken and speaks through creation, which is His handiwork, and through the human spirit made "in His image and likeness". He spoke through supernatural revelation in the Old Testament. He made Himself definitively known in His Son. And He has sent His Spirit to teach us all things and bring to our remembrance all that Christ said (cf. Jn 14:26).

## The Theological Treatise on God and Its Relationship with Natural Theology

We have just mentioned the diverse paths by which God reveals Himself: creation and the human spirit, the prophets, and His Son. Man's philosophical journey towards God finds its light and its foundation in the trace that God has left of Himself in creation and, especially, in man himself, created in His image and likeness. This journey takes as its point of departure the contemplation of created being—that is, the contemplation of the vestige of God as First Cause (*Causa Prima*) present throughout all creation—and it is pursued under the guidance of the light of natural reason. Consequently, it has as its goal God inasmuch as He is the First Cause: that is, the Supreme Being in relation to whom the world stands. At the culmination of this intellectual quest, God is not attained by human reason in Himself—in the intimate sanctuary of His being—but is attained exclusively as the term of the relation of total dependence of the universe (which is contingent) upon the Supreme Being, who is its Creator and the sole necessary Being. This is a knowledge which, though it does not attain to knowing *what* God is (*quid sit*), is sufficient to know *that* He exists (*an sit*).[^7] This philosophical journey is called *natural theology*: that is to say, reflection upon God conducted by the sole powers of reason and in the light of natural revelation.

At a distinct and higher level is situated the theological journey towards God. This journey takes as its point of departure the revelation which God has made of Himself, of His intimate life and of His nature. Moreover, it is a journey pursued in the light of faith. It is a knowledge both certain and obscure: God remains beyond all knowledge that we can attain through contemplating in the light of faith all that is offered to us in supernatural revelation.[^8] God remains a mystery; indeed, faith makes us see that this mystery is far more transcendent than could ever be suspected by mere natural reason. Yet even within its limits and its obscurity, faith—and consequently theological reflection—without entirely unveiling the mystery of God, leads us to a new knowledge immensely superior to that which can be attained by simple natural reason. It is through Revelation that we know that God is Father, Son, and Spirit; that is to say, we come to know something of the intimate life of God, of what God is in Himself. It is through Revelation that we know the merciful designs of God concerning man and history: what is the mystery of His will (cf. Col 1:26).

The revelation of Himself which God makes to man does not entirely dispel the mystery; at the same time that it reveals, it veils. It may be said that God reveals Himself by concealing Himself in human words and historical mediations. This is so because during man's earthly pilgrimage, God can only reveal Himself to him in human words, and there exists no human word capable of adequately expressing what God is. He is always beyond all expression, all utterance, and all created thought. As Walter Kasper writes: "God does not abolish His mystery in the act of revelation; He does not decipher it, as though we should subsequently know where we stand with God. Rather, revelation consists in God making manifest His hidden mystery: the mystery of His freedom and of His person. Revelation is therefore the revelation of the hidden God as such."[^9]

Sacred Scripture frequently intimates this dual character of manifestation and concealment that occurs in every revelation God makes of Himself. This is highlighted with singular beauty in the theophanies of Exodus (Ex 3:1–15; 19:16–25; 34:6–9), and especially in the second: God speaks to Moses in the midst of the thick darkness (Ex 20:21). In this second theophany, Moses learns that knowledge of the divine nature is unattainable to the human intellect. That is what it means to know in darkness. Saint Gregory of Nyssa comments:

> "In this consists the true knowledge of what we seek: in seeing in not seeing, for what we seek transcends all knowledge, being wholly encompassed by incomprehensibility as by a darkness."[^10]

To see God in not seeing: in this consists the highest summit that human knowledge of God can reach on this earth. This paradox proposed by apophatic theology is nothing other than the consequence of considering God's transcendence over all created thought. That is why this darkness is called luminous. It is luminous because it ushers the soul into the mystery, without attempting to rationalise it. Within it, Moses learns "what is necessary to know concerning God: that to know Him consists in not forming any concept of Him on the basis of things known according to the human mode of knowing."[^11]

It was Blaise Pascal who formulated with exceptional clarity the tension between the image of the God arrived at through natural reason and that of the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob: the living God of Jesus Christ—what he termed the "God of faith" and the "God of the philosophers". For indeed, if God remains mysterious even at the summit of His revelation in Jesus Christ, how much more mysterious will He appear in the mere discursus of reason! This radical divergence of planes has led some thinkers to present not merely as contrasting, but as utterly incompatible, the "God of the philosophers"—that is, the God who can be reached by the simple effort of reason—and the "God of faith", the God who has revealed Himself in Christ.

No one has succeeded in formulating the objections against the compatibility between the God known by the light of reason and the God known by revelation with greater precision and dramatic force than Karl Barth. Barth calls the "God of the philosophers"—that is, the God spoken of by Plato, Aristotle, Cicero, and Plotinus—an idol: a false god. And it is well known that the true God is incompatible with idols.[^12] Consequently, according to Barth, natural theology and the theological treatise on God would be mutually exclusive.

Such a position is plainly incompatible with Catholic doctrine concerning God, creation, man, and revelation. For according to the doctrine of the faith, God—the true God—"can be known with certainty by the natural light of human reason from created things, for since the creation of the world His invisible nature has been clearly perceived by the intellect through the things that have been made (cf. Rom 1:20)."[^13]

It should be noted that we are not debating here whether in the historical order of existence any man has ever been led to God exclusively through philosophical reasoning. As Jean Daniélou writes, this does not constitute a genuine problem, because conversion to God is in every instance an affair in which man is engaged in his totality; not merely his reasoning, but also his heart, his will, his freedom, and, above all, the grace of God.[^14] No, that is not the question; rather, it is a different though related enquiry, which may be formulated in these questions: Is it possible for natural reason, by its own effort, to arrive at the discovery of the existence of God starting from reflection upon its own existence and the existence of the world? And is this knowledge attained through purely natural ways and means compatible with the knowledge bestowed upon us in Christ as a gift?

As is well known, the theological tradition—from the Holy Fathers down to our own day—has always responded that it is possible for human reason, albeit amidst grave difficulties and limitations, to attain something of the knowledge of God. The Magisterium of the Church, especially at the First Vatican Council, has solemnly taught this, while at the same time insisting upon the moral necessity of revelation in order that all men may know easily, with firm certainty and with no admixture of error, "all those things in divine matters that are not of themselves inaccessible to reason."[^15]

It must therefore be affirmed that neither the "God of the philosophers" and the "God of faith" are incompatible, nor is theological knowledge of God incompatible with philosophical knowledge. Indeed, it seems clear that a certain natural knowledge of God is indispensable for receiving revelation. Without an elementary natural theology, theological discourse about God is impossible; the two discourses not only do not exclude each other, but mutually imply one another. Natural theology is the foundation of supernatural theology, and, in turn, supernatural theology protects and preserves natural theology from error. It is one and the same God: He of whom natural reason speaks—beholding Him reflected and revealed in His creation—and He of whom theology speaks as it operates in the light of faith.

Natural theology has the office of serving as a preamble of faith (*praeambulum fidei*); not only does it not contradict the knowledge of God received through faith, but that natural knowledge is a necessary presupposition for the reception of faith. It is evident that the God at whom the effort of reason arrives when contemplating being constitutes a mystery offered to us, not an object that can be dominated or defined by our mind. God cannot be affirmed except as He who is beyond all objects, as He who transcends every finite mode of being.

At the same time, it is equally clear that the God of Revelation—the God who discloses His intimate life—can only be accepted by man from the place where he finds himself: in this world, and within his own heart and mind. For this reason, natural knowledge of God not only does not contradict the knowledge of faith, but is a necessary presupposition of faith; the rejection of natural theology would render the knowledge of God through faith impossible. For in order to receive Scripture as the Word of God, it is first required to accept that God exists and that He is capable of manifesting Himself to man.[^16]

It is also clear that natural theology cannot of itself culminate in the knowledge of a Saviour God, because salvation depends upon the divine initiative, which is sovereignly free and cannot, therefore, be deduced *a priori*. Natural theology finds its meaning in serving as a preparation for receiving revelation, faith, and salvation. Man has received an intellect capable of knowing being and a will capable of loving and transcending what is ephemeral, precisely in order to be capable of receiving God's address; the divine action of revelation—of giving Himself to man through supernatural revelation—is preceded by the creation of man in the image and likeness of God: that is to say, with the creation of man as capable of God (*capax Dei*), bearing in his intellect and heart the question of the Infinite.

God created man in His image and likeness—with the capacity to receive a more intimate divine communication[^17]—and, moreover, He has stamped His imprint upon creation, so that man, contemplating the intelligibility of creation, may be raised up towards his Creator: "Man is by nature and vocation a religious being. Coming from God, going toward God, man lives a fully human life only if he freely lives by his bond with God."[^18]

## God is Trinity

God revealed Himself first in His oneness as a personal Being, and, when the fullness of time had come, He revealed that this unity is realised in the ineffable mystery of the Trinity of Persons. It is one and the same mystery: the mystery of God. It is logical, therefore, that its theological consideration should constitute one and the same treatise, structured perhaps into two parts for material convenience of study. Indeed, the only existing God is the God who is triune in Persons; therefore, the treatise on God as One (*De Deo Uno*), even though it may not yet formally make explicit the Trinitarian mystery, "neither should nor can make abstraction from the personal articulation of the revealed mystery."[^19]

Now, the only God who exists is tripersonal. To speak of Him considered in His Unity demands, therefore, never forgetting that one is speaking not merely of a single personal God, but of a God who, in His oneness, is tripersonal. And at the same time, consideration of the distinction of Persons must never allow us to forget that these are three Persons who constitute one sole God.

This requires that in theology all matters pertaining to the One and Triune God be considered in such close unity that, whatever the manner in which the treatise is structured, the various questions must be understood as forming an inseparable whole. On the one hand, it is fitting that the treatise on God, whilst it assumes, heals, and deepens—with the certainty and ease conferred by faith—all that is contained in natural theology, should be an authentic theological treatise elaborated in the light of faith, in which the radical novelty of Our Lord's message concerning His Father God is made manifest. The theological consideration of the unity of God must also prepare the way for a thorough consideration of the Trinity of Persons in whom the unity of God is realised. And at the same time, the consideration of the Trinity of God and of each Person in particular must be conducted without ever losing sight of the fact that these are three Persons who are one single God in all the fullness of meaning conveyed by the word *unity*. As the *Quicumque* Creed specifies: although the Father is almighty, the Son almighty, and the Holy Spirit almighty, yet they are not three almighties, but one almighty.[^20]

This is likewise the manner in which the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* structures its exposition of doctrine concerning the oneness of God and His attributes (nos. 200–231), before passing on to set forth the Trinitarian mystery, which it synthesises in these three affirmations: *the Trinity is one*; *the divine Persons are really distinct from one another*; and *the divine Persons are relative to one another* (nos. 253–255). Indeed, one must maintain simultaneously that "the confession of God's oneness... is inseparable from the confession of God's existence" (no. 200), and that the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity "is the mystery of God in Himself" (no. 234). Moreover, this is a mystery that must be considered under a twofold aspect: the immanent and the economic: "The *theologia* is revealed to us through the *oikonomia*, but conversely, the *theologia* illuminates the whole *oikonomia*. God's works reveal who He is in Himself; the mystery of His inmost Being enlightens our understanding of all His works" (no. 236).

Between theology and economy there exists, therefore, a circular relationship which does not permit the separation of the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity, though neither does it identify them.[^21] This carries with it not only the necessity of giving paramount importance to the theology of the divine missions—since they constitute the key to the economic Trinity—but also the need to display coherently the relationship existing between the treatise on God and Christology. Indeed, the God of whom we are speaking is He who has definitively revealed Himself in Christ.[^22]

---

## The Treatise on God within Systematic Theology

The living God—one and unique in His Trinitarian mystery—is the goal of man's pilgrimage and is likewise the goal of the theological enterprise. He occupies, therefore, the very centre of theology. He is the beginning and the end of contemplation. Yet God is above all knowledge, beyond all human utterance and all human expression. He is ineffable. In no domain so much as in the theological consideration of God does the paradoxical character of theology manifest itself. Saint Augustine formulated this paradox in beautiful terms:

> "We seek Him that we may find Him; we seek Him because we have already found Him. He is sought that He may be found, because He is hidden; He is sought having been found, because He is infinite. For He satisfies the seeker according to the measure of the seeker's grasp, and causes the one who has found Him to go on understanding that he must continue seeking, in order to bring to completion what he has already begun to comprehend."[^23]

The mystery of God occupies the centre of theology; all other realities are contemplated inasmuch as they are related to Him. As Saint Thomas Aquinas wrote: "Since in sacred doctrine all things are treated under the aspect of God (*sub ratione Dei*)—either because they are God Himself, or because they are ordered to God as to their principle and end—it follows that the subject of this science is God."[^24]

We refer to the living God just as He has revealed Himself in Christ. This is a knowledge of God which, assuming the truth about God contained in natural theology, goes far beyond a mere concept of God, so as to penetrate, through faith, into the mystery of His intimate life: into the Trinitarian mystery,[^25] into which man has in some measure already entered by virtue of his vocation as a child in the Son. For this reason, in the Christian consideration of God what is at stake is not only what God is in Himself (*in se*), but also what God is for us (*pro nobis*).[^26] This mystery of God is the centre of life in heaven and is likewise the centre of theological contemplation on earth.
