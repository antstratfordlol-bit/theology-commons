## Chapter 19: God, Primordial Truth {#mateo-seco-trino.19}

Truth is the splendour of being; it is, for that very reason, the splendour of the *Ipsum esse subsistens*. To the supreme Being it belongs to be supreme truth, infinite intelligibility, indefectible authenticity. For this reason, luminosity and transparency, steadfastness and veracity, eternally accompany the divine being. God is subsistent truth and is also primordial truth, that is to say, the truth from which every other truth is derived.[^1]

In studying the truth of God, we touch upon one of the gravest questions, bearing the most profound consequences not only in the theological field, but also in the metaphysical realm and in that of human conduct. Indeed, the consideration of the truth of God is not reduced to the study of God as subsistent truth; it brings with it the affirmation that God is the cause of all other truth. This means that created truth finds its foundation and origin in uncreated Truth. This conviction bears decisively upon the answer to the questions raised by philosophy concerning the nature of truth and the possibility of attaining it.

The consideration of God as truth encompasses, therefore, the broad panorama of truth: Truth as a proper attribute of the divine being and its Trinitarian dimension, and the truth that things receive from God.

### The Notion of Truth

In the Old Testament, the consideration of the truth of God is linked to his fidelity, his omnipresence, his infinite wisdom, and his eternity. In God there is neither falsehood nor error, for light is incompatible with darkness. Everything in God is wisdom and sincerity. The word of God is uncontestable truth; his promises are always fulfilled, because God is omnipotent and omniscient. In the New Testament, all these nuances are taken up and brought to their fullness and concrete realisation in Christ, who is the true light (cf. Jn 1:9).[^2]

In the apostolic writings, when truth is spoken of, the reference is fundamentally to Jesus and to man's salvation. The truth is in Jesus (cf. Eph 4:21). The fact that the Gospel is the revelation of the mystery of Christ allows Saint Paul to affirm that his apostolic work consists in manifesting the truth, in causing the splendour of the Gospel of Christ to shine forth, in bringing to radiance the knowledge of the glory of God that shines on the face of Jesus Christ (2 Cor 4:2–6). Truth, the word of Jesus, and salvation are as indissolubly linked as truth and salvation are joined to the word of Yahweh in the Old Testament.

This is seen with special force in the Johannine writings. The Law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ (Jn 1:17). He is the light (cf. Jn 1:4–9; 8:12; 9:5; 12:46). To delve into Christian truth is nothing other than to enter deeply into the mystery of Christ, that is to say, to share in the Truth and the Life. The mission of the Spirit, who is the Spirit of truth, has as its purpose to guide men into all the truth (cf. Jn 16:13).[^3]

Finally, the Christian concept of truth converges in the conviction that Truth is not something abstract, but something exceedingly concrete: an intimately close Person, Christ. Jesus not only affirmed of himself that he is the light of the world (cf. Jn 8:12), but also the way, the truth, and the life (cf. Jn 14:6). Jesus is at once the way and the goal. Thomas Aquinas comments:

> "He is the way according to his Humanity, the goal according to his Divinity. Inasmuch as he is man he said that he was the way; inasmuch as he is God he added that he was the truth and the life. These two terms adequately designate the end of this journey. For the term of this journey is the end of human desire. Now man desires two things above all: first, the knowledge of truth, which is proper to him; secondly, the perpetuation of his being [...] Christ is the way to attain the knowledge of truth, since he himself is the truth [...] Christ is the way to arrive at life, because he is life."[^4]

In the philosophical sphere, the consideration of truth falls primarily upon its relation to the intellect and to the being of things. Truth forms part of the perfection of every being, inasmuch as by the very fact of existing it bears an essential relation to intellect. We call things true to the extent that they correspond to the idea that gave rise to them, to the idea that is their archetype. This correspondence with the archetypal idea entails that things are, in turn, intelligible—that is, apt to be understood by other intellects that did not design them. A good example of this is the page of any book: the written signs answer to a thought that has endowed them with meaning, and for this very reason those signs are open to being understood by the reader. The intelligibility they possess is derived from the mind that wrote them, and is offered to be understood by the reader. This twofold reference makes possible a communion—however distant—between the mind of the writer and that of the reader.[^5]

The concept of *true* adds a transcendental nuance to the concept of being: its essential relation to intellect. In God, this relation of being to intellect is perfect, since in God his intellect and his being are identical, for the divine being is his very act of understanding; in created being, this twofold relation is qualitatively diverse: it proceeds from an intellect—the divine—and is open to every intellect. We find, then, a twofold relation of things to intellect: an essential relation to the intellect that made them; an accidental relation to the intellects that know them. A house bears an essential relation to the architect who conceived it, and bears an accidental relation to the intellects upon which it does not depend for its being, but to whose knowledge it is open. This relation signifies that things are capable of eliciting a true knowledge of themselves.[^6]

Hence arises the distinction between ontological or transcendental truth—that is, the objective truth that resides in things—and logical or formal truth—that is, the truth residing in the mind that knows them. Ontological truth implies that the being of things is true. This truth consists in the conformity of things with their idea, with their original archetype.[^7] This means, moreover, that everything that exists is intelligible in itself; proceeding from an intellect, it bears intelligibility inscribed within the innermost core of its being. Whence the well-known axiom *ens et verum convertuntur*, being and the true are convertible, for everything existing is a substantialisation of thought.[^8]

Logical truth is configured in precisely the reverse direction of ontological truth: whereas ontological truth is the relation of things to the intellect from which they proceed, logical truth is the adequation of the intellect to the things it knows (*adaequatio intellectus et rei*). On this account it is said that things elicit truth in the mind. This partial truth is, in turn, an invitation to press forward until coming face to face with subsistent Truth.

Ontological truth is, for that very reason, a revelation of God. Theologians customarily call it natural revelation. Indeed, the being unveiled in truth "never narrowly confines the knower; on the contrary, truth is always an opening not only for itself and in itself, but towards a broader truth. Truth discloses being and also the connections of being; it opens vistas within realms yet unknown; it has within itself the movement towards a broader truth."[^9]

Logical truth resides formally in the judgement. A proposition is true if it coincides with the reality of the thing known. If one says, for example, "in the classroom next door there are twenty students", the truth of that statement depends on there actually being twenty students in that room. It depends on nothing else. It is not the assertion as such—nor the subjective force of one's own certainty—that creates the truth; rather, truth rests on the correctness with which the proposition conforms to reality. Logical truth depends on the object known, not the object known on logical truth. In this sense, a halo of humility surrounds logical truth, since it can occur only in man's self-opening towards the being that gives itself to him, in the mind's disinterested reception of that being which opens itself as a gift.

Logical truth demands of man humility, trust, and love: humility to receive the power of being as one who receives a gift; trust before its light; love that leads to wonder before the being manifested to us. Logical truth cannot occur if there is no opening of the mind towards being. This openness is an affirmation that the truth of things is worthy of our trust; in ontological truth, being reveals itself to us as worthy of being received, as trustworthy.[^10]

### God, Subsistent Truth

Given his infinite simplicity, in God, "intellect, that which is understood, the intelligible species, and the act of understanding are one and the same thing; and therefore, when it is said that God is intelligent, no multiplicity is introduced into his substance."[^11] This means that in God there is a perfect identity between ontological truth and logical truth: his being is identified with his act of intellect, and his act of understanding is identified with his being. This is proper to the *Ipsum esse subsistens*.

If truth is in things to the extent that they conform to the intellect upon which they essentially depend, and resides in the intellect that knows them to the extent that this intellect is identified with them, it follows that God is subsistent and first Truth, for both identifications occur in the highest degree in God: his being not only conforms to his intellect, but is his very understanding; his intellect not only corresponds to the thing known, but his understanding is his very being.[^12]

Between being and understanding, between being and truth, no greater identity can exist than this. The essence of God is nothing other than being, truth, radiance, light. For this reason, God is simultaneously an infinite act of openness and communication. He is so in Trinitarian communion; he is so likewise in his openness towards created being. That which is infinitely perfect in being is infinitely manifest, infinitely open to intellect. The Being of God is, above all, an act of eternal and infinite patency to himself. The infinity of the divine act of understanding—if the expression be permitted—is the measure of his infinite intelligibility.

God is, therefore, subsistent Truth. He is all truth. He is the infinite truth from which every other truth is derived. In the same way that it is said that, after creation, there are more beings, but there is no more being, so too must it be said that, after creation, there are more truths, but there is no more truth. Between the infinite and perfectly simple Truth that God is and the multitude of truths that fill the spirit of man, there exists an analogous relationship to that between subsistent Being and the multitude of created beings. Created beings are contained in God as in their principle and exemplary cause; so too are truths contained in this supreme Truth, which is the rule of them all.[^13] For this very reason, created truths add no further truth to Truth. They are a revelation leading towards fontal Truth.

This statement carries with it another of great anthropological significance: to the extent that the human intellect embraces truth, it participates in supreme Truth. To seek the truth with a sincere heart—even the most modest created truth—means implicitly to seek God. As has already been seen, Saint Thomas Aquinas relies on the human desire for truth—a desire that is boundless—to defend his conviction that in the deepest depths of the human heart there dwells the desire to attain God.[^14]

### God and the Truth of Things

Being subsistent Truth, God is primordial truth, that is to say, the truth whereby all things are true. This means that the Truth of God is the measure of the truth of things. We find ourselves, therefore, before the affirmation that truth is first and foremost in an intellect: the divine intellect. And it is secondarily in things. The Truth of God creates the truth of things, which are true because they have received it together with their being.

The expression *primordial truth* signifies that, in God, logical truth is not measured by things; rather, the truth of things is measured by the truth of God. The knowledge God possesses of things does not take place by means of images of them. It is not a knowledge *per species*, but an originating knowledge. Divine knowledge "is not a knowledge of copies, but a knowledge of originals, which grounds the entity itself and affirms it in all its relations. God does not take the measure of the object from its already existing being; the object has its measure in the idea God has of it."[^15]

Indeed, to be first cause implies not only being the efficient cause of all that exists, but also its exemplary cause and its final cause. Things have been made by God, reflect divine truth, and find their meaning in God. God is, therefore, the measure of the truth of all existing things.[^16]

The expression *truth of things* has, therefore, a profound theological sense. It signifies that things conform to the measure of God, possessing in themselves veracity and intelligibility as an imprint of the Being who not only brought them into existence, but designed them in their deepest essence.[^17] The world stands, therefore, between two intellects: between the infinite intellect that designed it, and the finite intellect essentially tending to understand it,[^18] towards which the world opens itself as an invitation. Hence the justice of the affirmation that things are in themselves bearers of truth;[^19] hence too truth can be defined as "that which manifests what is", and the true as that "which declares and manifests being."[^20]

Things are bearers of truth, but truth is not primarily in them; it is in the intellect. Well known are the vacillations of philosophers concerning the nature of truth. A good example of this is what occurred in ancient philosophy. These vacillations stem largely from the perception of the essential reference that truth bears to intellect, and from the difficulty of reconciling this reference with the objectivity of the truth of things.

Aristotle affirmed that the true and the false are not primarily in things, but in the intellect.[^21] This would be so, above all, with respect to the false, which can in no way reside in things, which are always true. The false is always in the intellect, for falsehood is always an error of our appraisal, not an error of things.

Yet the assertion that truth resides primarily in the intellect and derives thence to things leads to grave difficulties if one is speaking of the human intellect—that is, if it is maintained that truth derives to things from the human mind. This would mean that truth belongs entirely to the realm of human subjectivity; that is, that it would be the human mind that creates the truth of things. The truth and meaning of things would then be the truth and meaning that man confers upon things which, in many cases, already exist before him.

Saint Thomas Aquinas, who unhesitatingly admits that truth derives from intellect to things, adduces two arguments to show the contradiction that would ensue if primordiality in truth were attributed to the human intellect. Quoting Saint Augustine, he observes that if truth were derived from the human intellect to things, then only that which is known by the human intellect would be true; furthermore, given the variety of human opinions, it would become inevitable to say that nothing is really true or really false, since two distinct intellects could "see" the same thing in opposite ways, whereby that thing would be, at one and the same time, both true and false. This would be equivalent to denying the principle of non-contradiction, that is, denying the axiom that a thing cannot both be and not be at the same time and in the same respect.[^22]

Nevertheless, the truth of things is not found primarily in the being of things either, but in an intellect, given that objective truth means that things are endowed with intelligibility—that is, they partake of intellect.[^23] This seems to lead to the most radical subjectivism: it would be the human intellect that creates the truth of things, turning itself into the measure of the truth of things. At this juncture, Saint Thomas draws attention to the decisive importance of the doctrine of creation for an adequate answer to the question of truth:

> "The ancient philosophers did not hold that natural things proceed from an intellect, but that they arise by chance; yet because, on the other hand, they saw that the true bears relation to an intellect, they were compelled to locate the truth of things in their relation to our intellect, whence follow the absurdities pointed out by the Philosopher. But these difficulties vanish if we maintain that the truth of things consists in their relation to the divine intellect."[^24]

The difficulties are obvious and have already been indicated. The gravest consequence would be that man would settle into the most absolute relativism. Truth as communion with being thus becomes impossible; all that remains possible is the self-assertion of one's own judgements, disregarding the objectivity of things. Man's task would then consist in measuring truth by blows of self-assertion, in imparting to things their ultimate meaning, without having been the one who gave them being. Totalitarian regimes and their propaganda departments are clear exponents of where human *hybris* leads when it deliberately attempts to "create" truth by sheer force of self-assertion and propaganda.

The problem of the "ancient philosophers" vanishes in the light of the doctrine of creation: God created the world through his intellect. Things are true because they reflect the truth existing in the divine intellect. And precisely because things receive their truth from an intellect, man can understand them with his intellect, which is made in the image and likeness of the intellect of the Creator. Of paramount significance is the Augustinian insight that God does not know things because they exist, but rather things exist because God knows them.[^25] The same applies to truth: things are true because the divine intellect confers truth upon them.

If, on the contrary, the power to constitute the truth of things were granted to the human intellect, we would find ourselves face to face with the most absolute subjectivism. Truth would not exist, but only "my truth", reduced to "differing points of view" concerning things. And what is more important, it would be absurd for one who has not designed things to bestow truth upon them. Only he who has conceived the ideas of things and given them shape can communicate truth to them. Taken in the full seriousness of its formulations, this is one of the absurdities inherent in atheism. If God did not exist, neither would the truth of things exist as something independent of the human mind. Man would automatically be turned into a demiurge, tasked with bestowing truth and meaning upon everything that exists. It is the enterprise of playing at "you shall be as gods", an enterprise that can at times prove tragic, and is always absurd.

One of the characteristics of the truth of things is its capacity to unite minds and hearts, since in it occurs the embrace between intellect and being. Nothing can unite men more firmly than truth. This characteristic of truth is accompanied by another that the ancients greatly admired: its immutability. Truth stands above opinions, discourses, and the erosion of time: 7 + 3 will always be 10.

Saint Augustine perceived with such clarity the indissoluble connection between the incorruptibility of truth and the causality of God that he used this perennity to demonstrate the existence of God.[^26] Indeed, if God did not exist there would be no immutable truths, for only an eternal and infinite intellect can bestow eternal perennity upon truth.[^27]

Strictly speaking, there exists only one eternal Truth: God himself. Indeed, if we consider ontological truth, only the truth of God is eternal and immutable, because only the being of God is eternal and immutable, whereas all created beings bear within themselves the stamp of mutability; and if we consider logical truth, it must likewise be said that the divine intellect alone is eternal and immutable, unlike all created intellects, which not only began to exist, but change frequently, passing from truth to error or from error to truth. In the same way that God alone is being in an absolute manner, so too the truth of God alone is eternal and unalterable in an absolute mode.[^28]

### Divine Veracity

Essentially Truth, God is incompatible with falsehood or error. The true God is essentially the God of truth. As is taught in every catechism, God can neither deceive nor be deceived (*nec fallere nec falli potest*). Here lies the supreme authority of his words, his judgements, and his promises. Any shadow of error or falsehood is absolutely incompatible with the being of God. So incompatible is it that whoever wishes to draw near to God must always do so in the most exquisite reverence for truth.

Divine veracity may be considered in its relation to the essence of God; it may also be considered in its relation to God's acting, thus placing it among the moral attributes. Here it suffices to emphasise that the veracity of God belongs to the very divine essence. He who is subsistent Truth is absolutely and infinitely truthful.

Upon this metaphysical truth rests the entire theology of revelation. The act of faith is a reasonable and just act: we believe to be true what God has revealed, says the First Vatican Council, not because we perceive the intrinsic truth of the revealed mysteries, "but on the authority of God himself who reveals them, who can neither deceive nor be deceived."[^29] There is nothing more reasonable and just than to trust in that authority.

The same holds true of divine promises. The God of truth can neither deceive nor be deceived. He cannot fail, for there can be not the slightest falsity in his promises. In making them, God already knew the entire future, for he holds it present in his eternal act of intellect. Absolute fidelity is an unequivocal manifestation of the divine being, for fidelity and loyalty belong to the very ipseity of God, that is to say, to his nature as subsistent Truth. "Truth is not only *alētheia*, the state of unconcealment; it is also *'emet*: fidelity, constancy, authenticity. Where there is *'emet*, one can entrust oneself, surrender oneself."[^30]

### The Truth of God in Its Trinitarian Dimension

Divine Truth is identified with the very being of God, who is a Trinity of Persons and essential Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:16). In God, Truth and Love are really identical. The Truth of God is the truth of Love. Both dimensions—the Trinitarian and that of Love—are inseparable from the affirmation that God is subsistent Truth. That Truth is not only far from being something abstract; it is a supremely concrete Being, who is an interpersonal communion of existence, knowledge, and love.

The theological consideration of God as Truth points necessarily to God's dialogue with himself, to his profound and inexhaustible inner life. God has a Word (*Verbum*). That Word is, for that very reason, his Wisdom. Through him all things were created. Employing the language of the early Fathers, it can be said that the Word has been "sown" throughout all creation, thereby endowing it with intelligibility.[^31] This intelligibility is a reflection of Him who is the radiance of the Father's glory (cf. Heb 1:3).[^32] The Word of God is, moreover, the Word that breathes forth Love: the Spirit of God, who is the Spirit of truth (cf. Jn 15:26). He is not only the Spirit of truth because he proceeds from Truth, but because he is true Love.[^33] The Spirit is the Truth of Love.

The divine being is the communion of the three Persons, in which unity and otherness are ineffably conjoined in a communion so intimate that the three are one and the same God. In that communion, Truth is the luminous manifestness of subsistent Being, and Love is communion in that Truth. Love and Truth are identified. The entire being of the world and the whole vocation of man towards communion with God proceed from this inexhaustible font whose essence is infinite Truth and Love.
