## Chapter 32: The Divine Persons in Salvation History {#mateo-seco-trino.32}

The theology of the divine missions encompasses a broad spectrum of questions, all of which are linked to God's communication of Himself to mankind. For it is through the missions of the Son and of the Holy Spirit that the Most Holy Trinity communicates Himself—gives Himself—to human beings in a personal self-bestowal.

In the Old Testament, God revealed Himself as a personal being. He never, however, said of Himself that, while being one, He is a communion of Persons. This revelation took place through the missions: we know that God is not solitary, but has a Son, because Jesus has revealed to us His divine sonship; we know that there is in God a Spirit, who is Gift and Love, because the Son has revealed Him to us, and the Spirit Himself has revealed His presence precisely in His mission. The divine missions have been the way chosen by God to reveal the mystery of His inner life.

Following this path, theological reflection has traditionally proceeded from the missions towards the immanent Trinity. Thus did St Basil and St Augustine, among others. St Basil, for example, defended the full divinity of the Holy Spirit on the basis of His sanctifying work;[^1] in the *De Trinitate*, St Augustine approached the speculative questions concerning the Trinity from the consideration of the missions.[^2]

The theme of the missions is, therefore, pivotal in the study of the Trinitarian mystery. Yet it is also a matter of foundational relevance across many other theological treatises. Any aspect of God's communication of Himself to man that is studied must necessarily refer back to the theology of the divine missions. Our Lord personally drew attention to the indissoluble bond linking His Trinitarian mission with the mission of the Apostles: *As the Father has sent me, even so I send you* (Jn 20:21). The Second Vatican Council highlighted that the Church springs from the Trinity and, more specifically, from the Trinitarian missions;[^3] within this framework, John Paul II underscored that the mission of the People of God is a further consequence—as it were a prolongation—of the divine missions.[^4] Consequently, ecclesiology constantly refers back to the divine missions.

It is fitting to insist upon the theological importance of this subject. The personal communication that God makes to men is always realised through the divine missions, for God does not merely bestow gifts upon men; He gives Himself in His personal reality. The whole of theological anthropology thus refers back to the theology of the missions, since the divinisation of man takes place through the indwelling of the Holy Spirit, who makes us sons of God in the Son. Furthermore, at its essential core, heaven is nothing other than the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul reaching its fullness through the clear vision in glory. As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states, "the ultimate end of the whole divine economy is the entry of God's creatures into the perfect unity of the Blessed Trinity (cf. Jn 17:21–23)."[^5] This entry into the divine intimacy is nothing other than the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul of the just.

In this light, the divine plan of creation and salvation appears in all its harmony and coherence: creative love springs from the Trinitarian mystery of God—a mystery of eternal and most intimate personal communication—unfolds in the work of creation, attains its culminating point in the joint mission of the Son and of the Spirit, and is prolonged in the mission of the Church until the creature has definitively entered into the perfect unity of the Blessed Trinity, wholly immersed in the vital current of Trinitarian love.

Naturally, in these pages we shall concern ourselves with the missions by considering them exclusively in their Trinitarian dimension, leaving the other aspects to the treatises in which they are customarily studied.

### The Existence of Missions in God

Sacred Scripture explicitly affirms the mission of the Son, frequently employing the verbs *to give* or *to send*: *For God so loved the world that he gave [édōken] his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life. For God sent [apésteilen] the Son into the world, not to condemn the world, but that the world might be saved through him* (Jn 3:16–17; cf., e.g., Jn 5:23, 36, 38; Gal 4:4–5).

The same holds true for the mission of the Holy Spirit. Here too the verbs *to give* or *to send* are employed to describe it: *And I will pray the Father, and he will give [dōsei] you another Counselor...* (Jn 14:16); *The Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send [pempsei] in my name...* (Jn 14:26; cf., e.g., Lk 24:49; Jn 15:26).

The divine missions are situated within the sphere of the fruitful expansion of God, who is Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:16). The missions take place through a sovereignly free and generous divine design; proceeding from God-Love, the missions always bear the character of gratuitous self-bestowal and personal presence. This is explicitly contained in the texts just cited: the sending of the Son is a gift which the Father makes to men (cf. Jn 3:16–17; Gal 4:5). The same is true of the mission of the Holy Spirit: He is sent as a gift (cf. Jn 14:17, 26; Rom 5:5).

The Trinitarian missions not only entail the sending of a Person to a created person, but, by establishing a new mode of presence, also involve a transformation in the human person who receives that grace: the Persons sent communicate to him something of their personal characteristic. This can be seen with special clarity in divine filiation, in which Christ communicates something of His eternal sonship—that is, He makes us share in it in a mysterious manner, according to the declaration of Romans 8: *You have received the spirit of sonship. When we cry, "Abba! Father!" it is the Spirit himself bearing witness with our spirit that we are children of God* (Rom 8:15–16; cf. Gal 4:4–6).

This means that Christ makes His faithful share in what is His own and personal: His sonship with respect to the Father. Thus, through the mission of the Son, God extends His paternity beyond the intra-Trinitarian relations to embrace all those who receive Jesus Christ (cf. Jn 1:12). For this reason, the familiar statement that we are made sons of God in the Son through the Spirit can also be expressed in this other way: the Father's paternity over us is realised in a new and ineffably intimate manner through the mission of the Son, "so that, just as we are *filii Dei in Filio*, God can describe Himself as *Pater noster in Filio per Spiritum Sanctum*."[^6]

### The Notion of Mission and Its Analogical Understanding

Scriptural texts are, obviously, the foremost point of reference when speaking of missions in God. They govern not only all that we know regarding the existence of missions in God, but also all that we know regarding their nature. The notion of mission formulated by theologians is nothing other than an enumeration of the characteristics manifested in the event of the missions as described by the texts of Scripture.

Following these texts, the following features of the Trinitarian missions are customarily established:

a) Sacred Scripture, in speaking of the sending of the Son and of the Spirit, says of the Father only that He comes to men (Jn 14:23), but never says that the Father is sent. It is He who sends the Son and the Holy Spirit.

b) Hence the mission of a divine Person is understood in dependence upon His procession from another Person. The Person sent must proceed from the Person who sends. That is to say, mission implies origin of the one sent with respect to the sender (*mittens*).

c) Through this sending, the Person sent is present in the rational creature in a new mode. In truth, we are speaking of real missions. The Incarnation is real: the Word truly *became flesh* (Jn 1:14); the Spirit was truly sent at Pentecost. This sending cannot be real unless it brings with it a real change in the presence of the Person sent in the one to whom He has been sent. St Thomas Aquinas writes:

> "In the concept of mission two things are included: one is the relation of the one sent to the one who sends; the other is the relation of the one sent to the term of the mission (...) Accordingly, mission can belong to a divine Person, on the one hand, insofar as it includes the relation of origin with respect to the sender, and on the other, insofar as it implies a new mode of existing in someone."[^7]

d) When speaking of the divine missions, it is necessary to keep in mind all that has already been said regarding the equality of the divine Persons with one another and their *circuminsessio*.

For this reason, the mission of a divine Person can be conceived neither as a command nor as a counsel on the part of the sending Person. Mission cannot spring from the superiority of one Person over another, but solely from the relation of origin.[^8]

As is the case with everything concerning God, it is necessary to conceive the divine missions by analogy with the missions known in human life, which serve as the point of departure for establishing the analogy. Therefore, one must exclude from its concept anything that includes any imperfection. The missions are not, then, a sending such as that of a servant or an ambassador. Unlike human missions—for example, the mission of an ambassador—a divine mission does not imply departure or separation of the one sent from the one who sends him; neither does it imply subordination with respect to the sender. It indicates nothing other than origin. St Thomas employs a comparison that introduces us to the close relation existing between missions and processions:

> "The fact that someone is sent reveals that the one sent proceeds in some manner from the sender, whether by mode of command (...) or also by mode of origin, as when it is said that the tree sends forth or emits a flower."[^9]

The image used by St Thomas is exceptionally felicitous. In the visible missions the reality of the internal procession in God is manifested; in them the Trinitarian mystery flourishes just as the blossom springs from the tree.

By reason of the infinite perfection of the divine Persons, mission must be conceived without any real mutation occurring within the Person sent. It has already been stated that He does not cease to be in the sender; neither does He begin to exist in the one to whom He is sent. The three Persons are everywhere present. For that reason it is specified that all the Person sent receives is a new mode of existing in the one to whom He is sent.

This new mode of presence does not entail a real change in the Person sent. The real change takes place in the one who receives the mission. Through this mission, the rational creature, who was already essentially bound to God by the presence of immensity, becomes bound to Him as Triune, with a new mode of relationship. It is the creature that undergoes a real mutation. Stated differently: the missions spring from the divine life in which the Three are equally eternal, infinite, and immense; they spring from the design of the Triune and One God to communicate the infinite richness of His personal life to the rational creature, and it is the rational creature that is really introduced into the intimate life of the Trinity. That is the real change it undergoes.

Owing to the *circuminsessio*, the mission of one divine Person—which implies a new mode of presence—carries with it the presence of the other two. The Father sends the Son and the Holy Spirit, but in sending them He makes Himself present together with them. For this reason, the missions can be described as the active presence of the Father, of the Son, and of their Spirit in the world of men.[^10] Although only the Son and the Spirit can be sent, it is the whole Trinity that communicates Himself to us through the missions.

Since M. J. Scheeben, it has customarily been noted that the mission of a divine Person implies two elements: the active gift of Himself, and the passive gift, that is, His donation by another Person.[^11] Both elements are inseparable: the Father hands over His Only-begotten for the salvation of the world (cf. Jn 3:16), and the Father's handing over of the Only-begotten is accompanied by the personal self-giving which the Only-begotten makes of Himself. The same happens with the Spirit, who, being essential Gift, is sent by the Father, and this sending entails that the Spirit Himself gives Himself to us as a gift.

The term of the mission, as has been said, is a new mode of presence. We are speaking of a presence of the divine Persons as distinct. Although the creation of the world and its conservation already imply a presence of God, this presence cannot be conceived as the result of a Trinitarian mission. Mission carries with it a personal relationship between God and men, a relationship which, because it is personal, can only occur between the divine Persons and created persons.

### Eternal Procession and Temporal Mission

It has already been stated that there is an indissoluble relation between eternal procession and temporal mission. Mission is, in a certain sense, the continuation in the created world of the intra-divine processes.[^12] St Augustine highlighted this when arguing from the divine missions for the distinction between Father, Son, and Holy Spirit:[^13]

> "Just as the Father begot and the Son was begotten, so the Father sends and the Son is sent. But the sender and the sent are one (...), because the Father and the Son are one and the same thing. And one with them is the Holy Spirit, because the three are unity. For the Son, to be born is to be from the Father: through the Father He was begotten; and for the Son to be sent is for His proceeding from the Father to be made known. For the Holy Spirit, to be the gift of God is likewise to proceed from the Father; and to be sent is to be known as proceeding from Him. Nor can we affirm that the Holy Spirit does not proceed from the Son, for not in vain is He called the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. I do not see what else He meant when breathing upon the face of the disciples He said: *Receive the Holy Spirit* (Jn 20:22). That material breath, proceeding from an earthly substance and acting upon bodily senses, could not be the substance of the Holy Spirit, but a symbol to demonstrate that the Holy Spirit proceeds not only from the Father, but also from the Son."[^14]

This is a passage of extraordinary beauty and great theological density. The whole being of the Son consists in being from the Father. The Son is totally and exclusively a filial relation to the Father. That filial relation is displayed in the mission of the Son to such an extent that this mission is nothing other than making Himself known—*cognosci*—as proceeding from the Father. It is understandable that it should be the Son who becomes incarnate, since His visible mission has as its end uniting us to Himself so as to transform us, through this union, into sons of God. In our adoptive sonship—the end of the mission of the Word—His eternal procession can be recognized. The mission of the Son is, then, the visibility—making known in time in all its efficacy—of His eternal procession as the Word of the Father. The bond existing between the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity could not be underlined with greater vigour.

The depth with which St Augustine develops the theology of the Trinitarian missions bears powerfully upon the question of the *Filioque*. In the passage cited, the *Filioque* does not depend on the consideration of the oneness of the divine essence, nor on the axiom of the opposition of relation. The affirmation that the Spirit proceeds from the Son flows naturally from the consideration that, on the evening of Easter, Jesus gives the Spirit to the Apostles. The bestowal of the Spirit through visible gestures is the "visibility" of the fact that the Spirit proceeds eternally from the Son. To deny that the Spirit proceeds from the Son would necessarily entail an impoverishment of the theology of the missions as the "visibility" of eternal procession. In truth, it is evident that the Son sends and bestows the Spirit.

This exceedingly close relationship between procession and mission is grounded, moreover, in the fact that the missions are the highest possible communication of the intra-Trinitarian life to the rational creature. Hence the affirmation that in the mission what is proper to each Person is made manifest. Indeed, the mission of a divine Person is the most intimate communication that this Person can make of Himself to the rational creature:

> "The whole divine economy is the common work of the three divine persons. For as the Trinity has only one and the same nature, so too does it have only one and the same operation (...) It is above all the divine missions of the Incarnation of the Son and the gift of the Holy Spirit that show forth the properties of the divine persons (...) The whole divine economy, work at once common and personal, displays both the property of the divine persons and their single nature. Hence the whole Christian life is a communion with each of the divine persons, without in any way separating them. Everyone who glorifies the Father does so through the Son in the Holy Spirit; everyone who follows Christ does so because the Father draws him (cf. Jn 6:44) and the Spirit moves him (cf. Rom 8:14)."[^15]

The notion of mission is, then, a personal notion for many reasons. It applies only to a divine Person insofar as bestowed or sent; that is, it is reserved for the self-bestowal of God Himself, not for the gifts of God. Only a Person can be sent. This Person is sent by virtue of His personal relation to the Person who sends. Finally, the Person is sent to the rational creature to establish with it a relationship of presence as a Person distinct from the other two. Hence the accuracy of Scheeben's observation: mission is a twofold donation—donation on the part of the sender, and donation on the part of the one sent.

And since this donation implies a real transformation in the one who is its recipient, what characterizes mission is that it establishes a mysterious relationship of the human person with the divine Person as distinct from the other two, though intimately united to them through the *circuminsessio*.[^16]

Mission is, therefore, temporal, but it always refers back to its eternal foundation. It is temporal, because mission entails a new presence in the one to whom the Person is sent; it always refers back to eternal procession, because the missions are the temporal prolongation of the eternal processions:

> "Mission not only implies procession from a principle, but in addition determines the temporal term of the procession; and for this reason mission is only temporal. Or it may also be said that mission includes eternal procession and adds something—the temporal effect—since the relation of the divine person to its principle is necessarily eternal. Whence, if one speaks of a twofold procession, eternal and temporal, it is not because the relations to the principle are multiplied, but because the duality arises from the term, which is temporal and eternal."[^17]

These words of St Thomas, in which he underscores the exceedingly close and indissoluble relationship between eternal procession and temporal mission, justify viewing the missions as the prolongation of the eternal processions in the rational creature.[^18]

### Visible Mission and Invisible Mission

Missions are divided into visible and invisible missions. Missions are visible when the communication of the Person sent is accompanied by a sensible sign, even if its meaning is clear only to the believer. The Incarnation of the Word and the descent of the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost are called visible missions. In both cases, the missions were accompanied by visible signs. A mission is called invisible when it takes place without any external sign. Such is the case, for example, with the indwelling of the Holy Spirit in the soul of the just.

In Sacred Scripture, the sending of the Son into the world and the sending of the Holy Spirit are spoken of explicitly. These are visible missions. Scripture also speaks clearly of invisible missions, for example, of the sending of the Holy Spirit into the soul. The invisible mission of the Spirit appears frequently linked with our adoption as sons: *When the time had fully come, God sent forth his Son (...) to redeem those who were under the law, so that we might receive adoption as sons. And because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, "Abba! Father!"* (Gal 4:4–6); *Hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us* (Rom 5:5); *Do you not know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit dwells in you? If any one destroys God's temple, God will destroy him. For God's temple is holy, and that temple you are* (1 Cor 3:16–17).

The missions—both visible and invisible—have as their end the sanctification of mankind: the former manifest the Persons sent to the world; the latter entail their self-bestowal upon those who receive them. The invisible mission—such as the indwelling of the Holy Spirit—elevates man above all created reality and introduces him into Trinitarian intimacy:

> "The soul is made conformable to God by grace. Consequently, for any divine person to be sent to anyone through grace it is necessary that assimilation to the person who is sent be brought about by some gift of grace. And since the Holy Spirit is Love, the soul is assimilated to the Holy Spirit by the gift of charity; hence the mission of the Holy Spirit is considered according to the gift of charity. The Son, on the other hand, is the Word, but not just any word, but the Word who breathes forth Love (...) Consequently, the Son is not sent for just any perfection of the intellect, but for such an instruction as breaks forth into affections of love."[^19]

The end of the divine missions is the embrace of love between man and the three divine Persons. The Father sends His Son and His Spirit into the heart of man, so that man may enter into direct relation with them as distinct Persons in a relationship springing from love, according to Our Lord's saying: *If a man loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him* (Jn 14:23). We are dealing here with an experience of God that takes place first in the night of faith and subsequently in the clear vision in glory. The mystical life is, as it were, a luminous though obscure experience of this presence.

In this light one understands the importance of the fact that the missions are the continuation of the intra-Trinitarian processions. Indeed, through the missions, the Persons communicate themselves to man in that which is most proper and distinctive to them: their personal distinction by reason of their origin. Only thus can man be fully introduced into the vital abyss of the divine intimacy.

### The Mission of the Word

Sacred Scripture bears superabundant witness to the mission of the Son to this world: *For God so loved the world that he gave his only Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish but have eternal life. For God sent the Son into the world, not to condemn the world, but that the world might be saved through him* (Jn 3:16–17).

The scriptural texts are numerous and explicit. The Son presents Himself as the one sent by the Father: *He who does not honor the Son does not honor the Father who sent him* (cf. Jn 5:23, 36–38); just as the Father sends the Son into the world, so the Son sends His Apostles into the world (Jn 20:21). *When the time had fully come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law...* (Gal 4:4–5).

The mission of the Son consists in the Incarnation. This is the primordial Good News. With the Incarnation God *has visited his people* (cf. Lk 1:68); He has fulfilled the promises made to Abraham and to his posterity (cf. Lk 1:55). He has fulfilled His promise beyond all expectation, for He has fulfilled it by sending His beloved Son (cf. Mk 1:11).[^20]

In the Incarnation the concept of mission is realised in all its fullness. The Son, substantially present in the Humanity of Christ, is sent into the world in such a way that St John can write: *That which was from the beginning, which we have heard, which we have seen with our eyes, which we have looked upon and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life—the life was made manifest, and we saw it, and testify to it, and proclaim to you the eternal life which was with the Father and was made manifest to us* (1 Jn 1:1–2).

The visible mission of the Son entails a relationship of presence to all men, for the Word, in becoming incarnate, not only becomes man and the Son of Man, but is constituted the New Adam (cf. Rom 5:12–19). "By his incarnation the Son of God has united himself in some fashion with every man."[^21] God involves Himself, through the Son, in what is most profound and universal in human history.

It is customary to say that the Incarnation is the work of the whole Trinity. And this is true. The Trinity wills and effects—as a work *ad extra*—the Incarnation of the Word. Yet considered as mission, the Incarnation proceeds exclusively from the Father: only the Father is the one who sends by reason of His hypostatic property, that is, His paternity. In speaking of His mission, Our Lord refers back to the Father and to the Father alone. And this holds true both when speaking of His doctrine and when speaking of His messianic task. He is nothing other than the envoy of the Father, obedient unto death (cf. Phil 2:5–9).

The mission of the Word manifests, in the first place, the Person sent. In Jesus—in His *Abba*, as theology ceaselessly repeats—and in His entire filial conduct, the sonship of the Word to the Father is manifested. He is essentially the Son, and His revelation possesses the utmost clarity. In the Incarnation, moreover, the other two Persons are revealed. The Father is revealed, for the whole being of Jesus points back to the Father to such an extent that whoever sees Him sees the Father (cf. Jn 14:9–10). In the mission of the Word there is reflected not only His sonship to the Father, but also His character as the Word eternally uttered, as essential divine revelation. In Jesus the Holy Spirit is likewise revealed: Jesus is conceived by the Holy Spirit; He is the Anointed of the Spirit. He is the one who sends the Spirit (cf. Jn 16:7). There is no need to labour this point.

As has already been noted, the visible mission exists for the sake of the invisible mission—that is, for the personal relationship established between the Person sent and the one to whom He is sent. The visible mission of the Word likewise has as its goal His invisible mission, which consists in the personal relationship of the Word with each individual human being. He Himself speaks at the Last Supper of how Christians must be grafted into Him, like the branch into the vine (cf. Jn 15:1–8).

This union is realised, first of all, through the word. Through the Incarnation, the Word became human speech in His gestures and in His conversation; and in revealing Himself, the Word gives Himself to man and gives with Himself the Most Holy Trinity, in accordance with that affirmation: *If a man loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him* (Jn 14:23).

The union of the Word with man takes place, in an ineffable manner, in the Eucharist. Through it, the Word gives Himself invisibly to the believer.[^22] The sacrament of Love is the fruit of the gratuitous gift which the Father makes to us of the Son; it is likewise the fruit of the generous self-giving with which the Son gives Himself to us; it is, finally, the fruit of the sanctifying action of the Holy Spirit. This supreme gift is the fruit of the eternal love among the divine Persons which is extended to us in the missions. St Josemaría highlights this with great vigour in a homily delivered on Holy Thursday. Here are some of his thoughts:

> "This Trinitarian current of love for men is perpetuated in a sublime way in the Eucharist. Many years ago, we all learned from the catechism that the Holy Eucharist can be considered as Sacrifice and as Sacrament; and that the Sacrament appears to us as Communion and as a treasure on the altar: in the Tabernacle (...).
> 
> "I was speaking of a Trinitarian current of love for men. And where can we perceive it better than in the Mass? The whole Trinity acts in the holy sacrifice of the altar. That is why I love so much to repeat in the collect, secret, and postcommunion those concluding words: *Through Jesus Christ, your Son, our Lord*—we address the Father—*who lives and reigns with you in the unity of the Holy Spirit, God, for ever and ever. Amen.*
> 
> "In the Mass, prayer to the Father is constant. The priest is a representative of the eternal Priest, Jesus Christ, who at the same time is the Victim. And the action of the Holy Spirit in the Mass is no less ineffable and no less certain. *By the power of the Holy Spirit*, writes St John Damascene, *the conversion of the bread into the Body of Christ is effected.*[^23]
> 
> "This action of the Holy Spirit is clearly expressed when the priest invokes the divine blessing on the offering: *Come, almighty Sanctifier, eternal God, and bless this sacrifice prepared for your holy name,*[^24] the holocaust that will render to the most holy Name of God the glory that is due to it. The sanctification that we implore is attributed to the Paraclete, whom the Father and the Son send to us. We also recognize that active presence of the Holy Spirit in the sacrifice when we say, shortly before communion: *Lord Jesus Christ, Son of the living God, who by the will of the Father and the cooperation of the Holy Spirit, brought life to the world through your death...*[^25]
> 
> "The whole Trinity is present in the sacrifice of the Altar. By the will of the Father, with the cooperation of the Holy Spirit, the Son offers Himself in a redeeming oblation. Let us learn to deal with the Most Holy Trinity, God One and Triune: three divine Persons in the unity of their substance, of their love, of their efficaciously sanctifying action (...).
> 
> "The love of the Trinity for men causes all graces to spring, for the Church and for mankind, from the presence of Christ in the Eucharist. This is the sacrifice prophesied by Malachi: *From the rising of the sun to its setting my name is great among the nations, and in every place incense is offered to my name, and a pure offering* (Mal 1:11). It is the Sacrifice of Christ, offered to the Father with the cooperation of the Holy Spirit (...).
> 
> "The Holy Mass thus places us before the primordial mysteries of the faith, because it is the very self-giving of the Trinity to the Church. Thus it is understood that the Mass is the centre and root of the Christian's spiritual life. It is the end of all the sacraments.[^26] In the Mass the life of grace, which was deposited in us by Baptism and grows strengthened by Confirmation, journeys towards its fullness. When we participate in the Eucharist, writes St Cyril of Jerusalem, *we experience the deifying spiritualization of the Holy Spirit, who not only configures us with Christ, as happens in Baptism, but christifies us entirely, associating us with the fullness of Christ Jesus.*[^27] The outpouring of the Holy Spirit, by christifying us, leads us to recognize ourselves as children of God. The Paraclete, who is charity, teaches us to fuse our whole life with that virtue; and *consummati in unum* (Jn 17:23), made one single thing with Christ, we can be among men what St Augustine affirms of the Eucharist: *a sign of unity, a bond of love.*[^28]"[^29]

The text cited is a magnificent example of the *connexio mysteriorum*, the attentive contemplation of which is one of the theologian's most important tasks. Within that connection, the theology of the missions shows itself to be one of its fundamental cores.

### The Mission of the Holy Spirit and Its Relation to the Mission of the Word

Christ speaks repeatedly of the sending of the Holy Spirit. This sending or mission is so closely united to the revelation of the existence of the Holy Spirit that they are almost always found together. The Spirit appears insofar as He is sent.

Such is the case in the Last Supper discourses recorded in St John: *The Counselor, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, he will teach you all things, and bring to your remembrance all that I have said to you (...) When the Counselor comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, who proceeds from the Father, he will bear witness to me* (Jn 14:26; 15:26). The Lord refers to the sending of the Spirit at Pentecost: *And behold, I send the promise of my Father upon you; but stay in the city, until you are clothed with power from on high* (Lk 24:49).

The mission of the Spirit is related to the mission of the Son to such an extent that both may be described as a joint mission. Thus does the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*:

> "He whom the Father has sent into our hearts, the Spirit of his Son (cf. Gal 4:6) is truly God. Consubstantial with the Father and the Son, the Spirit is inseparable from them, both in the inner life of the Trinity and in his gift of love for the world (...) When the Father sends his Word, he also sends his breath: a joint mission in which the Son and the Holy Spirit are distinct but inseparable."[^30]

This conjoining of the mission of the Word and of the Spirit is present in Jesus Himself. Jesus is conceived by the power of the Holy Spirit, anointed by the Spirit. The entire life of Christ is grounded in the fullness of this anointing (cf. Jn 3:34). After His glorification (Jn 7:39), Christ can, in turn, send the Spirit to those who believe in Him: He communicates His Glory to them (cf. Jn 17:22), that is, the Holy Spirit who glorifies Him (cf. Jn 16:14). From this point onward, the joint mission unfolds in the Church.[^31]

The sending of the Spirit may be regarded as the culmination of the work of the Son:

> "Christ's departure through the Cross has the power of the Redemption, and this also means a new presence of the Spirit of God in creation: the new beginning of God's self-communication to man in the Holy Spirit (...) At the price of the Cross which brings about the Redemption, by virtue of the whole Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ, the Holy Spirit comes in order to remain with the Apostles from the day of Pentecost, to be with the Church and in the Church, and through her in the world. In this way there is definitively brought about that new beginning of the self-communication of the Triune God in the Holy Spirit through the work of Jesus Christ, the Redeemer of man and of the world."[^32]

In His mission, the Spirit shows Himself to be entirely coherent with that which constitutes His Trinitarian property: to be the bond of union, Love, Gift. The Spirit is the one who unites men to the other two Persons of the Trinity. He who anointed Christ (cf. Lk 4:18) anoints the Christian, making him a sharer in Jesus' anointing, rendering him Christlike (*cristiforme*) and a participant also in the mission of Christ.[^33]

Precisely because the mission of the Spirit has as its end bringing the work of Christ to completion, His time is the time of the Church. The Church lives immersed in the event of Pentecost, that is, in the coming of the Spirit. St Josemaría underscores this forcefully:

> "The solemn coming of the Holy Spirit on the day of Pentecost was not an isolated event. There is hardly a page of the Acts of the Apostles in which we are not told of Him and of the action by which He guides, directs, and enlivens the life and works of the primitive Christian community: He is the one who inspires the preaching of St Peter (cf. Acts 4:8); who confirms the disciples in their faith (cf. Acts 4:31); who seals with His presence the call addressed to the Gentiles (cf. Acts 10:44–47); who sends Saul and Barnabas to distant lands to open new paths for the teaching of Jesus (cf. Acts 13:2–4). In a word, His presence and His action fill everything."[^34]

### The Visible Signs of the Mission of the Spirit

When speaking of the visible mission of the Holy Spirit and of the sensible signs in which this mission is manifested, theologians customarily enumerate at least four moments: two with respect to Christ Himself—at His baptism in the form of a dove (cf. Mt 3:16; Lk 3:22), and at the Transfiguration under the form of a cloud (cf. Mt 17:5; Mk 9:7; Lk 9:34–35; 2 Pet 1:17); and two with respect to the Apostles after the Lord's resurrection—one at the Easter apparition, when the Lord, breathing upon them, gives them the Spirit and the power to forgive sins (Jn 20:22), and the other at Pentecost, under the form of tongues of fire (Acts 2:3–4).[^35]

This is the same perspective maintained in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*.[^36] With regard to Pentecost, which is obviously the culminating point of the visible mission of the Spirit, it states:

> "On the day of Pentecost (...), Christ's Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Holy Spirit, manifested, given, and communicated as a divine person: of his fullness, Christ, the Lord (cf. Acts 2:36), pours out the Spirit in abundance. On that day, the Holy Trinity is fully revealed."[^37]

And passing from the visible mission to the invisible mission, it continues:

> "By his coming, which never ceases, the Holy Spirit causes the world to enter into the 'last days', the time of the Church, the Kingdom already inherited though not yet fully consummated."[^38]

The coming of the Spirit at Pentecost had as its sign "tongues as of fire."[^39]

### Immanent Trinity and Economic Trinity

The question of the relations between the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity has crossed our path more than once. Now, following our study of the missions, we find ourselves in a better position to gauge the importance of this question in Trinitarian theology. The immanent Trinity becomes the economic Trinity—revealing and communicating Himself to man—precisely in the missions of the divine Persons. The missions necessarily refer back to the origin of the Person sent. This is so by the very nature of the Trinitarian mystery and of man's salvation.

Indeed, man's salvation consists in personal union with God, in a dialogue of knowledge and love maintained with each of the divine Persons as distinct. This dialogue could not take place if, in their relationship with us, the divine Persons did not manifest what is proper to them, that is, what pertains to their origin. Hence the economic manifestation of the Trinity constitutes a manifestation of the immanent Trinity.

One can never insist sufficiently upon this aspect. The divine Persons, in their economic activity, always refer back to their eternal procession. The Son always points back to His sonship; the Spirit always points back to His character as Love and Gift. Yet the problem raised by K. Rahner in what he considers his *Grundaxiom* (fundamental axiom) is not exactly this. It is not a question of whether the immanent Trinity is manifested in the economic Trinity, but whether an identity can be established between the economic Trinity and the immanent Trinity.

Here is Rahner's formulation, which opened a wide-ranging theological debate in its day:

> "The fundamental thesis that establishes this connection between the treatises, and which highlights the Trinity as a saving mystery for us (in its reality and not merely as doctrine), could be formulated thus: The 'economic' Trinity is the 'immanent' Trinity and vice versa."[^40]

This thesis is placed in a section entitled "The fundamental axiom concerning the unity of the economic and immanent Trinity." The proposal could not be clearer: to take the unity between the economic Trinity and the immanent Trinity as the starting point for the structuring of the entire treatise. The underlying reasons belong to the most fundamental principles of Rahner's systematisation, both in Trinitarian matters and in theological anthropology. It is his entire conception of divine self-communication and his concept of man as the free event of that divine self-communication that converge in this fundamental axiom.[^41]

The axiom consists of two parts: the first is the identification of the economic Trinity with the immanent Trinity; the second is the identification of the immanent Trinity with the economic Trinity.

The first part presents no difficulty whatsoever. The theology of the missions does nothing other than reaffirm it. The Trinitarian manifestation of God corresponds to His inner being; the missions manifest the eternal processions in time. This affirmation belongs to the essential core of the theology of the missions. Through the missions, the Trinity bestows upon men a participation in His intimate life of interpersonal communion.

The second part of the axiom—the identification of the immanent Trinity with the economic Trinity, what K. Rahner expresses as "and vice versa"—does not appear acceptable. In Rahner's formulation, the two parts of the *Grundaxiom* are inseparable.[^42] The theological problem arises precisely from the end: *and vice versa*. The difficulty lies not in the fact that the economic Trinity reflects the immanent Trinity, but in the claim that it reflects it in such a way that it can be identified with it. The objections raised against the *Grundaxiom* invariably point towards this underlying reason. It suffices to cite several well-known theologians. The texts are so lucid that they render any further explanation superfluous.

Congar comments:

> "The self-communication of God Father, Son, and Holy Spirit will only be full self-communication eschatologically, in what we call the beatific vision (...). This self-communication is realised, in the economy, according to a status of 'condescension', of humiliation, of service, and, in a word, of *kenosis*. This obliges us to recognise a distance between the economically revealed Trinity and the eternal Trinity. It is the same Trinity, and God is truly communicated, but in a manner not connatural to the being of the divine Persons (...) The economic Trinity reveals the immanent Trinity. But does it reveal it in its totality? There exists a limit: the Incarnation has its own conditions, derived from its nature as a created work..."[^43]

The argument is clear. The missions, which manifest the immanent Trinity, do not manifest it in its totality. Hence they cannot be identified with it. The economic Trinity manifests the immanent Trinity, but the immanent Trinity is not the economic Trinity.

According to W. Kasper, the equation made in the *Grundaxiom* between the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity "is ambiguous and lends itself to misunderstanding."[^44] The arguments are similar to those of Congar. All point to the danger of dissolving the immanent Trinity into history, were the *Grundaxiom* to be applied like a geometrical axiom:

> "It would undoubtedly be a misunderstanding to strip the soteriological Trinity, by virtue of this equation, of its own historical reality, understanding it as a mere temporal phenomenon of the eternal immanent Trinity, not taking seriously the truth that the second Person exists, through the incarnation, in a new mode in history, and therefore not distinguishing the eternal generation from the bosom of the Father and the temporal sending into the world, within an inner connection. But at present the opposite misunderstanding is more probable: dissolving the immanent Trinity into the economic Trinity, as if the eternal Trinity were constituted in history and through history. In that case, the differences among the Persons would be modal in eternity and would only become real in history. Finally, it represents a misunderstanding of the axiom to take it as a pretext for more or less relegating the doctrine of the immanent Trinity and limiting oneself to the economic Trinity."[^45]

W. Kasper proposes the following in order to avoid these potential misunderstandings:

> "We can, therefore, modify Rahner's fundamental axiom in the following manner: the Trinitarian communication is present in a new way in the soteriological self-communication: under words, signs, and actions, above all in the figure of the man Jesus of Nazareth. It is a question, then, of safeguarding the free-gratuitous and kenotic character of the economic Trinity vis-à-vis the immanent Trinity, and thus preserving the immanent mystery of God in (not behind) His self-revelation."[^46]

What W. Kasper proposes completely alters the meaning that K. Rahner gave to the *Grundaxiom*. Kasper states plainly and simply that the immanent Trinity cannot be identified with the economic Trinity, since the economic Trinity manifests the immanent Trinity through *kenosis*, in a historical world in which human freedom and the freedom of God both play a part.

Commenting on the Rahnerian theory, J. H. Nicolas cautions:

> "Notional knowledge of the economic Trinity refers us to notional knowledge of the immanent Trinity: the latter, in turn, illuminates from above knowledge of the economic Trinity. One cannot identify these two knowledges, nor can they be separated. It is this distinction that the Greek Fathers safeguarded when distinguishing *Theology*—concerned with God in Himself—and *Economy*, concerned with the saving work wrought by the three Persons, which leads ultimately to the contemplation of the Trinity."[^47]

The complaint of Eastern Christians against Westerners is well known; they are troubled by what they regard as a Western tendency to rationalise the mystery of the Trinity. This tendency manifests itself with singular clarity in the claim to identify the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity. There exists a genuine, non-imaginary risk that this identification may draw perilously close to Hegelian rationalisation. The lesson of the Greek Fathers, distinguishing between *Theologia* and *Oikonomia*, is of paramount importance.

The observation made by A. Aranda is remarkably well targeted:

> "In Rahner's approach the difficulty will always remain that in fact, in the historical realization of the Incarnation of the Logos, sin was present. Hence the 'economic' Trinity is indeed the 'immanent' Trinity, but it is so in this historical donation of itself, through the self-emptying of the Word who not only becomes man, but who, without being a sinful man—all others except Him are—is made sin and assumes death (assumes defeat), and finally rises (vanquishes sin and death). Hence the 'immanent' Trinity is not simply the 'economic' Trinity. The Trinity, through the Incarnation of the Word, has mysteriously and out of love willed to involve itself in a human history that is a history of sin and salvation, and which would not have been thus had man not sinned. Without the sin of man, and so far as we can say in conformity with the doctrine of the faith, Christ would not have been made sin, and who then could be certain that there would have been death, and death on a Cross, and resurrection? How, then, could we conceive the donation of the Holy Spirit, and in Him the donation of participation in the divine communion? Therefore, between the 'immanent' Trinity and the 'economic' Trinity there does not exist the simple identity postulated in the fundamental axiom."[^48]

### Trinity and Communion

The immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity are not identical, but they are intimately related and, in sound theology, inseparable. The immanent Trinity is manifested in history as the economic Trinity in order to introduce us into the Trinitarian life, which is essentially an interpersonal communion of knowledge and love.

The theology of the Trinitarian missions thus becomes the key to the theology of mission and of *communio*. Indeed, Trinitarian mission springs from the mystery of interpersonal communion existing within the one God, and has as its end the donation of the uncreated Persons to created persons in order to unite them to themselves, making them share in their vitality and interior unity.

It is to this mystery of communion—a mystery of otherness and of unity—that Our Lord points in His farewell prayer. There He highlights the mysterious relation between the Trinity and the salvation of man. The Priestly Prayer of Jesus begins with these words: *Father, the hour has come; glorify thy Son that the Son may glorify thee* (Jn 17:1). This refers to eschatological glorification, that is, the fulfillment of the work of salvation; it is a glorification that takes place in the Spirit whom He Himself had promised to send shortly before (cf. Jn 16:13–15).

Within this theological framework, the Lord solemnly points back to the Trinitarian mystery in whose bosom His definitive glorification will take place. This glorification, indissolubly linked to the salvation of humanity, consists in the union of man with God, which now, during the time of the Church, must be reflected in the unity of the disciples: *I do not pray for these only, but also for those who believe in me through their word, that they may all be one; even as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be in us (...) I in them and thou in me, that they may become perfectly one* (Jn 17:20–21, 23).

It is the unity of Love vibrating eternally within the divine relations and communicated to man through the gift of Him who is Love and Gift. The Holy Spirit, in His hypostatic quality as *Donum*, is situated at the point of confluence between the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity.[^49] As John Paul II writes, "the Holy Spirit, consubstantial with the Father and the Son in divinity, is love and (uncreated) gift, from which derives as from a source (*fons vivus*) every gift to creatures (created gift): the donation of existence to all things through creation; the donation of grace to men through the entire economy of salvation. As the Apostle Paul writes, *God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us* (Rom 5:5)."[^50]

The *communio* to which human beings are called is, therefore, the manifestation and effect of the intra-Trinitarian communion.
