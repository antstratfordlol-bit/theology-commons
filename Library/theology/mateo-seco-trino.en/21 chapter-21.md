## Chapter 21: The Divine Processions {#mateo-seco-trino.21}

Jesus of Nazareth has made known to us that God, while being one, is at the same time Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. This distinction of Persons in the bosom of the one and only God leads us to consider the vital interchange that gives rise to and expresses the Trinity of Persons.

Faith affirms not only that the three Persons constitute one sole God, but also teaches that an intimate vital relationship exists among them: the Father begets, the Son is begotten by the Father, and the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. Thus, the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity is not only the mystery of God's being, but primarily the mystery of the fruitfulness of His immanent life. The Trinity is a fruitful and intimate vital relationship that has the Father as principle without principle. The Father communicates to the Son His own substance by way of generation and, through the Son, by way of love, to the Holy Spirit.

It is the Lord Jesus Christ Himself who invites us to contemplate the Trinitarian mystery, offering us the concept of procession as a key concept. This is evident even from the names by which He designates the three divine Persons: Father implies being the origin and principle from whom the Son proceeds; Son essentially includes the fact of proceeding from the Father; the Holy Spirit, who is Love, proceeds from the Father (cf. Jn 15:26) and receives from the Son (cf. Jn 16:14).

The oneness and trinity of God find their *raison d'être* in the origin that the Persons have from one another. This provenance grounds at once their distinction and their unity. It grounds their unity because, given the divine simplicity, that which proceeds immanently from a divine Person must be God; it grounds their distinction because the fact of proceeding means that He who proceeds cannot be identified with the principle from whom He proceeds. Procession in God is, therefore, the first question that must be addressed by anyone wishing to delve into the consideration of the Trinitarian mystery.

Concerning the divine processions, faith teaches us the following: (a) there are two processions in God; (b) the first is true generation, whereas the second is not; (c) the Son proceeds from the Father, while the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son; (d) there are no more than two immanent processions in God.

### The Existence of Two Processions in God

There exist two immanent processions in God: one by way of generation, the other by way of spiration. The Father proceeds from no one; the Son proceeds from the Father by way of generation; the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son. This has been amply demonstrated in the preceding chapters.

The first Person receives the name of Father. The Father is the principle from whom the Son proceeds by way of generation (cf. e.g. Jn 17:6). This is the name with which Jesus always addresses God, thereby revealing the radicality with which His paternity is to be understood.

The second Person receives the name of Son (Mt 3:17) and Only-begotten of the Father (Jn 1:14). These are names that tradition has taken in all their depth, understanding them as an authentic natural sonship. This signifies that the second Person not only really proceeds from the first, but proceeds by way of generation. The second Person is also called Word (Jn 1:1, 14; 1 Jn 1:1), Image of God (2 Cor 4:4), Radiance and Image (Col 1:15), the very Stamp of His nature (Heb 1:3)—expressions that concretise or make explicit the concept of generation. The Son is called Word because He is begotten by the Father in His act of knowledge; He is called Image, Radiance, and Stamp because He is the perfect Word: the perfect expression of the Father's substance.

The third Person receives the names of Holy Spirit (Mt 28:19), Love (1 Jn 4:7–8; Rom 5:5), Gift (Acts 2:38; 8:20), and Spirit of truth (Jn 14:15–19; 15:26; 16:7–15). It is never said of Him that He is begotten, but that He proceeds (*ekporeuetai*). His procession from the Father is, therefore, distinct from the procession of the Son.

Every other procession is excluded from the Trinitarian mystery. There is Trinity in God, not quaternity.[^1]

### The Concept of Procession Applied to God

In theological language, the term procession designates the origin or emanation of one being from another. Procession encompasses diverse modalities. Thus, for example, we say that our thoughts come from our intellect, or that our external actions come from our freedom. In the first case, we refer to something that proceeds from us and remains in us (immanent procession); in the second case, we refer to something whose effect goes outside ourselves (transient or transitive procession). Procession is called immanent when the term that proceeds remains within that from which it proceeds, as is the case with the mental word (*verbum mentis*), which proceeds from the intellect and remains within it. Yet, even though the term remains within that from which it proceeds, it is really distinguished from it. Our self-awareness remains within us, but at the same time is distinguished from us: it is the experience or image we have of ourselves.[^2]

In God there exists a transient procession: namely, creation. It is called transient precisely because the term of the creative action is found outside God. God is the cause of the world because the world proceeds from God by way of creation. Being originated by a transient procession, the world neither is God nor can be confused with God. It is really distinct from God. In God there exist also immanent processions—that is, processions whose term remains within the divine interiority. These are the terms inherent to knowledge and love. These terms, being immanent, are not distinct from God, but are God Himself.

It is necessary to recall once more that, given the infinite transcendence of God, all elements of the processions must be understood analogously. Indeed, they must be conceived as processions in an infinitely perfect being who has neither before nor after, nor any composition of act and potency.

In a certain sense, the error of Arius lay in conceiving the immanent processions as if they were transient—that is, as if the term of that procession had to be outside what is strictly divine. Hence he asserted that the Son is the first creature, posterior and inferior to the Father. This entailed that the Father would neither have been always Father, nor would He now be truly Father, since He would not have begotten someone of His own nature, but of an inferior nature. Arius therefore denied the possibility of an authentic immanent procession in God: according to him, every divine procession would have to be transient. Although Arius's position is untenable, it alerts us that, in applying the concept of procession to God, it is absolutely necessary to eliminate from it every trace of imperfection.

In God, processions cannot imply any movement, nor before or after, nor distinction between act and potency, nor change of any kind. This explains why Saint Thomas defined the divine processions as a pure *ordo originis*, as a simple order of origin.[^3] By this he meant to designate pure provenance, stripped of the imperfections with which procession is found in created being. In that procession there is no transition whatsoever from non-being to being; there is only a supremely simple communication of the purest act of God: the Father, in begetting the Son, communicates to Him His very same and indivisible substance. He communicates it really, yet indivisibly, in such a way that the Son is numerically the same God as the Father. He communicates it eternally, without before or after, without movement or change of any kind. The Son is likewise uncaused, just as the Father is uncaused.[^4]

These processions are conceived in various ways: the Greeks conceive them as self-givings that the divine Persons make to one another; Saint Augustine conceived them in analogy with the internal life of the human spirit—that is, as the procession of intellect and love; Richard of Saint Victor explained them through the demands of perfect charity.

In any event, it is clear that the explanations given must be coherent with the affirmation of the oneness and infinite perfection of God. Thus, being begotten—as pertains to the Son—is not to be understood according to the passivity with which we see material beings begotten. It is true that the terms of the processions are spoken of in passive form: it is said of the Son that He is begotten, and of the Spirit that He is spirated. These are analogous expressions, inasmuch as the processions in God, according to our way of thinking, present similarities to the passive production of one creature by means of another. But in God, who is pure act, there can be no passive processes understood in the strict sense.[^5]

In God, the term of the processions is not only immanent to the principle from which it proceeds, but possesses the most perfect immanence, since it is identified with the Godhead. Here is how Saint Thomas explains this, taking as an example the procession of the Word:

> "That which proceeds by a transient procession is necessarily distinct from that from which it proceeds. But that which proceeds immanently by an intellectual procession is not necessarily diverse; on the contrary, the more perfect its procession is, the more closely united it will be with its principle. It is clear that the more intimately a thing is understood, the more intimate is the intellectual conception to the one who understands, and the more one with him; for the intellect, in the very act of understanding, becomes one with the object understood. Therefore, since the divine intellect stands, as has been said, at the utmost summit of perfection, the divine Word must be perfectly one with that from which He proceeds, without any diversity whatsoever."[^6]

### Processions and Divine Operations

One cannot say that one thing proceeds from another without at the same time referring to the reason for this provenance. That reason is found in an action. Thus, for example, the Son proceeds from the Father because the Father generates Him through the action of generating. Every procession therefore presupposes an action that originates it, and every immanent procession presupposes an internal action of God. The nature of the action originating the procession and the nature of that procession correspond strictly, so that immanent processions can take place only through immanent actions.[^7]

The existence of immanent processions in God leads us, therefore, to the existence of immanent operations in God. These must be operations coherent with the infinite simplicity of God's being. They are not, therefore, actions in which the divine being unfolds and realises itself, as if God were perfecting Himself through His own acts. God is infinitely perfect in His eternity; in Him there is no potency and act, but only act. For this reason it can be said that procession in God is the eternal production, without any succession, of one divine Person by another in an immeasurable act of inner power.

Nor can these actions be conceived as something accidental, for in God no accident is possible. In a created being, its acts perfect it accidentally, since they unfold the virtualities contained in its nature. In God this is not so, for in Him being and act are identified. The acts that originate the processions are spiritual actions that remain in God and are God Himself.[^8] [^9] They are operations proper to spiritual activity, which consists in knowing and loving. These operations take place without introducing any imperfection into God; they show that God is not, so to speak, a static substance, but life and act.

The operations that give rise to the processions therefore take on the same characteristics as the processions they originate: they take place "without movement, priority or posteriority, diversity of nature, composition of act and potency, transmutation of any kind, succession or time; without distinction of cause and effect, nor any mixture of imperfection."[^10]

For this reason, although the most customary point of reference for speaking of these processions consists of the actions and processions immanent to the human spirit—the acts of knowledge and love—it is necessary not to forget that these human acts are only a point of reference, a distant analogy. In God, the ultimate reason for the immanent processions is the divine being itself, a supernatural mystery in its infinite transcendence. And, of course, neither the divine intellect nor the divine will can be conceived as faculties that are perfected by the performance of their acts.

### Processions According to Intellect and Will

The analogy of the processions with the life of the human spirit also helps to distinguish the two immanent processions in accordance with the diversity proper to the operations of knowing and loving. The Word proceeds from the Father by way of intellect; the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son by way of love.

The procession of the Word resembles the inner word by which we express what a thing is. The names Sacred Scripture uses to designate the procession of the Son lead necessarily to the understanding that this procession takes place by way of intellect. In the act of understanding, we produce within ourselves an image or likeness of that which is known. Hence its likeness to generation. According to Saint Augustine's well-known formula, the Son is Son because He is Word, and He is Word because He is Son.[^11]

By contrast, the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son by way of love. This is an assertion more difficult to demonstrate insofar as both the nature of love and the Holy Spirit are less known to us than the act of knowing or the theology of the Word. Even the names given to the Holy Spirit are not exclusive to Him, but are essential names appropriated to Him. Within this discreet penumbra, it is clear that the names attributed to Him—and the very mission of the Spirit—are linked to love and therefore to the will.

The Spirit is Love and Gift. If we return to the analogy with the life of the spirit, it should be noted that love belongs to the life of the spirit with the same essentiality as understanding, although it possesses quite distinct characteristics. Here is how Saint Thomas expresses it:

> "In God there are two processions: the procession of the Word, and another. In order to explain this, let it be noted that in God there is no procession except by reason of actions (...) which remain in the agent himself. Now, in an intellectual nature, this kind of action belongs to the intellect and to the will. By the action of the intellect the Word is produced; and by the operation of the will there is also in us another procession, which is the procession of love, whereby the object loved is in the lover, just as the thing said or understood is in the one who understands through the conception of the word."[^12]

### The Principle and Term of the Processions

Although the ultimate ground of the mystery of the Trinity, according to our mode of speaking, is the perfection of the divine essence, it must be borne in mind that the Persons do not arise from the divine essence. The divine essence as such is not the generating and spirating principle; nor is it the personal *I* that is begotten or begetting. For this reason the Father is called principle without principle. The Father does not proceed from the divine essence.

It suffices to recall the clarifications of the Fourth Lateran Council against Abbot Joachim: the divine essence "neither generates, nor is generated, nor proceeds, but it is the Father who generates, the Son who is generated, and the Holy Spirit who proceeds. So that distinction is in the persons and unity in the nature."[^13]

The Father proceeds from no one;[^14] source and origin of the whole Trinity, He generates insofar as He possesses vital fullness—or rather, insofar as He is absolute vital fullness, which is identified with the divine being. The Father generates because He is identified with the divine essence. But it is the Father who generates, not the divine essence.

This means that the *principium quod* of the processions is the Persons, whereas the *principium quo* is the single divine being, indivisible and belonging equally to the three Persons.[^15] From this there follow, among others, these two consequences:

Since only the divine Persons as such produce and proceed, the essence of God as such is neither multiplied nor divided. The Father begets a Person distinct from Himself, not an equal yet numerically distinct nature. In this communication that characterises the fruitful intradivine vital processes, the divine essence remains indivisible in its oneness. The processions are solely the reason why that essence exists as identical according to three distinct personal modes.[^16] For this reason there is distinction among the Persons, but there is no distinction in the essence. The three Persons are the one and same God.

Since the divine essence is of itself pure act, it follows that the three Persons are of themselves pure act. The fullness of life that exists in the Father, in the Son, and in the Holy Spirit has its foundation in itself; that is, to it belongs as an essential characteristic to possess in itself the ground of its existence: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are uncreated and uncaused, for they are the one and same *esse subsistens*.[^17]

### Essential Acts and Notional Acts

Since there are two kinds of processions in God, immanent and transient, it is necessary also to speak of immanent acts and transient acts. Transient acts are common to the three Persons by reason of the divine essence, whereas the acts upon which the immanent processions are grounded are exclusive to the Persons who are the origin and term of the procession. They are called notional acts. Indeed, to say that the Father—and only the Father—truly begets the Son is equivalent to affirming of the Father an act proper and exclusive to Him, an eternal act in which the Son receives His origin. Generation is, therefore, a notional act.

> "The distinction of the divine persons is taken from their origin. Origin cannot be suitably designated except by certain acts. Consequently, in order to designate the order of origin among the divine persons, it is necessary to attribute notional acts to them."[^18]

These notional acts are contrasted with essential operations, which are common to the whole Trinity. Notional acts are exclusive to the Persons: only the Father generates, only the Son is generated; only the Father and the Son spirate, only the Holy Spirit is spirated. Notional acts designate nothing other than the origins of the divine Persons. They are, therefore, four: active generation, passive generation, active spiration, and passive spiration.[^19]

Given the divine simplicity, notional acts are really identified with the divine essence: the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God. Nevertheless, there exists a virtual distinction (a distinction of reason with a foundation in reality) between the notional acts and the divine essence. This distinction explains the fact that the Persons, despite the oneness of essence, perform acts that do not belong to the other two. Or rather, it is the one and same essential act of divine knowing and loving, inasmuch as the Father's understanding generates the Son, and the love of the Father and the Son spirates the Spirit. Thus, while creating is an act of the divine essence and, by reason of it, common to all three Persons, generating belongs only to the Father, and being generated belongs only to the Son, and not to the divine essence, which neither generates nor is generated.[^20]

As has already been seen, it is also necessary to eliminate every imperfection from our concept of notional acts. They are, therefore, acts that exclude the notion of movement. For this reason, notional acts are identified with the relations of paternity, filiation, and spiration. Thus origins, notional acts, and persons are properly and formally identical.[^21] For this reason Thomas Aquinas writes:

> "Notional acts do not differ from the relations except in their mode of signification, for in reality they are absolutely identical with them (...) Since in God there is no movement, it follows that the personal action of the one who produces a person is nothing other than the relation of the principle to the person who proceeds from that principle; and these relations of reference are precisely the relations or notions themselves."[^22]
