## Chapter 5: The Pre-Nicene Era {#mateo-seco-trino.5}

The truth concerning the mystery of God was explicitly present in the Church from her very birth. This truth was captured especially in the act of baptism and was universally present in the confessions of faith, in the liturgy, and in prayers. From the very beginning, there was a clear awareness that the profession of faith in Christ is inseparable from the confession of faith in the intimate mystery of God—that is to say, in the mystery of His fatherhood. Conversely, it was equally clear that the confession of faith in God entails the confession of faith in Christ, who is His Only-Begotten. What the Church confesses is that Jesus of Nazareth is "the Christ, the Son of the living God" (Mt 16:16) and, consequently, that in God there exists fatherhood and sonship.

These affirmations regarding God are made within the context of salvation history. The Christian kerygma is summarised in the affirmation that in Jesus of Nazareth Yahweh who saves has definitively revealed Himself, and that with Him—with His Incarnation, His death, and His Resurrection—the fullness of time has arrived. This is evident in Peter's first discourse:

> "Men of Israel, hear these words: Jesus of Nazareth, a man attested to you by God with mighty works and wonders and signs which God did through him in your midst, as you yourselves know—this Jesus, delivered up according to the definite plan and foreknowledge of God, you crucified and killed by the hands of lawless men. But God raised him up, having loosed the pangs of death (...) This Jesus God raised up, and of that we all are witnesses. Being therefore exalted at the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this which you see and hear (...) Let all the house of Israel therefore know assuredly that God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified" (Acts 2:22–36).

Here is the essential core of the Christian message: God has raised Jesus from the dead and has constituted Him Lord and Christ. In this message, the apostolic experience and testimony are condensed. Every subsequent explication and formulation of this essential core will be at the service of fidelity in its transmission. Even the New Testament, which was born in the Church and is recognised by her as the inspired word of God, is first and foremost a confession of this same faith and, in many cases, a summary of this initial apostolic preaching. Indeed, the redaction of the New Testament took place in a Church that was already confessing and preaching her faith in the mystery of the Triune and One God and actualising this faith in the sacrament of Baptism; it took place, moreover, at least as regards the latest New Testament writings, at the very same time that the first Trinitarian testimonies were beginning to appear in non-canonical writings.[^1]

The life of the Church is essentially Trinitarian: in Sacred Scripture and in the earliest preaching of the Church, the teaching on the God who is One and Triune and the affirmation of our salvation in the Son made man constitute an inseparable unity and clearly emerge as the central focus of Christian life. This life, ultimately, consists in nothing other than participation in the eternal life of the Trinity, which is communicated in Jesus Christ.[^2] For this reason, Trinitarian doctrine does not arise primarily from theological reflection, but from the life of the Church.

### I. The Earliest Testimonies

We shall begin our exposition of the mystery of God in the pre-Nicene period precisely with those testimonies that express the faith of the Church most concretely: the baptismal liturgy, the professions of faith, the Eucharistic liturgy, and Christian prayer, before proceeding to the specific themes of theological reflection.

#### The Baptismal Liturgy

In the early Church, just as in the Church of all ages, Trinitarian faith was expressed with special force at those moments when the paschal mystery was celebrated—specifically, in Baptism and the Eucharist. Everything springs from the missionary mandate contained in Mt 28:19: *Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*. This mandate is fulfilled in the baptismal profession of faith, which is, in reality, the Trinitarian faith living within the baptismal communities and expressed in the Confessions or Creeds (Symbols of faith).[^3]

Through baptism one enters into membership in the Church. As the *Didache* testifies, only those who have been baptised in the name of the Lord may partake in the celebration of the Eucharist.[^4] For this reason, the baptismal practice of the early Church is a primary witness to the fact that Christian faith has been Trinitarian from the outset. Indeed, baptism brings about rebirth to a new life that carries salvation with it. This salvation is expressly bound to the sanctifying action of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, which causes the one baptised to be born anew (cf. Jn 3:3). And although it is true that, in some New Testament passages, baptism appears to be administered "in the name of Jesus" (cf., e.g., Acts 2:38; 8:37), by the second century it is already clear that baptism was universally administered with the explicit mention of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.[^5] The fact that in the earliest decades the baptismal formula was not always explicitly Trinitarian does not mean that the fundamental structure of baptism was not already Trinitarian at that time. Using this formula at the moment of administering baptism is not the only way in which the Trinitarian structure of baptism is given expression. This Trinitarian structure "came to be expressed above all in the baptismal confession which, in the form of baptismal interrogations and subsequently of the baptismal creed, traces its origin back to the mandate of Mt 28:19."[^6]

The *Didache* (c. 90/100), when setting forth the manner of baptising, speaks as the bearer of a very ancient tradition and prescribes the explicitly Trinitarian formula: "Concerning baptism, baptise in this way: having first recited all these things, baptise in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, in living water. But if you have no living water, baptise in other water; and if you cannot in cold, then in warm. But if you have neither, pour water on the head three times in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."[^7] An identical witness to the Trinitarian dimension of baptism is found in Saint Justin Martyr[^8] († 163/167) and Saint Irenaeus[^9] († c. 202).

The testimony of Saint Irenaeus concerning Christian baptism is especially valuable on account of his authority, not only because he informs us of an explicit Trinitarian formula, but also because he assigns a distinct role to each of the three divine Persons in the work of sanctification, thereby insinuating their distinctive notions and the mutual relationship between them: "For this reason our new birth—baptism—takes place through these three articles, granting us the grace of new birth in God the Father, through His Son in the Holy Spirit. For those who bear the Spirit of God are led to the Word, that is, to the Son; but the Son presents them to the Father, and the Father confers incorruptibility. Thus, without the Spirit it is not possible to see the Son of God, and without the Son no one can draw near to the Father, for the Son is the knowledge of the Father, and the knowledge of the Son is accomplished through the Holy Spirit."[^10] As G. Bardy observes, the fact that Irenaeus employs these formulas without further commentary proves that they express an undisputed doctrine within the Church.[^11]

In Christian baptism, there is a close connection between conversion, admission into the Christian community, and the Trinitarian profession of faith. This demonstrates that this profession of faith "was very early regarded as the decisive Christian hallmark. This is further confirmed by the historical hypothesis that the formula 'in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit' was introduced to distinguish Christian baptism—baptism in the name of Jesus—from other Jewish baptisms."[^12]

#### The Trinitarian Profession of Faith

It is precisely this profession of faith made at baptism, together with the necessity of a prior catechesis so that this profession might be made with clear awareness of what was being celebrated and confessed, that motivated the drafting of baptismal creeds. There is no doubt that even before the end of the first century the principal articles of Christian faith were condensed into brief formulas.[^13]

Numerous passages in the New Testament attest to the existence of an explicit profession of faith at the moment of baptism. This profession of faith was made, at times, by means of questions and answers (cf., e.g., Acts 8:37; Rom 10:9; Eph 1:13; 1 Tim 6:12; Heb 4:14). The same appears in the texts of the *Didache*, Saint Justin, and Saint Irenaeus cited above. Saint Irenaeus speaks explicitly of three articles of our faith: "And for this reason, in our new birth, baptism takes place through these three articles. Baptism grants us the grace of new birth in God the Father through His Son in the Holy Spirit. For those who bear the Spirit of God are led to the Word, that is, to the Son; the Son presents them to the Father, and the Father confers incorruptibility upon them."[^14] In Saint Hippolytus of Rome († 235), the baptismal interrogations have already developed into a complete tripartite creed in interrogative form, which expresses Trinitarian faith in its very structure.[^15] There is also ample evidence that by the middle of the third century the tripartite structure of the baptismal symbol was traditional in the East.[^16]

From the time of Saint Justin and Saint Irenaeus, testimonies to the existence of these creeds become very numerous. In his struggle against the Gnostics, Saint Irenaeus appeals to the faith of the Church expressed in the Creed: "This is the order and the disposition of our faith, the foundation of the building, and that which gives firmness to our conduct: God the Father, uncreated, uncontained, invisible, one God, creator of all reality; this is the first article of our faith. And the second article is this: The Word of God, the Son of God, Christ Jesus our Lord (...) through whom all things were created (...) And the third article is this: The Holy Spirit, through whom the prophets prophesied..."[^17] In *Adversus haereses*, he cites one of these primitive confession formulas: "The Church, though dispersed throughout the whole world even to the ends of the earth, has received from the Apostles and their disciples this faith: in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven, and earth, and the sea, and all things that are in them; and in one Christ Jesus, the Son of God, who became incarnate for our salvation; and in the Holy Spirit, who proclaimed through the prophets..."[^18]

Tertullian († 222/223) likewise appeals to the baptismal profession of faith in his polemic against heresies. This profession of faith—Tertullian argues—is a genuine oath (*sacramentum*), in which the neophyte, after renouncing Satan, answers the questions put to him during the ceremony, to such an extent that the profession of the Creed and baptism are two elements of one and the same baptismal rite and constitute the *sacramentum fidei*. Here is a beautiful Trinitarian description of Christian faith bequeathed to us by Tertullian: "To whom is truth made manifest without God? Who knows God without Christ? Who lives of Christ without the Holy Spirit? To whom is the Holy Spirit communicated without the sacrament of faith?"[^19]

The clear and definitive formula of the so-called Apostles' Creed is already well attested by the beginning of the fourth century.[^20] In this Creed, faith is not only professed in the Trinity, but we find that Trinitarian structure so characteristic of the symbols of faith, which facilitates the distinction among the divine Persons by dedicating a separate section to each.

Upon this foundation of faith solemnly professed in the Church, there was formed what is known already in Saint Irenaeus and Tertullian as the rule of faith, *regula fidei*.[^21] The rule of faith is not identical with the baptismal creed; rather, it denotes the faith of the Church in its entirety. It does not provide concrete formulas, but rather a brief normative summary of the faith handed down by the Apostles. This is why it is so significant that these brief summaries possess a Trinitarian character. They formed the bedrock of Trinitarian doctrine, not only for the earliest Christian thinkers, but also for the great theologians, such as Origen and Saint Augustine. This Trinitarian doctrine of the early Church is "the rule of faith and, as such, the decisive interpretation of Christian truth. It is the decisive exegesis of the Bible in the Church. It is the sum of Christian faith."[^22]

#### The Eucharistic Liturgy

Already Saint Justin testifies in his *First Apology* that the Eucharistic liturgy is intimately bound up with the Trinitarian mystery: "Then there is brought to the president of the brethren bread and a cup of wine mixed with water; and he taking them, gives praise and glory to the Father of the universe, through the name of the Son and of the Holy Ghost, and offers thanks at considerable length for our being counted worthy to receive these things at his hands. And when he has concluded the prayers and thanksgivings, all the people present express their assent by saying Amen."[^23] And a little further on he adds: "And for all things wherewith we are supplied, we bless the Maker of all through His Son Jesus Christ, and through the Holy Ghost."[^24]

The *Apostolic Tradition*, composed in Rome around the middle of the third century, exhibits this same scheme already present in Justin, but in a more developed form: "We give thanks to Thee, O God, through Thy beloved Son Jesus Christ, whom in these last times Thou hast sent to us as a Saviour, Redeemer, and the Messenger of Thy counsel; who is Thy Word inseparable, through whom Thou madest all things, and in whom Thou art well pleased; whom Thou didst send from heaven into the womb of the Virgin, and who, being conceived, was made flesh and was manifested as Thy Son, born of the Holy Spirit and the Virgin (...) And we pray that Thou wouldest send Thy Holy Spirit upon the oblation of the holy Church; that gathering them into one, Thou wouldest grant to all the saints who partake to be filled with the Holy Spirit for the confirmation of their faith in truth; that we may praise and glorify Thee through Thy Son Jesus Christ, through whom be glory and honour unto Thee, Father and Son with the Holy Spirit, in Thy holy Church, both now and to the ages of ages. Amen."[^25] The anaphora begins by addressing the Father, through the Son. In its progression, the distinction and equality of the three divine Persons are manifest. In the epiclesis, the Father is besought to send the Holy Spirit so that we may glorify Him through the Son. This invocation would undergo immense development in Eastern liturgies.[^26] The anaphora concludes with a doxology in which Trinitarian faith is once again expressed. This doxology is repeated frequently throughout the *Apostolic Tradition*.[^27]

This same Trinitarian dimension of the Eucharistic prayer is universally present across all testimonies that have come down to us.[^28] Suffice it to cite these examples taken from the *Didascalia Apostolorum* and the *Testamentum Domini* respectively: "And now (that is, when the deacon brings the gifts to the bishop upon the altar), let the pontiff pray over the oblations that the Holy Spirit may descend upon them and transform the bread into the body of Christ and the cup into the blood of Christ." "We offer unto Thee the Eucharist, eternal Trinity, Lord Jesus Christ, Lord Father, before whom all creation trembles and shudders, O Lord Holy Spirit (...) Grant that all who partake of Thy holy mysteries may be one with Thee, so that they may be filled with the Holy Spirit for the confirmation of their faith in truth and may offer unto Thee and Thy beloved Son, Jesus Christ, a doxology, whereby all praise and glory be given unto Thee with the Holy Spirit forever and ever."[^29] This universal presence of the Trinitarian confession in the liturgy shows that the Trinitarian dimension is essential to sacramental life. The encounter and union of man with God takes place in the Holy Spirit. In turn, this Holy Spirit is given to us by the Lord. This donation "is inaugurated in a personal Pentecost at Baptism, and is perpetuated in the life of the Church, in the Eucharistic mystery. The Holy Spirit is, finally, the Spirit of adoption, thanks to whom we are called children of God, in whom we have the boldness to call God Father. The Holy Spirit is thus the giver of the Trinitarian presence."[^30]

#### Christian Prayer

The liturgical texts just cited show how Christian prayer, like Christian faith, is essentially Trinitarian. As A. Hamman observes, Christian prayer inherits many traits from Jewish prayer, but distinguishes itself by one fundamental feature: it is addressed to God through the mediation of Jesus Christ, our Lord.[^31] Indeed, according to the teaching of the New Testament, Christ is always and universally the mediator and high priest of the New Covenant. Hence, from the earliest times, it was the Christian custom to address prayer to the Father through the Son.

Within this framework stand the Christian doxologies, which were to possess such immense importance in the development of Trinitarian doctrine.[^32] They constitute an explicit acknowledgement of the divine attributes, especially of God's *doxa*, His glory. These doxologies are present in the earliest testimonies of Christian prayer. They appear profusely across the New Testament writings.[^33] We have already observed them in the earliest liturgical texts and patristic writings. Origen relates that Christian communities adopted the Jewish custom of beginning and ending every prayer with praise to God through the Son in the Holy Spirit.[^34]

The use of the doxology "Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Spirit" was already widespread by the mid-fourth century. It is found in the hymn *Phos Hilaron* ("Joyful Light"), dating from the second to third centuries.[^35] This brief doxology was soon used to conclude the recitation of the Psalms. It was customary for the Fathers to conclude not only their prayers but also their treatises with a brief doxology.[^36] As noted, Hippolytus preserves two doxological formulas: a brief one, addressed to the Father through the Son, and a solemn one, addressed to the three divine Persons.

The confession of faith in the Trinity was made not only within liturgical life, but also before the entire world.[^37] Not without reason does the confession of faith in the Son include the confession of faith in the Father. The prayer recorded in the *Martyrdom of Polycarp* is an entire lesson in Trinitarian theology, significant on account of its antiquity and its formulations, which mirror venerable liturgical formulas:

> "Lord God Almighty, Father of Thy beloved and blessed Son Jesus Christ, through whom we have received the knowledge of Thee, God of angels and powers and of all creation, and of the whole race of the righteous who live in Thy presence: I bless Thee that Thou hast counted me worthy of this day and hour, that I should take part in the number of Thy martyrs, in the cup of Thy Christ, unto the resurrection of eternal life, both of soul and body, in the incorruption of the Holy Spirit. Among whom may I be accepted this day before Thee as a rich and acceptable sacrifice (...) Wherefore I also praise Thee for all things, I bless Thee, I glorify Thee, along with the everlasting and heavenly Jesus Christ, Thy beloved Son, through whom, with Him and the Holy Spirit, be glory both now and to the future ages. Amen."[^38]

The prayer is addressed to God the Father. Polycarp adores Him as the Creator of all (*pantokrator*), but above all as the author of salvation history. As Th. Camelot comments, this is a title that occurs frequently in Judaism and in the Apocalypse (cf. Rev 1:8; 4:8; 11:17), particularly in liturgical acclamations. This God is the Father of Jesus Christ, a traditional formula so frequent in Saint Paul (cf. Rom 15:6; 2 Cor 1:3; 11:31; Eph 1:3; Col 1:3). The title given to Jesus—Son (the Greek text uses the word *pais* rather than *hyios*)—reflects the antiquity of the passage, for in primitive Christian literature the word *pais* was frequently used because it combined the meanings of "son" and "servant". To call Jesus "beloved son" is equivalent to calling Him only Son. Jesus is presented, therefore, as the servant, yet not as a slave, but as the well-beloved Son. The words used by Polycarp to address God—"I praise Thee, I bless Thee, I glorify Thee"—evoke the great liturgical doxologies, such as that of the *Gloria*: "we praise Thee, we bless Thee, we adore Thee, we glorify Thee."[^39] The Holy Spirit is designated as the giver of incorruption to both soul and body.[^40]

#### Confession of Faith, Theological Reflection, and Dogmatic Development

There is a profound doctrinal and terminological coherence across the various manifestations of faith in God within the early Church. This faith, in its simplicity of expression, is clear and firm: there exists one only God, who is Father, Son, and Spirit. As with other theological questions—though perhaps in the case of the Trinity it can be perceived with greater lucidity—Christian doctrine, which would be formulated in subsequent centuries with greater technical precision, is not exclusively the fruit of the profession of faith, but also the fruit of reflection upon the relations existing between Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and upon the unity of God and His trinity of Persons.[^41] The confession of faith is a simple and steadfast profession of faith in revealed truth; Trinitarian doctrine adds something decisive to this simple confession of faith: it presents the Trinitarian mystery at a speculative level, defining concepts and forms of speech, demonstrating the interconnection among the various truths of faith, and illuminating the place this mystery occupies within the entirety of Divine Revelation.

This doctrinal corpus grows and is enriched through theological reflection and dogmatic formulations. Theological reflection does not stand on the same level as dogmatic formulations, but on a far more modest level: the level proper to the reflection of the theologian. It is a reflection grounded in the doctrine of the faith, yet carried out through rational effort. From the viewpoint of faith, the level of theological reflection is more modest than the confession of faith; nevertheless, without deep and enduring theological reflection, a mature dogmatic formulation would be impossible, and the growth of the doctrinal corpus would likewise be unachievable. At the same time, dogmatic formulation contributes definitively to the advancement of theological knowledge.

Dogmatic formulas, by which the Church comes to express and define her faith in the Trinity, are not themselves revealed qua formulas. Even the term "Trinity" (*Trinitas*, *Trias*) was not revealed. What is revealed is the existence of one only God—the God of Israel—and the affirmation that this God is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, who, while distinct from one another, are simultaneously the one, true God. And thus have They manifested Themselves in the history of salvation. All theological reflection, and even subsequent magisterial formulations, have no other function than to serve this Revelation and ensure fidelity in its transmission.[^42] Any fixation of dogma in increasingly refined formulas always refers back to the faith of the Church, which these formulas serve by approaching and safeguarding. It is the faith of the Church that must always be kept in view, including within theological construction. Progress in formulation does not take place by substituting one definition for another, but by enriching the preceding faith.[^43]

Theological reflection very early demonstrated the intimate bonds uniting one truth of faith with another and, above all, highlighted the relation between the mystery of Christ, the mystery of God, and human salvation. The emergence of heresies made it urgent to discern between truth and the errors that threatened this growth. Heresies, indeed, attempt to establish a rational knowledge of the mystery that deforms and nullifies it.[^44] This explains why the Church very quickly created an appropriate terminology for the formulation of Trinitarian dogma with the aid of notions of philosophical origin: substance, person, hypostasis, relation, etc.[^45]

### II. The Beginnings of Theological Reflection

The writings of the New Testament and the earliest testimonies of the Church's life contain clear professions of faith and unambiguous teaching regarding the Trinitarian mystery. This teaching is expressed in extraordinarily concrete language. Terms such as "person", "relation", or "missions" are not used there; rather, all this rich doctrinal content is conveyed as the narrative unfolds of God's salvific activity in history and the sanctification wrought by Christ in the Spirit. Almost from the beginning, the holy Fathers found themselves obliged to undertake the task of expressing this faith and formulating this teaching using terminological and conceptual precision that would foster its comprehension and avert ambiguity or misunderstanding. Naturally, the Fathers carried out this work in close dialogue with the cultural environment in which they lived, often impelled by the need to respond to the challenges posed by erroneous doctrines.

Thus, faith in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit belongs to the very core of the New Testament experience. Yet, in order to explain the development of Trinitarian doctrine and dogma, it is not enough to point to this root or essential core of the revealed message; it is also necessary to take into account a long history of events and intellectual endeavours that converged in the crystallization and refinement of Trinitarian doctrine.[^46]

#### The Cultural Background

Patristic thought concerning God is anchored in the teaching of Sacred Scripture, and is marked not only by the task of instructing and catechising Christians and refuting heresies, but also by controversy with non-Christians concerning the nature of the true God—the struggle against polytheism, and the defence of the mysteries of the Most Holy Trinity, the Incarnation, and the Redemption against those who deemed them incompatible with the divine nature: in a word, by dialogue with Hellenic philosophy.[^47]

The Old Testament already offered the Fathers a notion of God which, contemplated in the light of the New, acquired a new relief and dimension. From the Old Testament, passages such as Ex 3:14 and Wis 13:1–9 (cf. Rom 1:20; Acts 17:27–28) particularly drew their attention. This God, who is affirmed to be above all things, has definitively revealed Himself in Christ with total closeness to man; He was born of a woman, truly died, and truly rose again, as they would insistently proclaim in open combat against the Docetists.

In expounding the Christian concept of God, the Holy Fathers had to contend with the ideas about God prevalent in their era, both in the pagan and Jewish worlds. They had to struggle, first of all, with the concept of God maintained by Judaism, demonstrating that this one God is the Father of Jesus Christ, who is Son in the fullest sense and, consequently, true God from true God. That is to say, they had to demonstrate that Trinitarian doctrine is not incompatible with monotheism. The Holy Fathers also had to contend with the dualism so widespread in their day, showing that there is no other principle of all existing reality than God, who is the creator of everything and the sole Lord of all things. They also had to combat polytheism, demonstrating that even natural reason recoils from a multitude of gods.

In this struggle on multiple fronts, and even in their more serene elaboration of doctrine concerning God, the Holy Fathers maintained a genuine dialogue with philosophical thought, especially Greek philosophy. Beginning with the Apologists, many of these Fathers were converts from paganism, philosophers by education and temperament—such as Saint Justin, Athenagoras, Tatian, and Clement of Alexandria—who had found the true God in the midst of their personal search for truth, that is, through their philosophical inquiry. Hence, they felt a duty of fidelity toward whatever good they had discovered on their philosophical journey, and sought to present Christianity to the culture of their time using the language and concepts of philosophy.

The Holy Fathers based this stance on many grounds. Sacred Scripture supported them, particularly those passages in Wisdom (Wis 13:1–9), Romans (Rom 1:18–23), and Acts (Acts 17:22–29) where it is clearly affirmed that God has revealed Himself in His creation, and where the capacity of human reason to attain some knowledge of Him is upheld. They were moved to maintain this openness toward philosophy by their conviction of the universality of God's call to all men, and therefore by the conviction that God had also made Himself accessible through philosophy—as seen, for instance, in Clement of Alexandria. In any event, the Fathers never forgot that normative teaching resides in Sacred Scripture, and not in philosophical speculation.[^48]

This dialogue with the multifaceted culture of the era never caused the Fathers to lose sight of the radical novelty of the Christian message concerning God. Indeed, Christian thought does not begin its reflection on God from the furthest boundary reached by paganism; rather, it rests upon divine Revelation which, in its fundamental affirmations—one God, Creator, Provident, Judge of the good and the wicked—is chronologically prior even to classical philosophy. For this reason, in deepening their grasp of the revealed message, Christian thinkers had recourse to philosophy as the organ of natural reason. This was an instrumental use of philosophy, which was in turn perfected through contact with Revelation, human reason's discoveries concerning God being rectified and healed by the light of divine truth.[^49] Hence it can be affirmed that, across patristic literature as a whole, the authenticity of Christian thought was not compromised in this dialogue, in which, as in all human affairs, both errors and insights are to be found. It is within this dialogue that the intellectual and moral phenomenon known as Christian humanism first took shape.

##### a) Jewish Christianity

The context in which Christian doctrine was born was unambiguously Semitic and Jewish. The God whom Jesus addressed as His *Abba* is the God whose interventions in history are recorded in the Old Testament. Not only was Jesus Jewish, but the Apostles and many members of the primitive Church were Jews who expressed themselves with a Jewish mentality and within a typically Semitic framework of thought. Naturally, these earliest expressions of doctrine concerning God and Christ possess immense significance, for they must be regarded as the initial link connecting the apostolic kerygma with its transmission to the wider world. We refer here to Jewish Christianity considered in terms of its mindset, its overarching view of the world and history, and its fundamental axes of thought.

This was a mode of Christian thought expressed within the framework established by Judaism, although as such it implied no ongoing organic link to the Jewish synagogue community. It encompassed individuals who, having broken completely with Jewish ritualism, continued to think in Semitic categories.[^50] These categories, proper to Jewish converts to Christianity, were also adopted by many converts from paganism, especially in the earliest period; we find them present, in certain formulations, in the doctrine preached across all ages.[^51]

The general framework of Jewish-Christian theology was apocalyptic. Apocalyptic categories are intrinsically bound to cosmology and, above all, to a linear view of history divided into two: the present age (*aeon*) and the future age. Thus the Incarnation is depicted as a descent from the highest heavens to the earth, and the triumph of Christ is proclaimed as an ascension into the heavens.

Within this overarching framework, it is necessary to distinguish between orthodox Jewish Christianity and heterodox Jewish Christianity. In many instances, heterodox Jewish Christianity was a continuation of heterodox Judaism. Here belong a series of sects such as the Ebionites and the Elkasaites. These sects regarded Jesus as the greatest of the prophets, but did not confess Him as God, being unable to integrate such a confession with their rigid monotheistic faith.[^52] It cannot have been easy for the Fathers to forge a path toward the correct expression of faith in the Triune and One God in the midst of this heterogeneous early Jewish-Christian world, in which many converts found it exceedingly difficult to reconcile their confession of faith in Christ with the unyielding affirmation of the divine uniqueness. Like Ignatius of Antioch, they had to insist repeatedly on the incoherence of calling oneself Christian while judaizing: "It is monstrous to talk of Jesus Christ and to practice Judaism. For Christianity did not believe into Judaism, but Judaism into Christianity."[^53] Yet heterodox Jewish Christianity must be regarded as only a fragment of Jewish-Christian theology, which even in its apocalyptic modes of expression was the first recipient of the Gospel message, to the extent that it may be affirmed that the Jewish-Christian perspective is inseparable from the theology of the second century.[^54]

The fact that the earliest theology developed within an apocalyptic framework facilitated a profound grasp of the relation between the Trinitarian mystery and the work of salvation—that is, the intimate connection between *theologia* and *oikonomia*. The inner mystery of God was revealed to us in His salvific action. More concretely, the ultimate and full revelation of God's inner life is unveiled in the historical mystery of the Pasch. "On the Cross it was revealed that the Son had always given Himself to the Father, and in the Resurrection it became evident that this gift of the Son, accepted by the Father, had always found its fulfilment in the Holy Spirit. Precisely from this consideration, the early Christians were necessarily led to administer baptism—by which, according to their understanding, they were inserted into this mystery—in the confession of faith in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit."[^55] The nexus between the economic Trinity and the immanent Trinity is perfectly highlighted within this framework, and in its light it becomes easier to penetrate the Trinitarian structure of Christian life and salvation history.[^56]

A prominent characteristic of Jewish Christianity was the significance attributed to angels. At times, this angelology served as a vehicle to express the Trinitarian mystery, especially in an environment that found abstract conceptualisation difficult. The Word and the Spirit are occasionally depicted as two supreme angels.[^57] Yet this manner of presenting the mystery of the Trinity does not appear to have been fundamental, even in the earliest times, to mainstream Christian thought, which remained rooted in the baptismal mandate and sober confessions of faith.[^58]

These categories of thought and forms of expression gradually receded as Christianity penetrated the Hellenic world, leading to the abandonment of the apocalyptic world-picture. The opposition between the present age and the age to come was replaced by that between the intelligible world and the sensible world, accompanied by an increasing tendency to employ metaphysical categories. These were not two entirely antagonistic visions, but they were certainly very distinct. Many features of the Jewish-Christian mentality—being thoroughly biblical—remained permanently embedded in Christian thought: for instance, the linear conception of salvation history and the historical interventions of God. These elements corrected and transformed many substantial perspectives of Hellenic thought, which was never accepted uncritically by the Church.[^59]

##### b) The Legacy of Greek Thought

All philosophical traditions, by virtue of their comprehensive inquiry into reality, were compelled to address the question of God—His existence and His nature—and provide an answer of one kind or another. Leaving aside the negative response (atheism), it may be said that affirmative answers fall into one of three fundamental categories: God as the ordering cause of the universe; God as the nature or intimate essence of the world (pantheism); God as the creative cause of the universe and supreme moral lawgiver.[^60]

The most primitive philosophical conception of God was that of the orderer of the universe. Anaxagoras is commonly cited as the first proponent of this view on account of his affirmation of an ordering cosmic mind (*Nous*). Plato's position may be ascribed to this line, particularly if the Demiurge of whom he speaks can be identified with God; for the Demiurge is clearly not a creator in the strict sense, being limited in power on the one hand by the Ideas as eternal exemplars of all things, and on the other by pre-existent matter, which limits the perfect instantiation of the Ideas. A similar situation obtains with Aristotle and his conception of the Unmoved Prime Mover. In the *Physics*, this Mover, who moves all things, acts as an efficient cause, whereas in the *Metaphysics* its character as a final cause is emphasised. Aristotle, proceeding from his resolution of the potency-act distinction, concludes that the first mover and first cause of all must be Pure Act, devoid of potentiality, and therefore supreme Life. This pure activity of God, according to Aristotle, cannot be conceived as transitive, but as turned entirely upon itself, as pure self-understanding—as *noesis noeseos*, the thought of thought. Aristotle denies to God any capacity for relation with the world, enclosing Him within pure self-immanence. Consequently, according to Aristotle, there is no divine providence over the cosmos.

Regarding the moral order, both Plato and Aristotle held that the moral order finds its ultimate ground and guarantee in God. Yet, precisely because of the immanence with which the divine being is conceived, God does not appear as the supreme lawgiver, and moral laws must be deduced exclusively from human nature. Thus the ultimate foundation of morality is human nature; nevertheless, both thinkers understood that man's ultimate end is the knowledge of the divine, that is, the contemplation of God to the limited degree of which so frail a creature as man is capable.

Pantheism also finds its initial manifestations in ancient philosophy. Xenophanes of Colophon, of the Eleatic school, identified God with the All; the Stoics conceived God as the World-Soul—that which, belonging to the cosmos, dwells in its innermost depth, such that the world is conceived in some sense as the body of God. Consequently, the entire cosmos was divine, proceeding from God by emanation. The most widespread forms of pantheism emerged among the Gnostics, in Neoplatonism, and especially in Plotinus. For the Gnostics, this emanation involved a genuine ontological degradation. In one way or another, the material world is likewise an ontological degradation of the divine in Neoplatonism and Plotinus. This emanation is presented as a necessary moment in the unfolding of the godhead, whereby divinity is conceived as a vast cosmic process. Morally, pantheistic doctrines tend to conflate the moral order with the physical order of the universe. If God is a necessary process from which the world derives, there is no authentic human freedom, nor morality in the strict sense; man's moral duty is reduced to aligning himself with the cosmic order, which he must do either willingly or by compulsion.[^61]

Although the concept of creation *ex nihilo* is in principle accessible to natural reason, it was not discovered—at least with clarity—by pre-Christian philosophy, appearing in the history of philosophy only when Christianity entered the ancient world. Even those philosophers who treated God most profoundly, such as Plato and Aristotle, attained only the concept of God as the architect of cosmic order and the author of motion.

Naturally, the absence of the concept of God as Creator entailed intractable problems regarding the divine nature itself. This is evident in Aristotle. The Prime Mover of the *Physics*, acting as the efficient cause of all cosmic motion, cannot be entirely unmoved, since it possesses an end outside itself: the movement of the cosmos. For this reason, in the *Metaphysics*, in order to preserve the Mover's absolute immobility, Aristotle reduces its causality to final causality alone, exercising no efficient causality in cosmic governance. A similar difficulty arises with Plato: despite being the metaphysician of transcendence *par excellence*, he failed to reach a precise concept of God as a personal and transcendent being—distinct from and superior to the intelligible world—since if God is identified with the Good, the Good in Plato is an Idea and not a personal subsistent Being.[^62]

Nevertheless, with all its limitations, pre-Christian thought achieved remarkable insights in its contemplation of the divine, providing Christian thinkers with an invaluable conceptual and linguistic apparatus with which to express revealed doctrine more perfectly and unpack its implications. Pre-Christian philosophy cannot be dismissed as entirely erroneous, though it was replete with errors and ambiguities. Both the Holy Fathers and subsequent theologians, while correcting pagan concepts, deeply appreciated the arguments of the greatest classical philosophers and made fruitful use of them, regarding the elements of truth contained in pre-Christian reflections as an authentic *praeparatio evangelica*.

This *praeparatio evangelica* is especially conspicuous in Plato and Aristotle. "Plato, like Aristotle after him, contemplates the world as a unity. This unity does not derive from the material element, which is distinct for each being, as Aristotle himself would specify. Unity derives from the idea of the One: unmoved, incorruptible, immortal. At times Plato calls it the Good, the object of Love whose permanent possession is desired. Here is what, in Plato, matters far more than all his references to the gods or to the divine: the intuition that man must ascend, moved by *eros*, by the desire that love engenders, to that true realm where the divine is beautiful, wise, and good—that is, where in the unity of God, the most just being that exists, Truth, Goodness, and Beauty coalesce into one."[^63]

Aristotle's influence on Christian thought concerning God was likewise profound. This influence is linked fundamentally to three elements: (a) his conception of science as knowledge receiving its unity and validity from first principles—that is, from metaphysics; (b) his understanding of God as the Cause of causes, Unmoved Mover, Pure Act, and Thought of thought; (c) his primacy of substance over all other categories. Thus the tireless search for truth that characterizes scientific endeavour becomes in reality a search for God as the first cause of the universe—the Cause of causes, pure intellect. This was his *praeparatio evangelica*, and simultaneously the constraint he imposed upon those who drew near to him. Aristotle offered philosophy and theology a rigorous framework within which to operate; at the same time, theology had to accept that God, who is Spirit, is that eternal, immaterial substance of which Aristotle spoke.

As has been noted, "it is true that Aristotle would promptly suggest that this substance is act and immaterial—which represents an enormous achievement for the Philosopher, since substance primarily denoted corporeal being. It is also true that Aristotle glides imperceptibly from substance to the act of understanding; yet, for all that, a certain toll is levied upon reflection on God: (a) this is a God who explains nature as a whole; (b) it maintains an intellectualist abstraction before which no one feels moved to pray."[^64] And yet, the sheer grandeur of the concluding reflections of Aristotle's *Metaphysics* remains striking:

> "There must, then, be such a principle, whose very essence is action. Their substance, also, must be without matter (...) For how will there be movement, if there is no actual cause? (...) And thought thinks on itself because it shares the nature of the object of thought (...) If, then, God is always in that good state in which we sometimes are, this compels our wonder; and if in a better this compels it yet more. And God is in a better state. And life also belongs to God; for the actuality of thought is life, and God is that actuality; and God's essential actuality is life most good and eternal. We say therefore that God is a living being, eternal, most good, so that life and duration continuous and eternal belong to God; for this is God."[^65]

With Aristotle, the rational demands of the Prime Mover converge toward a Pure Act of infinite simplicity, in which the intellect, the act of understanding, and the object understood are identical, such that it can truly be said that God is His very act of being. He is Truth, Love, and Life.

##### c) Notes on the "Hellenisation" of Christianity

While fully conscious of the radical novelty of Christian doctrine concerning God, the Holy Fathers—and the Apologists in particular—are distinguished by having reflected upon the mystery of God with the help of insights contributed by Hellenic thought. This attitude—openness to every ray of truth that might enlighten human reason—became characteristic of the Church across two millennia. In the historical trajectory of Christian thought, "the two greatest syntheses of classical thought were established by Saint Augustine and Saint Thomas Aquinas: the former achieved the boldest assimilation of Neoplatonic transcendence through his theology of the Word, the source and receptacle of those forms and essences which Platonism hypostasised; the latter assimilated Aristotelian realism, elaborating a conception of creatures that made possible, within man, the flourishing of Grace and the virtues in harmony with human freedom."[^66]

Martin Luther rejected the use of reason—especially Greek philosophy—in theological investigation. This rejection stemmed not only from his nominalist training, but was intimately bound to his doctrine of the effects of original sin upon human nature. If sin has corrupted man intrinsically, it must also have blinded his reason with respect to truths necessary for salvation.[^67] Rooted in this perspective, there arose between 1880 and 1890 the intense controversy concerning the so-called "hellenisation of Christianity"—namely, the debate as to whether the use of Greek philosophy by early Christian theologians (especially the Apologists) constituted a betrayal of the radical novelty of the Gospel message.

Among the foremost exponents of the initial phase of this debate were Adolf von Harnack and Friedrich Loofs, who sought to depict Christian faith as antithetical to all philosophical thought. Consequently, they maintained that the earliest Christian theologians had confused the distinct planes of reason and faith—which they regarded not merely as distinct, but as fundamentally incompatible.[^68] Thus the Apologists—and Saint Justin in particular—were alleged to have so thoroughly rationalised the concept of God through their reliance on Greek philosophy that they turned Christianity into a deistic religion for the cultivated world.[^69] This would have amounted to a Hellenisation that stripped Christianity of its essential originality, representing nothing less than an adulteration of the faith.

This controversy, rooted in Reformation prejudices concerning reason's inability to attain God—and hence against the legitimacy of metaphysics in theology—touched upon the fidelity of early Christian thinkers to the apostolic message. It prompted an extensive literature and a nuanced reassessment of earlier positions.[^70] This is evident, for example, in Wolfhart Pannenberg's position.[^71] According to Pannenberg, the presence of philosophical elements in early Christian theology concerning God, as exemplified by the Apologists, deserves a far more positive assessment than that accorded by Ritschl and Harnack, who viewed the process far too externally.[^72] The Apologists utilised Greek philosophy for authentic theological reasons rather than mere cultural accommodation, because "the universalist claim of the God of Israel could only acquire compelling force for all humanity if the Jewish and later Christian mission presented Him as the true God sought by philosophy. The claim of the God of Israel to be the only God relevant to all men provides the theological justification for Christian faith having to address the philosophical question regarding the true nature of God, and having to speak of it and answer it today (...) The Apologists were the first who, on the basis of their theories of the *Logos*, began to assimilate philosophical theology on a large scale into Christian thought."[^73]

This theological assimilation of the philosophical concept of God entailed, according to Pannenberg, exploding the philosophical concept itself from within: "Christian theology could only establish a link with the philosophical idea of God by simultaneously shattering it. On the one hand, it had to maintain the philosophical inquiry concerning the true God, bringing this inquiry to authentic fulfilment (...) On the other hand, Christian theology could only execute this connection with the philosophical concept of God by undertaking a transformation of this concept reaching down to its very roots."[^74]

The critique levelled against Pannenberg's position focuses precisely on the assertion that the philosophical concept of God must be "shattered", viewing the philosophical and Christian concepts as fundamentally incompatible. As Cornelia J. de Vogel writes: "According to Pannenberg, theology must shatter the philosophical notion of God—not complete or transcend it, but transform it radically (...) Two things must be observed: (1) For Pannenberg, the philosophical concept of God and the Christian concept are two complete, self-contained ideas that mutually exclude each other; (2) The Christian idea of God must appropriate the philosophical idea, but must do so through a critical process of transformation dominated by biblical testimony."[^75]

These nuances are of decisive importance when assessing the theological validity of the Apologists' achievement or the manner in which theology ought to engage in dialogue with contemporary culture. It seems fair to conclude that one must go a step further: Pannenberg is correct regarding universality and the legitimacy—indeed, the duty—of the theologian to employ philosophical reasoning. "What Pannenberg failed to see is that it is formally possible for Greek philosophical thought to add something of positive value to concepts which, though implicit within the Jewish spiritual horizon, had not found explicit formulation there. In such cases, a concept conceived with greater clarity and precision could enrich and deepen the Jewish conception. This is what occurred in several significant respects."[^76]

The Apologists did not seek to explode classical philosophical thought; they recognised that the truth God revealed concerning Himself is not incompatible with the genuine insights which, though mingled with errors, human beings had attained when contemplating creation with philosophical discernment. An open attitude toward philosophy is entirely legitimate, as expressed in these words:

> "I do not think that the introduction of Hellenic rationalism to set forth and explain the facts of Christian history was illegitimate. Finite minds can never adequately express the Infinite. Yet human reason is a valid instrument for unfolding what is implicit in human experience. The rational method has nothing specifically Hellenic about it, still less anything pagan, save for the fact that the Greeks enjoyed the providential privilege of its discovery and development. In itself it is part of the endowment with which human nature was furnished by God, the Creator of humanity. From this point of view, Harnack's famous attack upon Christian rationalism as a process of secularisation appears as an invitation to nourish illiberal prejudices. There are always dangers in thinking. Thinking that violates its premises and fails to explain the data upon which it rests will inevitably lead to error. Theology, like any other science, can show examples of such outcomes; but far worse are the perils of refusing to think."[^77]

In any case, it should not be forgotten that those who attempted an illegitimate Hellenisation of Christian faith were not the Apologists, but the Arians. They "borrowed from Hellenism, more or less openly, the idea of a subordinate god (*deuteros theos*) or intermediary, and even that of a demiurge (...) The Arian heresy demonstrates clearly what the dogma of the divinity of Christ would look like had it originated in philosophical Hellenism rather than divine Revelation. At the Council of Nicaea in 325, the Church defined that the Son is consubstantial with the Father (*homoousios*), thereby rejecting the Arian compromise with Hellenism, while profoundly modifying the Greek metaphysical framework, particularly that of the Platonists and Neoplatonists."[^78]

#### The Apostolic Fathers

The era of the Apostolic Fathers encompasses those Christian writers of the first and second centuries whose teaching may be regarded as a direct echo of the preaching of the Apostles, some of whom they knew personally. The writings of this period are pastoral in nature, aiming simply to expound Christian doctrine to those who were already believers; many of them are epistles. They are closely linked to the New Testament writings, particularly the Pauline epistles and the Johannine corpus, and exhibit a pronounced Jewish-Christian influence. To this period belong Saint Clement of Rome († c. 101), Saint Ignatius of Antioch († 107), Saint Polycarp of Smyrna († 156), the *Epistle of Barnabas* (96/98), the *Shepherd of Hermas* (140/155), the *Didache* (90/100), and the *Epistle to Diognetus* (second century). The Apostolic Fathers sought neither to construct an apology for Christian faith nor to offer a scientific exposition of it. Their writings contain no philosophical speculation concerning God's existence, nature, or attributes; they simply synthesize the biblical doctrine of God as Creator and Father of all things. Their fundamental character is that of witness, and their writings breathe an ardent longing for the physical presence of the Lord.

The monotheistic faith upon which Israel's religion was founded is everywhere present in the Apostolic Fathers, who were acutely conscious that the dividing line between the Church and paganism lay precisely here: in the confession of one only God, Creator of all reality.[^79] This was an understanding they received exclusively from Sacred Scripture and apostolic preaching, remaining largely untouched by contemporary philosophy, of which they seem almost entirely unaware. Perhaps the only passage echoing the philosophy of the time—specifically Stoicism—is found in Saint Clement of Rome's reflections on the beauty and harmony of the cosmos.[^80]

Doctrine concerning the one God, Father and Creator, constitutes the bedrock and undisputed premise of the Apostolic Fathers' teaching. This doctrine of divine uniqueness, which the New Testament sets forth in continuity with the Old, was the bulwark protecting them from pagan polytheism, Gnostic emanationism, and Marcionite dualism.[^81] For the Apostolic Fathers, the affirmation of the divinity of Jesus Christ entailed neither a denial of God's uniqueness nor a dilution of Old Testament monotheism.

This biblical concept of God appears explicitly across all these authors. In the *Shepherd of Hermas*, for example, it is declared fundamental to Christian life to "believe that God is One, who created all things and set them in order, bringing them out of non-existence into existence."[^82] This is the God who "by His invisible power and great wisdom created the universe, and clothed His creation with beauty, and established the heavens by His Word, and founded the earth upon the waters."[^83] The same is found in the *Didache*[^84] and the *Epistle of Barnabas*.[^85] Saint Clement of Rome writes to the Corinthians: "Let us consider how near He is, and that none of our thoughts or discourses escapes Him."[^86] This God is described as supremely Wise, Holy, and True,[^87] the supreme Judge who rewards the righteous and punishes sinners without partiality.[^88]

Of the patristic texts concerning God from this period, two are especially noteworthy: the first from Saint Clement of Rome, and the second from Saint Polycarp of Smyrna, already examined. Both texts are admirable confessions of faith in one only God, Creator of heaven and earth. Saint Clement focuses upon cosmic beauty, observing that this beauty stems from cosmic harmony, and this harmony from the divine decree that each element remain in its appointed place. Clement uses this reflection to urge the factious Christians of Corinth to obey their bishop:

> "Let us look steadfastly to the Father and Creator of the universe, and cleave to His magnificent and surpassing gifts of peace and benefits. Let us contemplate Him with our mind, and look with the eyes of the soul upon His patient purpose; let us consider how gentle He is toward all His creation. The heavens, revolving under His governance, are subject to Him in peace. Day and night perform the course appointed by Him, in no wise hindering each other (...) All these the great Creator and Master of the universe ordained to exist in peace and concord, doing good to all things, and especially to us who have fled for refuge to His mercies through our Lord Jesus Christ, to whom be glory and majesty forever and ever. Amen."[^89]

The martyrdom of Polycarp took place around 156.[^90] We have already noted the Trinitarian confession of faith in his prayer, rich with scriptural echoes. The martyr addresses the Lord, God Almighty, *Theos pantokrator*—a solemn formula frequent in the Apocalypse (cf. Rev 1:8; 4:8; 11:17; 15:3; 16:7; 21:22). This Almighty God is the Father of Jesus Christ, a traditional phrase in early Christianity (cf. Rom 15:6; 2 Cor 1:3; 11:31; Eph 1:3; Col 1:3; 1 Pet 1:3).[^91]

The Apostolic Fathers engage in no abstract speculation concerning the Trinitarian mystery, confining themselves to witnessing to apostolic preaching. This Trinitarian testimony is framed within a theocentric perspective of salvation history: an initiative of the Father, accomplished through the Son in the Spirit. Particularly significant are the letters of Saint Clement and Saint Ignatius, deeply shaped by the liturgy.[^92] For Clement, Jesus Christ is the Lord, who fully reveals the Father as "the radiance of His majesty", being begotten of the Father: "concerning His Son the Lord said thus: *Thou art my Son, today have I begotten thee* (cf. Ps 2:7)."[^93] We have one God, one Christ, and one Spirit of grace poured out upon us, making us partakers in the inner life of the Father and the Son in the Holy Spirit.[^94]

Similar expressions abound in Saint Ignatius of Antioch. The affirmation of the equal dignity of the three Persons is set within a soteriological context. Jesus Christ is the Son of the Father,[^95] the eternal Word proceeding from the abyssal silence of God.[^96] It has been rightly observed that Ignatius reflects Johannine theology concerning the *Logos*: "Jesus Christ, our God, being in the Father, was made manifest";[^97] "Christ does nothing without the Father, for He is one with Him"; "There is one God, who has manifested Himself through Jesus Christ His Son, who is His Word proceeding from silence, who in all things pleased Him that sent Him."[^98]

In Clement and Ignatius, the Holy Spirit is mentioned in His relationship to the Church. For Clement, the Church is the sign of the Father's love for the Son in the Holy Spirit. The Holy Spirit is present in the Church precisely to keep her united to the Lord and urge her to proclaim the kingdom of the Father.[^99] "You are stones of the temple of the Father," writes Saint Ignatius, "prepared for the building of God the Father, hoisted up on high by the crane of Jesus Christ, which is the cross, using the Holy Spirit as a rope."[^100] Ecclesial unity is the work of the Spirit, for "the Spirit who is from God gives cohesion to ecclesial unity by binding the brethren in love to one another and to the bishop, the representative of Jesus."[^101] The Church is thus the privileged locus for the contemplation of the Apostolic Fathers' Trinitarian theology, appearing in many texts as "loved by the Father, built by the Son, and vivified by the Spirit."[^102]

#### The Apologists

With the Apologists, the influence of philosophical thought becomes prominent. This is evident in Aristides (c. 140), Saint Justin († 163/167), and Athenagoras (c. 177). Apologetic literature addressed three principal audiences: pagans, to whom they sought to demonstrate not only that Christians were not atheists, but that only the Christian God—the Father of Jesus Christ—could be the true God, polytheism being intrinsically absurd; Jews, to whom they sought to show that the true God—the God of Israel—is the Father of Jesus Christ, and that this does not contradict Old Testament monotheism; and finally, the diverse dualistic movements, Gnostic sects, and Docetists, against whom they argued that there are not two gods (one good and one evil, one of the Old Testament and one of the New), and that the world originated not by emanation, but by divine creation. The earliest theological reflections on the Trinity, especially regarding the divinity of the Son, emerged with the Apologists.

The doctrine of the one God, Father and Creator, has always constituted the undisputed foundation of the Church's faith. This doctrine of divine uniqueness, presented in continuity with the Old Testament, was the shield defending the Apologists against pagan polytheism, Gnostic emanationism, and Marcionite dualism.[^103] In every arena, it was urgent to demonstrate the speculative coherence of Christian monotheism, affirming that there is only one God, Creator of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible. The doctrine of the uniqueness of God is explicitly declared in the earliest Christian Creeds: *We believe in one God, Maker of heaven and earth*. The Apologists also demonstrated the existence of God, emphasising His spirituality and transcendence.

A fine example is the *Apology* of Aristides, written around 140. Its opening chapters constitute a clear declaration of principles concerning God: contemplation of cosmic motion, order, and beauty leads to the existence of a supreme Being, Sovereign and Lord of all, who created everything for man. He is uncreated, without beginning or end, immutable, perfect, omnipotent, omniscient, unnameable, incorporeal, without gender; the heavens cannot contain Him, yet He contains all things, for all reality exists through Him.[^104]

Similar arguments appear in Tatian's *Address to the Greeks* (c. 165). Alongside traditional doctrine, Tatian emphasizes that God has no flesh (*asarkos*) and no body (*asomatos*), ridiculing pagan belief in warring, lustful deities subject to human passions.[^105] Athenagoras, in his *Supplication for the Christians* (c. 177), enumerates the divine attributes to demonstrate that Christians are not atheists simply because they distinguish God from matter and proclaim the infinite gulf separating idols from the uncreated, eternal God.[^106]

Among the Apologists, Saint Justin stands out for his intellectual vigour. Martyred in Rome around 165, he founded a philosophical-theological school there. Of his works, two *Apologies* (perhaps originally one) and the *Dialogue with Trypho the Jew* have survived. Justin was the first who deliberately sought to establish a relationship between the Christian message and Hellenic philosophy. His principal contribution lay in demonstrating that the supreme being conceived by philosophers corresponds to the God of the Christian message—Creator of the universe and author of salvation history.

In his effort to persuade the Hellenic world, Justin did not hesitate to identify himself unequivocally as a Christian above all else:

> "I confess that I both boast and with all my strength strive to be found a Christian; not because the teachings of Plato are different from those of Christ, but because they are not in all respects equal, as neither are those of the others, Stoics, and poets, and historians."[^107]

In his *First Apology* (c. 150/155), Justin refutes the charge of atheism by declaring faith in "the true God, the Father of righteousness and temperance and the other virtues, who is free from all impurity."[^108] For Justin, God is Sovereign, Father, Lord of all, Judge, whose knowledge extends to past, present, and future, searching the human heart: one true God, immutable, eternal, unbegotten, omnipotent, impassible.

In his *Second Apology*, Justin accentuates divine ineffability:

> "To the Father of all, who is unbegotten, there is no name given. For by whatever name He be called, He has as His elder the person who gives Him the name. But these words, Father, and God, and Creator, and Lord, and Master, are not names, but appellations derived from His good deeds and functions."[^109]

While emphasizing divine ineffability in the *Second Apology*, Justin presents in the *Dialogue with Trypho* a vision of God drawn directly from Sacred Scripture, observing that "philosophy does not of itself yield the knowledge of God; that knowledge must be sought from those who spoke under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit: the prophets and, above all, Jesus Christ, the Son of God."[^110] Justin insists that he worships the same God in whom Trypho believes, adding that this God is the Father of Jesus Christ. Justin deduces from the baptismal mandate that divine attributes belong to the Son, yet he reserves the absolute title "God" (*ho Theos*) primarily for the Father.[^111] Some of his expressions carry a subordinating nuance, though this does not justify classing Justin among strict subordinationists.[^112]

Central to Justin's theology is his doctrine of the *Logos*. According to him, the *Logos* proceeds from the Father's will.[^113] Influenced by Platonism, Justin sought to defend divine transcendence while distinguishing Father and Son as mediator between God and the world; he suggested that the Old Testament theophanies should be attributed exclusively to the Son, while the Father remains exalted and unapproachable.[^114]

The Apologists, and Justin in particular, distinguish between *logos endiathetos* and *logos prophorikos*—the immanent word conceived within the mind and the word uttered externally. This distinction derived from the Stoic analysis of human thought, wherein a concept is first conceived internally and subsequently expressed in spoken language. In applying this distinction to the *Logos* of God, they occasionally seem to make the distinct subsistence of the Word depend upon His being "uttered" for the purpose of creation.

Hence, in assessing Justin's thought, the central question is when the Word was begotten. For Justin, the Word was begotten before creation. But was this generation eternal, or was it oriented toward creation? In some passages, generation appears to coincide with His role as *logos prophorikos*, uttered with a view to creating the world: "His Son, who alone is properly called Son, the Word, who also was with Him and was begotten before the works, when at first He created and arranged all things through Him..."[^115] We are at the very dawn of Trinitarian theology, where terminology had not yet been crystallized, nor all theological consequences foreseen. As Scheffczyk points out, this should not be taken as making the Word's generation contingent upon cosmic creation: "the usual phrase concerning the generation of the *Logos* at creation (*2 Apol.* 6) must be understood in the sense of His outward revelation."[^116] Justin says little about the Holy Spirit, mentioning Him chiefly in connection with the inspiration of the prophets.[^117]

A similar perspective appears in Tatian († c. 170), who, while affirming that the *Logos* is God, calls Him the first-begotten work of the Father,[^118] employing the distinction between *logos endiathetos* and *logos prophorikos* more rigidly than Justin.[^119] Athenagoras († c. 190) also exhibits early Trinitarian reflection:

> "We have sufficiently demonstrated that we are not atheists, since we acknowledge one God (...) Who, then, would not be astonished to hear men called atheists who speak of God the Father, and of God the Son, and of the Holy Spirit, and who declare both their power in unity and their distinction in order?"[^120]

Saint Theophilus of Antioch († c. 182), like other early writers, begins with the affirmation of God's existence and uniqueness as Creator.[^121] Theophilus is the first extant author to use the noun "Trinity" (*Trias*) to designate "God, His Word, and His Wisdom", although it cannot be said with certainty that he conceived these as three divine Persons in the strict later sense.[^122] The term would later be used by Origen in a strictly Trinitarian sense. Theophilus likewise employs the distinction between *logos prophorikos* and *logos endiathetos*.[^123]

The Apologists were influenced by Middle Platonism and sought to express the Christian mystery within its framework. This approach carried risks—notably that of conceiving the second and third Persons as subordinate to the first—yet they successfully affirmed the existence of three divine Persons and formulated an initial theology of the *Logos* as a divine Person. They did not merely assert the distinction between Father and Son, but identified its foundation: generation. Father and Son are distinguished precisely in that the Father begets and the Son is begotten.[^124] They boldly highlighted the radical novelty of the Christian revelation of God, confronting the intellectual difficulty of three Persons within the strict unity of the Godhead. Nevertheless, they did not arrive at a positive conceptual formulation of this interior divine unity;[^125] that task would be accomplished by the Church in her subsequent defence of orthodoxy against heresy.

#### Saint Irenaeus and the Trinitarian Structure of Salvation

Saint Irenaeus of Lyons is the pre-eminent theologian of the second century. Born in Smyrna between 140 and 160, he sat at the feet of Saint Polycarp. By 177 he was a presbyter in Lyons in Gaul. Following the martyrdom of Bishop Pothinus, Irenaeus succeeded him as bishop, dying at the beginning of the third century. Irenaeus was not an apologist, yet he was compelled to refute Gnostic theology. His famous treatise *Adversus haereses* establishes him as a master of theological controversy. Gnosticism conceived God as a supreme source emanating subordinate *aeons* in descending ontological degradation, some sects postulating two principles (one good, one evil). Gnostics lacked the concept of creation *ex nihilo*, viewing matter as intrinsically evil.[^126]

Against this, Irenaeus vigorously asserts the absolute unity of God, denying the existence of eternal matter as the origin of evil. God is one; there is no inferior demiurge who fashioned this world. This Creator God is holy and just, the author of both the Old Testament and the New, revealed in Jesus Christ. The dominant theme of *Adversus haereses* is the unity of God—the first principle of nature and the ultimate goal of salvation history.[^127] Irenaeus affirms that God is the transcendent, omnipotent source of all being and goodness,[^128] absolutely simple and devoid of parts,[^129] whose universal providence and knowledge embrace past, present, and future.[^130] He notes that God had no need to create man, since from all eternity Father and Word glorified each other.[^131]

For Irenaeus, the affirmation of divine uniqueness is fundamental:

> "It is proper, then, that I should begin with the first and most important head, that is, God the Creator, who made the heaven and the earth, and all things that are therein (...) and that I should demonstrate that there is nothing either above Him or after Him (...) He made all things by His own free and sovereign will, without being prompted by anyone; for He is the only God, the only Lord, the only Creator, the only Father, the only Ruler of all, who gives existence to all reality. How could there be another fullness, another principle, another power, or another god above Him? For God must contain all things in His infinitude, while He Himself cannot be contained by anything. If there were anything outside Him, He would no longer be the fullness of all things, nor would He contain all."[^132]

The confession of the one God is the foundation of all Christian doctrine:

> "This is the rule of our faith, the foundation of the building, and that which gives firmness to our conduct: God the Father, uncreated, uncontained, invisible, one God, creator of the universe; this is the first article of our faith."[^133]

This one God infinitely transcends human comprehension:

> "He who knows the Scriptures, and has been taught by the truth, knows that God is not like men, and that His thoughts are not like human thoughts. For the Father of all is far above human passions and affections. He is simple, without composition or parts, wholly uniform and self-consistent, being all mind, all spirit, all perception, all thought, all reason, all hearing, all seeing, all light, and the total source of all good. This is what the devout say concerning God. And yet God is above all these things, and is, for this reason, ineffable. He may properly and truly be called a Mind that comprehends all, yet He is not comparable to the human mind. Likewise He may properly be called Light, yet He is nothing like the light known to us. And so with all else: the Father of all is in no way comparable to human littleness. All these attributes are spoken of Him in so far as they manifest His love; yet we understand that His greatness transcends them all."[^134]

Within this one God, there is a clear distinction between Father, Son, and Spirit, known only through revelation. Irenaeus emphasizes more than his predecessors that the generation of the Word is eternal, discarding the philosophical speculations of the Apologists. He abandons the distinction between *logos endiathetos* and *logos prophorikos*, returning to the earliest Trinitarian tradition,[^135] which speaks of the Trinity as revealed in salvation history: God the Father creates the world through His Word; the Word sends the Holy Spirit. Irenaeus was not a speculative philosopher, but a pastor defending the apostolic faith against Gnosticism.[^136]

For Irenaeus, a Christian is primarily one who believes in the Trinity:

> "Those who belong to the Church follow one single voice that spans the entire world. It is a secure tradition handed down from the Apostles, causing us to receive one and the same faith, all believing in one and the same God the Father, all believing in the same economy of the Incarnation of the Son of God, all acknowledging the same gift of the Spirit."[^137]

The affirmation of the three divine Persons is consistently made in connection with the economy of salvation, rather than in the speculative philosophical context of the Apologists.[^138]

Alongside this economic perspective, Irenaeus affirms that the Christian God—who is Triune—is the God of the Old Testament, who eternally possessed His Word and Wisdom.[^139] The Word is as intimate to the Father as thought is to the thinker; He is in the Father and has the Father within Himself.[^140] The Son is co-eternal with the Father.[^141] The Holy Spirit is likewise eternal: "God always has with Him His Word and Wisdom, the Son and the Spirit."[^142]

Irenaeus frequently employs a celebrated image: the Son and the Spirit are the "two hands" of the Father. The Father designs His works, the Son accomplishes them, and the Holy Spirit perfects them.[^143] Divine action originates in the Father, proceeds through the Son, and attains completion in the Holy Spirit. What occurs in salvation history mirrors the inner Trinitarian life: the Father, as source of the Trinity, acts in salvation through the Son in the Spirit.[^144]

Here is expressed what later theology would consistently emphasize: the Father is not only *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*, but also the source of salvation history. For Irenaeus, "the Father is He in whom all things have their subsistence and to whom all initiative must be traced; the Son is shown to be the image and power through whom the Father accomplishes all things; and the Spirit is the principle of order who sustains all things in Himself."[^145]

#### Two Great Third-Century Thinkers: Clement of Alexandria and Origen

Theological thought attained an extraordinary development in the East around the year 200, inaugurating a new epoch characterised by a systematic and harmonious presentation of Christian doctrine. This intellectual flourishing reached its zenith in the third and fourth centuries through the emergence of "theological schools"—"academies of scholars that may be regarded as the cradle of theological science."[^146] These schools formed distinct currents of thought with well-defined approaches to biblical exegesis and dogmatic emphasis, the most prominent being Alexandria, Antioch, and Caesarea Maritima.[^147] Of their numerous teachers we shall focus on two who taught in the Catechetical School of Alexandria: Clement of Alexandria and Origen. The school of Alexandria or *didaskaleion*[^148] became the pre-eminent intellectual centre of the Christian East.[^149] Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–c. 215)[^150] understands, as Tertullian would subsequently do, that man is a religious being and that the soul is *naturaliter christiana*.[^151] Clement points to three paths for the rational discovery of God: contemplation of cosmic beauty and harmony,[^152] the activity of contingent beings,[^153] and the human soul, which as the image of God reflects the divine.[^154] According to Clement, while God's existence is accessible to human reason, His nature transcends created intellect, so that the divine intimate life is accessible to man only through revelation.[^155]

In Clement, the unity of God is vigorously maintained, especially against Marcionite dualism. Marcion held that infinite goodness and infinite justice were mutually exclusive, concluding that the God of the Old Testament (wrathful and severe) was distinct from and opposed to the God of the New Testament (merciful and loving).[^156] Clement refutes this error by demonstrating that goodness and justice belong intrinsically to one and the same divine Person;[^157] there is absolute harmony between the Law and the Gospel, for the Creator of man is He who wills his salvation.[^158]

Clement also defends divine uniqueness against pagan polytheism using rational proofs for God's existence: reason demonstrates the necessity of a supreme, infinite Being, and an infinite Being must necessarily be unique.[^159] Here is a classic statement of Clement's apophatic theology:

> "There is nothing more difficult than to speak of God. For the principle of any thing is hard to find out; how much more the first and most ancient principle of all, which is the cause of existence and endurance to all other things? How can that be expressed which is neither genus, nor difference, nor species, nor individual, nor number, nor accident, nor subject of accident? (...) Nor can any parts be predicated of Him, for being one, He is indivisible. He is infinite, not because we conceive Him as immense, but because in Himself He has no dimensions and no limit. He has no form, nor can He be named. If we name Him the One, or the Good, or Mind, or Absolute Being (*auto to on*), or Father, or God, or Creator, or Lord, we do not utter His proper name; rather, in our deficiency, we employ these appellations so that the mind, having something upon which to rest, may not go astray. None of these terms taken singly expresses God; taken together, they signify the power of the Omnipotent (...) What remains, then, but to know the unknown God by divine grace and through the mediation of the Word who is with Him?"[^160]

Clement's argument proceeds through three steps: first, negative theology removes from God whatever is unworthy of Him ("He is neither genus, nor difference, nor species..."); second, divine transcendence is affirmed ("If we call Him the One, the Good, Mind, Absolute Being, we do not use proper names..."); third, divine incomprehensibility and ineffability are proclaimed ("What remains, then, but to know the unknown God by divine grace through the Word?").[^161] Divine names are appellations preventing thought from wandering; unable to grasp God in His essence, we speak of Him through approximations. References to the Holy Spirit as distinct from the Father are infrequent in Clement, the Spirit being depicted primarily as the divine *dynamis* operating in history.[^162]

Alexandrian theology reached its apogee with Origen, Clement's successor and one of the most prolific theologians in Church history.[^163] Origen develops a doctrine of God following Clement's trajectory while deepening many of its aspects. Like Clement, Origen regards God's existence as accessible to natural reason and part of the universal patrimony of mankind. Contemplation of the visible cosmos and the interior witness of the soul are the two avenues to knowledge of God.[^164] The cosmological argument is Pauline (cf. Rom 1:20); the psychological argument emphasizes the moral law written in the human heart and accountability before the supreme Judge.[^165]

Divine uniqueness is for Origen inseparable from the very concept of God, and therefore accessible to reason.[^166] It is the primordial truth concerning the Godhead:

> "There is one God, Creator and Arranger of all things, who brought the universe into being out of nothing, the God of all the righteous from the beginning of the world (...) a God both just and good, the Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, author of the Law and the Prophets, of the Gospel and the Apostles, God of both the Old and New Testaments."[^167]

Origen addresses the harmonization of divine goodness and justice: far from being opposed, they are inseparable. Goodness is the foundational attribute in which all others unite: God is substantial goodness, the fountain of all goodness, in whom being and goodness are identical.[^168] Evil originates not in God, but in the misuse of creaturely freedom.[^169]

Origen places great emphasis upon the spirituality of God. In *De Principiis*, he refutes anthropomorphic conceptions: biblical bodily images are pedagogical metaphors, not ontological descriptions. Corporeality would render God composite, divisible, and corruptible—attributes repugnant to reason.[^170]

Being incorporeal, God is invisible: "We affirm that God is invisible because He is without body."[^171] Origen distinguishes between bodily vision and intellectual knowledge: God cannot be seen with bodily eyes, but can be known by the intellect, albeit imperfectly, because He is incomprehensible. The soul must rise from the beauty of creatures to the beauty of the Creator.[^172]

Wholly spiritual, God is supremely independent, immutable, impassible, and eternal. Transcending all things, He is present to all without compromise to His transcendence—not as a Stoic world-soul, but as an omnipotent power sustaining creation.[^173]

Origen vigorously defends divine omniscience and providence, addressing the coexistence of divine foreknowledge and human freedom. Celsus had argued that prophecy (such as Peter's denial or Judas's betrayal) eliminated genuine freedom. Origen's classic answer—adopted by subsequent tradition—is that events do not happen because they were prophesied; rather, they were prophesied because they were going to happen:

> "Celsus thinks that a predicted event occurs precisely because it was predicted by virtue of foreknowledge. But we do not accept this; rather, we say that the prophet is not the cause of the future event because he foretold it; it is rather the future event, which would have occurred whether foretold or not, that causes the prophet to predict it."[^174]

God is *Pantokrator* (Ruler of all) and *Pantodynamos* (Omnipotent). Origen considers whether omnipotence implies that God can do evil. Celsus had caricatured divine omnipotence as arbitrary power. Origen clarifies:

> "God can do all things, so long as what He can do does not contradict His being God, His goodness, or His wisdom. But Celsus, demonstrating that he has not understood in what sense God is said to be omnipotent, says: 'He will not will anything unjust', admitting that God can do injustice, but does not will it. We, by contrast, maintain that just as what is sweet cannot produce bitterness, and what illuminates cannot darken, so God cannot commit iniquity; the power to do injustice is repugnant to His divinity and to all the power proper to His godhead."[^175]

There is an intrinsic metaphysical impossibility of God doing evil. Denying God the ability to sin is not a limitation of omnipotence, but the preservation of its perfection.[^176]

In Trinitarian doctrine, Origen adopts an economic framework akin to Irenaeus: the Father is the unoriginate source; the *Logos* is the mediator; the Holy Spirit is present wherever there is holiness.[^177] Against Modalism, Origen asserts the distinct subsistence (*hypostasis*) of each Person;[^178] against Gnosticism, he affirms that the Son proceeds not by division or material emanation, but by an immaterial act of the intellect.[^179] Since everything in God is eternal, this generation is an *aeterna ac sempiterna generatio*:[^180] the Son has no beginning, and there never was when He was not (*non est autem quando non fuerat*).[^181] This excludes radical Arian subordinationism, pointing toward equality of substance (*homoousia*).[^182] Nevertheless, Origen occasionally calls the *Logos* a *deuteros theos* (a second God),[^183] and passages with a subordinating tendency appear alongside those asserting essential equality.

This ambiguity stems from Origen's conception of the *Logos* as mediator. The Word is the image, wisdom, and revelation of the Father, containing the divine archetypes of creation.[^184] The Word stands in a twofold relation: toward the Father (as Wisdom and Word) and toward creation (as mediator and revealer). Because an image depends upon its archetype, Origen reserves the title *alethinos autotheos* (true God of Himself) for the Father alone, viewing the divinity of the Word as derived.[^185] In this respect, the Father transcends the Word, just as the Word transcends creatures.

Yet Origen firmly upholds the distinct subsistence and divinity of the Holy Spirit:

> "All this shows us the great authority and dignity belonging to the Holy Spirit as a substantial being, to the extent that the baptism of salvation cannot take place except through the supreme authority of the Trinity, through the invocation of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, so that the name of the Holy Spirit is joined to the Unbegotten Father and the Only-Begotten Son."[^186]

The *Logos* reveals the Father, but the Son is known only through the Holy Spirit. The Father works in us through Christ, and we return to the Father in the Spirit through Christ. This soteriological orientation ultimately prevents Origen from viewing the Trinity as an ontological hierarchy of three unequal beings,[^187] for salvation is the indivisible work of all three divine Persons.
