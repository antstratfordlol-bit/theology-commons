## Chapter 7: From Nicaea to Constantinople {#mateo-seco-trino.7}

In the fourth century, a profound development of Trinitarian doctrine took place in both East and West. The opening years of the century were marked by the rise of the Arian heresy: around the year 318 the first clash occurred between Arius and his bishop, Alexander of Alexandria († 328), and around 320 the *Thalia* was published. In the year 325 the Council of Nicaea was held; there the full divinity of the Son was affirmed, Arius was condemned, and the Creed was proclaimed which would later be completed at the First Council of Constantinople (AD 381). The Council of Nicaea was followed by a period of fierce struggles over the confession of faith professed in its Creed—specifically, concerning the *homoousios*, which was its most characteristic expression. Alongside the reaffirmation of the perfect divinity of the Son, there emerged an explicit rejection of the divinity of the Holy Spirit. The defence of the Trinitarian faith culminated at the First Council of Constantinople with the definition of the divinity of the Holy Spirit and the condemnation of the Pneumatomachians and Macedonians.

We begin this chapter with an examination of what took place at Nicaea and, more especially, an analysis of the Creed proclaimed there with all solemnity. We then proceed to study the great Greek theologians—Saint Athanasius, Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nyssa, and Saint Gregory of Nazianzus—who span the period between Nicaea and Constantinople; and we conclude with an analysis of the questions raised by that Council.

### The First Council of Nicaea and the Consubstantiality of the Father and the Son

As has already been seen, Arius radicalised the subordinationist tendencies present in earlier Origenist tradition to the point of denying the divinity of the Son, reducing him to a *poiēma*, a mere creature. Arius flatly denied the possibility of an authentic generation in God and, consequently, had no hesitation in affirming that the Sonship of the Word does not come about by generation, but by grace. What is more, the existence of the Word would be contingent upon the creation of the world, since God was said to have created the Word in order to create the world through him.

The Council of Nicaea was convoked by the Emperor Constantine, who was concerned that theological controversies might undermine the unity of the Empire. The Emperor also assumed the honorary presidency. Around three hundred bishops attended the Council.[^1] Western representation was rather sparse: four bishops—including Hosius of Cordoba, who seems to have represented the See of Rome—and two presbyters. Arius, who was only a presbyter, appeared several times—according to Rufinus—since his doctrine formed the central subject of the debates. The Council also dealt with the date of Easter and certain disciplinary matters.

The key document of the Council is the Creed (*Symbolum*), in which the perfect Sonship and divinity of the Word—that is to say, his consubstantiality with the Father—is explicitly professed. It is the Creed that was used in the Church of Caesarea, to which only a few clauses were added to make it more apt for refuting Arianism.[^2] In an appendix added to the Creed, likewise of a dogmatic character, the principal Arian propositions are rejected as they had been gathered in the previously cited Letter of Alexander of Alexandria to the bishops. The Nicene Creed is not, therefore, a formula drafted *de novo*; indeed, the specifically Nicene additions are very few. Yet they are truly significant and clarify the sense in which the Fathers understood the statements of the Caesarean Creed.[^3] Here is the text:

> "We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of all things visible and invisible;
> 
> and in one Lord Jesus Christ, the Son of God, the Only-Begotten, begotten of the Father, that is, of the substance of the Father, God of God, Light of Light, true God of true God, begotten, not made, consubstantial (*homoousios*) with the Father, through whom all things were made, both in heaven and on earth; who for us men and for our salvation came down and was incarnate, was made man, suffered, and rose again on the third day, ascended into heaven, and shall come to judge the living and the dead;
> 
> and in the Holy Spirit.
> 
> And those who say: 'There was once when he was not', and 'Before he was begotten he was not', and 'He was made out of nothing', or who assert that the Son of God is of a different hypostasis or substance (*ousia*), or created, or mutable, or alterable, the Catholic and Apostolic Church anathematises."[^4]

A first observation is called for: the structure of the Creed is clearly Trinitarian; that is, the Creed is composed according to a schema of three cycles: the first dedicated to the Father, the second to the Son, and the third to the Holy Spirit.

The Father is confessed as the principle and source of unity in the Trinity. We find ourselves before a genetic exposition of the Godhead, which flows from the Father and pours forth into the Son and the Holy Spirit.[^5] To the Father has always been attributed the title of the only God, as equivalent to Yahweh. It thus takes up the monotheistic revelation of the Old Testament—that of the one Yahweh, who stands opposed to the multitude of gods. This 'one God' does not directly and formally designate the divine substance here, but refers to God as Person—specifically, to the Person of the Father, who is the one manifested in the Old Testament.[^6]

Then follows the cycle dedicated to the Son. In view of Arius's subterfuges to deny the perfect divinity of the Son, the Fathers of Nicaea resolved to include a gloss of supreme importance: 'that is, of the substance (*ousia*) of the Father'. The Fathers attending the Council, especially Saint Athanasius, who attended as deacon to Alexander of Alexandria, would later bear witness to the Council's intention in introducing this clause: to underline the strict natural generation of the Son by the Father. The Council wished to proclaim unmistakably that the Son is not something made by the Father, but a communication of the Father's own being by way of generation.

The explanatory clause *that is* (*hoc est*) is of the greatest doctrinal and theological weight. The Fathers of Nicaea were convinced that the additions they introduced did not go beyond the affirmation that the Son is begotten by the Father. It was not a matter of a development or evolution of previously professed doctrine, but of indicating the precise sense in which that same doctrine must be understood. The term *begotten* must therefore be taken in all its radical force. It refers to a generation in which the Father truly 'bestows' his own substance upon the Son. It is not, therefore, an adoption by grace, but a generation by nature.[^7]

Of particular theological moment is the fact that, in order to reaffirm all that was being said concerning the Sonship of the Word, a philosophical and non-biblical term was employed: *homoousios*.[^8] Precisely because the Father bestows his own substance upon the Son in begetting him, it is necessary to say that the Son possesses the very same substance as the Father. The Council's intention is patent: to reject the 'Hellenisation' of Christianity championed by Arius. And in order to reject the Arian compromise with Hellenic philosophy, it utilised a term native to Greek culture.[^9]

Furthermore, we are at the very beginnings of the employment of philosophical terminology to express the faith. This explains why the language was still fluid, and the terms had not yet acquired the precise technical sense they would attain later in the fourth century. Here *hypostasis*—etymologically, that which stands underneath—appears as equivalent to *ousia*, substance or essence, what a thing is. The distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis* would be established a few decades later by the Cappadocian Fathers. In any case, Nicaea, by stating that the Son is begotten of the *ousia* of the Father, affirms that he is begotten of the essence or substance. The Word proceeds from the essence, 'as from the very inmost depths of the Father, so to speak, and is not, like creatures, the external fruit of an act of his omnipotent will.'[^10]

Here is the *iter idearum* of the Creed in its use of *homoousios*: Jesus Christ is the Son, therefore he is begotten; he is begotten, therefore he comes forth from the very essence of the Father; he comes forth from the essence of the Father, therefore he has no essence diverse from his, since every son receives the same nature as the father.

In light of this reasoning, one can understand the intention with which the Fathers chose the other expressions to reaffirm the same doctrine: the Word is 'God of (*ex*) God, Light of (*ex*) Light'. The preposition *ex* denotes origin and the reality from which something comes. These expressions, in turn, evoke numerous biblical passages (cf. e.g. Jn 1:1–9; 5:20; 8:12; 1 Jn 1:5). Moreover, they are admirably suited to signify the procession of a spiritual being, who proceeds without the One who imparts being suffering any diminution in his substance.

The divinity of the Son is thus clearly professed, grounded precisely upon the fact that the Son proceeds from the Father by way of generation. To eliminate ambiguities of language, the Nicene Fathers insisted further, explicitly rejecting the Arian use of the term *poiēma* as an expression applicable to the Word. Thus it is excluded that the Word could be conceived as a creature.

The nerve centre of the Creed is the *homoousios*. It was a term that from the very outset troubled not only the Arians, but also the Semi-Arians. As will be seen presently, the struggle for the acceptance or rejection of Nicene doctrine was fought precisely around this word: those who defended the Nicene faith recognised each other by their acceptance of *homoousios*; those who rejected it were termed Anomoeans (who utterly denied any substantial likeness) and Homoiousians (who rejected identity of substance, but accepted similarity of substance).

At first, the term *homoousios* implied no more than the assertion that the Son possesses the same substance or essence as the Father, without determining whether this participation implies that the two substances or essences—that of the Father and that of the Son—are numerically one and the same. This was a point that would be clarified later. In the earliest debates with the Arians, the distinction between specific unity and numerical unity of essence between the Father and the Son was not raised; what was under dispute was whether the nature of the Son was created or uncreated—that is, whether the Son belongs to the sphere of the Godhead or to the sphere of created reality. Hence, speaking from a historical perspective, one cannot say that the term *homoousios* was originally employed at Nicaea to designate the numerical unity of the divine essence.

Nonetheless, in the Nicene Creed this numerical unity of the divine essence is indeed affirmed. This affirmation, so fundamental to Trinitarian doctrine, is implicit at the confluence of two assertions made at Nicaea: first, God is only one, a single substance that can neither be multiplied nor divided among diverse subjects; second, the essence of the Son is equal in all things to that of the Father with perfect identity. From these two premises it follows that the substance of the Son must be numerically identical with that of the Father, since the Son could not have received the paternal substance by multiplication or division. Consequently, either the Son possesses the same substance, even in number, as the Father, or he has not received the same substance at all.[^11]

### The Struggle over Nicaea

At first it appeared that with the affirmation of the *homoousios* the Arian crisis had been resolved; it soon became clear that far more efforts and clarifications would be necessary before the crisis could be settled. Nicaea had laid the foundations for defending in all its radicality the New Testament affirmation of Jesus' divine Sonship. It had safeguarded the Trinitarian faith against both subordinationism and modalism. Yet a series of facets of the greatest importance in Trinitarian theology had not been clarified. Thus, for instance, in introducing the *homoousios*, it had not been specified whether this consubstantiality implied not only equality of essence, but also numerical identity.[^12] Nor had it been specified whether the generation of the Word is eternal. The *homoousios* was, so to speak, left too exposed and vulnerable to equivocal interpretations.[^13]

The years following the Council of Nicaea demonstrated, on the one hand, the gravity of the Arian heresy and, on the other, how wise had been the decision to confront it with the term *homoousios*. Saint Athanasius found himself obliged to explain to the Easterners the precise meaning of the term *consubstantial* so that it would not be taken in the modalist sense in which Paul of Samosata († post 270) had employed it; Saint Hilary of Poitiers († c. 367) had to explain it to the Westerners.[^14]

The controversy that followed the Council, centred upon the acceptance, rejection, or 'reinterpretation' of the *homoousios*, may be outlined in three phases:[^15]

a) The first phase extends from Nicaea until the death of Constantine in 337. Some of those who had accepted the Nicene faith, led by Eusebius of Nicomedia, began to disavow it. Arius was rehabilitated after making an ambiguous profession of faith that touched only in passing on the crucial issue. Athanasius, who had attended the Council as deacon to Alexander, succeeded him in the See of Alexandria in 328; in the year 335 he was deposed for refusing to readmit Arius into his Church. Arius was reinstated while Athanasius was exiled. In 336 Arius died. In 337 the Emperor Constantine died.

b) The second phase spans the reign of Constantius and Constans (337–361). These were the years of the triumph of Arianism, which flourished first in the East under the patronage of Constantius. Eusebius of Nicomedia was elevated to the See of Constantinople. Upon the death of Constans in 350, Constantius sought to impose the religious unity of the Empire on an Arian foundation. Exiles of those loyal to Nicaea multiplied: Pope Liberius, Hosius, Athanasius, and Hilary of Poitiers all suffered exile.[^16] By around 360, the Arian victory appeared complete. The use of the term *substance*—and consequently that of *homoousios*—was banned, under the pretext that using such terminology to speak of the mystery of God caused scandal among the faithful because it was not found in Scripture.

c) The third phase runs from 361 to 380. It is characterised by schisms and fractures among the Arians themselves. In 361, Julian the Apostate restored freedom to the Church, allowing the exiled orthodox bishops to return to their sees. There was, therefore, a vigorous resurgence of the Nicene faith. For its part, Arianism split into three factions: it degenerated into strict, uncompromising Arianism,[^17] namely the Anomoeanism of Eunomius of Cyzicus († c. 394), who claimed that the Son bore no resemblance whatsoever to the Father; into that of the Semi-Arians or Homoiousians, who called the Son similar in substance—*homoiousios*—to the essence of the Father, led by Basil of Ancyra († post 363); and a third group, the Homoeans, who used the epithet *homoios* simply to say that the Son is similar to the Father, guided by Acacius of Caesarea († c. 365).[^18] If one adds to this the fact that theological language was not yet sufficiently clarified, and if one considers, moreover, the convoluted political situation of the Empire, it is understandable that the decades following Nicaea were thoroughly confused and fertile ground for political and ecclesiastical intrigue. Saint Basil compared the situation to the chaos of a naval battle, and the historian Socrates to the confusion of a night battle, where a man can scarcely tell friend from foe.

As an illustration of the confusion prevalent in those years, one need only cite Marcellus of Ancyra († c. 374). Initially, Marcellus was one of the staunch defenders of the Nicene faith; he attacked not only the Arians, but also the Eusebians—that is, the followers of Eusebius of Caesarea († c. 340), who leaned towards Semi-Arianism.[^19] Marcellus's point of departure was monotheism. It is necessary to begin, he argued, from the unity (*monas*), from which the triad will flow, since it is impossible to arrive at unity if one starts from three hypostases. God is an indivisible unity, not three hypostases. Of the Word we know that he was in God from the beginning as active energy, and that God is undivided. Hence, the Word is consubstantial with the Father, *homoousios*.

In what sense did Marcellus understand *homoousios*? It appears to have been in a sense very close to that of Paul of Samosata, examined earlier. The Word would proceed from the Father with a view to the creation of the world. Marcellus admitted the eternity of the Son and of the Spirit as divine powers, but did not admit that they proceeded from the Father to the extent of constituting distinct Persons. His doctrine was thus regarded as a fresh resurgence of Sabellianism.[^20] Furthermore, Marcellus added a Christological error: the union between the Word and the humanity of Christ would not be eternal, but would terminate at the Parousia, for the Word would divest himself of Jesus' humanity to return to being in God as he was before the creation of the world. Marcellus was condemned at the First Council of Constantinople. The clause in the Creed—'and of his kingdom there will be no end'—appears to be directed against him and Photinus († post 362).[^21]

From the year 362 onwards, with the celebration of the Council of Alexandria, Trinitarian terminology began to take clear shape, and the explicit defence of the divinity of the Holy Spirit was inaugurated in a powerful theological movement led by Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, and Saint Gregory of Nyssa, which would culminate in the First Council of Constantinople.

### Saint Athanasius, Defender of the Faith Proclaimed at Nicaea

The great figure of the first phase was Saint Athanasius († 373). As noted earlier, he attended the Council of Nicaea as deacon to Alexander and succeeded him as Bishop of Alexandria. Athanasius is often presented as the great adversary of Arius, but it was Alexander who truly confronted Arius directly; Athanasius carried forward Alexander's work. Athanasius had an intimate knowledge of what had transpired at Nicaea and directed his pastoral and theological efforts towards resolving the Arian crisis. He suffered five exiles and died without seeing the end of the controversy.

This crisis concerned not only the mystery of the Persons in God, but also the manner in which man's salvation takes place.[^22] For his part, Athanasius's primary theological interest was soteriological. The core of Athanasian theology is the Incarnation of the Word with a view to the divinisation (*theosis*) of man. Athanasius considered both the perfect humanity of the Word and his perfect divinity from the perspective of man's divinisation; consequently, it was from this vantage point that he developed his Trinitarian doctrine.[^23]

The Arian contentions also touched upon the nature of the divine attributes. This compelled the Fathers to address not only the 'personality' and divinity of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, but also to treat of the essence and attributes of God. In this arena, at the beginning of the fourth century, theology had already achieved a definitive establishment of its essential lines of thought. The challenges posed by paganism and various dualisms had already receded to a secondary plane,[^24] but the definitive gains of earlier theology—especially that of Origen—concerning the divine nature provided the fourth-century Fathers with solid foundations upon which to anchor their arguments against Arianism.

Athanasius insisted upon the divine simplicity and infinity, which exclude all composition in God;[^25] he insisted that God is immaterial and incorporeal.[^26] God possesses all perfections in an infinite degree, to the extent that he stands in need of nothing.[^27] God transcends the whole of creation and, at the same time, is intimately near to all things. This divine transcendence means that, in the order of knowledge, God is beyond all human thought, and in the order of creation, God is present in all places, yet contained by none.[^28] Arius had relied upon the argument that if God is immutable and eternal, the Word cannot be God, since he is begotten: if he is begotten, Arius reasoned, he must have had a beginning at some point, and consequently he is not eternal. He thus forced the Fathers to consider the Trinity of Persons in light of the divine simplicity and immutability.

Saint Athanasius regarded Arianism primarily as a peril to Christian soteriology. Indeed, our salvation consists in participating in the divine life by means of the spirit of adoption we have received through being incorporated into Christ. But how could this salvation occur if Christ were not God? And how could Christ be God, if the Word were not true God?[^29]

To answer these questions, Saint Athanasius insisted upon two key truths: the oneness of God and the divinity of the Word. His reasoning was straightforward: "The Trinity was not made, but is eternal. One is the Godhead in the Trinity, one the glory of the Holy Trinity. And you dare," he said, addressing the Arians, "to divide the Trinity into different natures. The Father being eternal, you say of the Word who is with Him (cf. Jn 1:1) that there was a time when he was not(...) Christians recognise in their faith the blessed Trinity as immutable, perfect, ever in the same state(...) The Word is eternal, even as the Father is eternal."[^30]

Saint Athanasius deduced the eternity of the Son from the infinite perfection of the divine nature, which demands that there be no change in God: "Never was the substance of the Father imperfect, such that what belongs to it accrued to it later. The generation of the Son is not like human generation, subsequent to the existence of the father. He is begotten of God, and being begotten of God and being the Son of the eternal God, He exists from all eternity."[^31] And, as a profound knower of the Nicene Creed, he argued from the indivisibility between light and its radiance to speak of the simultaneity of the Father and the Son.[^32]

Athanasius insisted that it is necessary to affirm that the generation of the Son is eternal. The nature of God requires this, for never was the Father's substance imperfect. Were the Son not eternal, there would have been a moment when this substance received its ultimate perfection precisely by begetting the Son. Proceeding from this, he drew attention to the fact that generation in God is utterly distinct from human generation.[^33]

Arius had argued that the Son cannot be God because God is unbegotten (*agennetos*); likewise, he cannot be God because God is *anarchos*, without beginning, whereas the Word has a principle (*arche*), the Father. The Arian objection served to refine Athanasius's precision of expression: "If the Arians," he wrote, "understand by *agennetos* an existent who is not begotten and has no father, we answer them that in this sense there is only one *agennetos* in God, the Father, and they will gain nothing from this assertion. To speak thus of God, as *agennetos*, is not to affirm that the Son is a creature, since it is evident(...) that the Word is equal to him who begot him. For if God was not produced, neither will his image be a production, but a generation, which is his Word and his Wisdom."[^34]

Saint Athanasius was genuinely eager to secure the return of the Semi-Arians to the Church. Hence he did not frequently employ the term *homoousios* consecrated by Nicaea; it was enough for him that the Homoiousians accepted the truth contained in that term: "Those who accept all that was decided at Nicaea," wrote Saint Athanasius, "and have difficulties only with the term *homoousios*, ought not to be treated as our enemies, nor do we contend against them as though they were Arians(...) If they accept that the Son is of the substance of the Father and not of another reality, and that He is not a creature or a thing made, but a true and natural generation, and that He is from all eternity united to the Father, they are not far from accepting also the word *consubstantial*."[^35]

With respect to the Holy Spirit, Saint Athanasius contented himself with affirming his divinity, as the Nicene Creed itself had done. When, well into the fourth century, Basil of Ancyra and his followers began expressly to deny the divinity of the Holy Spirit, Saint Basil of Caesarea († 379) would employ the same style of argumentation that Saint Athanasius had used for both the Son and the Holy Spirit: if the Spirit were not God, he could not make us partakers of the divine life.[^36]

Saint Athanasius taught with vigour the equality of nature among the three divine Persons. He was not, however, able to express with equal clarity the distinction between them. For Saint Athanasius, *ousia* and *hypostasis* remained virtually synonymous, while he avoided using the term *prosopon* to denote what in Latin was understood as *persona*. The result was that he lacked adequate terms and concepts to speak of the existence of three distinct subsistents in God, and this left his Trinitarian doctrine still somewhat rudimentary on that score.[^37] Nevertheless, Athanasius himself, at the Synod of Alexandria in the year 362, proposed the distinction between the three hypostases and the one divine essence, thereby initiating the first real distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis*.

### The Cappadocian Fathers

This distinction would be deepened by the Cappadocian Fathers—Saint Basil († 379), Saint Gregory of Nazianzus († c. 390), and Saint Gregory of Nyssa († c. 396)—whose doctrinal contribution brought about, among other achievements, a profound development of Trinitarian theology and the definitive refutation of Arianism. Saint Athanasius had contributed to Trinitarian doctrine his magnificent defence of the Nicene faith and his clear affirmation of the Trinity of Persons in God. Other Trinitarian questions now remained to be tackled: What is a person in God? How can there be three Persons in one sole God? Whence arise the differences that distinguish the three divine Persons—that is, whence arise the distinctions whereby the Father is not the Son, and the Son is not the Holy Spirit? The Cappadocians found themselves at the opportune historical moment and equipped with the conceptual tools necessary to answer these questions.[^38]

In their Trinitarian exposition, the Cappadocians embarked upon a path different from that of Saint Athanasius. Athanasius had begun from the unity of God, followed by the affirmation that the Son is of the same nature as the Father; the Cappadocians began from the threefold personal reality existing in God. They thereby better avoided the danger of Sabellianism in their defence of the *homoousios*. At the same time, by distinguishing between *ousia* and *hypostasis*, they found the way clear to affirm the distinction among the divine Persons without thereby compromising the unity of the essence or substance. The Cappadocians understood by *ousia* the nature, which is common to all beings of the same species, whereas by *hypostasis* they understood those same qualities concretised in an individual existence, in which the generic receives concrete and individual expression.[^39]

The new problem that arose at this point was this: can the divine essence or substance be understood in a generic manner, and the divine Persons as the concretisation of that essence—that is, can the divine essence be considered as the indeterminate and the Persons as that which determines the indeterminate? If this were so, the mystery of the Trinity would vanish. For then the unity existing among the three divine Persons would no longer be the unity of one sole God, but a generic unity. In the same way, three men are said to possess the same humanity, yet they are manifestly three numerically distinct men. Unity in God would not then be the unity of one single God; rather, one would be speaking of three numerically distinct individuals who possess the same generic essence.

Saint Basil addressed this issue frequently: "The *hypostasis*," he wrote to his brother Gregory, "is not the indefinite notion of substance which finds no place to stand on account of the generic quality of the thing signified, but rather that which restricts and circumscribes what is indeterminate by means of proper distinguishing characteristics."[^40]

Saint Basil affirmed explicitly that the divine nature is numerically one; within the Godhead, distinction can exist only through the distinguishing characteristics that cause each Person to be himself and incapable of being another. "Notwithstanding the proper marks of each Person, by which the distinction of the hypostases is recognised, in that which concerns immensity, ineffability, etc., there exists in the life-giving nature—that is, in the Father, in the Son, and in the Holy Spirit—no diversity whatsoever(...) There is in them an ineffable mystery of communion and of distinction. The difference of the hypostases does not sunder the communion of nature, nor does the communion of *ousia* confound the personal characteristics."[^41]

The Persons, therefore, are distinguished solely by their proper 'characteristics'. What are these 'personifying' characteristics? According to Basil, what is proper to the Father is to be *agennetos*, unbegotten: the Father is the only Person of the Trinity who proceeds from no other. In this sense, it may be said that he is greater than the Son—not in the sense of being greater in nature, but in the sense of being the principle (*arche*) of the Son. The Son is *gennetos*, begotten; He is the only one who is begotten and, therefore, the only one who is Son. What is proper to the Holy Spirit is to be known in the Son and to receive together with Him his substance from the Father.[^42] The personal characteristics may be designated as paternity, sonship, and sanctifying power.[^43]

And whence arise these personifying characteristics, this 'something' that differentiates the three divine Persons without differentiating the substance? That 'something', which does not belong to the substance of God, must belong to his intimate life—that is, to his acts of knowledge and love. Towards this points an important Trinitarian passage from Gregory of Nyssa:

> "The doctrine of our religion knows how to discern a distinction of hypostases within the unity of nature. Even those who are strangers to our faith will acknowledge that God is not devoid of intelligence. Whoever confesses that God is not devoid of intelligence (that God is not *alogos*) must admit that God has a Word (*Logos*)(...) If he should say that he conceives the Word of God in a manner similar to our own word, he may then be led to a higher conception. For it is utterly necessary to believe that the Word, like everything else, corresponds to the nature from which it proceeds(...) In the transcendent nature, everything that is predicated of it must match the greatness of that which is contemplated. Consequently, when speaking of the Word of God, one must not think that his subsistence resides in the vibration of the voice and therefore loses real existence after the manner of our spoken word. On the contrary, just as our nature, being corruptible, has a corruptible word, so too the incorruptible and enduring nature has an eternal and substantial Word. And if, following the logic of this reasoning, it is admitted that the Word of God subsists eternally, it will be utterly necessary to accept that the substance of the Word possesses life, for it would be sacrilege to think that the Word subsists inanimate, like stones(...) Now, piety has led us to recognise in the divine nature the existence of a Spirit, just as it has recognised the existence of a Word of God(...) It would demean the greatness of divine power were we to conceive the Spirit that is in God after the manner of our breath. On the contrary, we consider him as a substantial power in himself, with his own subsistence, inseparable from God in whom he exists, and from the Word of God whom he accompanies(...) Whoever carefully scrutinises the depth of the mystery can grasp in his soul, in an ineffable manner, a modest understanding of the doctrine concerning the knowledge of God: how it is that the same reality is numerable and yet escapes numeration; how it is seen separately and at the same time conceived as a unity; and how it is subject to distinction according to person, yet not divided in substance. Indeed, the concept of person distinguishes the Spirit from the Word, and likewise from Him whose Spirit and Word they are. But once you have understood what distinguishes them, you will also see that the unity of nature admits of no partition, so that the power of the monarchy cannot be divided and fractioned into different deities."[^44]

The opening sentence is the starting point of the Nyssen's Trinitarian theology: there exists a distinction of hypostases within the unity of the divine nature. This distinction is grounded in the inner life of God, who is spirit. Gregory begins his description of the divine life with an easy wordplay: God is not an unreasoning being (*alogos*), therefore he possesses a *Logos*. This argument was already traditional.[^45] The divine *Logos* cannot be fleeting like ours, but subsistent; he must possess all the attributes proper to the Godhead, since the divine simplicity and infinity prevent anything belonging to God from being limited. This argument was also well known long before Gregory.[^46] It is precisely upon this that the Nyssen relies to defend the subsistence and distinction of the hypostases of the Son and of the Spirit: he extends to them the attributes proper to the divinity of the Father. In God there is nothing unsubstantial; thus neither the Word nor the Spirit lacks subsistence—that is, they are not *anhypostaton*, but subsistents who are distinguished from one another precisely by their origin.[^47] Evoking John 14:6, Gregory stresses that the Word does not merely participate in life, but *is* Life.

The Nyssen understands that the distinction of Persons is given by the immanent relations they maintain among themselves as a consequence of their origin. According to Gregory, only these relations distinguish the Persons from one another; in works *ad extra*, the three always act conjointly, each in accordance with its personal characteristic.[^48]

This leads Gregory to address the question of number in God. How is it possible to speak numerically of Him who is infinitely simple? Saint Justin had already observed that the Son, in number (*en arithmō*), is distinct from the Father, without it being possible to think that the substance of the Father is divided between Father and Son.[^49] Gregory calls this an ineffable mystery, for it is impossible to explain "how the same reality is numbered and yet escapes all numeration; how it is considered divided and yet is one single thing; how it is distinct in hypostasis and not divided in its substance, although the Word and the Spirit are distinct."[^50]

This Trinitarian doctrine is developed in a very similar fashion in Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, who takes great pains to avoid simultaneously the error of Arius and the error of Sabellius.[^51] He speaks with greater clarity than Basil of the divinity of the Holy Spirit and of his consubstantiality with the Father.[^52] Gregory underlines the unity of the Trinity starting from the divine monarchy: the Father communicates to the Son and the Spirit indivisibly the whole of the Godhead, for it is most simple and indivisible. The Nazianzen emphasises even more strongly than Gregory of Nyssa that the distinction among the Persons is grounded in their relations. It is these relations that configure, so to speak, the proper notion of each Person—that is, which prevent any confusion among the three divine Persons: the Father is not confused with the Son, nor the Son with the Father.

Here is an eloquent text: "The Father is Father without beginning, because he proceeds from no one. The Son is Son and is not without beginning, because he proceeds from the Father. But if you speak of beginning in time, He too is without beginning, because he is the Maker of time and is not subject to time. The Holy Spirit is the Spirit of truth, who proceeds from the Father, but not in the manner of sonship, because he does not proceed by generation, but by procession(...) For neither did the Father cease to be unbegotten by begetting, nor did the Son cease to be begotten by proceeding from the unbegotten(...) Nor has the Spirit become Father or Son by the fact of being God."[^53]

Gregory emphasises that the three divine Persons differ only by their relation of origin: "The Son is not the Father, for there is only one Father, but the Son is all that the Father is; the Holy Spirit is not the Son, because He proceeds from God, for there is only one unique Son; but the Holy Spirit is the same as the Son. The three are one in the Godhead; the One is three in their personalising properties."[^54] Once the personal distinction has been vigorously affirmed, Gregory—just like Saint Basil and Saint Gregory of Nyssa—grounds their unity in the Father, who is the source from which springs the divinity of the Son and of the Spirit.[^55]

The difference among the Persons is reflected in the actions *ad extra* of the Trinity. Referring to God's action in the world, Gregory speaks of the Father as the cause of all things, of the Son as the maker of all things, and of the Holy Spirit as the one who brings all things to perfection.[^56]

Gregory's doctrine regarding the procession of the Holy Spirit is especially clarifying. Basil confessed that he was unable to specify the mode of procession of the Holy Spirit. It was Gregory who coined the term *ekporeusis* (*processio*) as distinct from generation, a distinction of immense importance in the history of pneumatology.[^57] With the distinction between generation and procession, Gregory advanced in expressing the distinction between the Son and the Spirit. He also affirmed more clearly than Saint Basil that the difference among the Persons corresponds to the difference of manifestation or relation among them.

### The Notions of Ousia and Hypostasis

In studying the Trinitarian mystery it is indispensable to refer to the concepts of *nature* and *person*, in that they designate distinct realities: nature answers the question *what* a thing is (*quid*), whereas person answers the question *who* (*quis*). The fourth-century theological debate, in both the Greek and Latin spheres, makes clear that the clarification of these concepts and precision in their use were essential for understanding Christian doctrine in its true dimensions, both in Trinitarian theology and in Christology. Christian faith confesses, indeed, that in the Holy Trinity there is one nature and three persons—*mia ousia, treis hypostaseis*—whereas in Jesus Christ we confess that there exists one person in two natures (*mia hypostasis en dyo physein*). History bears witness that through these two formulas a definitive advance was achieved in the formulation of Christian doctrine.[^58]

Etymologically, *hypostasis* means that which stands underneath, the foundation. The New Testament sometimes uses it to designate that which has substantial reality, objective reality, as opposed to what is subjective (cf. 2 Cor 9:4; Heb 1:3; 3:14; 11:1). It was so used, for example, at the Council of Nicaea, when the Arian position asserting that the Son is 'of another hypostasis or of another *ousia*' than the Father was rejected. The Council used both terms univocally, giving them the meaning of substance, nature, or essence. Saint Athanasius commented that the Council of Nicaea did well to use both terms as synonyms, since "these two words are used to signify whatever is most real in being."[^59]

From the third century onward—prior to Nicaea, therefore—the term *hypostasis* began also to be used to designate the divine subsistents in so far as they are distinct from one another; consequently, the meaning of *hypostasis* in these instances was contrasted with that of *ousia*. Origen, for example, though at times using *ousia* and *hypostasis* as synonyms, avoided designating the three divine Persons with the term *ousia*, employing *hypostasis* instead.[^60] Dionysius of Alexandria already spoke of three hypostases in the unity of the Godhead,[^61] and Saint Dionysius of Rome condemned those who admitted in the Godhead hypostases separated from one another.[^62] Prior to the year 362—the year of the celebrated Council of Alexandria—the word *hypostasis* retained the meaning of objective reality, and when applied to the divine essence it could mean the same as *ousia*; but applied to the divine Persons considered in themselves, it took on the more precise meaning of complete substance, that is, of an independent subject as distinct from *ousia*.[^63]

The Cappadocians played a primary role in this terminological clarification. They understood by *ousia* that which is common to individuals of the same species—that is, what all possess equally; and by *hypostasis*, the *ousia* completed by the individuating characteristics that determine it.[^64]

*Hypostasis* signifies, then, the *ousia* as individuated—that is, as concretised and distinguished from other individuals participating in the same *ousia*. This distinction occurs by means of what Saint Basil calls the distinctive properties (*idiomata*). In God, the *ousia*—goodness, eternity, etc.—is common to the three hypostases, which are distinguished solely by the 'characteristics' of paternity, sonship, or sanctifying power.[^65] Saint Gregory of Nyssa followed his brother in the use of these terms, bringing the meaning of *hypostasis* close to that of person, while maintaining the distinction between *hypostasis* and *ousia*; he understood *hypostasis* as the *ousia* distinguished from all others by virtue of its properties, its *idiomata*.[^66]

### The Formula Mia Ousia, Treis Hypostaseis

The distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis* enabled the celebrated formula *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis* ('one substance, three hypostases') to yield its full elucidating efficacy. The formula was already known to Origen and was used by theologians of Origenist formation. The Council of Alexandria in 362 was a council that sought, above all, reconciliation with all who accepted the faith proclaimed at Nicaea, even if they hesitated to embrace the term *homoousios*. The use of the formula *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis* was accepted.

Here is how Saint Athanasius recounts it in his *Tomus ad Antiochenos*:

> "Some were accused of using the expression 'three hypostases', which was viewed with suspicion because it was not scriptural(...) In view of the controversy that had arisen, we asked whether by it they understood, along with the Arians, hypostases that are completely different, diverse, and of distinct substance, each subsisting separately in itself, as happens with creatures and as happens with sons begotten by men(...) They vigorously affirmed that they had never thought anything of the kind. We then asked them: 'Why then do you speak in this manner and use these expressions?' They replied: 'Because we believe in the Holy Trinity, not in name only, but as real and subsistent: the Father truly exists and subsists; the Son is substantial and subsistent; the Holy Spirit is subsistent and truly existing. We speak neither of three gods nor of three principles(...) We acknowledge the Holy Trinity, the one Godhead, the one principle, the Son consubstantial with the Father, as the Fathers have handed down to us, and the Holy Spirit, not a creature, not an alien, but proper to and indivisible from the substance of the Son and of the Father.'"[^67]

The text speaks eloquently of the misunderstandings the Council had to confront. Good will enabled all to recognise one another in the same faith, despite their divergent language. The Council accepted both formulations: understanding *hypostasis* either as *ousia*, or as person. Consequently, it was agreed that the formula *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis* could be used in the sense in which Nicaea had affirmed the unity of substance and the distinction of Persons. This formula possessed the great virtue of affirming simultaneously both the unity and the diversity existing in God.

As was natural, the acceptance of the formula did not imply that it was immediately used by everyone. Thus, for instance, Saint Athanasius, having accepted it, continued at times to use *hypostasis* as a synonym for *ousia*. Terminological clarification required time to become universal. The Westerners encountered greater difficulty in accepting this formula, but its use steadily gained ground throughout the fourth century. In the East, the Cappadocians already employed it with complete naturalness.[^68]

The debates following Nicaea had made it clear that *consubstantial*, taken on its own, remained ambiguous. This was seen, for example, in Marcellus of Ancyra, who, despite accepting the consubstantial, failed to avoid the pitfalls of Sabellianism. The *consubstantial*, indeed, affirms divine unity, but says nothing of the distinction of the three subsistents. Nor is the affirmation of three subsistents sufficient by itself to preserve the mystery of the Trinity. This is seen, for example, in Arius, who spoke of three subsistents of diverse nature. It is necessary, therefore, to speak of one single substance in three subsistents.

### The Question of the Divine Names in the Controversy with Eunomius

Eunomius was the great exponent of Anomoeanism. In the year 361, Eunomius published his *Apology* to defend his fundamental thesis: that the Son is of a completely different nature from the Father.[^69] He took as the starting point of his argumentation the beginning of the Creed: "We believe in one God, Father and Creator, from whom are all things." Following this affirmation of the uniqueness of God, Eunomius added that, according to the teaching of the Fathers and common sense, this God, creator of all, comes neither from himself nor from another. Consequently, this God must be essentially *agennetos*—that is, he can neither be made nor begotten. Being an unoriginate principle belongs to divine being to such an extent that *agennetos* adequately designates the very divine essence.[^70]

Two questions of supreme importance intertwine here: 1) whether the fact that God is the principle of all things means that he cannot have a Son—that is, whether it means there cannot be divine Sonship in Him; 2) whether there exists a name that can adequately signify the divine essence. The answer to these questions had already been provided by numerous earlier Fathers. One need only re-read the arguments of Origen and Saint Athanasius: 1) the whole Trinity is *agenetos*, in the sense that it was not made; only the Father is *agennetos*, for the Son is truly begotten; 2) there is no name that can adequately express the divine essence, for God is beyond all thought and beyond every name.

To defend his thesis, Eunomius relied on the name that God revealed of himself in Exodus 3:14: "I am who I am". This, according to Eunomius, was a positive name that adequately expressed the divine essence. Consequently, the term *agennetos*, in adequately expressing the divine essence, which is simple and indivisible, expressed God himself, not a part of God. If, then, being unbegotten—*agennesia*—is what is most proper to the divine essence, and the Son by definition is begotten, it follows that the Son is not only distinct from the Father, but cannot be called God with the same reality as the Father is called God. Thus the diversity of names between Father and Son would manifest not merely the personal distinction between Father and Son, but their diversity of nature.

Eunomius confused innascibility with aseity, or being of oneself (*a se*). But Eunomius went a step further: *agennesia* adequately and fully designated the divine essence, whereas all other names attributed to God spoke of Him only as mere subjective designations of our own, without expressing anything objective in God. What was truly objective had already been expressed by the word *agennesia*, and since God is utterly simple, that one word signifies everything. This raised a third issue: the other perfections we attribute to God—justice, beauty, mercy, etc.—would be mutually interchangeable, since they would convey nothing objective about Him.

Saint Gregory of Nyssa defended, therefore, that none of these names designates the divine nature in itself, but that, at the same time, each of them intimates something that the human mind can conceive of the divine nature.[^71] As G. C. Stead writes, "Eunomius makes two obvious mistakes. The first is to say that only one term is properly applicable to God as signifying his essence. The second is to suggest that this term is *agennetos*, as that which in the proper sense gives a complete specification of all that is contained in the name of God(...) Justin [*Apology* 2, 6, 2] says that Father, God, Creator, Lord, Master applied to God are not names, but appellations derived from his beneficent actions. Basil and Gregory, broadly speaking, follow him along this path."[^72]

Saint Basil responded to Eunomius around the year 364 with his three books *Against Eunomius* (*Adversus Eunomium*). Eunomius replied with a work entitled *Apology for the Apology*, composed in 378, shortly before Saint Basil's death.[^73] Following Basil's death, his brother Gregory took up the task of refuting this second work of Eunomius and his entire thought, writing what are known as the twelve books *Against Eunomius* (*Contra Eunomium*), which are in fact a collection of four treatises against Eunomius.[^74] Around the year 381, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus delivered in Constantinople his second Theological Oration, directed against Eunomius.

The controversy raised by Eunomius was, in a certain sense, subtle. Eunomius, by asserting that the name *unbegotten* directly designates the divine substance, opposed this name to all other names or concepts applied to God *kata epinoian*—that is, based upon the mode of knowing proper to the human intellect. These attributes, such as omnipotent, wise, powerful, immutable, immense, which spring from the knowledge man can acquire of God by considering his activity in the world, lacked, according to Eunomius, any objective value and in the end all meant the same thing.

The fifth chapter of the first book of Saint Basil's *Adversus Eunomium* is of capital importance, for there the Bishop of Caesarea answers directly this issue raised by Eunomius. Saint Basil observes that even when we refer to a corporeal reality, our intelligence perceives in that single, selfsame reality a manifold series of qualities, such as colour, hardness, dimension, and so forth. For this reason, we distinguish one quality from another by our reason, *kata epinoian*, even though in reality this quality coincides with other qualities in a single subject.[^75] "Why then," Saint Basil asks, "deny that one may legitimately form names concerning God, and deny that they correspond in some way to the reality of God?"[^76] Saint Basil concludes his argument by showing that if this mode of speaking about God is rejected, one falls into absurdity and confusion. Indeed, it is not the same thing to say that God is immutable as to say that he is good, that he is merciful, or that he is eternal. In applying these names to God, although he is infinitely simple, we are pointing to distinct aspects of that most simple reality. The fact that we designate distinct aspects of the single, most simple divine reality is due, Saint Basil continues, not to God's being in himself, but to the limitation of our intellect, which is incapable of expressing with a single notion or word—inevitably limited—the infinite divine perfection.

Divine simplicity would be violated, Saint Basil continues, if those names were applied to God as designating parts of Him—that is, if one were to say that God is partly immutable, partly life, partly light, partly justice, partly mercy, whereas God is entirely Life, Light, Justice, Goodness, and Mercy.

Gregory of Nyssa followed the same line of argument as his brother. According to Gregory, the unity and infinite simplicity of the divine nature is not called into question by the fact that we employ diverse names to speak of God. The multiplicity of names with which we speak of God arises from our own mode of knowing, which is limited and therefore does not attain directly the divine nature. What is more, our manner of speaking of God with multiple names finds its foundation precisely in the eminence and ineffability of the divine being.[^77]

Gregory likewise defended the legitimacy—indeed, the necessity—of using names referring to God *kata epinoian*, according to the limited mode of knowing proper to the human spirit. Precisely because we know that God is the first cause, we deduce that He cannot come from another; the same holds for the rest of the divine names: precisely because we know that God is the cause of the life that exists in the world, we know that He is Life, and so on. If these names, Gregory argued, all possessed one and the same meaning, why would Sacred Scripture call God just, good, true, and so on?[^78]

### Distinctions between the Attributes and the Divine Simplicity

Eunomius admitted only one proper name for God: *agennetos*. Saint Basil replied that no name can adequately grasp or express the divine essence. The divine essence, utterly simple, is at the same time infinite in perfection. No concept can fully express its perfection, and yet it is necessary to employ many concepts to refer to this infinite perfection.[^79]

Saint Basil distinguished, moreover, between positive names and negative names. Positive names signify what God is: Good, Just, Creator, etc. Negative names signify what God is not: God is in-corruptible, im-mortal, in-visible; these names signify that in God there is no corruption, no death, no corporeality. By joining these two modes of speaking about God, says Saint Basil, we form a certain idea of God.

Now, *a-gennetos* is a negative name. One can now understand Saint Basil's insistence that negative names are improper for speaking of God. This insistence had a clear objective: to demonstrate against Eunomius that *agennetos* not only cannot be the proper name of the divine essence, but is among the least proper names to apply to God.

Eunomius contrasted *gennetos* and *agennetos* to show that the Son cannot have the same substance as the Father. Saint Basil introduced a new distinction: absolute names and relative names, taking up in them the distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis*. Absolute names are those that signify things in themselves—that is, their substance; relative names signify a relation of one thing to another. *Gennetos* and *agennetos* are relative names: they do not designate the divine substance considered in itself, but only the relation existing between two Persons who possess the same divine nature, such as the Father and the Son.

The distinction between absolute and relative names has the following consequence when applying them to God: absolute names, referring to the divine essence, are applied equally to the three divine Persons; relative names, precisely because they refer to the relations existing among the three Persons, are not applied to them equally.

Thus, Gregory of Nazianzus would argue from this distinction of Saint Basil: the term *agennetos*, taken in the sense of uncreated (*agenetos*), is an absolute and negative term designating the divine substance, and therefore designates both the Father and the Son; taken in the relative sense, as unbegotten (*agennetos*), it belongs only to the Father, who begets the Son and, in begetting, communicates to him his own uncreated substance.[^80] The Son is uncreated and, at the same time, is begotten by the Father.

Returning to Eunomius's initial theme—the existence of a name that adequately designates the divine essence—Saint Gregory insisted that even positive names, which certainly convey something of God, given the divine infinity and the limitation of our knowledge, are not proper names of the divine essence, even if they are the most suitable for designating it. Such is the case, for example, with the attribution to God of the name 'He who is' in Exodus 3:14.[^81]

Saint Gregory of Nyssa likewise combated Eunomius's position by appealing, on the one hand, to the transcendence of God and, on the other, by drawing attention to the way human beings speak of God. Since the Divine is ineffable and inconceivable, Gregory argued, Sacred Scripture is obliged to use many names to designate the manifold aspects we can glimpse within the infinity of God.[^82] Furthermore, when human beings speak of a thing, it is not the name that imparts being to the thing; rather, it is the being of the thing that determines whether the name is applied truthfully.[^83]

For Gregory, the being of things is far more important than their name, since it is the being of things—and not the name given to them by men—that makes them intelligible. Hence, if the appellation is correct, it is nothing other than a simple transparency of what the thing is. Hence too the multiplicity of attributes by which we speak of God does no violence to the divine simplicity.[^84]

Saint Basil was already aware of the fundamental error committed by Eunomius: believing that a created name—whatever it might be—could adequately express the divine essence. Indeed, when Eunomius claimed that *agennetos* was the proper name of God, he was asserting that this name yields a comprehensive knowledge of the Godhead. Saint Basil rebuked Eunomius precisely for intimating that human language could express God with exhaustive perfection.[^85] An identical doctrine is found in Saint Gregory of Nazianzus.[^86] This is so, the Nazianzen argued, because while God is simple, our knowledge takes as its starting point the data furnished by the senses—that is, material beings, which are composite.

Gregory of Nyssa insisted upon God's transcendence as the clearest truth upon which to rely in order definitively to refute Eunomius's doctrine. The good is boundless and infinite, Gregory argued, and consequently there can be no degrees in the Godhead. The Son, in order to be truly Son, must possess the same nature as the Father. If he were not of the same nature as the Father, he could in no way be called Son in the true sense.[^87]

The divine infinity requires a multiplicity of human concepts to express it, ever inadequately. For this reason, this infinity places God above every created concept, which is necessarily always limited: "For that which truly exists is true life. And this is inaccessible to knowledge(...) Learn from what has been said that the Godhead, by its very nature, is boundless, for it is circumscribed by no limit. For if the Godhead were conceived with some limit, it would be necessary to consider together with the limit that which lies beyond that limit."[^88] For Gregory, the infinite Being is essentially living, essentially wise, essentially powerful, since there is nothing that can limit Him.[^89]

### Macedonianism

During the first half of the fourth century, theological dispute was centred upon the divinity of the Son. The very fact that the Nicene Creed mentions faith in the Holy Spirit simply, without adding any further qualification, indicates that the questions concerning the Holy Spirit had not yet become explicitly problematic. Yet, according to strict logic, the Arian position inevitably led to the denial of the divinity of the Holy Spirit. If the divinity of the Son was denied, *a fortiori* the divinity of the Spirit had to be denied. The explicit denial of the divinity of the Holy Spirit arose, however, not in Arian circles, but in Semi-Arian quarters.

The earliest news of this heresy against the Spirit is given to us by Saint Athanasius in his *Letters to Serapion*, written around the year 360. Those who denied the divinity of the Holy Spirit were given the name *Pneumatomachians* ('fighters against the Spirit') or *Macedonians*, because they were led by Macedonius, Bishop of Constantinople († post 360). Macedonius, who accepted the divinity of the Son, taught that the Holy Spirit was not true God, but only a messenger or angel of God.

No written work of Macedonius has survived, so that, apart from the fact that he denied the divinity of the Holy Spirit, nothing further is known of his thought. In any case, it is clear that the Pneumatomachians denied the divinity of the Holy Spirit while accepting the divinity of the Son. These were years in which it was vital for the resolution of the Arian crisis that all defenders of the Nicene faith remain united. This explains the prudence with which Saint Basil acted when speaking of the divinity of the Holy Spirit, whereas Saint Gregory of Nazianzus expressed himself with greater vigour and clarity.[^90]

### The Pneumatological Doctrine of Saint Basil

Saint Basil was the first to write a treatise *On the Holy Spirit* (*De Spiritu Sancto*). To him is owed the major advance in pneumatological doctrine that culminated in the First Council of Constantinople.

Saint Basil's pneumatological doctrine rests fundamentally upon the theological contemplation of the mission of the Holy Spirit—that is, upon his role in the economy of salvation. John Paul II summarised Saint Basil's pneumatological doctrine as follows: "It is the Spirit given to everyone who is baptised who infuses charisms into each one and brings to their remembrance the precepts of the Lord; it is the Spirit who animates the entire Church, ordering and vivifying her with his gifts, making of her a spiritual and charismatic body. From this Saint Basil ascends to the serene contemplation of the glory of the Spirit, mysterious and inaccessible, confessing Him, above every creature, as King and Lord, because through Him we have been divinised, and as Holy, because through Him we are sanctified. Thus Saint Basil, having contributed to the formulation of the Church's Trinitarian faith, speaks still today to her heart and consoles her, especially through the luminous confession of her Comforter."[^91]

In his pneumatological doctrine, Saint Basil remained deeply indebted to Saint Athanasius, while at the same time surpassing him in theological precision. Indeed, whereas for Athanasius the mystery of the procession of the Spirit remained impenetrable and it would be foolish to attempt to scrutinise it, Saint Basil, while stressing that the manner of the Holy Spirit's existence remains ineffable, added something further by distinguishing the mode of proceeding of the Holy Spirit—as breath from the mouth of God—from the procession of the Son. Later theology, as it developed in the West under the increasingly marked influence of Greek thought, would come not only to distinguish the processions of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, but also to attribute to that of the Spirit the character of love (*ratio amoris*).[^92]

The first thing Saint Basil affirmed was the perfect divinity of the Holy Spirit. To show that this is a revealed truth, he adduced the baptismal mandate of Matthew 28:19. The con-numeration of the Spirit with the Father and with the Son, he argued, is not a human invention, but something taught by Jesus Christ himself. The baptismal mandate demonstrates not only equality among the Persons, but also communion among them. It is a communion in all things: the three divine Persons possess the same attributes and, consequently, deserve equal honour.[^93]

In fact, it was the justification of *homotimia*—the equality of honour rendered by the liturgy to the three divine Persons—that motivated his treatise *On the Holy Spirit*. Saint Basil used the following formula: "Glory to the Father, with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit", instead of the formula "Glory to the Father through the Son, in the Holy Spirit". Some bishops were taken aback by this usage, which highlighted the *homotimia* among the three divine Persons. To justify this doxology, Saint Basil wrote his book, demonstrating that equal honour is rendered to the three Persons because all three are consubstantial.

In fourth-century theology, the Holy Spirit was already considered in his work as life-giver, sanctifier, and deifier. He is substantial holiness and, as Saint Basil insisted, is united to the Father and to the Son as intimately as unity to unity. From this perspective, Saint Basil repeatedly cited the New Testament texts that speak of the Holy Spirit as the Spirit of Christ. "He is called the Spirit of Christ," he commented, "because he is intimately united to Him by nature, such that if anyone does not have the Spirit of Christ, he does not belong to Christ."[^94]

The union between the Son and the Spirit is indissoluble and entails within itself the indissoluble unity of the Trinity. Here is how Saint Gregory of Nyssa developed his brother's thought: "Whoever piously receives the Holy Spirit recognises in that same Spirit the glory of the Only-Begotten, and, knowing the Son, knows the Image of the Infinite, and, in the Image, knows the Archetype."[^95] To know the Son in the Spirit, and in this manner to know the Father. It is understandable that Saint Basil liked to place on the same level these two doxologies: "Glory to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit" and "Glory to the Father, with the Son, with the Holy Spirit". Indeed, if Father, Son, and Spirit were not of the same nature and did not therefore deserve the same adoration, one could not know the Son in the Spirit and, in that knowledge, know the Father.

### The Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed

At the First Council of Constantinople (AD 381), the divinity of the Holy Spirit was defined and the Nicene Creed was completed, receiving the redaction known as the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed. This Creed acquired its definitive theological status upon its solemn acceptance by the Council of Chalcedon (AD 451).[^96] Of this Creed, our interest at this moment centres upon its pneumatological doctrine, which constitutes its most characteristic feature:

Of the Holy Spirit it confesses:

> "And [we believe] in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the Giver of Life, who proceeds from the Father, who with the Father and the Son is together worshipped and glorified, who spoke through the prophets; in one holy, catholic and apostolic Church."[^97]

First of all, the divinity of the Holy Spirit is defined. In affirming the equality of the Spirit with the other divine Persons, it was not possible, as in the case of the Son, to appeal to the requirements of an authentic generation, since the Holy Spirit does not proceed from the Father by way of generation. The debate with the Macedonians and, in particular, the arguments of Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, and Saint Gregory of Nyssa provided abundant material for formulating the pneumatological faith.

The divinity of the Spirit is already intimated in the very epithet accompanying the mention of the Spirit: *Holy*. It is an epithet already applied to him in the New Testament (Lk 1:35; Jn 14:26), which, taken in its radical meaning, reveals him as a divine Person: God alone is holy. It is this absolute holiness that enables him to be sanctifier in the absolute sense and diviniser of man. This is the argument Saint Basil had already used to demonstrate the divinity of the Spirit.[^98] And in professing its faith in the holiness of the Spirit, the Church professes its faith in its own holiness and in the sanctifying power of baptism.[^99]

It is said of the Spirit, first, that he is 'the Lord' in a manner similar to how Christ is affirmed in the same Creed to be 'the Lord', intending the word *Lord* (*Kyrios*) as a divine title. The Spirit is Lord and therefore does not belong to the level of creatures—that is, he is not a ministering spirit like the angels, but belongs to the sphere of divine lordship.[^100]

The affirmation 'Giver of Life' (*zoopoion*, vivifier) also appears frequently in the writings of Saint Basil as applied to the Holy Spirit. This assertion encompasses everything from sanctification in the strict sense—the fact that the work of the Spirit is sanctification, which brings about that man is recreated, made a 'new creation' in Christ—to the definitive redemption of the body, that is, the resurrection of the dead, which will take place through the agency of the Spirit (cf. Rom 8:11).

The clause 'who proceeds from the Father' is manifestly placed there to signify the divine origin of the Spirit. The Spirit proceeds (*ekporeuetai*), but without being begotten; he proceeds, but is neither made nor created. It incorporates the affirmation of Our Lord recorded in John 15:26 ("the Spirit of truth, who proceeds [*ekporeuetai*] from the Father") and the marvellous exposition of this given by Saint Gregory of Nazianzus.[^101]

There follows the proclamation of the *homotimia* of the Holy Spirit with the Father and the Son. This was always one of the most powerful arguments for demonstrating the full divinity of the Spirit. The adorability of the Spirit together with the Father and the Son seems proclaimed especially against the Pneumatomachians, who admitted the adorability and hence the divinity of the Son, but not that of the Spirit.[^102]

The clause 'who spoke through the prophets' has the same intention: to reaffirm the divinity of the Holy Spirit, for to this argument—the prophets speaking under the inspiration of the Spirit—the Fathers likewise had recourse to reaffirm his divinity.[^103]

In the Creed, then, the divinity of the Holy Spirit is confessed by attributing to the Holy Spirit: a) a divine name: *Lord*; b) divine operations: giving life; c) an immanent origin from the Father: *proceeds*; d) equality of adoration (*homotimia*).

The formulations of the Creed are exquisitely weighed. The Spirit is not explicitly called *God*, because Scripture itself does not call him God. Nevertheless, equivalent titles are given to him to such an extent that it is beyond doubt that faith in the divinity of the Holy Spirit is unreservedly confessed. Nor is it said of Him that he is *consubstantial* with the Father and the Son. Yet the entire sequence is designed to affirm this consubstantiality in other words. The sequence reproduces, indeed, the patristic argumentation, especially that of Saint Basil, to show equality of nature. The Spirit proceeds from the Father; he is not created, but consubstantial, since he is of divine origin.

Finally, in the first canon of the Council, the Sabellians, the Arians, and the Macedonians are explicitly condemned, whereby the denial of the divinity of the Spirit was definitively surmounted. The addition of the *Filioque*, which would take place in the era of Charlemagne, will receive detailed attention later.
