## Chapter 8: Latin Trinitarian Theology {#mateo-seco-trino.8}

In the preceding chapters we have studied the Greek Trinitarian doctrine of the first centuries, paying particular attention to the events, authors, and questions of the fourth century. We now turn to the study of the Trinitarian doctrine that developed in the West during these same centuries. Latin theology concerning the mystery of God possesses well-defined traits, both in the themes it accentuates and the framework within which it situates them, as well as in its determined effort to discover ever clearer formulations for confessing the mystery.

The first great figure of Latin theology is Tertullian († post-220), who was followed by Novatian († c. 257)—the first author to write a treatise *De Trinitate*—and Hilary of Poitiers († c. 367), the Western thinker most deeply versed in the Trinitarian problems debated in the East. Latin patristic thought reaches its summit in the figure of Saint Augustine († 430), with whom Trinitarian doctrine attains its definitive orientation.

As regards the Magisterium of the Church, the West received without hesitation the doctrine of the Ecumenical Councils held in the East; its faith was expressed pre-eminently in the various Creeds, in whose composition the doctrine of Saint Augustine exerted a decisive influence.

### Trinitarian Doctrine in Tertullian

Tertullian is the first Latin author to oppose Trinitarian heresies vigorously. He is the most resolute adversary of monarchianism. He likewise opposes, as has already been seen, modalism, Gnosticism, and Marcionite dualism.[^1] Like his Greek contemporaries, Tertullian fought strenuously to make manifest the uniqueness of God and his transcendence over all created reality. His principal contribution to Trinitarian doctrine consists in certain formulations of such precision that they were incorporated into theological terminology forever.[^2] Tertullian is the first in the West to apply the Latin word *Trinitas* to the Trinitarian mystery. To him also belong expressions as significant as "the Trinity of one divinity, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit",[^3] as well as the distinction between *substantia* and *persona*, which closely approximates the Greek distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis*.

#### Importance of the *Regula Fidei*

In studying the beginnings of Trinitarian doctrine, we highlighted the paramount importance of the confession of faith as manifested in baptism, in Christian prayer, and in the rule of faith (*regula fidei*). One of the chief characteristics of Tertullian's theological endeavour is precisely his resolve to remain faithful to this *regula fidei*—that is to say, his determination to preserve fidelity to the faith of the Church in one single God who is simultaneously Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Christian faith demands belief not only in divine unity, but also in a real trinity of persons. In *Adversus Praxeam*, composed around the year 213, when he had already embraced Montanism, Tertullian, appealing to the *regula fidei*, points out that the three of the Trinity are not three distinct modes whereby the one Father manifests himself, but three distinct subsistents; and that this distinction of persons exists within the unity of a single God. Here is an outline of the Trinitarian doctrine of *Adversus Praxeam*:

1. The rule of faith indicates that in God there is only one substance and three Persons, who possess equal status (*status*).[^4]
2. These persons differ not in being diverse, but in being distinct in order (*gradus*).[^5]
3. The Trinity of Persons in no way invalidates the uniqueness of God, for the other Persons proceed from the Father.[^6]
4. The Second Person is the Word (*Sermo*) of the Father, and the Third is the Holy Spirit.[^7]
5. There is only one God, that is, one single divine substance,[^8] for the Father is the origin of the Trinity. Tertullian expresses this truth in language that is still inexact: the Father is the entire substance; the Son, by contrast, is a derivation and a portion of the whole, as He himself confesses when saying: "The Father is greater than I" (Jn 14:28).[^9]

With assertions such as this, it is understandable that Tertullian did not manage to free himself entirely from subordinationism, particularly if one bears in mind that he admitted, like the Apologists, the ancient philosophical distinction between *logos endiathetos* (the internal word) and *logos prophorikos* (the uttered word). There are in Tertullian certain texts that seem to insinuate that the Son's generation depends upon creation; yet there are others in which he clearly affirms that the Father has never been alone and, consequently, that the Word has always been with the Father.[^10] For this reason, perhaps the correct interpretation of Tertullian's Trinitarian thought is this: the Father has never been alone; from all eternity he generates the Word, and the Father and the Son spirate the Holy Spirit; but with creation, salvation, and sanctification, they can manifest themselves *ad extra*.[^11] In any event, it is clear that for Tertullian it is the processions—that is, the fact that the Son and the Spirit proceed from the Father—that confer upon them both unity in the divine substance and mutual distinction among themselves.[^12]

The economy in which the divine Persons are manifested *ad extra* expresses the order existing in the intimate life of God. This order springs from the order of origin in which they proceed from the Father. It is this origin, in turn, that allows one to speak of unity in the Trinity. Tertullian's explanation of how unity and trinity are reconciled in God rests upon his conviction that the divine substance is supremely simple and unique. Hence, the fact that the Father communicates his substance to the Son and to the Holy Spirit implies that he imparts it in its totality and uniqueness, for this substance is indivisible.

#### Divine Unity

Tertullian speaks of divine unity keeping in mind not only Trinitarian doctrine, but also the refutation of polytheism, Gnosticism, and the various dualisms of his day. He does so even to demonstrate the reasonableness of the existence of God. To show God's existence he makes use of two principal arguments: the grandeur of creation, and the testimony of the human soul (*testimonium animae*).[^13] This God, attained through the contemplation of created reality and the spontaneous impulses of the soul, is good, wise, and just. He possesses in an unlimited manner the perfections we behold in creatures.[^14]

He is a God who possesses fullness of life, who has created all things from nothing, and who governs the cosmos with his providence.[^15] As Tertullian writes against Marcion: "God is the supreme great, established in eternity, unbegotten, unmade, without beginning, without end."[^16] Precisely because he is the supreme good and the greatest of all beings, God must be unique: "That which is the supreme great must be unique, having no equal, so as not to cease to be the supreme great (...) If God is not one, he is not God."[^17]

For this reason there is in him but one substance, one status, one power.[^18] The multiplicity of persons does not multiply the divine substance.

There is unity in God, but not solitude. For Tertullian, divine unity is not "an abstract monad, but a living, successive gradation in revelation, an economic triad. The Trinitarian economy in the world thus becomes the cause of our knowledge of the Trinitarian order. Tertullian does not understand the economy as something purely extrinsic to God, but as something that penetrates into the internal differences between God, his Word, and the pre-existent Spirit."[^19]

#### *Substantia* and *Persona*

Tertullian employs the term *substantia* (substance) to affirm the reality of the Son against the modalists. By this term he intends to convey that the Son has ontological consistency, precisely because he proceeds from the substance of the Father. The Incarnation, therefore, is not a mere mode of manifestation of the Father, but the visible manifestation of Someone who is distinct from Him. The Son is of the same substance as the Father, but he does not possess that substance in the same manner as the Father. According to Tertullian, only the Father possesses the totality of the divine substance (*summa substantia, tota substantia*), a living and fruitful substance that is communicated without undergoing division. The Son is a derivation (*derivatio totius et portio*).[^20]

Tertullian utilises the term *persona* (person) to express the distinction existing in God among the three who constitute the Trinity. He took the term *persona* from legal terminology. This allowed him to consider each of the divine Persons as divine "titleholders" (*titulares*) of the substantial unity,[^21] without running the risk of modalism inherent in *prosopon*, its Greek equivalent, which was borrowed from the masks worn by actors on the theatrical stage.

This does not mean that Tertullian employed the noun *persona* with the full philosophical depth that subsequent authors would accord it. Tertullian does not attempt to probe into what constitutes the "personality" of the three; it suffices for him to use this word to designate the three who possess the one and identical divine substance. Yet this in itself is an immense contribution to Trinitarian doctrine. It is fair to say that, in this matter, "his chief title to renown is to have opposed the Trinity of Persons to the unity of substance. He saw the necessity of reserving one word to express what is common and unique in God, and another word for what is distinct and plural. It is not a mere question of words, but of profound reflection."[^22]

#### *Una Substantia in Tribus*

With Tertullian the formula *tres personae, una substantia* began to take shape in the Latin Church, a formula destined to become foundational in Trinitarian theology.[^23] Father and Son, Tertullian writes, "are two, by reason of person and not of substance, under the aspect of distinction and not of division. Everywhere I maintain one substance in three who cohere."[^24]

The text cited looks directly to the union of the divine Persons in the economy of creation and salvation, but it is clear that this distinction points likewise to the intratrinitarian order, whereby the distinction between person and substance opens explicitly onto a formula symmetrical to that which we have seen employed by the Greeks: three hypostases, one substance or essence (*tres hypostaseis, mia ousia*). Indeed, while it is true that Tertullian perceived the distinction between Father and Son in connection with the *oikonomia*, he also took note of the fact that this distinction is ultimately grounded in the *dispositio* of the divine substance itself.

Tertullian's great campaign against monarchianism occurred during his Montanist period. He had already spoken of the Holy Spirit earlier, along the lines of Saint Irenaeus. It is natural that during his Montanist period—given the pre-eminence accorded by Montanism to the revelations of the Spirit—he took an even keener interest in the theology of the Spirit. This theology was likewise framed with the *oikonomia* as its principal reference point. The Spirit is considered above all within the framework of salvation history. This holds true especially for his assertion regarding the origin of the Spirit: he proceeds from the Father through the Son (*a Patre per Filium*).[^25] Yet the fact that he viewed the order of the *oikonomia* as so intimately united with the internal disposition of the Trinity enabled Tertullian to refer this procession to the very being of God. Hence he considered the Son and the Spirit as *consortes* (sharers) in the Father's sole divine substance, which is "one substance in three who cohere" (*una substantia in tribus cohaerentibus*). They are distinguished as Persons, without being divided in substance.[^26]

### Contributions of Saint Hilary of Poitiers

Tertullian was followed by Novatian († c. 257), whose *De Trinitate*, composed to refute monarchianism, provides the first systematic synthesis of Trinitarian doctrine written in the West. There follows, in the fourth century, the rich personality of Saint Hilary of Poitiers; his *De Trinitate* occupies a paramount place in the development of Trinitarian doctrine. In the West, Hilary spans the same epoch that in the East extends from Saint Athanasius to Saint Basil. Born around the year 310, apparently in Poitiers, he was elected bishop of that city in 350. His struggle against the Arians led to his exile in Phrygia (356), where he gained direct acquaintance with the theological disputes unfolding in the wake of the Council of Nicaea among Anomoeans, Homoiousians, and Homoousians, and came to appreciate the diverse doctrinal nuances present in the positions of the semi-Arians. It was during these years of exile that he composed a substantial part of his twelve books *De Trinitate*, with the purpose of setting forth orthodox Trinitarian doctrine and refuting Arianism.[^27] He focused, therefore, upon the questions concerning the Father and the Son, without entering in detail into the questions regarding the Holy Spirit.

The starting point of Hilary's theology is an unreserved adherence to the teaching of the New Testament, and more specifically to the Trinitarian doctrine contained in the accounts of Jesus' baptism and in the missionary mandate (Mt 28:19). From Tertullian he inherited the distinction *natura (substantia) – persona*. Grounded upon this distinction, Hilary refuted not only Arianism, but also modalism and Sabellianism.[^28] His doctrine rests upon two fundamental assertions: the divinity of the Son does not imply the existence of two gods; and the unity of God does not imply that God is a solitary God, but rather a communion of substance between Father and Son.[^29]

When analysing the relationship between Father and Son, Saint Hilary takes generation (*generatio*) as his key concept to argue for both their unity and their distinction. The consubstantiality of the Son with the Father means that he was made neither out of nothing nor from pre-existing matter, as though his origin were the result of a creative act; rather, he proceeds from the Father by an ineffable generation, without severance, division, or partition of the divine substance. The Father communicated his entire substance to the Son without forfeiting anything of himself, for this generation must not be understood as an extension (*protensio*) or a transfusion (*transfusio*), but as an eternal generation (*aeterna generatio*). Consequently, the one and identical nature exists in both (*in utroque*): living from the Living One, true from the True One, perfect from the Perfect One (*a vivente vivus, a vero verus, a perfecto perfectus*).[^30] In the light of these affirmations, it was straightforward for Hilary to distinguish the eternal filiation of the Word from our adoptive filiation.[^31]

For this reason, the generation of the Word can only be conceived as eternal, so that it cannot be said of him that there was a time when he did not exist. It suffices to consider—argues Saint Hilary—the reciprocal relationship between fatherhood and sonship to be convinced that there was never a moment when the Father was not Father, nor a moment when God was devoid of thought.[^32]

Saint Hilary speaks with complete lucidity of the distinction of persons (*discretio personarum*) in God as something contrasted with the unity of nature.[^33] The unity of nature arises from the fact that the three divine Persons are one and the same God; the *discretio personarum* arises from their origin. The formulas used by Saint Hilary display great clarity: between Father and Son there is an undifferentiated, undivided nature (*natura indifferens, indiscreta*);[^34] there is an equality of nature (*aequalitas naturae*), a natural unity (*unitas naturalis*), an undivided nature (*natura non dividua*).[^35] The distinction of persons in God entails no separation among them. Between Father and Son there exists a perfect communion of substance. Both are the one and identical God: *unus Deus uterque* (both are one God).[^36] Hilary, employing language that had already become familiar, reiterates that God is *unus*, but not *solitarius*.[^37] God is *unus uterque*—God is one and, at the same time, the one and the other—in the personal property (*proprietas*) of the persons, in the relationship of perfect reciprocity existing between them.[^38]

This reciprocity can exist only between two persons in whom there is numerical unity of substance,[^39] owing to the fact that in God generation is spiritual, not material. This can occur solely in the generation of the Word, and it implies that the Father abides in the Son and the Son in the Father. "It is not a condition of corporeal natures that they should be mutually in one another, so as to have the perfect unity of subsisting nature, or that the abiding generation of the Only-Begotten should be inseparable from the truth of the Father's divinity. This belongs uniquely to the Only-Begotten God."[^40]

Saint Hilary begins here to use the expression *subsistentis naturae* with reference to the Persons—a concept that would assume immense importance in subsequent centuries when refining the definition of person. Of equal importance is his insistence, alongside the *discretio personarum*, upon the reality of *circuminsessio* (mutual inexistence or coinherence)—namely, the fact that the divine Persons are distinct, yet abide perpetually one in the other: the one is in the other, the Father is in the Son and the Son in the Father on account of the indissoluble unity of nature, without being confused with one another and without being separated.[^41]

Saint Hilary's doctrine concerning the Holy Spirit is less developed than that concerning the Father and the Son. Aiming simultaneously to oppose the subordinationism of Arius and the modalism of Sabellius, Saint Hilary underscores that the Holy Spirit is not a creature, for Scripture clearly affirms—he argues—that the Spirit proceeds from the Father, even though the mode of this procession has not been revealed to us (cf. Jn 15:26). The Holy Spirit is the *donum* (gift) and *munus* (bounty) of God to mankind for their sanctification. As such, the Holy Spirit must not be confused either with the Father or with the Son, who sends him.[^42] Hilary suggests that the Holy Spirit, as gift, proceeds from the Father and the Son: *Patre et Filio auctoribus confitendus est* (he must be confessed as having the Father and the Son as his authors).[^43] Nevertheless, Saint Hilary does not designate the Holy Spirit as *persona*, but as *res*, perhaps because "Hilary bound the concept of divine person to that of generation (...) to the extent of avoiding the term 'person' in connection with the Holy Spirit lest He, too, should appear to be begotten."[^44]

From the passages cited, it is evident that Saint Hilary's pneumatology is situated fully within an economic-salvific perspective; that is to say, in most passages the Spirit appears in his character as the gift of the Father and the Son, without explicit mention of his intratrinitarian life. Nonetheless, Saint Hilary describes the activity of the Holy Spirit *ad extra* as possessing the characteristics proper to a person, and at no point does he restrict the being of the Holy Spirit to the sphere of the economy.[^45] His "personality" is therefore sufficiently intimated.[^46]

### The Doctrine of Saint Augustine

With Saint Augustine we reach beyond doubt the culminating peak of Latin patristic thought. He is one of the most eminent geniuses of humanity, as much for his philosophical depth as for his theological stature and mystical elevation; he is the Father of the Church whose thought exerted the greatest influence upon medieval theology. Much of this treatise is nothing other than a continuous dialogue with his thought; hence in this chapter we shall confine ourselves to presenting the essential lines of his teaching concerning the mystery of God, so as not to leave incomplete the historical survey we have been pursuing.

Saint Augustine was born in Thagaste in the year 354, only a few years after the celebration of the Council of Nicaea (325). His mother, Saint Monica, instilled in him the fundamental Christian truths. After his initial schooling in Thagaste, Augustine was sent to Madaura, where he received a thorough classical education. It was there, through contact with pagan classics, that his Christian faith grew cold. In 371 he studied rhetoric in Carthage. In 373, upon reading Cicero's *Hortensius*, he experienced that conversion to philosophy which he recounts in the third book of the *Confessions*, and adhered to Manichaeism.[^47] In 374 he returned to Thagaste. His ties with Manichaeism gradually cooled until in 383, now in Rome, he abandoned Manichaeism and underwent a crisis of scepticism. In 384 he moved to Milan as professor of rhetoric.

In Milan, Augustine attended the preaching of Saint Ambrose every Sunday for two years (from 384 to 386). Ambrose's sermons, by resolving several Manichaean objections regarding the nature of God and the origin of evil, marked the beginning of Augustine's return to the Church. In 386 Augustine's conversion took place.[^48] That same year, he read deeply the works of the Neoplatonists, meditated at length on Saint Paul, decided to renounce both the profession of rhetorician and marriage, and prepared himself to receive baptism. His baptism took place in Milan on the night of Holy Saturday in 387 at the hands of Saint Ambrose, together with his friend Alypius and his son Adeodatus.

In 387 Augustine journeyed to Africa and returned to Rome. In 388 he left Rome for good and returned to Thagaste. There he led a monastic life with a small circle of friends. In 391 he was ordained priest in Hippo. Probably in 395 he was consecrated coadjutor bishop to Valerius, the bishop of Hippo. In 396, upon the death of Valerius, Augustine succeeded him as bishop of Hippo. From 400 until 430 Augustine wrote his major works. He died in the year 430, while the Vandals under Genseric were besieging Hippo. The thought of Saint Augustine constitutes a sublime synthesis of classical culture, philosophy, and Christian faith—a synthesis that remains a watershed moment in the history of Christian thought.

The fundamental lines or principles of Augustinian philosophy may be summarised, following Agostino Trapé, in three primary traits:

a) The first is borrowed from the Neoplatonists and consists in an invitation to interiority: in the inner man, says Saint Augustine, dwells the truth.[^49] This interior truth is superior even to the human mind; it cannot be perceived by the senses, but only by the intellect, and is endowed with the following characteristics: objectivity, necessity, universality, and therefore irrefutability.

b) The second is the principle of participation. It consists in the affirmation that every good is either good by its very nature, or good by participation. In the first case, it is the Supreme Good; in the second case, it is a limited good. This is a principle that Saint Augustine applies with profound wisdom to demonstrate the existence of God, enunciating it thus in *De vera religione*: "Every good is either God or from God."[^50]

c) The third principle may be defined as the principle of immutability. Saint Augustine formulates it as follows: "True, genuine, and authentic being belongs solely to immutable being."[^51] Only immutable being is absolutely simple.[^52]

Augustinian philosophy, resting upon these three fundamental principles, centres upon two essential themes: God and man. Thus Saint Augustine expresses it in the *Soliloquies*: "I desire to know God and the soul. Nothing more? Nothing whatever."[^53] So too he expresses it in this celebrated prayer: "O God, who art ever the same, let me know myself, let me know thee."[^54] In Augustinian thought, God and man are inseparably united, for in Augustine's reflections on man what stands out above all is that man is the image of God (*imago Dei*). The image ever reflects the model, and the model appears reflected in its image. For this reason, Augustine studies man in order to know God, as in the concluding books of *De Trinitate*, and studies God in order to know man in his history, as in many pages of the *Confessions*, which open, as it were, with these two questions: *What art thou to me, Lord? What am I to thee?*[^55]

#### The Itinerary of Man to God

It has been written that not even during his transient crisis of scepticism did Saint Augustine come to doubt the existence of God and his providence. The denial of God's existence always seemed to Augustine the madness of a few (*insania ista paucorum est*). Although God is not the object of immediate intuition, his existence is so evident to human reason that a simple reflection suffices to affirm it with complete certainty.[^56]

Perhaps for this reason Saint Augustine did not set out to construct a systematic series of "proofs" for God's existence, methodically arranged, but rather strove to describe the itinerary of the mind to God. In doing so, he adduced a considerable number of cosmological, psychological, and moral arguments. These arguments practically all share the same schema. As Gilson observes, "all the Augustinian ways to God follow analogous itineraries, from the exterior to the interior, and from the interior to the superior. Discovered by this method, the God of Saint Augustine offers himself as a reality at once intimate to thought and transcendent to thought. His presence is attested by every true judgement, whether scientific, aesthetic, or moral; yet his nature eludes us."[^57]

The earliest and most extensive Augustinian exposition of the itinerary of the mind to God is found in *De vera religione* (29, 52 – 39, 73). Saint Augustine describes a movement that ascends from exterior things to interior things, from inferior things to superior things, and from the mutable to the immutable.[^58] Its essential moments are three, according to Augustine's recommendation: question the world, turn within yourself, transcend yourself.[^59] Here is how he describes this spiritual itinerary in the *Confessions*:

> "I asked the earth, and it said: 'I am not he'; and all things that are in it confessed the same (...) I asked the heavens, the sun, the moon, and the stars: 'Neither are we the God whom you seek,' they answered. And I said to all the things that stand about the door of my flesh: 'Tell me of my God, since you are not he; tell me something of him.' And they cried out with a loud voice: 'He made us' (...) Then I turned toward myself, and said to myself: 'Who are you?' And I answered: 'A man.' And behold, there are in me two parts, a body and a soul; the one exterior, the other interior. By which of these ought I to have sought my God, whom I had already sought through bodies from earth to heaven? (...) The interior part is the better, for to it all bodily messengers report, as to a presiding officer and judge (...) The inner man knows these things through the ministry of the outer; I, the inner man, know these things; I, the soul, know them through the senses of my body."[^60]

What the inner man perceives upon admiring the mutable beauty of the world is that this beauty must proceed from the Immutable: "Behold, heaven and earth exist, and they cry out that they were made, for they change and vary. Indeed, whatever is not made and yet exists cannot have anything in it that was not there before (...) They cry out also that they did not make themselves (...) It is thou, Lord, who madest them; thou art beautiful, for they are beautiful (...) Yet they are neither beautiful, nor good, nor do they exist in the manner in which thou art, their Creator; in comparison with whom they are neither beautiful, nor good, nor have they being."[^61]

Having received the message of God's existence upon contemplating the beauty of mutable things, the mind does not terminate its inquiry with the mere conclusion that a necessary Being exists who has created all things; rather, it is now, upon knowing himself, that man begins the true itinerary, the ascent to God. Man recognises himself and intuits himself as a being who exists, who thinks, and who loves. He can therefore ascend to God along the pathway of being, of truth, and of love.[^62]

Here is one of the celebrated passages in which Saint Augustine proposes the ascent to God from within man:

> "Do not wish to go outside; return into yourself, for in the inner man dwells the truth; and if you find that your nature is mutable, transcend yourself. But remember, when you raise yourself above yourself, that you are raising yourself above your rational soul. Direct your steps, therefore, to where the light of reason itself is kindled."[^63]

For upon entering into himself, man discovers truth and goodness in the deepest recesses of his soul. Thus the transition is made from the multiple to the one, from the mutable to the immutable, from partial truths to absolute and eternal Truth.[^64]

#### Ontological and Epistemological Foundation

Alongside the proof of God's existence from the order, beauty, and contingency of the world, Saint Augustine employs with great vigour what is called the psychological way—namely, the demonstration of God's existence from the presence in our minds of universal and necessary ideas. In synthesis, this argument may be expressed thus: the ideas of truth, justice, and other kindred notions that govern our lives cannot originate from ourselves, for they transcend us in their necessity and universality. They can proceed solely from the action of God, who is the principle of all being and, on that account, the principle of all knowledge.[^65]

Here is an eloquent passage:

> "Guided by thee, I entered into my inmost being; I was able to do so because thou didst become my helper. I entered, and beheld with the eye of my soul (...) above that same eye of my soul, above my mind, an immutable Light (...) It was not above my mind as oil is above water, nor as heaven is above earth; it was above me because it made me, and I was below it because I was made by it. He who knows the truth knows this Light, and he who knows it knows eternity. Love knows it. O eternal Truth, true Love, and beloved Eternity! Thou art my God; for thee I sigh day and night. And when first I knew thee, thou didst lift me up, that I might see that there was something for me to see, and that I was not yet capable of seeing it."[^66]

The Augustinian reasoning is lucid: man discovers in the acts of his spiritual life—in his thinking and in his willing—eternal and necessary truths. He may ignore them, be mistaken about them, or rebel against them; yet they remain invulnerable, exalted above all things, sitting as judges over the human spirit and human knowledge. They are subject neither to space nor to time; they are nothing that proceeds from mortal man, but rather, in revealing themselves within man, they disclose another Being who is superhuman and supratemporal. Behind all that is imperfect, we touch the perfect; behind the relative, we touch the absolute; behind the human lies the transcendent. By this path we draw near to God.[^67]

It should be made clear that in this Augustinian framework, God is not reached along the path of a syllogism based strictly upon the principle of causality—whereby contingent being requires a necessary cause; rather, God appears present insofar as he is reflected in whatever truth, beauty, and goodness is manifested in created things. For this reason, already in what is true, small, and limited among the goods of this world, the immutable Good is manifested to us. This is so because this eternal and immutable Good is the ontological and epistemological foundation of every good and of all truth.

Augustinian reasoning is grounded upon his understanding of ontological truth—that is, truth as an objective reality existing beyond the individual human intellect. It is a reality that is universal, eternal, and the judge of human knowledge. This truth is superior to the human intellect, since the human intellect is judged by it. Augustine's example is well known: the truth $7 + 3 = 10$ is a purely intelligible, universal, and eternal truth: always and everywhere it will be true that seven and three make ten. It is, then, an immutable truth, just as the rules of wisdom—moral principles—are immutable truths. Truths of this kind are normative: they govern our reason, which judges the rectitude of things according to those truths and grounded upon them; but reason does not judge those truths. It is they that dictate what rectitude is. Such truths are, therefore, norms of our judgement; they judge our reason and are consequently superior to it. In contemplating these ideas, we find ourselves in the presence of something superior to reason: necessary truths, which can only be a reflection within us of eternal Truth—that is, they can be nothing other than a reflection of God, who illumines us.

In this statement two affirmations are contained: these truths are eternal because they exist in God as eternal ideas; and we attain and know these truths as necessary because God illumines our intellect. We are thus in the presence of the Augustinian theory of illumination.[^68]

The theory of illumination occupies a central place in Augustinian thought. Saint Augustine formulates it in these words: "Our illumination is a participation in the Word, that is, in the life which is the light of men" (cf. Jn 1:4).[^69] It is a theory that has given rise to diverse interpretations, and stands in close connection with other key themes of Augustinian doctrine, foremost among which is the theory of exemplary ideas.

Saint Augustine, of course, reinterprets the Platonic theory of exemplary ideas, understanding it in a Christian sense and imparting to it a Trinitarian dimension. The exemplary ideas—models or archetypes of all things—reside in the Eternal Word, equal in essence and consubstantial with the Father and the Holy Spirit. This must be so, Augustine argues, because if God is the Creator of all, there must be contained in his intellect, as archetypes, the ideas of all possible things, just as in the mind of a craftsman there exists the idea of what he intends to fashion or construct. Given God's simplicity, these ideas are not distinct from God, but are identical and consubstantial with the divine essence itself. For Augustine, the realm of ideas is not an entity alien to God, but consists of the very ideas existing in his eternal and consubstantial Intellect, which is the Word:

> "Ideas are primary forms, stable and immutable reasons of things, which have not been fashioned by themselves, and on that account are eternal, always subsisting in the same manner, and contained in the divine intellect; and since they neither come into being nor perish, it is said that everything that comes into being and perishes, and everything that can come into being and perish, is formed according to them."[^70]

Thus these exemplary ideas are the fountainhead of being, the immutable foundation of all mutable realities in the sensible world, and at the same time the source of the intelligibility of things and the ground of certainty and science. Things exist, are intelligible, and are true insofar as they correspond to the exemplary ideas existing in the divine intellect from all eternity. Consequently, all beings—and pre-eminently the human soul—are images of God, for they are fashioned in accordance with these exemplary ideas. This occurs with particular splendour in the human soul, which bears imprinted upon itself the image of the Trinity. The soul is a mind (*mens*), an intellect from which springs knowledge (*notitia*), and in relation to knowledge love (*amor*) is brought forth. This exemplarism enables the Augustinian endeavour to ascend from self-knowledge to the knowledge of God, and even to ascend from man to the mystery of the Trinity, precisely through contemplating the trace of the divine image in man in his acts of knowing and loving.[^71]

In seeking in man the analogy of the Trinity, Saint Augustine finds it in the life of the soul, and formulates it in those celebrated triads: *mens, notitia, amor*, or *memoria, intelligentia, voluntas*:[^72]

> "When the mind loves itself, two things exist: the mind and its love; and when the mind knows itself, there are likewise two realities: the mind and its knowledge. Therefore the mind, its love, and its knowledge (*notitia*) are three things, and the three are one; and if they are perfect, they are equal (...) When the mind knows and loves itself, the trinity—mind, knowledge, love—subsists in those three realities, without mixture or confusion. And although each has subsistence in itself, they are mutually all in all: one in two, two in one, and consequently all in all."[^73]

#### The Augustinian Tradition and Ontologism

The theory of illumination occupies a central place in Augustinian doctrine, and has been diversely interpreted with regard to its nature and scope. Indeed, the Augustinian theory of illumination seems to presuppose an immediate contact between the mind and the intelligible—that is, it seems to imply in man an intuition of the intelligible without necessarily passing through the cognition of bodily objects.[^74]

It is certain that Saint Augustine speaks of an illumination of the human intellect by the Word in order that the intellect may attain truth. Three fundamental reasons support his affirmation of this illumination:

1. The first arises from the intrinsic characteristics of human judgements, which always possess an absolute and universal value. Indeed, if I affirm something to be true, I necessarily exclude its contradictory as false. Whence comes this absolute value to the judgement of the mind? The question is clearer in moral precepts, for example, in the concept of justice. Our idea of justice is immutable, Augustine argues, because it is a reflection of the divine idea of justice; therefore, our idea of justice must be illumined by the light of divine justice. Here is an important text: "It belongs to a higher reason to judge of these corporeal things according to spiritual and eternal reasons, which, unless they were above the human mind, would certainly not be immutable; and unless something of our own were conjoined to them, we could not judge of corporeal things according to them."[^75]

2. A similar conclusion is reached by considering that the discovery of truth is an entirely personal act. According to Augustine, a teacher can communicate to the disciple only certain signs of the truth; the disciple must find it within himself, since signs merely guide and direct, but are not the truth itself. Here is an eloquent passage: "In all that we understand, we consult the truth, not as speaking from outside, but as present within the mind itself, after our words have prompted us to consult it."[^76]

3. Finally, according to Saint Augustine, illumination is a corollary of man's creation by God and of the manner in which God concurs with every operation of secondary causes. If we require God in order to exist, we also require him in order to act. Here is the argument applied by Saint Augustine to the act of cognition: "Since our nature has need of God as its author, there is no doubt that to know the truth we must have God as our teacher (*doctor*)."[^77] For Augustine, the created intellect requires the light of God—which is its sun—to attain truth, just as the will requires the grace of God—the Supreme Good—to attain goodness, which is virtue.

How is this Augustinian thought to be interpreted? Throughout history, three principal interpretations have been advanced:

a) *The Pantheistic Interpretation.* According to this reading, the Augustinian theory of illumination would mean that God, as universal reason and unique intellect, perceives the truth within us, and we perceive it in him and through him. Augustine would thus be an Averroist *avant la lettre*.[^78]

b) *The Ontologistic Interpretation.* According to this interpretation, the Augustinian position would imply that our soul contemplates the divine Being in himself, and in beholding him directly, sees in him the divine ideas, the eternal and immutable truths. Consequently, it would know creatures from the archetypal ideas of God. This was the view of Malebranche, for instance. Ontologism, as is well known, reappears periodically in philosophical and theological thought (e.g. Gioberti and Rosmini). It seems clear that ontologism—the conception that God is not only our *primum ontologicum*, but also our *primum logicum*—cannot legitimately claim the support of Augustine's theory of illumination, nor shelter behind the authority of the Bishop of Hippo. In the first place, because Saint Augustine explicitly denies that man enjoys an intuitive vision of God in this earthly life;[^79] in the second place, because for Augustine God—the Light of the soul—never appears as an object that we behold and from which we see other beings, but rather as Someone who sheds upon our intellect that light by which we are enabled to know.

Augustine's analogies are telling: God is the Master, the Sun, the Light. These images convey that God is not the object whose vision enables us to perceive eternal ideas, but the light that illumines other objects. Saint Augustine speaks of the influence of this light upon man as an impression that transfers eternal truth to our soul, stamping it there much as a signet ring leaves its impress upon wax without departing from the ring.[^80]

c) *The Thomistic and Scholastic Interpretation.* The manner in which Saint Thomas Aquinas, following numerous Scholastics, understands the Augustinian doctrine is as follows: God is the Light of the soul as the creative cause of the intellect and as the source of all truth, for the divine ideas are the archetype to which all knowledge must conform if it is to be true.[^81]

It may be said that for Augustine, our intellect—an imperfect yet real image of the supreme Intellect—possesses a cognitive power derived from God's own knowing power, and is an expression in the world, in human fashion, of ineffable Truth. It is capable of knowing creatures because they represent lower degrees of the same reality. This accounts, according to Saint Augustine, for our knowledge of first notions and the general principles of reason, which subsequently enter into all further cognition. It is the knowledge of the transcendental notions—being, unity, goodness, beauty—bestowed upon all men along with human nature.

This emerges from a celebrated Augustinian passage highlighting both the activity of the human intellect and its dependence upon divine illumination:

> "I ran over all those fields and strived to distinguish and evaluate each thing according to its worth, receiving some through the senses and questioning them, feeling others mingled with myself (...); yet neither was I myself, when doing this, nor my faculty by which I did it, nor even that faculty itself, was Thou; for thou wert the unfailing Light by which I consulted concerning all things: whether they were, what they were, and how they were to be valued; and I heard thee teaching and commanding me."[^82]

For Saint Augustine, God is the centre of all things. He is the Creator and the fountain of being; he is supreme Truth and intellectual Light, the source of the truth of things—a truth attained by the human intellect because the intellect is itself a participation in and an image of the divine Intellect, and possesses God's illuminating power to attain Truth. As Charles Boyer writes, the doctrine of illumination is an invitation to unceasing prayer in the search for truth. Indeed, if God is our inner Master and our knowledge presupposes his actual influence, our prayer will be to beseech that his light may penetrate to the depths of our soul and the depths of truth.[^83] This is not ontologism, but the simple confession that God is more intimate to man than man is to himself: upon him rests man's existence, and upon him depend man's acts of knowing and loving.

#### Providence

Augustine confesses that even during the period when he was seduced by Manichaeism, he never doubted divine providence: "I always believed," he writes, "that thou didst exist and hadst care of the human race, although I was ignorant of what was to be thought concerning thy substance, and what way led or brought us back to thee."[^84]

His intimate acquaintance with Manichaeism and his spiritual struggle to escape it impelled him throughout his life to deepen his understanding of the providence of God. This was one of the pivotal battlegrounds of Christian doctrine against the Manichaeans. It was urgent to demonstrate against them that the existence of evil contradicts neither the existence of a single First Principle nor the supreme goodness of this sole Source of all things. Later, Saint Augustine had to confront the Pelagians, who denied that the miseries besetting man were consequences of original sin. To deny original sin, the Pelagians argued that it would be unjust for divine providence to permit the penalties of original sin in infants who had not yet been born when that sin was committed. Saint Augustine had to defend the justice of God while maintaining that hereditary consequences of original sin do exist and are transmitted with the sin itself, and that this in no way impugns God's goodness and providence. Amid such diverse difficulties, Saint Augustine retained the firm conviction that it is evident, even to natural reason, that all things are subject to divine providence, and that the universe is a harmonious hymn in the variety of its notes and silences.[^85]

The question is this: *If God is good, whence comes evil (unde malum)?* Saint Augustine adopts three lines of argument to resolve this dilemma:

1. The first is the affirmation of the necessary imperfection of the creature. That is to say: either one denies to God the possibility of creating, or one must concede that every created being—by the mere fact of not being perfection itself, but possessing perfection only in a limited measure—is imperfect, and consequently vulnerable to evil. Yet this vulnerability of the imperfect, created being must not obscure the goodness of the perfections it possesses. It is this goodness that justifies God's decree to create it.

2. The second line addresses the existence of moral evil. In a certain sense, physical evil—even suffering—is a relative evil. Its existence poses specific questions to divine providence. But moral evil—sin—is evil in an absolute sense and introduces different urgencies. Moral evil resides in human freedom. Saint Augustine demonstrates the coexistence of created freedom—and hence its fallibility—with divine providence as the explanation of sin. The limitation of created freedom, which divine providence sovereignly respects, is the condition under which sin occurs. Knowing how the Manichaeans denied freedom, Augustine vigorously defends the reality of free will (*liberum arbitrium*).[^86]

Sin arises, then, from the defectibility of human freedom. Yet freedom in itself can be perfect, in which case it would be impeccable, as is the case in God. Hence this difficulty arises: if freedom can exist without the imperfection of being able to sin, why was it not created wholly impeccable? Saint Augustine replies by pointing to the harmony and beauty of the universe, composed of lights and shadows. Following Saint Paul, he asserts that God permitted this because he could draw a greater good from sin: "God judged it greater power and greater perfection to bring good out of evil than to suffer no evil to exist."[^87] Thus, in the interplay between divine providence and human freedom, Saint Augustine vigorously upholds against the Pelagians the necessity of grace to perform any good, while simultaneously underlining God's profound respect for human freedom and directing attention to the perfection that God can draw from imperfection itself, and even from sin.

3. The third line is the constant affirmation of God's lordship over history. Saint Augustine is the first theologian to develop a comprehensive theology of history, found principally in *De Civitate Dei*. Indeed, all the books of *De Civitate Dei* constitute a meditation upon divine providence in history and, at the same time, an affirmation that history is genuinely made by men. Divine providence respects human freedom without ever relinquishing its sovereign power over history itself. On the one hand, God is immutable, perfect, and eternal, and cannot be identified with historical becoming; on the other hand, whatever represents good and progress in history springs from the inclination imparted by God to human nature and will, whereas all decline is the fruit of sin. No resistance can thwart the fulfilment of God's designs. God's permission of evil remains sheer permission; God is constrained neither by historical events nor by the free choices of the human will.

#### The Treatise *De Trinitate*: Person and Relation

With Saint Augustine we reach the zenith of Latin patristic thought. His Trinitarian doctrine constitutes a definitive advance upon prior tradition and determined the subsequent development of Western theology to such an extent that Saint Augustine deserves to be called not only the *Doctor Gratiae*, but also the *Doctor Trinitatis* on account of his monumental contributions to this mystery.[^88] His Trinitarian teaching is condensed pre-eminently in the fifteen books of *De Trinitate*, to which we shall direct our attention.

*De Trinitate* is Saint Augustine's primary dogmatic work and one of his masterpieces. It was composed in two stages: the first twelve books between 399 and 412, and the final three around 420 and 426.[^89] The architecture of the work is as follows: confession of faith and careful examination of Scriptural teaching on the Trinity (Books 1–4); defence of the dogma and speculative treatment (Books 5–7); introduction to the mystical knowledge of God (Book 8); inquiry into the image of the Trinity in man (Books 9–14); and summary and completion of the treatise (Book 15).[^90]

Saint Augustine is ever conscious of the transcendence of the mystery of God; no one has insisted more forcefully upon the incomprehensibility of God. At the same time, convinced that man is created in the image of God, he searches within man for vestiges of the divine intimacy. Above all, his theological labour and speculative vigour are governed by one supreme ideal: the adoration and love of God. Here are words from the close of *De Trinitate*, vibrating with mystical fervour, which disclose the spirit in which the work was conceived:

> "O Lord my God, I believe in thee, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Truth would not say: *Go, and baptise all nations in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit* (Mt 28:19), unless thou wert a Trinity. Nor wouldst thou command us, Lord God, to be baptised in the name of one who is not Lord God. Nor would the divine voice say: *Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God is one Lord* (Deut 6:4), unless thou wert at once a Trinity and one single Lord God (...) Directing my attention to this rule of faith, I have sought thee according to my strength, as far as thou didst give me power, and I have desired to see with my intellect what I believed by faith. I have striven and laboured much. O Lord my God, my only hope, hear me, lest through weariness I should cease to seek thee, but may ever ardently seek thy face. Give me strength to seek, thou who hast caused me to find thee, and hast given me hope of finding thee more and more (...) When the Wise Man speaks of thee in his book, he says: *We say many things, and yet fall short; the sum of our words is: He is all* (Sir 43:27). When we come to thy presence, these many things which we now speak without fully understanding will cease, and thou shalt remain all in all (cf. 1 Cor 15:28). Then we shall sing an eternal song, praising thee who art one, made also one in thee. O Lord, One God, God Trinity! Whatever I have said in these books of mine that comes from thee, may they who are thine acknowledge; if anything in them comes from myself, do thou, Lord, forgive me, and may they who are thine forgive me also."[^91]

Saint Augustine inherited from prior tradition a Trinitarian doctrine already articulated in its essential lines: the three Persons share equally in the one divine nature; this unity is manifested in the fact that, outside their strict personal being, the three Persons are equal in all things. Augustine condenses this insight in the celebrated axiom: *in divinis omnia sunt unum, ubi non obviat relationis oppositio* (in God all things are one where there is no opposition of relation),[^92] a formula in which unity is attributed to the nature, while differences are placed solely in the relations of the Persons to one another.[^93] According to Saint Augustine, therefore, it is necessary to express in the singular all attributes proper to the divine essence: goodness, omnipotence, wisdom, etc. There is only one God who is good, wise, almighty. And one must express in the plural only what pertains to the relative.

The fundamental lines of Augustine's systematisation of Trinitarian theology are framed, moreover, by the following guiding principles:

a) The consideration of the unity of the divine nature as logically prior to the consideration of the diversity of Persons;
b) The uncompromising insistence with which all operations *ad extra* are attributed to the three Persons conjointly (*opera Trinitatis ad extra indivisa sunt*), in order to maintain uncompromised the unity of nature;
c) The explicit appeal to the metaphysical structure of the rational creature as the image of God to establish the analogy of the Trinitarian mystery, grounded upon a psychological explanation.[^94] Convinced that God made man in his image (Gen 1:27), Saint Augustine ascends from the triad *memoria – visio interna – voluntas* (on the sensitive plane), to the triad *memoria – notitia – amor* (on the spiritual plane), and thence to the highest analogy: *memoria Dei – intelligentia Dei – amor in Deum*.

In considering this analogy, it must be borne in mind that Saint Augustine was acutely aware that even the most complete image of the Trinity in the soul never corresponds adequately to the reality of the Trinity itself. For this reason, in the final book he underscores the differences that persist, despite all likeness, between the archetype and the image. While man *has* intellect and love, the entire divine Trinity *is* God.[^95]

In Augustine's contemplation of the Trinitarian mystery, a primary objective is to demonstrate the equality of the Persons (*aequalitas personarum*), precisely because in this equality the unity of the divine essence shines forth. Saint Augustine even leans upon the analogy drawn from the human soul to accentuate this *aequalitas personarum* within the one God. Because the three Persons are fully equal and inseparable both in being and in acting, they constitute one sole and unique God. It is important to note that Augustine "maintains in this way an exposition of the Trinitarian faith that is far closer to the Eastern tradition than might appear at first sight."[^96]

As Augustine states at the opening of the first book, his theological itinerary is this: "First, it must be demonstrated, grounded upon the authority of the Holy Scriptures, whether this is what the faith declares. Then, if God wills and aids us, we may perhaps assist those contentious critics—more puffed up than capable, and on that account afflicted with a grievous ailment—to discover something of which they cannot doubt, so that, because of what they cannot understand, they may question their own intellect rather than the truth itself or the validity of our reasons."[^97] The first part of *De Trinitate* is dedicated, therefore, to demonstrating that the faith of the Church coincides with the teaching of Sacred Scripture, having particularly in view the Arians and semi-Arians. From that foundation Augustine proceeds to demonstrate speculatively the coherence of Christian affirmations concerning the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity.[^98]

The starting point of Augustinian speculation is, then, the unity of God: "With the help of the Lord our God," he writes, "I shall attempt to respond, as best I can, to the question raised by my adversaries: that the Trinity is one only, true God, and how rightly it is said, believed, and understood that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are of one and the same essence or substance."[^99] Augustine endeavours to show that God is Trinity—that God is at once One and Triune: "The Holy Scriptures teach that the Trinity is God; that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are of one and the same substance in inseparable equality; that there is divine uniqueness, and consequently that they are not three gods, but one God."[^100]

Augustine reiterates formulas chosen specifically to accentuate the essential unity of God: *Deus-Trinitas, Trinitas-Unitas, Deus Trinitatis*.[^101] Here is how he summarises the Trinitarian faith: "We believe that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are one God, the Creator and Ruler of all creatures; that the Father is not the Son, nor the Holy Spirit the Father or the Son; that they are a Trinity of Persons in mutual relations, and one single and equal essence."[^102]

Conscious of being the heir to the terminological refinement wrought by preceding Fathers, Augustine took pains to define precisely the terms he employed:

- *Substantia*: "Substance is understood in another sense as that which we are, whatever we are (...) God is a certain substance (...) Substance, therefore, is to be something (...) Hence, in that God is, by that very fact he is substance."[^103]
- *Essentia*: "From that which is being (*esse*), it is called essence (*essentia*) (...) which the Greeks call *ousia*."[^104]
- *Persona*: "What according to our usage we have said of persons must be understood as said of substances (*hypostaseis*) according to the usage of the Greeks. For they say three substances (*hypostaseis*), one essence, just as we say three persons, one essence or substance."[^105]
- *Persona – Substantia*: "Hence for God to be is the same as to subsist, and therefore the Trinity is one essence, and also one substance. Perhaps, then, it is more fittingly said three persons than three substances."[^106]
- *Persona – Substantia*: "We dare not say one essence and three substances, but one essence or substance and three persons."[^107]
- *Persona – Substantia*: "What is meant by of one substance? For instance: if the Father is gold, the Son is gold, and the Holy Spirit is gold. When we say what the Father is, we do not refer to his essence. For Father is not said in relation to himself, but in relation to the Son. In relation to himself he is called God. Hence in that he is God, in that very respect he is substance. And since the Son is of the same substance, without doubt the Son is also God. But when we say that the Father is, because Father is not the name of the substance, but is relative to the Son, we do not say that the Son is the Father in the same way as we say that the Son is God."[^108]

Latin Trinitarian vocabulary thus received its definitive orientation. The concepts of substance and essence (*ousia* and *essentia*) were identified as applying to what God is in his nature—to the absolute in God—while the name *persona* (*hypostasis* and *prosopon*) was reserved for the relative—namely, for the three who subsist in the one identical substance or essence.

The hesitation of the Easterners regarding vocabulary is well known. Saint Athanasius, while admitting the distinction between *ousia* and *hypostasis*, did not condemn those who spoke of three hypostases at the Council of Alexandria in 362. It was Saint Basil, followed by the other Cappadocians, who established the formula of one *ousia* and three *hypostaseis*. The Latin formula—one essence and three persons—seemed suspicious to the Orientals precisely because *persona* translated *prosopon*, whose theatrical connotation seemed to favour a modalist interpretation. Saint Augustine experienced no such misgivings; indeed, he was unacquainted with Saint Basil's writings on the matter. "The Greeks," he writes, "have said one essence, three substances; the Latins: one essence or substance, three persons."[^109] "Yet they, just as they say three substances—three hypostases—might also say three persons."[^110] The reason is simple: these terms—*persona, hypostasis, prosopon*—are not proper names, but are applied to God in an accommodating manner, in order to express the mystery in some way.[^111]

The concept of person would subsequently be refined by Boethius and Richard of Saint Victor, with the aim of applying it to God as accurately as possible. Saint Augustine confined himself to indicating that the term *persona* signifies an absolute perfection, an incommunicable concrete subject. In the Trinity, each Person is referred to the others, and the three are the one substance.[^112]

#### The Relations of Origin

It has already been noted that for Saint Augustine, God is essence or *ousia*. He is a single essence; an essence without accidents; consequently, only God is immutable substance. The Eunomians rested upon this premise to argue that God's essence consists in being unbegotten (*ingeneratus*). For if in God there is nothing accidental, everything predicated of him is predicated according to substance; hence the difference between unbegotten and begotten would entail a difference of essence. Saint Augustine deployed the same fundamental premise to refute them: if everything said of God is said according to substance, when Jesus said "I and the Father are one" (Jn 10:30), he was affirming that Father and Son are consubstantial.

It is clear, however, that not everything predicated of God is predicated according to substance; there is something in God that is predicated neither according to substance nor according to accidents, for in God there are no accidents. There is something within the divine essence that denotes relation: paternity, filiation, spiration. This relation cannot be accidental in God. The Father is eternally related to the Son, and the Son is eternally related to the Father. The Father cannot cease to be Father, nor the Son cease to be Son; and where eternity is the measure of being, no accidental inherence can exist.

It is evident that the Father is Father only because he begets a Son, and the Son is Son only because he is begotten by a Father. Both, however, are the same essence or substance, because they are the one identical God. The relation of paternity-filiation existing between them is compatible with the infinite perfection of God, provided that the relative term is not conceived outside of God, but within the divine essence and perfection itself, and provided that relation in God is never conceived as an accident.[^113] Here is how Saint Augustine formulates it:

> "In God nothing is said according to accident, for nothing can happen to him; yet not everything predicated of him is predicated according to substance. In created and mutable things, whatever is not predicated according to substance is predicated according to accidents; in them everything can be lost or diminished, whether dimensions or qualities (...) In God, however, nothing is asserted according to accident, because there is nothing mutable in him. Yet not everything enunciated of him is said according to substance. Sometimes God is spoken of according to relation (*ad aliquid*). The Father is related to the Son, and the Son to the Father, and this relation is not an accident, because the one is always Father and the other always Son (...) And if the Father were Father in relation to himself and not in relation to the Son, and the Son were related to himself and not to the Father, the name Father and the term Son would be substantial.
>
> "But because the Father is Father by having a Son, and the Son is Son by having a Father, these relations are not according to substance, because each of these persons is related not to himself, but to another person. Nor yet can it be said that relations in God are accidents, because to be Father and to be Son is in them eternal and immutable. Consequently, although it is one thing to be Father and another to be Son, their substance is not diverse (*Quamobrem quamvis diversum sit Patrem esse et Filium esse, non est tamen substantia diversa*), because these names are said not according to substance, but according to relation; and the relative is not an accident, for it is not mutable."[^114]

One single God, one essence, one numerically identical substance. At the same time, we know through revelation that this single essence is possessed by three subsistents—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—who are one and the same God. They are three distinct from one another, yet identical with the divine essence. They are distinct solely and exclusively in their mutual relations; they are not distinct in respect of the divine essence. They are called persons, not because this term adequately expresses what they are, but because no better term is to be found: "When it is asked what (*quid*) these three are, human language labours under great poverty. Yet we say three persons, not in order to express it, but lest we should remain silent."[^115]

We are here at the intimate core of Augustinian Trinitarian thought: in the pre-eminence acquired by the concept of the *relative*—Augustine does not yet employ the abstract noun *relatio*—as opposed to the *substantial*. Not everything predicated of God is predicated in reference to his substance, but in reference to three who are mutually relative: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Augustine thus transcends purely philosophical categories. Guided by the language of Scripture, he examines its teaching with the aid of philosophical terms—substance, accident, relative—imparting to them an entirely new meaning. "In this manner he discovered an ontological reality that had no place in philosophy: the non-accidental relative, the relative that exists in substance and is therefore substantial (...) With the distinction between substance and relation, Saint Augustine was already on the path toward creating a precise terminology proper to the Trinity."[^116] As for the use of the term *persona* to designate the three relatives, we are still at the threshold of a long development, for Augustine had not yet drawn out the full philosophical wealth latent within the concept.[^117]

In any case, it must not be forgotten that what Saint Augustine sets in the foreground is the truth taught by Scripture: that there are in God realities mutually relative and identical with the supremely simple divine essence: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. "Saint Augustine's Trinitarian approach culminates in a concept whose introduction was Augustine's first great achievement in the realm of speculative Trinitarian doctrine: the concept of relation as the expression of the personal differentiation of the one God (...) The coherence of his speculation is shown by the fact that, in order not to endanger the real character of the relations (and therewith of the divine Persons), he never loses sight of the idea of absolute being, to which relation refers. This absolute being can be nothing other than the divine essence, which must furthermore be affirmed as identical with relation. This explanation guarantees likewise the conception of these three relations as purely substantial. They are not accidental determinations of the divine essence, nor do they alter it; they are rather this single divine essence under the aspect of an immanent correlativity. Accordingly, the Persons considered in themselves are the one true God and one identical reality with absolute divine being; whereas in their correlative relationship to one another, they are Father, Son, and Holy Spirit."[^118]

Saint Augustine received from prior tradition as a truth already peacefully possessed that the relationship among Father, Son, and Spirit is grounded in the origin (*origo*) of the divine Persons. This origin takes place through generation in the Son, and through spiration in the Holy Spirit. Augustine also received the explicit doctrine regarding the mode of origin of the Son: the Son proceeds from the Father by generation. The legacy he received concerning the origin of the Holy Spirit was far less explicit. Saint Augustine contributed decisively to the theology of the processions by demonstrating the difference between the manner in which the Son proceeds and that in which the Holy Spirit proceeds. Because he saw in the Holy Spirit the *communio Patris et Filii*, he was compelled to reflect upon the distinction between the origin of the Son and that of the Holy Spirit. To this end he resorted pre-eminently to the psychological analogy. According to Augustine, the procession of the Son is analogous to that of the *intelligentia* from the *mens* or *memoria*, whereas the procession of the Spirit is analogous to that of the *voluntas*, which proceeds from both *mens* and *intelligentia*.

"With the doctrine of the Holy Spirit as the *amor Patris et Filii*, Augustine undoubtedly introduced a most notable development into Trinitarian doctrine. Although it was Hilary of Poitiers who coined the formula *Aeternitas in Patre, species in imagine, usus in munere*, only Augustine carried through consistently to the end the comparison of the Spirit, who proceeds from the Father and the Son, with the love that derives from the lover and the beloved and binds them together."[^119]

The procession of the Holy Spirit is one of the questions that Saint Augustine explicitly proposes to investigate in *De Trinitate*: "It is also asked how the Holy Spirit belongs to that Trinity, since he is begotten neither by the Father nor by the Son, nor by both together, although he is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son."[^120] The Augustinian answer is clear: although not begotten, the Spirit possesses the identical divine substance, because he receives it from the Father and the Son as from a single principle; he receives it *principaliter* from the Father, for the Father is the principle of the divinity and gives also to the Son—who receives all things from the Father—the power to spirate the Holy Spirit.[^121] Saint Augustine thus accepts in all its force the axiom that the Father is the *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*. The Holy Spirit proceeds as Love, and therefore is not begotten, for what is proper to love is not to be an image, but to be weight, communion, gift:[^122]

> "Not without cause in this Trinity is the Son of God alone called the Word of God, and the Holy Spirit alone called the Gift of God, while God the Father alone is he from whom the Word is begotten and from whom the Holy Spirit principally proceeds. I have added 'principally' because the Holy Spirit is found to proceed also from the Son.
>
> "Yet this too the Father gave to him: not as to one already existing and not yet possessing it, but whatever he gave to the Only-Begotten Word, he gave in begetting him. He so begat him, then, that the common Gift should proceed also from him, and the Holy Spirit should be the Spirit of both.
>
> "(...) Hence it is that the Word of God is properly called the Wisdom of God, though the Father also and the Holy Spirit are wisdom.
>
> "And if any of the three divine Persons is appropriately named Love, who could more rightly be so called than the Holy Spirit? Provided always that in that simple and supreme nature, love is not one thing and essence another, either in the Father, or in the Son, or in the Holy Spirit. Nevertheless, the Holy Spirit is with all propriety called Charity (*charitas*)."[^123]

#### The Proper Names of the Persons

In the writings of Saint Augustine, the names of the First Person are three: Father, Principle, and Unbegotten (*Ingenitus*). All three are relative names: "Father is a relative name, as is also Principle. They differ in that whereas Father is said in relation to the Son, Principle is said in relation to all that proceeds from him."[^124] The name Unbegotten is likewise relative, for it is evident that Begotten is a relative name.[^125]

The names of the Son are three: Son, Word, and Image. These three names are equivalent and mutually illuminate one another. The Son is Son as Word, and the Word is Word as Son. The same holds true of the name Image: the Word is Image as Son.

The names of the Holy Spirit are three: Holy Spirit, Gift, and Love. These three names are the most difficult to apply properly to a single divine Person, because they can also be predicated of the divine essence: God is spirit, is holy, and is love. Saint Augustine justifies the application of these names to the Holy Spirit from the *oikonomia*: if the Holy Spirit distributes the gifts of God (cf. 1 Cor 12:11), he is himself Gift in the interior of the Trinity; if he sheds abroad the love of God in our hearts (cf. Rom 5:5), he is himself Love in the Trinity. With good reason it has been written that the theology of the Holy Spirit is Augustine's most original contribution to the treatise on the Trinity.[^126]

#### The Trinitarian Image in Man

In his resolve to penetrate the mystery of the Trinity, Saint Augustine strived to discover images and likenesses drawn from nature and pre-eminently from man, resting upon the conviction that man is created in the image and likeness of God. The reason is straightforward: if God is essentially Trinity, there must be found in man, created in his image, a vestige of that Trinity in which the unity of God is realised.

Saint Augustine discovers in the human mind three images of the Trinity, or rather a single likeness on three ascending levels: a) *mens, notitia, amor*; b) *memoria sui, intelligentia, voluntas*; c) *memoria Dei, intelligentia, amor*. The first two are drawn from the consideration of the soul's operations in general; the third is drawn from the consideration of the soul's operations in the contemplation of God. Each of these images enables one to approach the mystery from a different vantage point, but with a single aim: to demonstrate the consubstantiality of the three divine Persons and, at the same time, to show their distinction and the diverse manner in which the Son and the Holy Spirit proceed from the Father. The diversity existing between the acts of knowing and loving proves an effective aid in establishing this distinction in unity.[^127]

##### a) Mens, Notitia, Amor

> "The mind cannot love itself unless it also knows itself, for how can it love what it does not know? (...) How can the mind know another mind if it is ignorant of itself? (...) When the mind loves itself, two things exist: the mind and its love; and when the mind knows itself, there are likewise two realities: the mind and its knowledge. Therefore the mind, its love, and its knowledge (*notitia*) are three things, and the three are one; and if they are perfect, they are equal (...) When the mind knows and loves itself, the trinity—mind, knowledge, love—subsists in those three realities, without mixture or confusion. And although each has subsistence in itself, they are mutually all in all: one in two, two in one, and consequently all in all. The mind is indeed in itself, for it is called mind in relation to itself; but as knowing, known, or knowable, it is referred to its knowledge; and with reference to the love wherewith it loves itself, it is called lovable, loved, and lover. And knowledge, although referred to the mind that knows and is known, can nonetheless in relation to itself be called knowing and known; for the knowledge by which the mind knows itself cannot be unknown to itself. And love, although referred to the mind that loves and whose love it is, is nevertheless love in itself, having its own subsistence; for love is loved, nor can love be loved except by love—that is, by itself. And thus each of these three realities exists in itself. And they are reciprocally in one another: the mind that loves is in its love; love is in the knowledge of the loving soul; and knowledge is in the mind that knows. Each is in the other two. The mind that knows and loves itself is in its love and in its knowledge; the love of the mind that knows and loves itself is in its mind and in its knowledge; and the knowledge of the mind that loves and knows itself is in its mind and in its love, because it loves itself knowing and knows itself loving. Thus there are two in each, for the mind that knows and loves itself is with its knowledge in love, and with its love in knowledge; love and knowledge are simultaneously in the mind that knows and loves itself."[^128]

Our thought—our soul—loves itself. Love for itself is nothing other than its natural affirmation of existing. We have, then, two realities inseparably joined: the soul and its love. But this love would be impossible were the soul not conscious of itself—that is, if it had no knowledge (*notitia*) of its own existence. On the other hand, the soul, being spiritual and therefore perfectly knowable, cannot fail to know itself. Thus, in the innermost core of man, in his immanent operations, man reflects the internal unity and distinction of the divine Persons. He reflects, above all, the mutual inexistence of the divine Persons in one another. The doctrine of *circuminsessio* finds in Saint Augustine its supreme exponent.

##### b) Memoria Sui, Intelligentia, Voluntas

In the first analogy, Saint Augustine focused upon the immanent operations of the soul, taking as his central object the natural love of the soul for itself. In the second analogy he takes a further step, considering the faculties of the soul as such. By *memoria* is understood the presence of the soul to itself—that is, self-consciousness.

> "These three faculties, memory, intellect, and will, just as they are not three lives, but one life; nor three minds, but one mind; so they are not three substances, but one substance. Memory, as life, mind, and substance, is referred to itself; but as memory, it has a relative meaning. The same may be affirmed of intellect and will, for they are called intellect and will in that they are related to something. In themselves, each is life, mind, and essence. And these three, in that they are one life, one mind, one substance, are one reality (...) They are three according to their mutual relations; and were they not equal, not only when one is related to another, but when each of them is related to all, they would not mutually comprehend one another. For each is known by each, and one knows all. I remember that I have memory, intellect, and will; I understand that I understand, will, and remember; I will to will, to remember, and to understand; and simultaneously I remember my whole memory, intellect, and will. What of my memory I do not remember is not in my memory. Nothing in my memory is so present to memory as memory itself (...) In like manner I know that I understand all that I understand, and I know that I will all that I will, and I remember all that I remember. Consequently, I remember my whole intellect and my whole will (...) In conclusion, since each and all mutually comprehend one another, there is equality between the whole and the parts, and the three are unity: one life, one mind, one essence."[^129]

##### c) Memoria Dei, Intelligentia, Amor

Exalted above these analogies stands a third, far more important, because it contemplates the soul not in relation to itself, but in its vital relationship with God, in whose image it was made. Here memory signifies memory of God (*memoria Dei*)—that is, consciousness of that God who is more present to the soul than the soul is to itself; knowledge signifies knowledge of God; and love signifies love of God. These are not merely acts performed by the soul through its natural powers, but pre-eminently acts elicited by the soul elevated by divine grace—acts whereby it is most intimately united to God. It is when the image of God in the soul is directed toward God that it attains its full splendour:

> "This trinity of the mind is not the image of God because the mind remembers, understands, and loves itself; but because it can remember, understand, and love its Maker. If it does this, wisdom dwells within it; if it does not, even though it remembers, understands, and loves itself, it is foolish. Let it then remember its God, in whose image it was created; let it understand him and love him."[^130]

Although God is ever with man, man is not always with God. To be with God means to remember him, to know him, to love him; it is equivalent to renewing within ourselves the image of God, for by this memory, this intellect, and this will, one participates in that life wherewith God knows and loves himself.[^131] Indeed, if the mind is by nature an image of the Triune God, its perfection and beauty consist in increasing its vital likeness to God. To think of God, to know God, to love him each day more intensely—that is the progress of the soul along the luminous paths of holiness. The immediate, face-to-face vision of the divine essence will succeed the enigmatic and mirror-like vision (*in aenigmate*) of earthly life.[^132] Then we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is (1 Jn 3:2). Likeness to God reaches its consummation precisely in full participation in the intimate life of God—that is, in knowledge and in love.[^133]

### Trinity and Christology

The influence of Saint Augustine upon subsequent Christian thought has been immense. Grandiose also is the speculative vigour of his theology, and pre-eminently of his Trinitarian doctrine. *De Trinitate* has rightly been called "the most significant literary monument dedicated to Trinitarian speculation."[^134] Yet this speculative power must not obscure the fact that Augustine's fundamental aim was ever fidelity to the rule of faith and living communion with the tradition of the Church.

In this sense, it must be underlined that Augustine's Trinitarian theology stands in intrinsic connection with the economy of salvation. We have observed this in his discussion of the proper names of the Holy Spirit. It is evident likewise in the prominence accorded to the theology of the divine missions. Above all, it emerges from the fact that Trinitarian contemplation and Christological truth are presented in indissoluble unity.

*De Trinitate* contains the most extensive Christological treatise that Saint Augustine ever penned. In its pages he analyses with acute penetration the formulas required to speak with precision of the union of the two natures; and he unfolds a profound explanation of the motive of the Incarnation, of the mediation of Jesus Christ, and of the centrality of Christ as the supreme theophany of God. God has definitively revealed himself in Christ. This is perhaps the most fitting perspective from which to read, in their authentic atmosphere, the sublime and at times ardent pages of *De Trinitate*.
