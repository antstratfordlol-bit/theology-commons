## Chapter 11: The Trinitarian Dogma in the Church's Magisterium from the 13th Century {#mateo-seco-trino.11}

During the Middle Ages, while the speculative structuring of the treatise *De Trinitate* was reaching its culmination, Trinitarian theology received fresh impetus and clarification from the Magisterium of the Church on several points, particularly those concerning the Holy Spirit. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215), the Second Council of Lyons (1274), and the Council of Florence (1438–1445) occupy an important place in the history of pneumatology, for they are situated in an epoch in which, following the consummation of the rupture with the Greeks, rapprochement was once again sought—and in part achieved. For all of this, it was necessary to dispel the misunderstandings surrounding faith in the Holy Spirit and, specifically, surrounding the meaning and history of the *Filioque*. After studying these Councils, we continue our course by paying special attention to the teaching concerning God of the First Vatican Council (1870) and to the interventions of the Magisterium in the second half of the twentieth century—specifically, the Second Vatican Council and the *Credo of the People of God*. The chapter concludes with some references to the Trinitarian doctrine of the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*.

### The Fourth Lateran Council

During these centuries, the Church found it necessary at times to reaffirm the proclamation of faith in the oneness of God in order to counter various Manichaean tendencies which upheld the existence of a twofold principle of creation, or of one God of the Old Testament and another of the New. It was likewise necessary, with relative frequency, to reaffirm the mystery of the unity of the Trinity.[^1]

The Trinitarian teaching of the Fourth Lateran Council is closely connected with the doctrine of Abbot Joachim of Fiore († 1202).[^2] Abbot Joachim accused Peter Lombard of introducing a kind of quaternity (*quaternitas*) into the Trinity, because Lombard held that the divine essence, common to the three Persons, was neither *generans*, nor *generata*, nor *procedens*. Joachim, for his part, conceived the unity of the three Persons as a unity in species—something akin to what occurs in three men, who coincide in belonging to the human species, yet are three numerically distinct individuals.[^3]

The Trinitarian doctrine of Joachim of Fiore, in which the influence of Gilbert of Poitiers (*Porretanus*) can be perceived, bordered upon a form of tritheism according to which unity in God is neither true nor proper, but in a certain sense reduces to a collective and analogical unity.[^4] Hence the Profession of Faith of Lateran IV declares:

> "We firmly believe and simply confess that there is only one true God, eternal and immense, almighty, unchangeable, incomprehensible, and ineffable, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit: three persons indeed, but one essence, substance, or nature entirely simple. The Father is from no one; the Son is from the Father alone; and the Holy Spirit is equally from both: without beginning, always, and without end, the Father begetting, the Son being born, and the Holy Spirit proceeding; consubstantial, co-equal, equally almighty, and co-eternal, the single principle of all things..."[^5]

Following this straightforward affirmation of the Trinitarian mystery, the Council proceeds to the direct condemnation of Abbot Joachim, explicitly naming Peter Lombard, to whose doctrine it adheres:

> "With the approval of the sacred Council, we believe and confess with Peter Lombard that there exists a certain single supreme reality (*una quaedam summa res*), incomprehensible and ineffable, which is truly Father, Son, and Holy Spirit: the three Persons together and each one of them in particular. Consequently, in God there is only Trinity and not quaternity, because each of these Persons is that reality, namely, the divine substance, essence, or nature. It alone is the principle of all things [...] And this reality does not beget, nor is it begotten, nor does it proceed; but it is the Father who begets, the Son who is begotten, and the Holy Spirit who proceeds, so that the distinctions are in the persons and the unity in the nature."[^6]

This constitutes a vigorous affirmation of the numerical unity of the divine essence:
a) There is identity between the Persons and the divine essence, whether they are considered singularly or all three together;
b) There is identity between the divine essence and the Father as begetting the Son, with the Son as begotten of the Father, and with the Holy Spirit inasmuch as he proceeds from both;
c) It is denied that the essence itself begets;
d) There is therefore no quaternity in God, for the divine essence cannot be understood as something existing separately from the Persons.[^7]

Lateran IV closes off every avenue leading to a conception of the Trinity in which the Person of the Father would be made to emerge from a prior impersonal essence. That is to say, there is no priority of the divine essence over the Persons. It is not the divine essence that begets, but the Father. The affirmation that the Father is the source and origin of the whole Trinity (*fons et origo totius Trinitatis*) is thus emphatically underscored.

Consequently, the divine substance or essence is not the principle of the processions. Only the Persons are this principle. For this reason, the Council concludes: unity is in the nature, distinction in the Persons. The consubstantiality of the Persons therefore implies not only a specific unity of substance or nature, but also their numerical unity. The precise formulations of the *Quicumque* Creed and of the Eleventh Council of Toledo are thus gathered up. In chapter 2 the position of Abbot Joachim is condemned, whilst in chapter 3 praise is accorded to the monastery founded by him, as well as to Joachim himself, who had directed that his writings should be submitted to the Holy See.

### The Second Council of Lyons

The Council was convoked specifically to restore union with the Greeks. The union was formally achieved, as Emperor Michael Palaeologus was eager to obtain military assistance from the West. Yet it proved to be an ephemeral union.[^8] The pivotal Trinitarian issue was, naturally, the procession of the Holy Spirit in its twofold aspect: a) his procession from the Father and the Son; b) that both constitute no more than a single principle.[^9]

> "We confess faithfully and devoutly that the Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son, not as from two principles, but as from one principle; not by two spirations, but by a single spiration [...] With the approval of the sacred Council, we condemn and reprove those who dare to deny that the Holy Spirit proceeds eternally from the Father and the Son; or who rashly assert that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from two principles and not as from one principle."[^10]

This represents an important clarification within Latin theology which unquestionably forestalls the excesses to which filioquism might give rise: neither do two spirations exist, nor is the Son another separate principle of spiration. The purpose is to protect the affirmation that the Father is the sole source of the entire Trinity.[^11]

### The Council of Florence

At the Council of Florence, too, the Trinitarian questions that caused concern were those relating to union with the Greeks; hence the question of the *Filioque* occupied a pre-eminent position. The Council approved various decrees of union: union with the Armenians (the bull *Exsultate Deo*, 22 November 1439); union with the Jacobites (the bull *Cantate Domino*, 4 February 1442); and union with the Greeks (the bull *Laetentur caeli*, 6 July 1439), which marked the culminating moment of the Council. In the latter, the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Father and the Son as from a single principle is insisted upon:

> "...we define, with the approval of this holy Council of Florence, that this truth of faith is to be believed and received by all Christians and thus that all are to profess: that the Holy Spirit is eternally from the Father and the Son, and has his essence and his subsistent being together from the Father and the Son, and proceeds eternally from both as from one principle and by a single spiration. As to what the holy Doctors and Fathers say, that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son, we declare that this manner of speaking is intended to convey that the Son is also, like the Father, cause (according to the Greeks' mode of expression) or principle (according to the Latins' mode of expression) of the subsistence of the Holy Spirit. And since all that the Father has he gave to his only-begotten Son in begetting him—except being the Father—this very proceeding of the Holy Spirit from the Son, the Son himself has eternally from the Father, by whom he is also eternally begotten. Furthermore, we define that the addition of the word *Filioque* was lawfully and reasonably appended to the Creed, for the sake of clarifying the truth and under the pressure of urgent necessity at that time."[^12]

We find ourselves, therefore, before a solemn proclamation of faith in which it is taught that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from a single principle and by a single spiration, receiving from them his essential and personal reality. The equivalence of the formulas *ex Patre Filioque* and *a Patre per Filium* is affirmed, and the introduction of the *Filioque* is legitimated by the necessities of the time—that is, to safeguard the perfect divinity of the Son.[^13]

In the bull *Cantate Domino*, alongside a succinct exposition of the mystery of the Trinity,[^14] the unity of the three divine Persons is stressed, underlining that this unity is affirmed in the *circuminsessio* (*perichoresis*): the Persons, though distinct by their origin and mutual relationship, are in one another by virtue of the unity of their essence:

> "Because of this unity, the Father is wholly in the Son and wholly in the Holy Spirit; the Son is wholly in the Father and wholly in the Holy Spirit; the Holy Spirit is wholly in the Father and wholly in the Son. None precedes another in eternity, or exceeds another in greatness, or surpasses another in power. From eternity and without beginning the Son has his origin from the Father; from eternity and without beginning the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. Whatever the Father is or has, he does not have received from another, but from himself; he is principle without principle. Whatever the Holy Spirit is or has, he has received together from the Father and from the Son. But the Father and the Son are not two principles of the Holy Spirit, but one principle, just as the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are not three principles of creation, but one principle."[^15]

Precisely out of a desire to dispel misunderstandings with the Greeks, the conciliar teaching emphasizes what unites Latins and Greeks in their confession of the truths concerning the Holy Spirit. Specifically, it underscores not only that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, but above all that he proceeds from the Father and the Son as from a single principle; that the Son, in being begotten, receives everything from the Father, and therefore even the spiration of the Holy Spirit; that the Father receives nothing from any Person, since he is principle without principle; and it insists that the formula *ex Patre Filioque procedit* is not contradictory to the formula *ex Patre per Filium procedit*.[^16]

### The Filioque Question

The introduction of the *Filioque* into the liturgical use of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed in its Latin version constitutes a major Trinitarian issue and is, moreover, pivotal in the dialogue between the Western Church and the Byzantine Church. The question has a twofold aspect: on the one hand, the theological meaning of the *Filioque*; on the other, its insertion by the Latins into the Creed. Here is a summary of its history and of the theological reasons that gave rise to the controversy.

Latin theology, as we have already seen, was oriented from its beginnings towards the affirmation that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. According to Tertullian, the statement that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son has a meaning equivalent to the statement that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son.[^17] The term *Filioque* was already used by Saint Ambrose.[^18] He was followed by Saint Augustine, in whose Trinitarian doctrine the procession of the Spirit from the Father and the Son occupies a central place.[^19] Saint Augustine emphasizes that the Spirit proceeds from the Father *principaliter* (principally or fundamentally), whereas he proceeds from the Son *communiter* (in common), because the Father, in generating the Son, bestows everything upon Him.[^20] Through Saint Augustine, this conception became universal in the West. Pope Saint Leo the Great employed equivalent expressions.[^21]

It is important to emphasize that the West expressed its faith in the *Filioque* through its Fathers and Councils at a time when communion between East and West was still fully intact.[^22] For centuries, therefore, the two formulas coexisted peacefully in the Church: *qui ex Patre procedit* and *qui ex Patre Filioque procedit*. Neither did Westerners take offence at Eastern formulas, nor Easterners at Western ones.[^23] It is also true, as B. Studer points out, that the Council of Constantinople (381) had itself modified the Nicene Creed without concerning itself with the opinion of Westerners, and that the Council of Ephesus (431), which forbade professing any faith other than that of Nicaea, was unaware of the Constantinopolitan version.[^24]

Both Easterners and Westerners approached the Trinitarian mystery in different ways, yet both accepted in their integrity the data offered by Sacred Scripture: the Father is principle without principle; the Son proceeds from the Father; the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, is sent by the Son, and is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. The Second Council of Constantinople (553) mentions, alongside the Greeks, Latin Fathers and Doctors whose doctrine it confesses to follow in its entirety, and who clearly professed the *Filioque*. Among these authors are Saint Ambrose, Saint Augustine, and Saint Leo the Great, whom we have seen using the expression *a Patre et Filio procedit*.

The confession that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son appears explicitly in the *Fides Damasi* and in the *Quicumque* Creed. The *Filioque* was already present in Spain, in the liturgy of Toledo between the years 446 and 447. It was present at the Third Council of Toledo (589) with the profession of faith of King Reccared. It was used there precisely to counter Priscillianist and Arian errors, highlighting, on the one hand, that the Son is equal to the Father, since he spirates together with Him, and, on the other hand, that the Holy Spirit is of the same nature as the Father and the Son.[^25] Following this event came the formulas of faith of the Fourth, Sixth, Eleventh, Sixteenth, and Seventeenth Councils of Toledo.[^26] The *Filioque* was so universal in the West that it was believed in good faith to have derived from Nicaea-Constantinople; so much so that around the year 790 the *Libri Carolini* accused the Greeks of being the ones who had suppressed the *Filioque* from the Creed.[^27] This was a regrettable lack of information and an unjust accusation. Alcuin, Smaragdus, and Theodulf composed various treatises debating whether one ought to say *Filioque* or *per Filium*.[^28]

Naturally, the Greek Fathers also addressed the question of the Spirit's bond with the Son. This is seen, for example, in Saint Athanasius,[^29] Saint Basil,[^30] and Saint Gregory of Nyssa,[^31] who always speak of a bond between the Spirit and the Son. It could not be otherwise, if it is understood that the Spirit is one God together with the Son, and that, moreover, he is sent into the world by the Son. Both the Eastern and the Latin traditions have naturally read these venerable texts attentively, seeking to illuminate their respective positions from them. It may be said, nonetheless, that while fourth-century Fathers clearly affirm that the Spirit proceeds from the Father, and do not explicitly state that the Spirit proceeds also from the Son, they do speak of a natural, eternal, and personal bond between the Son and the Spirit. That is to say, the Son plays a certain role—as yet not fully defined—in the procession of the Spirit.[^32]

In the fifth century, Saint Cyril of Alexandria affirmed that the Spirit proceeds from the Son: "The Holy Spirit is the Spirit of God the Father and in like manner of the Son, since he flows forth substantially from both, that is to say, from the Father through the Son."[^33] Such expressions, however, must be read with prudence, for they may refer directly to an "economic" origin. This is how Orthodox theology has understood them,[^34] whereas many Latin theologians have understood them as referring also to the intra-Trinitarian life. In any case, it seems fair to say that Saint Cyril's texts, even when situated within the "economic" perspective, do not exclude, but rather point towards the intra-Trinitarian perspective. In Cyril, the eternal bond of the Spirit with the Son seems certainly to imply a bond of origin.

A similar teaching is found in Maximus the Confessor, when in 655 he wrote to Pope Martin I, concerned about the Latin use of the *Filioque*, stating that he accepted the origin of the Spirit from the Father through the mediation of the Logos.[^35] Maximus defended this expression in order to "safeguard the unity and identity of essence by means of the statement on the procession (*proiénai*) through the Son, without turning the Son into the cause (*aitía*) of the procession (*ekpóreusis*) of the Holy Spirit."[^36]

Saint John Damascene summarized the Trinitarian faith as follows: "The Spirit accompanies the Word and manifests his energy [...] He is a Power who comes from the Father and rests upon the Word, a Power who manifests the Word, unable to be separated from God in whom he is, and from the Word whom he accompanies [...] We do not say that the Son is the cause [...] nor do we say that the Spirit proceeds from the Son, but we say that he is the Spirit of the Son [...] The Spirit is the Spirit of the Father [...] but he is also the Spirit of the Son, not because he proceeds from the Son, but because he proceeds from the Father through Him, for there is only one sole cause, the Father [...]"[^37] It cannot be said that these texts exclude all procession of the Spirit as proceeding also from the Son. It is understandable that some have considered them witnesses to the formula *from the Father through the Son*.[^38]

Such were the diverse paths followed by theology in elaborating the doctrine of the Spirit's procession in an unpolemical atmosphere. The paths appeared divergent, but not incompatible. The question of the *Filioque* assumed a different complexion in the era of the schism. In the year 867, Patriarch Photius, in a series of writings, condemned not only the inclusion of the *Filioque* in the Creed, but its doctrinal meaning. According to Photius, the Holy Spirit proceeds solely from the Father. Even against those Greek Fathers who spoke of a procession through the Son, Photius insisted not only that the Father and the Father alone is the cause (*aitía*) of the Spirit,[^39] but that this causality excludes the Spirit's proceeding also from the Son: the Spirit, according to Photius, proceeds solely from the Father (*ek mónou tou Patrós*). It cannot be denied that Photius radicalized the divergent positions of the Greeks, to the point of severing the theology of the missions completely from its relation to the immanent Trinity.[^40]

It is only just to recall that the divergence surrounding the *Filioque* was not an invention of Photius. This divergence had its roots in the most classical Greek theology: in the Greeks' particular way of approaching the mystery of the Trinity. Photius was not the creator of the divergence, but he was the one who radicalized it. And in doing so, he impoverished his entire Trinitarian theology, minimizing the relationship between Word and Spirit and rendering himself unable to account for the theology of the Spirit's mission.[^41]

Yet the reason for the divergence lay not only in the differing approach to Trinitarian theology. A significant reason was also found in difficulties of vocabulary. This has already been seen with the use of the term *aitia* (cause). Furthermore, while the Latins had only one word to designate procession (*processio*), the Greeks possessed two words to speak of it: *proiénai* for the procession of the Son, and *ekporeústhai* for that of the Holy Spirit, following the Greek text of the New Testament (cf. Jn 8:42 with regard to the Son and Jn 15:26 with regard to the Holy Spirit).[^42] In turn, the Latin position can be understood only as the outcome of the way the Latins framed the consideration of the Trinity and, above all, as the result of the strength with which they realized that Son and Spirit can be distinct from each other only if there exists between them an opposition of relation; consequently, either the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son, or the Son proceeds from the Holy Spirit, or they are not distinct from each other. For the Greeks, by contrast, the distinction among the Persons is rooted solely in their personal properties of origin, and not in their mutual relations. According to this view, the possession of these personal properties is sufficient to eliminate any risk of confusing the Persons with one another.

At this point it is necessary to distinguish two questions: the doctrine of the *Filioque*, and the inclusion of the *Filioque* in the Creed. The former is a question pertaining to Trinitarian doctrine; the latter pertains to ecclesiology and Church history. From the historical point of view, it must be recalled that for a long time the Roman See proved reluctant to accept the inclusion of the *Filioque* in the Creed. Thus, for example, Pope Leo III (795–816), who was in agreement with the doctrine, in the face of pressure to insert the *Filioque* into the Creed, had the Creed engraved without this addition, in Greek and Latin, upon two silver plaques which were hung on either side of the entrance to the Confession of Saint Peter. For this reason, Photius did not accuse Rome of the interpolation.[^43]

Yet Photius did not merely condemn the inclusion of the *Filioque* in the Creed; he condemned its doctrinal dimension. According to him, the Holy Spirit proceeds solely from the Father. To argue this, he relied upon the following premise: an operation is either common to the three Persons—and then it derives from the nature—or it is strictly personal, and one Person cannot share it with another Person. Since the Holy Spirit does not proceed from the divine nature, but from the Person of the Father, the Father cannot share spiration with the Son. That is to say, the Holy Spirit cannot proceed from the Person of the Son.

The vicissitudes of the controversy between Orthodox and Catholics are well known through the history of the Church. We have had occasion to consider—in connection with the Second Council of Lyons and the Council of Florence—the fleeting high points at which union was reached and the theological arguments deployed on both sides. To arrive at the definitive schism, it was necessary not only to reject the Latin position outright without attempting to understand it, but also to ignore numerous passages of Greek patristic literature that spoke of *a Patre per Filium*.[^44]

Here are the principal arguments adduced by Orthodox theologians against the *Filioque*:

1. The relations among the Persons manifest the personal distinction between them, but they neither constitute the Persons nor are they the ground of their distinction. To say that the Spirit proceeds from the Son—which is not clearly stated in Scripture—in order to justify personal distinction better on rational grounds is an attempt to rationalise the mystery.
2. According to the Orthodox, the Latins absorb the trinity of Persons into the unity of essence, and the *Filioque* is the expression of this, for Father and Son are thus presented as an anonymous principle of the Holy Spirit.[^45]
3. The *Filioque*, by attributing the procession of the Holy Spirit also to the Son, would render the unity of the Trinity impossible, since the Father would no longer be the sole principle of the Persons.[^46]

Here are the replies to these objections on the part of Catholic theology:

1. It is not an attempt to rationalise the mystery when, once accepted by Revelation, one endeavours to contemplate it bearing in mind the laws of thought. Indeed, statements such as "the Persons can be distinguished from one another only by opposition of relation" or "the Holy Spirit would not be distinguished from the Son if he did not proceed from Him" do not constitute any attempt to rationalise the mystery, but point out the path for arriving at an exposition of the mystery that is not pure self-contradiction. To affirm the *Filioque* does not mean, therefore, transposing Revelation onto philosophical terrain.
2. To say that the Father and the Son spirate the Holy Spirit is not equivalent to saying that they constitute an anonymous principle of spiration: it is the Father and the Son perfectly united in Love. This is what constitutes them as a single *Spirator*.
3. Attributing the procession of the Holy Spirit also to the Son is equivalent neither to denying that the Father is *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*, nor to denying the unity of the Trinity. The Father loses nothing of what he communicates to the Son; and what distinguishes him from the Son, even in that which is common to Him with the Son—the spiration of the Holy Spirit—is that He is the principle and source of all that the Son has, for all that the Son possesses he has as received from Him.[^47]

The efforts of the Second Council of Lyons and of Florence did not bear lasting fruit, at least as regards overcoming the schism. Yet it can be said that they laid the foundations for future efforts by pointing out two methodological principles: delving deeper into the common tradition—the doctrine of the Fathers—and seeking a legitimate variety of formulas of faith. In turn, the controversy has revealed the dangers of filioquism, as will be seen below.[^48]

### From the Council of Florence to the First Vatican Council

Between the Council of Florence and the First Vatican Council, the Magisterium of the Church intervened on several occasions to counter certain Trinitarian errors—which no longer possessed the gravity and influence of the issues mentioned above—or in proposing new confessions of faith.

Such was the case, for instance, with Paul IV when rejecting the position of the Socinians, who denied the Trinity;[^49] or in the professions of faith proposed by Gregory XIII to the Greco-Russian Church, a profession of faith in which explicit acceptance of the Council of Florence was added to the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed and the familiar considerations regarding the *Filioque* were reiterated;[^50] or in the profession of faith prescribed by Benedict XIV for the Eastern Churches in 1743.[^51] Such was also the case with the general rejection of Hermes, Günther, Froschammer, and Rosmini-Serbati, whose philosophical positions impinged upon Trinitarian matters.[^52]

In this period, Trinitarian questions gave way, so to speak, to questions concerning the capacity of human reason to attain to the knowledge of God. Almost all the Magisterial interventions of these centuries, culminating in the First Vatican Council, refer to this theme, countering currents incompatible with Christian teaching on God and on man. These problems arose from philosophical quarters, almost all of them concerning the relations between faith and reason. The interventions of the hierarchy ranged across a spectrum running from rationalism and semi-rationalism to fideism, traditionalism, and ontologism.

*Fideism* denies that human reason has the power to attain to God and, therefore, that it is capable of grasping the reasonableness of faith. The sole justification of faith would be exclusively faith itself. Hence the name fideism. The Church countered it by requiring its proponents to subscribe to a series of propositions in which they explicitly accepted the capacity of reason to attain to the knowledge of God. This occurred with L. Bautain (1796–1867), a professor at the Seminary of Strasbourg.[^53]

The Congregation of the Index also intervened against the *traditionalism* of A. Bonnetty (1798–1879).[^54] According to the traditionalists, revelation would have been necessary for humanity even to acquire knowledge of fundamental metaphysical truths, including the existence of God. This revelation would have reached us through tradition, handed down from one generation to another. Hence the name traditionalism. There exists in man, therefore, an authentic physical inability to attain to God through the powers of reason. Fideism and traditionalism are very close: both partake of the same pessimistic view of human reason.[^55] In rejecting these positions, the Church cautioned about the importance that human reason holds in the journey towards God.

The hierarchy of the Church also had to intervene against *ontologism*. The ontologists hold as their key affirmation that man knows God immediately already in this life. This knowledge of God is what enables knowledge of other beings. In this way, God would be not only the *primum ontologicum*, but also the *primum logicum* for man. We intuit Absolute Being precisely because all beings are contained in Him. Furthermore, it is in this knowledge of God that we know all other things. Hence ontologism appears closely akin to pantheism. Malebranche (1638–1715) is regarded as one of its precursors, and V. Gioberti (1801–1852) as one of its best-known proponents. Ontologism was rejected on several occasions.[^56] Consequently, God is not presented as immediately known by human intelligence.

The First Vatican Council devoted special attention to these questions in the Dogmatic Constitution *Dei Filius* (24 April 1870), which constitutes a serene exposition of the doctrine on God and a balanced stance against pantheism, rationalism, and fideism.

Chapter 1 presents a dense synthesis of Christian doctrine concerning God:

> "The holy Catholic, Apostolic, and Roman Church believes and confesses that there is one true and living God, Creator and Lord of heaven and earth, almighty, eternal, immense, incomprehensible, infinite in intellect and will and in every perfection; who, being one unique and singular spiritual substance, entirely simple and unchangeable, must be declared really and essentially distinct from the world, supremely blessed in and of himself, and ineffably exalted above all things which exist or can be conceived outside of Him."[^57]

Faith is proclaimed in a single God who transcends all created reality. The attributes with which he is described are thoroughly biblical. It is emphasized that this God, who is a personal being, is a spiritual substance. The conception according to which nothing exists except material beings is thereby rejected. There follow phrases underscoring the distinction of God with respect to the world, in order to banish any shadow of pantheism.

The conciliar text continues with a synthesis of Christian doctrine concerning creation, drawing largely upon the words of Lateran IV: from the beginning of time, God created both the material and the spiritual world freely, not to increase his beatitude or perfection, but to manifest his glory by imparting goods to creatures.

The canons corresponding to this chapter insist with even greater clarity upon this same orientation. Indeed, it had been agreed at the Council that canons would be reserved for the formulation of those propositions which were in direct contradiction with the doctrine set forth in the chapters.[^58] The first canons pay particular attention to materialism and pantheism;[^59] the last canon is devoted to reaffirming the doctrine of creation against the theories of G. Hermes (1775–1831) and A. Günther (1783–1871).[^60]

The second chapter of *Dei Filius* opens with paragraphs devoted to the capacity of human reason to attain to God, crafted especially to counter agnosticism and traditionalism:

> "The same Holy Mother Church holds and teaches that God, the beginning and end of all things, can be known with certainty by the natural light of human reason from created things: *for since the creation of the world his invisible attributes are clearly seen through the things that are made* (Rom 1:20). Nevertheless, it pleased his wisdom and goodness to reveal himself and the eternal decrees of his will to the human race by another, and that a supernatural way, as the Apostle says: *In many and various ways God spoke of old to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days he has spoken to us by his Son* (Heb 1:1–2)."[^61]

The canon corresponding to this paragraph insists upon the same doctrine and with the same nuances.[^62] The capacity of reason to attain to God by contemplating the works of his hands is affirmed. That is to say, the possibility of knowing God is affirmed; it is not asserted that reason left to itself actually arrives at this knowledge. And above all, precisely by displaying the harmony between natural reason and revelation, the utility of supernatural revelation in assisting reason to reach this knowledge with ease and certitude is highlighted. The conciliar Fathers supported their assertions with two eloquent passages of Scripture: Romans 1:20 and Hebrews 1:1. In the first, the entire Jewish tradition so beautifully gathered in Wisdom 13:1–5 is reaffirmed, where the responsibility of anyone who fails to recognise the Creator while contemplating the beauty of creation is asserted; in the second, a synthesis is presented of the economy which the God of the Covenant followed in his revelation until reaching its definitive manifestation in Christ.

We find ourselves, then, before paragraphs that are brief yet momentous, balanced, and illuminating. In them is condensed the rich experience which the preceding two centuries provided to Vatican I on this question. And this experience is reflected in the firmness with which the human capacity to perceive truth and goodness is temperately defended. Looked at from God's standpoint, the teaching may be formulated thus: God transcends man, yet is neither inaccessible nor distant from him. Looked at from the standpoint of human intelligence, it may be formulated thus: intelligence not only possesses the capacity to rise to the knowledge of the existence of a supreme being, but also—"and this is decisive for understanding what is Christian—to receive a communication or word through which God unveils to man his mystery, that is to say, his life."[^63]

These affirmations of Vatican I are of decisive importance for the theological task and for the conception of man's journey towards God. As has been noted, these statements are immersed in the long Augustinian and medieval tradition that regards man as *capax entis* and *capax Dei*.[^64] We are dealing, therefore, with anthropological assertions that may be described as a moderate optimism, which the Second Vatican Council took up when pointing out that the highest reason for human dignity consists in man's vocation to communion with God.[^65] The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* vigorously reflects this same doctrine in one of its opening sections, placed under the heading *Man is "capable" of God*:

> "The desire for God is written in the human heart, because man is created by God and for God; and God never ceases to draw man to himself. Only in God will he find the truth and happiness he never stops searching for."[^66]

### Trinitarian and Pneumatological Doctrine in the Second Vatican Council

The Second Vatican Council was convoked as a pastoral and ecclesiological council, with no intention of addressing Trinitarian questions directly. Nevertheless, from the beginning of the century, a theology attentive to salvation history had been developing, characterised by a strong scriptural, patristic, liturgical, and ecclesiological orientation, whose doctrinal richness would be vividly present at the Second Vatican Council.[^67] In fact, the Trinitarian mystery is a constant point of reference throughout the theology of Vatican II.[^68]

Thus, although from the strict standpoint of Trinitarian dogma no new doctrinal definitions were attempted, a substantial teaching was presented regarding the relation of the Trinitarian mystery to the salvation of mankind. The Trinity was placed at the very centre of Christian doctrine. Suffice it to recall the opening of the Dogmatic Constitution *Lumen gentium* (nos. 2–4), with its magnificent vision of the Church in which the saving design of the Father is manifested, the Church's relation to the mystery of the Trinity, the description of man's relation to the Trinitarian mystery in the Pastoral Constitution *Gaudium et spes* (no. 22), or the Trinitarian mystery as the horizon within which Sacred Scripture must be read.[^69]

Precisely because of its greater attentiveness to salvation history, the Second Vatican Council developed a Trinitarian theology closely linked to the economic vision of the Trinitarian mystery. To the Father is attributed the creative and saving design, the sending of the Son and of the Spirit, and the call to share in the Son's divine filiation. The Father is the term and goal of the action of Christ and of the Spirit. To the Son is assigned the revelation of the Father, redemption, and the gift of the Spirit. To the Holy Spirit is assigned the sanctification of the Church and of souls, the generation of unity in the Church, the distribution of gifts and charisms, and configuration to Christ.[^70] The following passage serves as an example of the consistency with which Vatican II points to the Trinity as the source and goal of the entire history of salvation:

> "When the work which the Father gave the Son to do on earth was accomplished (cf. Jn 17:4), the Holy Spirit was sent on the day of Pentecost in order that he might continually sanctify the Church, and thus, all those who believe would have access through Christ in one Spirit to the Father (cf. Eph 2:18)."[^71]

The consideration of the Trinitarian mystery in the light of the divine missions gave fresh impetus to the renewal of pneumatology, precisely by bringing into prominence the action of the Spirit in the Church. This same perspective is the most appropriate for situating the entire Christian mystery within a Trinitarian framework. The Church is the Body of Christ animated by the Spirit. The Christian is made a child of God in Christ through the action of the Holy Spirit.[^72] Everything points towards the intimate relationship between the mission of the Son and the mission of the Spirit.

### The Credo of the People of God

Paul VI wished to commemorate the nineteenth centenary of the martyrdom of the Apostles Peter and Paul with a Year of Faith. The culmination of that year took place with the celebration of the Eucharist and the profession of faith made by Paul VI at the conclusion of the Liturgy of the Word on 30 June 1968. This was not an *ex cathedra* definition. Nonetheless, it possesses the value of having been solemnly professed in the name of the entire Church. The Credo was drafted using the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed as its foundation. The Pope introduced several phrases to express the faith more adequately as demanded by the circumstances of the time, and insisted that he was speaking in the name of the whole People of God, of all sacred pastors and faithful Christians, to bear a most firm witness to divine truth.[^73]

The profession of faith opens by confessing the mystery of the One and Triune God:

> "We believe in one only God, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, Creator of things visible..."

This is one sole God, who is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. The structural difference in comparison with the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed is evident: it begins by considering God in his unity and not with the Person of the Father.[^74] This represents an emphasis upon the oneness of God as the starting point for the Trinitarian confession of faith, recalling the profession of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council. The first significant consequence of this perspective is that creation is not attributed solely to the Father, as in the Creed of Nicaea, but to the entire Trinity.[^75]

This oneness of the divine essence is explicitly affirmed by setting it in relation to the divine attributes:

> "We believe that this one only God is as absolutely one in his most holy essence as in all his other perfections: in his omnipotence, in his infinite knowledge, in his providence, in his will, and in his love. He is *He Who Is*, as he revealed to Moses (cf. Ex 3:14); and he is *Love*, as the Apostle John taught us (cf. 1 Jn 4:8): so that these two names, Being and Love, express ineffably the same divine essence of Him who wished to manifest himself to us, and who, *dwelling in light inaccessible* (1 Tim 6:16), is in himself above every name and above every created thing. God alone can give us a right and full knowledge of himself by revealing himself as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, in whose eternal life we are called by grace to share..."

With this final statement, all that had been taught by the previous Magisterium—especially by the Fourth Lateran Council and the First Vatican Council[^76]—concerning the unknowability of the Trinitarian mystery by the sole light of natural reason is reaffirmed. Moreover, the Trinitarian mystery is thereby protected against any attempt at rationalisation, and the oneness of God continues to be highlighted by applying to the entire Trinity both the divine name of Exodus 3:14 and the Johannine definition of God (1 Jn 4:8). It is thus insisted that God is Being, and also that He is Love. Next, the Credo proceeds to develop faith in the trinity of Persons:

> "The mutual bonds which constitute the three Persons from all eternity, each of whom is the one and same divine Being, are the intimate and blissful life of the most holy God, which infinitely surpasses all that we can understand in a human manner."

The cited paragraph opens with the statement that the Persons are constituted by the mutual bonds whereby they are related to one another. It is fair to say, therefore, that this refers to relations as constitutive of the Persons. The theme is, moreover, constant in the Magisterium from the Eleventh Council of Toledo onwards. At the same time, the Credo deliberately insists upon the fact that the three Persons, although distinct from one another, are identical with the divine essence—that is, that "each of them is the one and same divine Being, according to the formula of the Fourth Lateran Council."[^77]

The divine processions are then set forth:

> "We believe, then, in God who in all eternity begets the Son; we believe in the Son, the Word of God, who is begotten from all eternity; we believe in the Holy Spirit, the uncreated Person who proceeds from the Father and the Son, as their everlasting Love."

The Son proceeds from the Father by generation. Verbs in the present tense are used in this Credo to signify that the generation of the Word is an eternal present—which coincides with the expression *begotten before all ages*, employed to the same end: to indicate that the generation of the Word is not temporal, being prior to all ages.[^78] Of the Holy Spirit it is said that he proceeds from the Father and the Son, as has been done in the West since the Third Council of Toledo. Paul VI added to the *Filioque* the phrase *as their everlasting Love*. This encapsulates the entire theological tradition that beholds the Holy Spirit as Love, in contrast to the Son who is contemplated as the Word. The Eleventh Council of Toledo had already indicated that the Holy Spirit is the "Spirit of both", proceeding from both together, "because he is the love or the sanctity of both."[^79] The paragraph concludes with a quotation from the *Quicumque* Creed: we must venerate the unity in Trinity and the Trinity in unity.

Finally, without pausing over the Person of the Father, the Credo devotes a paragraph to the Word, another to the Incarnation, and another to the Holy Spirit, in which it insists respectively upon the *homoousios* and upon the titles given to the Holy Spirit by the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed. The Credo concludes its mention of the Holy Spirit by underscoring the Spirit's action in the Church and in the interior of the soul.

### The Teaching of John Paul II and the Catechism of the Catholic Church

John Paul II devoted great attention to the Trinitarian mystery. In addition to an extensive catechesis in his Wednesday general audiences dedicated to each of the divine Persons,[^80] he published a trilogy of Trinitarian encyclicals of dense theological content, especially the last, *Dominum et vivificantem* (18 May 1986).[^81] In his pontificate, particular prominence belongs to the theological work embodied in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*.

The *Catechism* develops the exposition of the articles of faith following the Apostles' Creed, complemented by the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed.[^82] First of all, it notes the importance of the expression *I believe in God*, which is understood as the affirmation of the existence of a single God:

> "Our profession of faith begins with God, for God is the First and the Last (Is 44:6), the beginning and the end of everything. The Credo begins with God the Father, for the Father is the first divine person of the Most Holy Trinity; our Creed begins with the creation of heaven and earth, for creation is the beginning and the foundation of all God's works"; "'I believe in God': this first affirmation of the Profession of Faith is also the most fundamental. The whole Creed speaks of God, and when it also speaks of man and of the world it does so in relation to God. The other articles of the Creed all depend on the first, just as the remaining commandments unpack the first. The other articles help us to know God better as he revealed himself progressively to men. 'The faithful first confess their belief in God' (Roman Catechism, I, 2, 2)."[^83]

After insisting that "confession of God's oneness is inseparably linked to confession of the existence of God" (no. 200), the *Catechism* presents the divine attributes on the basis of a biblical understanding: it speaks of God as one (no. 201), personal (no. 203), living (no. 205), holy (no. 208), merciful (no. 210), truth (no. 215), and love (no. 218).[^84]

The *Catechism* presents the immanent and economic dimensions of the Trinitarian mystery harmoniously interwoven:

> "Through the *Oikonomia* the *Theologia* is revealed to us; but conversely, the *Theologia* illuminates the whole *Oikonomia*. God's works reveal who he is in himself; the mystery of his inmost Being enlightens our understanding of all his works."[^85]

After briefly considering the Persons of the Father and the Son (nos. 232–242), the *Catechism* pauses over questions relating to the Holy Spirit (nos. 243–248), paying special attention to his origin. These are carefully crafted paragraphs that seek to gather respectfully the doctrinal richness of the entire Christian tradition, both Eastern and Western. Specifically, the procession of the Holy Spirit is formulated in the three ways in which it has come to be expressed throughout history: first, the formula of the Council of Constantinople without the addition of the *Filioque* is recalled, insisting that the Church recognises the Father as the source and origin of the divinity (no. 245); next, it enters into the explanation of the Latin tradition of the *Filioque*, that is, the examination of its history and its doctrinal meaning (nos. 245–246);[^86] finally, it indicates the validity of the Eastern formula according to which the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son, and its convergence with the Western formula.[^87]

Following this, the *Catechism* turns to set forth the strict dogmatic formulation of the Trinitarian mystery, which it expresses in three major propositions following the classical order: 1. The Trinity is One; 2. The divine Persons are really distinct from one another; 3. The divine Persons are relative to one another (nos. 253–255). Finally, the *Catechism* devotes four numbers (nos. 257–260) to explaining that the works *ad extra* are common to the three divine Persons and reveal both the "properties" of the divine Persons and their single nature, insisting at the same time upon the relation of the Christian life to the Trinitarian mystery:

> "The whole divine economy is the common work of the three divine persons. For as the Trinity has only one and the same nature, so too does it have only one and the same operation [...] However, each divine person performs the common work according to his personal property [...] It is above all the divine missions of the Son's Incarnation and the gift of the Holy Spirit that show forth the properties of the divine persons. Being a work at once common and personal, the whole divine economy makes known both what is proper to the divine persons and their one divine nature. Hence the whole Christian life is a communion with each of the divine persons, without in any way separating them. Everyone who gives glory to the Father does so through the Son in the Holy Spirit; everyone who follows Christ does so because the Father draws him (cf. Jn 6:44) and the Spirit moves him (cf. Rom 8:14)."[^88]

By confessing the Spirit as he 'who proceeds from the Father' (Jn 15:26), it affirms that he *comes from* the Father *through* the Son (cf. AG 2).
