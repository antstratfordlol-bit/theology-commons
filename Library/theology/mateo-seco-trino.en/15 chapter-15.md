## Chapter 15: The Nature and Attributes of God {#mateo-seco-trino.15}

Having studied the questions concerning the existence of God and the manner in which man can know him and speak of him, we now enter upon the consideration of the divine nature, seeking to answer the question *quid sit Deus*, what God is. In speaking of the divine nature, we refer to that which Saint Paul designates as *Theiótes*, Godhead or divinity (cf. Rom 1:20); that to which theologians point when they say of God that he is one in his nature and three in persons, safeguarding the divine unity precisely by emphasising what is common to the Persons: their nature. The history of theology shows how fruitful in Trinitarian theology was the formulation of the Cappadocians: *mía ousía, treis hypostáseis*, one substance, three hypostases. The Trinitarian mystery embraces both the affirmation of the Trinity and the affirmation of the unity of God.

In the study of the divine nature, it is fitting to bear in mind these three realities, so that they may serve as constant points of reference:

a) We enter upon the study of the divine nature with our eyes fixed on Christ's missionary mandate (cf. Mt 28:19), upon which rest the Christian confessions of faith in a God who is simultaneously unity and Trinity. We seek above all what this God preached by Christ is. We inquire into that which the divine Persons have in common, that which constitutes them as one sole God, for the affirmation of the Trinity implies no diminution of the monotheistic affirmation of the Old Testament (cf. Mk 12:29; Deut 6:4), but rather its most plenary manifestation.

b) Whatever has been said regarding the reasons for and importance of negative theology must likewise serve as a constant point of reference. Accurate indeed is the sober statement which Saint Thomas places precisely on the threshold of the questions concerning the divine attributes: "Because we cannot know what God is, but rather what he is not, we cannot consider how he is, but rather how he is not."[^1] The divine nature transcends all knowledge; consequently, our affirmations concerning its properties must be made with the utmost modesty. The enumeration and ordering of the divine properties must therefore be carried out with a clear awareness of the infinite distance separating our concepts from what God is.

c) Precisely for this reason, everything said regarding the divine properties must be stated analogically, making correct use of the three ways already discussed: the ways of affirmation, of negation, and of eminence (*via affirmationis, via negationis, via eminentiae*).

### The Concepts of Essence and Nature Applied to God

To speak of what God is, we have no other starting point than our knowledge of the material world in which we move. The concepts of essence and nature are drawn from it, with all that this implies of perfection and limitation. By the essence of a thing we understand that whereby a thing is what it is. A man is a man and not a horse. By essence we understand that whereby man is precisely man and not something else. This meaning of essence arises logically from the relation which the noun *essence* bears to the verb *to be*. By the term *essence* we refer to what a thing really is.

The term *nature* designates the same reality, but considered from the perspective of its birth or origin (*natura* from *nasci*), since each thing receives its being precisely at birth. Hence so frequently—in theology as well—the terms *essence* and *nature* are treated as synonymous. This is especially true in the case of God. However, the noun *nature* is cognate with the concept of being born and is consequently closely bound up with the corporeal sphere. But God is not born; he is eternal; nor does he belong to the corporeal world. The term *nature*, therefore, can only be applied to him in a truly remote and analogical manner, using it to designate, according to our mode of knowing, that in which the unity of God is rooted: that which is common to the three divine Persons, that which, so to speak, constitutes God in his being God.

Precisely because the material world is the unavoidable starting point of human cognition, when inquiring into the divine essence we must bear closely in mind that we are proceeding from realities that are intrinsically composite and limited, whereas we are inquiring into the nature of that which is infinitely simple and perfect. The things that serve as our starting point are composed of essence and existence; but in God there is no such composition. His being cannot be conceived as a potentiality actualised by existence. In God there is no potentiality whatsoever. His nature or essence cannot be conceived, therefore, as a term to which existence is added. Saint Thomas Aquinas's observations concerning the divine essence are of extraordinary theological importance: the divine essence is nothing other than his own existence.[^2] God is pure act (*actus purus*); that is to say, God is his own act of existing. But can this be called a true essence?

From this perspective, the Dionysian affirmations that God is super-essential (*hyperousios*), as being beyond all essence, appear profoundly reasonable. Indeed, God cannot be conceived as an essence brought into act by existence. God does not have an essence; he is a pure act of existing, unrestricted by any finite form of being. God does not have being: he is being. God does not have life: he is boundless life. Yet, after making these qualifications, it is necessary to affirm anew that there exists a divine essence or nature: that is, a fullness of perfections that constitutes him as God and distinguishes him from everything created. God is God: that is, he is an infinite and eternal being. He is not a vague or generic concept, but a concrete and personal Being possessed of infinite perfection.

The question concerning the divine essence is equivalent to this other question: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God; in what, then, does being God consist? Theological tradition has answered it by relying precisely on the concept of the divine *ousia*, the divine substance. The term *substance* must likewise be taken in an analogical sense. In God, who is utterly simple (*simplicissimus*), there is nothing *quod substet*, nothing underlying accidents. It is necessary, therefore, to strip the concept of substance of all limitation, avoiding any conception of the divine *ousia* as merely the most permanent substrate of being. But once these qualifications are made, it is right to apply to God the concepts of substance, nature, and essence, for "the better the divine essence is understood as absolutely transparent—that is, as infinite and all-perfect existence, which gives itself as love (pure act) without any formal limitation—the more safely the term essence may be employed."[^3]

What is truly signified by the term *essence* is everything divine, for in God there is nothing accidental. As we shall see below, the Trinity of Persons cannot be conceived as something added to the divine essence; they are identified with the divine essence: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are really one and the same God. There is in God no composition of essence and Persons. And yet—and this aspect is truly important—composition does exist in our concepts, that is, in our mode of knowing. Nor is there in God any composition among the infinite perfections of being. God is in fact a single infinite perfection, a pure act of being. And yet we speak of diverse perfections in God and arrange them according to a logical order. This composition stems from the limitation inherent in our way of knowing: we know by way of abstraction.

Accordingly, once the Trinity of Persons in God is known, we inquire into that in which these Persons are consubstantial. And they are consubstantial in those properties that belong to the divine nature, essence, or substance. We inquire, then, into what these divine properties consist of and how they are united among themselves and with the divine essence.

### The Ipsum Esse Subsistens

Both revelation and philosophical reason point to divine aseity as the ultimate ground of the perfections that, according to our manner of speaking, constitute the essence of God. For Christian theology, the revelation at Sinai (Ex 3:14) is absolutely decisive. God is he who is and who will be. Although this statement may initially have been understood as a divine promise to be with Moses, the Septuagint translation already understands it primarily in the ontological realm: God is he who truly exists, he who exists without measure. This is the meaning likewise found in Wis 13:1, where God is called *He who is* (*ton onta*). The divine includes being the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the Living One (cf. Rev 1:17; 22:13). Philosophical reason likewise points to considering existence as the metaphysical essence of God, as the formal constitutive of the divine nature.[^4] That is to say, existence is not only included in the divine essence, but is the ground of all its perfections. The ground and cause of everything existing must contain within itself the reason for its own existing. Existence must belong to him as his primary attribute precisely because he is that: a pure act of existing.[^5]

Saint Thomas says that *He who is* (cf. Ex 3:14) is the most proper of all names applied to God, adducing three arguments to demonstrate it: a) because it truly signifies God's being; b) because by its universality it is suited to designate the unlimited essence; c) because it clearly indicates that God's being knows only the present:

> "Since our intellect cannot in this life know the essence of God as it is in itself, whatever determines what we know of God to any mode of being whatsoever serves to remove it from the mode of being that belongs to him; and therefore, the less determined and the more common and absolute names are, the more properly we apply them to God."[^6]

*He who is* is the most apt name to designate the divine essence, since by its very universality it serves to designate being in all its plenitude of perfections. The divine essence is not restricted to any particular mode of being, but contains within itself, transcending them, all modes of being. This is possible only if the divine essence is identified with existence. Unlike everything we know, God does not have being: he is being. Implicit in this affirmation is the recognition of God's infinite transcendence with respect to the world. Indeed, He who is Being cannot be subsumed within an ontological genus that would encompass both him and the world simultaneously.

> "Between the being of God and the having being of creatures there is an infinite qualitative difference [...] Thomas explicitly affirms that God is not contained within any genus, nor within being. He stands at an infinite distance from all other reality."[^7]

Saint Thomas refers in this place not only to Ex 3:14, but to two Fathers of paramount importance—Saint Augustine and Saint John Damascene—to underline that his affirmation is rooted in the deepest theological tradition.[^8] This is no mere philosophical question, but the consideration of a profound theological truth in its metaphysical dimension. Saint Thomas regards the affirmation of the *Ipsum esse subsistens* as the ultimate core upon which the divine perfections rest. He is led to this conviction by the reading of Ex 3:14; he is led to this conviction by the ultimate term to which the Five Ways supporting his demonstration of God's existence lead: the necessary being must possess in its own essence the reason for its existence. He must exist of himself (*a se*), not by participation. He must be pure act. Now, "every existence distinct from essence stands to it in the same relation as act to potency. But in God there is no potentiality whatsoever. Therefore his essence is his very existence."[^9]

The *Ipsum esse subsistens* means that God exists in himself, for himself, and from himself. He is the necessary being and therefore is not a caused being, not even by himself. God possesses within himself the ultimate reason for his own existence—he is not a being that arises by chance or whose existence lacks sufficient reason—but this ultimate reason cannot be conceived as causality, but rather as the necessity flowing from what he is: that is, the necessity flowing from the identity between his essence and his existence.[^10]

For this reason, in God his essence is not really distinguished from his life. He is pure act, indefectible life. Anyone who understood the *Ipsum esse subsistens* as something abstract, neutral, or inert would have fundamentally misunderstood what this designation implies. Indeed, many Thomists identify this definition with another that seems to point more directly to the inner life of God: the divine essence would consist in being the *Ipsum intelligere subsistens*, that is, in being a subsistent act of intellection.[^11] They argue that intellective life is the supreme degree of being in act, and therefore, if God is understood as pure act, he must necessarily be understood as a pure act of understanding.

It is evident that infinite being includes within itself life, and therefore the acts of intellect and will (love), since in God life and being are identical. And it is clear that God is his own act of intellect and love. But the precise question is not whether understanding constitutes the divine essence, but which of the divine perfections is, according to our mode of knowing, the root of all the others.[^12] And according to our mode of knowing and the logical order of concepts, God's acts of intellect and love are rooted in the plenitude and infinity of the divine act of existing, and not vice versa. He is an infinite act of Love because he is an infinite act of being.[^13]

It has justly been observed that the scholastic synthesis, although it may appear abstract in its formulation of the perfections adorning the divine being, is in its content and intentionality far closer to biblical thought than might seem to a superficial observer. The God spoken of here is the God described in the Bible as gracious, righteous, wise, faithful, and merciful. In identifying essence and existence, essence and life in God, Saint Thomas stands very close to the dynamic conception of the divine being as unfolded in Sacred Scripture. All he does is express this message in a rigorous metaphysical formulation.[^14] This is a task that remains necessary in theology, especially when treating the question of God.[^15] Patristic theology demonstrated this from the very beginning—from the Apologists onwards—ever attentive to dialogue with the thought of its era.

The *Ipsum esse subsistens* is, moreover, a formulation very close to many contemporary philosophical perspectives that highlight the significance of freedom in the constitution of personal being. God is an infinite and eternal act of freedom. God possesses within himself the reason for his own existence precisely because he is his own act of existing. He is not an essence that realises itself by unfolding in its acts, but an infinite act subsisting in itself.

This does not mean that the *Ipsum esse subsistens* can be conceived as the cause of itself. The concept of *causa sui*, taken seriously, is self-contradictory, for it supposes that something is simultaneously the cause and the effect of its own origin. Applied to God, this concept implies, moreover, the assertion that God is a caused being, with all the consequences that this entails: among others, that he is not a necessary being, that is, that he does not possess in his own essence the entire reason for his existence. It would then be intellectually more honest to deny outright the existence of a transcendent God—a denial already implicit in the assertion that he is a caused being, even if by himself.[^16] Certainly, this God has nothing in common with the God of the Bible, who always reveals himself as a personal Being transcendent to the world: an intelligent and free Being, not a nature that unfolds in a process of modification of its own substance.[^17] Nor does it have anything in common with what in theology is designated by the name *Ipsum esse subsistens*.

### The Divine Perfections

The *Ipsum esse subsistens*, precisely by reason of its plenitude of being, possesses all perfections in an infinite degree. Even this manner of expression, which seems obvious, reveals the limitation of human language. In truth, strictly speaking, God does not possess all perfections in an infinite degree; rather, he is a single perfection in an infinite degree, containing within himself all perfections. But human discourse is incapable of conceiving an infinite perfection without "breaking it down" into the cluster of perfections that this infinity entails and which it contemplates distributed throughout the extra-divine world.

> "All our speaking about God is built upon the foundation that God is Being. The other names do no more than express the inexhaustible richness contained in subsistent Being, which encompasses all perfections. Nevertheless, if owing to the limited power and penetration of our ideas we need even to divide the essences of created things and consider their various aspects successively, this is all the more necessary in the case of God, who is All Being, inexhaustible for the created intellect. Hence we must employ many names for God, and they will never be sufficient. But just as nothing can be known except by resolution into being, neither are the names of God understood except upon their foundation, which is the highest metaphysical truth: God is being."[^18]

Thus, whatever we say concerning the divine perfections is nothing other than the making explicit of the perfection contained in the *Ipsum esse subsistens*: that is, the "refraction" of a single perfection, just as a rainbow refracts the utterly simple light into its marvellous colours.

In God are found all the perfections found in created beings. These perfections are necessarily found in him as in their cause. But not all are found in the same manner. There are some perfections whose concept entails no imperfection whatsoever. These are called simple or pure perfections, such as beauty or goodness. There are others whose concept entails imperfection. These are called mixed or impure perfections, such as corporeality. Simple perfections are found in God formally: that is, according to their proper formal character (*ratio*), although in an infinite degree and in a manner superior to the way in which they can be found in any created being. God is truly and properly wise, just, powerful, full of mercy. God is Love (1 Jn 4:16). He is this in an infinite and utterly simple degree, even though we, through the limitation of our intellect, conceptually distinguish the various facets of that single divine perfection.[^19]

Mixed perfections, precisely because they entail imperfection in their very concept, are found in God only virtually: that is, inasmuch as they proceed from him as from their cause. Indeed, mixed perfections are so inextricably intertwined with limitation and imperfection that it is impossible to liberate them from that imperfection without destroying them. Thus, for example, corporeality is so subject to the limitations of divisibility and corruptibility that it is impossible to apply it to God except metaphorically and virtually. It cannot be applied to him formally and properly, since to strip it of all divisibility and corruptibility means that it ceases to be corporeality. And yet all that corporeality contains of perfection is found in God as in its cause, though in a manner infinitely more sublime than that which is directly signified by its concept.

### The Divine Attributes

For this reason, although all perfections are found in God, not all receive the name of divine attributes. This name is reserved exclusively to designate simple perfections which, entailing no imperfection in their concept, are found necessarily and formally in God. They are, as it were, the properties of the divine being, the notes that characterise it. Divine attributes may therefore be defined as the qualities or virtues that constitute the essence of God and which, according to our logical order of knowing, flow from the metaphysical constitutive of his essence: that is, from the *Ipsum esse subsistens*. These are perfections common to the three divine Persons, for each of them possesses in common and without division the entire divine essence. For this reason, matters concerning the Trinitarian relations are not categorised as divine attributes, since they are not common to the three divine Persons, but proper to each of them individually.

In enumerating the divine attributes, human cognition must follow the pathways already described in the preceding chapter as the ways of affirmation, of negation, and of eminence. In God all perfections are found, liberated from their limitations and in an infinite and utterly simple manner. This infinity entails, as has already been seen, the fact that one perfection includes all the others. This statement holds true even when speaking of attributes that, according to our way of conceiving, appear mutually contradictory. Thus, for example, God is infinitely wise, infinitely just, and infinitely merciful. God is all these things at the same time. Every work of God is full of justice, wisdom, and mercy. His mercy is just and wise, and it would not be infinitely perfect mercy if it lacked justice or wisdom. The *via eminentiae* therefore assumes immense importance when speaking of the divine perfections. It entails pursuing to its ultimate conclusions the affirmation of God's infinity, which carries with it the unity with which all perfections are contained in him, such that, in reality, they are inseparable from one another, even though conceptually distinguished.

Consequently, it is possible to speak of the divine perfections only analogically. Any univocism with creatures is necessarily mistaken, even when speaking of simple perfections. Indeed, these perfections are found in God in an ineffable manner.[^20] Hence the aptness with which patristic thought, especially Pseudo-Dionysius, called God *polyōnymos* (or *panōnymos*), *anōnymos*, and *hyperōnymos*: the One of all names, the One of no name, the One above every name.[^21] This unity of all divine perfections is what makes God's actions in salvation history so baffling to human intelligence. Truly, God's ways are not our ways (cf. Is 55:8).

The attributes really constitute the divine essence, which is utterly simple. This raises the question of how they are united and how they are distinguished among themselves and with respect to the divine essence. Given divine simplicity, the divine attributes cannot be understood as really distinct. If they were so understood, there would be composition in God of really distinct perfections. This is the way Gilbert of Poitiers (*Porretanus*) understood them.[^22] Such a real distinction would run counter to the axiom already employed by Saint Augustine that, in God, "all things are one where there is no opposition of relation."[^23] Nor can the divine attributes be taken as mere synonyms.[^24] The concept of what we designate by the name of justice is not identical to that of wisdom or mercy. Even when applied to God, the concept of being is not the same as the concept of goodness or truth, although in reality being, truth, and goodness are identified. There exists among these concepts an authentic distinction of reason with a foundation in reality (*distinctio rationis ratiocinatae*). This means that although in reality the divine perfections mutually include one another, in our cognition they are distinguished as the explicit and the implicit: when we say that God is infinitely merciful we take for granted that this mercy is just, and when we say that he is infinitely just we take for granted that his justice is merciful; but when we say that God is just, we are not saying precisely the same thing as when we call him merciful.[^25]

### Classification of the Divine Attributes

Given this distinction of the divine attributes among themselves and with respect to the divine essence, it is logical to structure their enumeration according to a logical order that takes as its point of reference man made in the image and likeness of God.

Sacred Scripture enumerates the divine attributes in diverse ways, without attempting, as has already been seen, an exhaustive enumeration or the establishment of a systematic order among them.[^26] Nor do the Holy Fathers offer a complete scheme of the attributes, among many other reasons because they do not employ the concept of divine attribute in as precise a sense as later theology would. They do, however, frequently provide various enumerations of the divine perfections.[^27] We likewise find in the texts of the Magisterium abundant enumerations of the divine attributes, without any pretension to exhaustiveness or to establishing a hierarchy among them, but with the firm conviction that something of what God is is truly being stated.[^28]

The Holy Fathers already established several classifications of the perfections or names of God that were to exert a decisive influence on subsequent theology. The first is the distinction between absolute attributes and relative attributes. Generally, they call absolute attributes those applied to God considered in his unity, and relative attributes those referring to God considered in his operation *ad extra*. At other times they use this distinction to designate the perfections common to the three divine Persons (absolute attributes) and those proper to each of them (relative attributes). The second distinction is between negative attributes and affirmative attributes. This distinction was of great importance in the Eunomian controversy, as has already been seen. Affirmative attributes designate attributes as existing in God inasmuch as he is the cause of those same perfections in created things; negative attributes express those perfections by negative names, in order to show forth the transcendence of the divine essence.[^29]

Following these initial distinctions from the patristic era, theology frequently employs the following divisions:

a) Absolute and relative attributes: absolute attributes belong to God as subsisting in himself, and relative attributes belong to him as he relates to created being;

b) Negative and positive attributes, according as they are enunciated in negative or positive form. In employing this distinction, it must be borne in mind that every negative proposition simultaneously contains an affirmation, and that every affirmative proposition implies a negation. When we say that God is infinite, we wish to indicate not only that he has no limits, but that this lack of limits is due to his plenitude of being; and when we say that he is good, we mean that he is good infinitely, and not in a limited manner like created beings;

c) Communicable and incommunicable attributes: some divine perfections are incommunicable to creatures, such as innascibility; others are communicable, such as love or wisdom;

d) Attributes of divine being and of divine activity (entitative and operative attributes), according as they are used to designate the being of God or the qualities of divine activity. This distinction is customarily used as a primary foundation for structuring the arrangement of the divine attributes.[^30]

e) God's activity *ad extra* is free. Just, therefore, is the observation that the properties or attributes of divine action cannot always be deduced directly from the divine essence. Indeed, freedom imparts to these actions of God a distinctive nuance not simply contained within the properties of the divine essence. This is specifically the case with many of the attributes by which God's action is described in Sacred Scripture. These are affirmations concerning God's historical-salvific action, in which the essence of God is certainly manifested—because it is God himself who is manifested in history—yet manifested in a manner that cannot be deduced from general affirmations regarding the divine essence. From the fact that God is infinitely good, the mystery of the Incarnation cannot simply be deduced. The Incarnation is profoundly congruent with divine goodness, but it responds to a free act of God. In it God manifests his goodness in a manner that is not simply deducible from the divine essence. Hence it is logical to add to the divine operative attributes—which regard above all the immanent acts of intellect and love—the consideration of God's ways of acting in the history of salvation, as a subdivision in which the divine attributes are considered on the basis of God's interventions in history: interventions wherein God manifests what he is like precisely in his free and, in a certain sense, unpredictable acting. The God of whom theology speaks is the God whose face has been definitively revealed in Christ. He is certainly the same God of whom creation and metaphysics speak, but theology speaks of him in a radically new way and with radically new characteristics, since its starting point is the manner in which God has revealed his way of being through his action in history.[^31]

In systematising the divine attributes, Saint Thomas Aquinas employs a division that is not strictly symmetrical. He first studies the attributes concerning the divine being, in order then to enter upon the questions relating to divine life, without directly considering this life as an attribute. The operative attributes are studied from the perspective of divine operations, both immanent—knowledge and love—and transient (*transeuntes*), especially the power of God.[^32]

For our part, within this broad division between entitative and operative attributes, we shall structure the study of the attributes in three chapters, seeking especially to underscore divine transcendence: a) the transcendence of God with respect to the corporeal world; b) the transcendence of God with respect to time and space; c) the transcendence of God with respect to created spirits.
