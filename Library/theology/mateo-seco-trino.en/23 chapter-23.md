## Chapter 23: The Procession of the Second Person {#mateo-seco-trino.23}

Jesus is the beloved Son in whom the Father is well pleased (cf. Mt 3:17). In Jesus' filiation to the Father—in the mystery of Christ's filial consciousness—the existence of the first procession in God is revealed: a procession that takes place by way of generation. The names by which the divine nature of Jesus is designated leave no room for doubt in this regard. Jesus is Son, Word, and Image of the Father. As has already been seen, Jesus calls God His Father with such radicality that, from the very beginning, the primitive community confesses that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of the living God (Mt 16:16).

This confession of Jesus' divine sonship is the central content of the Gospel preached by Saint Paul, which he characterises as the Gospel of the Son of God (cf. Rom 1:3). In the Prologue of Saint John's Gospel, the Son is identified as the Word or *Logos*, who was with God from the beginning and who is the Only-Begotten of the Father (Jn 1:1–14). Filiation, Word, and *Logos*, which make the Son the perfect Image of the Father (cf. Col 1:15).

Jesus has revealed that God is Father in a sense new to that of the Old Testament: God is His Father in the strictest and most profound relationship of paternity. For this reason the Apostles confess Jesus as the Word who in the beginning was with God and was God (cf. Jn 1:1), as the image of the invisible God (Col 1:15), and as the splendour of His glory and the express image of His essence (Heb 1:3).[^1]

### Generation in God

It is explicitly revealed that Jesus Christ is the Son of God. This carries with it the affirmation that, in God, there are a Father and a Son, and, consequently, that generation exists in God. This affirmation entails another: there is a procession in God that takes place by means of an action analogous to what in our human speech is termed generation. It is an analogous expression, as corresponds to the difference between God and creatures. Yet, as is obvious, this analogical language cannot be taken as a mere metaphor. The affirmation of the divine filiation of Jesus—the Only-Begotten of the Father, God from God, Light from Light, true God from true God, of the same substance as the Father[^2]—requires an equally firm affirmation of the existence of generation in God.

This generation is an immanent, intradivine action; for to beget is really to communicate existence, by a vital action, to someone distinct from oneself, yet of the same nature. That which is begotten must therefore remain within God. Saint Thomas Aquinas describes the concept in this manner:

> "In God, the procession of the Word is called generation. To explain this, it must be observed that we use the word *generation* in two senses. In a general sense, for everything that can be generated and corrupted: and in this sense generation is nothing other than the change from non-being to being. In another way, we use it in a proper sense, for living beings; and in this sense generation signifies the origin of a living being from a conjoined living principle, and this is properly called birth. However, not everything that is produced in this way can strictly be called begotten, but only that which proceeds according to the ratio of likeness; and for this reason hair and nails do not have the character of being begotten, nor of sons (...); but for the true ratio of generation it is required that it proceed according to the ratio of likeness that comes from having the same specific nature, which is how man proceeds from man and horse from horse."[^3]

This twofold concept of generation marks the difference between Arius and the Christian faith. Arius takes the generation of the Son in the first, generic sense. Strictly speaking, that is not generation, but production. Hence Arius conceived this peculiar generation as a production *ad extra*, that is to say, as a creation. Christian doctrine takes the generation of the Son in the proper sense: that is, precisely as we see that living beings beget and are begotten. The begotten proceeds from a living being, by a vital action, which makes it like unto the one who begets it. The vital action is key when speaking of a true generation. Indeed, a vital action is the only way in which a living being can communicate its own nature to another living being. It is essential to the concept of a son that he proceed from the father, receiving from him his very nature because the father gives it to him. It is also essential that the begotten be of the same nature as the begetter. In God, the Son cannot be a creature, but God from God, remaining within the intimate simplicity of the divine being.

Having said this, it is necessary to insist on the requirement that the application of these concepts respect the laws of analogy. Generation occurs in God in a proper sense, yet without the imperfections that we are accustomed to observe in material generation. Here are some of these points: in the generation of created beings, there is a transition from non-being to being, and therefore the father is prior to the son, and the son begins to be at his generation; nor is the communication of the nature made by an earthly father to his son perfect: the father gives the son a nature identical in species, but not identical in number. In God, by contrast, the generation of the Son involves no transition from non-being to being: the Son exists eternally and is eternally being begotten by the Father; the Son receives the same substance as the Father, not only in species, but also in number. For this reason it is affirmed that the Father and the Son are one and the same single God. Furthermore, given the infinite divine simplicity, the Father cannot give His own nature except in an infinitely perfect manner, that is, without division or distinction.[^4]

Generation in God is, therefore, a most perfect immanent action: eternal and immutable, that is to say, without beginning, end, or succession. It is not a process, but an infinitely simple act. This means that the generation of the Word, so to speak, has always attained its term and yet is always being realised. The Son is always receiving His being—He is always proceeding—from the Father, in such a way that He never has anything of Himself: He is always being grounded in His personal being by the generating action of the Father.[^5] And yet, He never began to be; His generation does not imply a transition from non-being to being. The Holy Fathers were fond of quoting Psalm 2:7 to refer precisely to this peculiarity of generation in God: *Filius meus es tu; ego hodie genui te* ("Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee"), which they frequently understood as the generation of the Son in the eternal today of God.

### The Procession of the Word According to the Operation of the Intellect

Every procession presupposes an action.[^6] Following the affirmation that generation exists in God, the question arises as to how this generative action in God is to be conceived. It is obvious, given the infinite simplicity of God, that it makes no sense to conceive this generation in a sexual manner; it must be conceived, as befits the divine infinity, in a manner that transcends all division and composition. That is, it must be conceived in analogy with the processions that take place in the acts of knowledge and love.

The New Testament provides abundant clues pointing to the generation of the Son by an act of knowledge. Jesus is not only called Son, but also Word and Image. Saint Augustine formulated this relationship with clarity when writing in his *De Trinitate*:

> "The Father and the Son are together one essence, one greatness, one truth, and one wisdom. But the Father and the Son together are not one Word, because they are not together one Son. For Son is relative to the Father, not to Himself; and so, when the Word is spoken of, the Word is relative to Him whose Word He is. By the very same reason that He is Word, He is Son; and by the very same reason that He is Son, He is Word."[^7]

The generative action, then, is situated in the realm of knowledge. It is an intellectual generation. According to Saint Augustine, in God, Son and Word are identified, because both designate the same personal reality according to its manner of proceeding: that which constitutes the second Person as Word is that which constitutes Him as Son, and vice versa. The Son is begotten in an act of intelligence. The analogy between the human act of knowing and the divine act supports this affirmation:

> "In everyone who understands," writes Saint Thomas, "by the very fact of understanding, there proceeds within him something that is the conception of the thing understood, which comes from the intellectual power and proceeds from the knowledge or apprehension of the thing. This conception is expressed by the outer word, and is called the word of the heart (*verbum cordis*)."[^8]

The analogy lies precisely in the structure of knowledge. The act of knowing is endowed with fecundity and implies a certain distinction between the knower and that which is known, even when we refer to the consciousness that each one has of his own act of existing. A clear difference exists between our act of existing and the perception we have of it. In explaining this matter, the following passage from Saint Thomas is classic:

> "It is evident that God knows Himself. The object known is in the knower insofar as it is known. Indeed, to know means nothing other than the intellectual expression of the object known. And thus our knowledge, in knowing itself, is in itself, not only as identical with itself in essence, but also as spoken or expressed by its own intellection. It is necessary, then, that God should be in Himself as understood, in His own self-knowing. Now the object known, present to the intellect, is nothing other than the intelligible representation conceived by the intellect, that is, the word. Therefore, in understanding Himself, God has within Himself the Word of God, which is as God Himself understood, in a manner similar to how the concept of a stone, present in our intellect, is the stone itself insofar as it is understood."[^9]

Saint Thomas's final caution must be taken with its full force: God has within Himself the Word of God in a manner similar to how man has the human word. Only in a similar manner. We are moving in the terrain of mystery and analogy: both prevent the establishing of a strict equation between the way in which the human intellect utters its word and the manner in which the Word proceeds from the Father. The analogy refers primarily to the consideration that the intellect is fruitful, conceiving and bringing forth within itself the object known. The act of knowing presents itself to us, then, in its highest perfection as an authentic generation. This is the essence of the analogy established between the human word and the Word of God.

The correct use of analogy demands that one point out the differences between the production of the human word and the generation of the Word of God. Here are some of them: in man, the word is an accident, whereas in God, the Word is essential and a subsistent person; the human word is produced in order to be able to understand, whereas the divine Word is not pronounced out of indigence, nor in order to understand, but through the infinite fecundity of the Father's intellection.

The prudence with which it is necessary to use this analogy becomes evident if one considers, for example, that the Son and the Holy Spirit participate in the single divine act of understanding, and yet the Word proceeds solely from the Father, while neither the Word nor the Holy Spirit begets another Word in knowing. For this reason, the analogy rests fundamentally on the fact that it is proper to the intellect to beget the word. But it would be a theological blunder to attempt to turn this analogy into a proof in the strict sense, in the Hegelian fashion, as though every intellect, in knowing and by the mere fact of knowing, were necessarily required to beget a mental word. John of Saint Thomas points to the latitude with which this analogy must be used when he notes that, since it is proper to intellectual nature to be manifestative of itself, it is reasonable to conceive within the bosom of the divinity the existence of a Word as the subsistent reflection of infinite wisdom.[^10]

As has already been seen in the preceding chapters, the characteristics of the human word can only be applied to generation in God if every kind of imperfection is eliminated from this act of knowing and begetting. It is, for example, an eternal generation, which does not involve a transition from non-being to being. The Word has always existed with God, for it is absurd to think, as Saint Gregory of Nyssa argued, that there was ever a moment in which God was alogical—that is, in which God was without the *Logos*—and it is likewise absurd to think that anything divine has begun to exist.[^11] This means that the Father is eternally begetting the Son, and the Son is eternally being begotten by the Father. Father and Son are mutually related in so indissoluble a manner as the Fathers at Nicaea intended to signify when using the image of light and splendour to refer to the generation of the Word: the Son is Light from Light. It is impossible to separate light from its splendour, or splendour from the light from which it proceeds. Both are, in fact, one and the same thing, and yet the splendour proceeds from the light.

Even within its perfection, the analogy of the human word has its limits in explaining the generation of the Word in God.[^12] It should also be borne in mind that it belongs strictly to faith only that the Son proceeds from the Father by generation, not that this generation takes place by an act of knowledge. That the generation of the Word takes place by way of intellect is a theological assertion firmly rooted in Scripture and Tradition, and commonly defended by theologians, to the point that it would seem impossible to deny it without grave temerity.

The teachings of the New Testament—especially the Johannine theology of the *Logos* and the Pauline theology of the Son as the image of the invisible God and splendour of the Father's glory (cf. Col 1:13–20; Heb 1:3)—are something more than a mere suggestion; they seem rather a serious call to conceive it as a generation by way of intellect, a call that was rigorously followed by the Greek Fathers, especially Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, and Saint Gregory of Nyssa. This call was likewise followed by the Latin Fathers, especially Saint Hilary and Saint Augustine. The latter brought the analogy between the Son and the human word to a decisive formulation when, in *De Trinitate*, he used the spiritual life of man—knowledge and love—as an analogy to delve more deeply into the explanation of the Trinitarian mystery.[^13]

### Uniqueness and Perfection of the Divine Word

Continuing with the analogy of the human word, theologians have always found an argument for the uniqueness of the Word in God precisely in His perfection as Word. The same holds true when speaking of the Son. Both the Word and the Son express the Father most perfectly. It follows that, in God, the Son can only be the Only-Begotten. Divine generation is of an infinite and most simple perfection: the Father gives His whole being to the Son; the Father utters in the Word the whole of the unfathomable divine wisdom. From whatever point of view it is examined, the infinite perfection and simplicity of God requires the uniqueness of the Son: the very act of begetting requires it, since its perfection includes infinity in the Father's communication of His own substance of Father to the Son, to the point that the Son is one and the same single God with the Father; the perfection of the Son and Word as the term of this generation likewise requires it, since in His infinity He expresses the Father perfectly.

It belongs to faith that the Word is the only Son of the Father, as is affirmed in the New Testament (cf., e.g., Jn 1:18; 1 Jn 4:9). The theological reason is also clear: the perfection of generation in God excludes all multiplicity.[^14] A multiplicity of sons would not signify perfection in generation, but rather the inability to communicate the entire substance to the Son, just as a multiplicity of words indicates the incapacity to say everything in a single, simple, and infinite word. Here is the argument as formulated by Saint Thomas:

> "Since God, in understanding Himself, understands all other things, and understands Himself with a simple and single intuition, because His understanding is His being, the Word of God is necessarily one. And since in God the generation of the Son and the conception of the Word are one and the same thing, it follows that in the Godhead there is only one generation and only one Son begotten of the Father. Whence Saint John says: *We have seen him as the only-begotten of the Father* (Jn 1:14)."[^15]

### Jesus as Eternal and Temporal Word of the Father

The uniqueness of Jesus Christ—given the mystery of the hypostatic union—leads to considering Him as the Father's only Word, in whom the Father, both eternally and temporally, says everything He has to say. Perhaps no one has expressed this truth in words as beautiful as those of Saint John of the Cross:

> "For in giving us, as He has given us, His Son, who is His Word—and He has no other—He spoke all to us together and once for all in this single Word, and He has no more to speak (...) Wherefore he that would now inquire of God, or seek any vision or revelation, would not only be guilty of foolish behaviour, but would offer an offence to God, by not fixing his eyes entirely upon Christ, desiring some other thing or novelty. For God might answer him in this manner, saying: If I have already spoken all things to you in My Word, which is My Son, and I have no other, what can I now answer you or reveal to you that is more than this? Fix your eyes on Him alone, for in Him I have spoken and revealed all to you, and you will find in Him even more than you ask and desire."[^16]

Jesus is the eternal Word of the Father, who has become flesh and spoken to men in human language. It has rightly been pointed out that it was through divine revelation that the Apostles and Saint Paul himself attained to the knowledge of the exalted Christology that they confessed and preached. It is a Christology that sinks its roots into the history of Jesus, but enters deeply into the intimate mystery of God. Jesus is the Word who was with God from the beginning, through whom all things were made, who reveals the glory of the Father, and who is the definitive witness and testimony of God before the world.[^17]

For this reason, the word of Jesus cannot be explained in merely prophetic categories. The word does not come upon Him; He *is* the eternal Word of the Father. Heaven and earth will pass away, but His words will not pass away (cf. Mt 24:35). The whole of Jesus—in His life, in His gestures, in His words, and in His silences—is the manifestation of the unique Word of God. Hence the Apocalypse presents His word as a sharp two-edged sword proceeding from His mouth (cf. Rev 1:16; 2:12). Jesus is essentially the faithful and true witness (cf. Rev 1:5), for He alone, as the Son, knows the Father (cf. Mt 11:27).

In Christ there is but one filiation to the Father. He is essentially and absolutely filiation to the Father. The same holds true if He is considered as Word. He is essentially and exclusively Word. And this because, as the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states, the Incarnation implies that "the Son of God communicated to his humanity his own personal mode of existence in the Trinity. In his soul as in his body, Christ thus expressed humanly the divine ways of the Trinity."[^18]

### The Name Word, a Personal Name in God

Three names are especially attributed to the second Person: Son, Word, and Image. These are three names that designate the same reality and differ from one another only in their mode of signifying: *Son* is said as relative to the Father; *Word*, as relative to the One who speaks it; *Image*, as relative to the exemplar it reflects. The essential aspects of the name *Son* have already been studied in the preceding chapter, since paternity and filiation are correlative notions. We shall now dwell upon the names *Word* and *Image*.

The correction made by Saint Thomas on this subject is paradigmatic. In his early writings he maintained that the name *word* could be applied in God in an essential sense or in a personal sense.[^19] In the *Summa Theologiae* his position is decidedly the reverse: the name *Word*, if used in its proper sense, can only be applied to God in a personal sense, and in no way in an essential sense. The fundamental reason has already been alluded to when noting the need to use the analogy of the mental word with the Word of God with prudence: the three divine Persons know in the pure and essential act of the single divine intellect, whereas only the Father speaks the Word; the Son and the Spirit do not utter a word in knowing:

> "Just as the word *Word*, properly speaking, is attributed to God in a personal and not an essential sense, the same is true of the word *to speak*; wherefore, just as the Word is not common to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, so neither is it true that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are a single speaker."[^20]

For this reason, when we say that God has a Word, we are using this name in a personal sense, that is, as the proper name of the second Person. And in no way can it be used in an essential sense, since it is not the divine essence that speaks the Word, but only the Father.[^21] As has already been seen, the Persons do not proceed from the divine essence, but from the Persons. The Word proceeds solely from the Father, whereas that which is essential in God is common to the three Persons.

The New Testament attributes to Our Lord the name of Son far more frequently than that of Word. The name of Word is attributed to Him in only three places: John 1:1–14; 1 John 1:1; and Revelation 19:13. This is perhaps the reason why the early Christian community preferred the name *Son* to that of *Word*. The Fathers also made more frequent use of the title *Son*, although from the second century onwards a rich theology of the *Logos* began to develop. The name *Word* is not only a personal name in God, but expresses the reality of the second Person with equal propriety as the name *Son*. Saint Augustine, as has already been seen, formulated this with enviable clarity: the second Person is Son by the very reason that He is Word, and is Word by the very reason that He is Son.[^22] Saint Thomas, drawing upon this thought, observes that in the name *Word* the same property is included as in that of *Son*:

> "Indeed, the very birth of the Son, which is His personal property, is designated by various names, all of which are attributed to the Son to express His perfection in diverse ways: to designate Him as connatural with the Father, He is called Son; as co-eternal, Splendour; as altogether like, He is called Image; and to signify that He is begotten immaterially, He is called Word; and this, because it was not possible to find a single name suited to designate all these things."[^23]

The difference, then, between Word and Son is that, while both terms refer to the same reality, the term *Word* designates more explicitly the manner in which the Son is begotten by the Father: as the word that is spoken in the act of knowing. The name *Word* provides the reason why the procession of the Son is a generation.[^24]

The Word not only manifests God, but, precisely by manifesting Him in all perfection, also manifests creatures. Indeed, the Word proceeds from the Father insofar as the Father knows the divine essence most perfectly, not only in itself and in the divine Persons, but also as the efficient and exemplary cause of all things:

> "Since God by one sole act understands Himself and all things, His only Word expresses not only the Father, but also all creatures."[^25]

The name *Word*, in its mode of signifying, presents a horizon distinct from that of *Son*: the horizon of the knowledge of God, of the wisdom that manifests the infinite richness of the divine essence, which stands in relationship to the role of the Word in the creation of the world. The Word not only expresses the Father, but also expresses and acts in creation. The Fathers relied upon this aspect of the theology of the Word to show that it was fitting that it should be the Word who came to save what was lost. The earliest theology of the Cross was likewise grounded in this conception of the Word present in all creation as Word, who, upon the Cross, draws all things to Himself.[^26]

### The Name Image

Saint Paul designates the Son as the Image of the Father and the figure of His substance (cf. Col 1:15; Heb 1:3). The Greek Fathers understood that the name *Image* also belongs to the Holy Spirit, for in the Holy Spirit, insofar as He proceeds from the Father, the entire perfection of the Father is reflected. They wished in this way to emphasise that the Spirit really proceeds from the Father and therefore possesses and reflects His very divinity. Furthermore, they not only call the Spirit the image of the Father, but also the image of the Son.[^27] Latin theologians, especially from Saint Thomas onwards, prefer to consider the name *Image* as a personal and exclusive name of the second Person. On this point, the following passage from Saint Augustine, to which Saint Thomas refers, is highly revealing:

> "*Word* has the same meaning as *Image*; the Father and the Son cannot both be Image, for only the Son is the Image of the Father, just as He alone is His Son, since the two cannot both be Sons."[^28]

It is not that the Latins, obviously, deny the equality between the Spirit and the Father; rather, they prefer to continue delving into the theology of the Word as essentially expressive and manifestative of the divine substance, following the analogy with the human word. To this end, they accentuate the radicality with which the term *image* must be taken and, above all, its connection with the Son's mode of proceeding. Here is Saint Thomas's reflection:

> "It must be said that, just as the Holy Spirit is not called born, although by proceeding from the Father He receives His nature from Him as does the Son, so also, although He receives a form like that of the Father, He is not called image; because the Son proceeds as Word, of whose essence it is to have likeness in species to that from which it proceeds; which does not belong to the essence of love, although it belongs to that love which is the Holy Spirit, insofar as it is divine love."[^29]

This represents a different way of applying the analogy of knowledge and love in Trinitarian theology. In employing the word *image* in the broad sense of equality of likeness, without concerning themselves with whether this equality is essential to the mode of proceeding, the Greeks designate the Holy Spirit as the image not only of the Father, but also of the Son; consequently, the name *Image* is not considered proper and exclusive to the Son. In this way, equality in the Trinity is highlighted. Nor did the Greeks orient themselves towards a theology of the Word in which the analogy with the human word was used with absolute precision as the image in which knowledge is expressed. The Latins did so, and hence preferred to reserve the name *Image* for the second Person, as corresponds not only to the fact that He proceeds from the Father, but to the fact that He proceeds from the Father as Word; and for this reason they consider Him reduplicatively as His Image. The Latins add another reason for this way of proceeding: namely, that Sacred Scripture never applies the name *Image* to the Holy Spirit.

In any event, if one wishes to understand Latin theology on this point, it is necessary to bear in mind that the three names applied to the second Person are not applied to Him as distinct personal notes, but as one and the same note considered under diverse aspects. Paraphrasing Saint Augustine, we might say that, for the Latins, the Son is Word insofar as He is Son, and He is Image insofar as He is Word. The name *Image* is applied to Him exclusively only in the sense that this name designates the essential note of the Word: to be the most faithful image of the thing known.

### The Beloved Son

The analogy of the Son of God with the human word has yielded invaluable fruit in theological exposition. It has already been pointed out that, like every analogy, it is exposed to serious limitations. Among these limitations is the tendency to conceive the Son as the exclusive fruit of an act of intellect, prescinding from Love. The Word is the Word of the Father, but a Word that is not pronounced without Love; and it is, in turn, a Word that spires Love, that is, the Holy Spirit.

Contemporary theology, without altering anything that classical theology has said regarding the procession of the Word—the Son is begotten by the Father as interior Word—likes to draw attention against a dichotomy that would deprive this generation of Love. The argument is usually made by analogy with the divine filiation of Jesus. It is clear that the faith of the Church rests upon the filial consciousness manifested by Christ, upon Jesus' *Abba*. The filial consciousness of Jesus reflects the eternal generation of the Word. Not without reason does the Word communicate to Him His own mode of existing in the Trinity. This consciousness is the consciousness of being the beloved Son.

Indeed, in the New Testament one finds texts of particular importance in which the divine filiation of Jesus is revealed as a sonship springing from love. It suffices to recall the Baptism of Jesus: *Thou art my beloved Son; with thee I am well pleased* (Mk 1:11; cf. Mt 3:17; Lk 3:22). Along an itinerary similar to that followed to deduce the filiation of the Word from the filial consciousness of Jesus, contemporary theology poses questions such as this: "How is the beloved Son to be understood in the depth of the divine life?"[^30] The answer can only be that the Son is also, in His eternal procession, the Beloved Son, begotten as beloved. Here is a characteristic text of this current of thought:

> "What characterizes the Son can be grasped in His being born of another, in filiation: if in the Father resides the fontality of love, in the Son resides the receptivity of love. The Son is pure welcome, eternal obedience of love; He is loved before the foundation of the world (Jn 17:24) (...) The eternal Lover is distinguished from the eternal Beloved, proceeding from Him by the overflowing fullness of His Love; the Son is the other in love, the One upon whom rests the movement of the infinite generosity of fontal Love. The Lover is the principle of the Beloved; fontal Love is the source of welcoming Love, in the unfathomable unity of eternal love. This process whereby the Living One in fontal love gives origin as principle to the Living One in receptive love, indissolubly united to Him, may be called generation; the eternal act of this eternal process is the eternal birth of His Son, His coming forth from the bosom of the Father."[^31]

One understands the renewed importance that Richard of Saint Victor has received in contemporary theology, which, attentive to the divine simplicity, avoids an unyielding dichotomy between knowledge and love, and, above all, loves to consider the eternal mystery of the Word reflected in the mystery of Jesus. He is the Beloved Son. In the text just quoted, emphasis is laid upon the presence of love in the generation of the Son, taking in all their radicality three indisputable affirmations in Trinitarian theology: God is Love; the Father is the source and origin of the whole Trinity; the Father gives the Son, in begetting Him, His entire substance. All this will have fruitful repercussions when treating of the procession of the Holy Spirit.

From the nature of love, moreover, in harmony with Richard of Saint Victor, the two characteristic notes of the Trinitarian mystery are indicated: unity and distinction. The love that unites the Trinity in a most close unity—designated by the terms *circuminsessio* or *perichoresis*—is that which, at the same time, distinguishes the Persons. Lover and Beloved are one and the same thing in the unity of Love, in the Holy Spirit, the *condilectus*, the bond of intratrinitarian communion, who proceeds from the *communis spiratio* of the Father and the Son. Latin theology is accustomed to draw attention to the fact that the Word is *Verbum spirans amorem*: "The Son is Word, but not just any word, but a Word that spires Love."[^32] That is to say, in the very fact of His divine generation, Love is included.
