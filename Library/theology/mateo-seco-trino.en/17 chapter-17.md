## Chapter 17: God's Transcendence Regarding Space and Time {#mateo-seco-trino.17}

The limitations of material being become especially palpable in its dependence on the coordinates of space and time. Space and time are the measure of the limits in the extension and duration of beings. To say that God is superior to the world entails affirming that He is not subject to the coordinates of space and time—that is, that He is supracosmic and supratemporal.

Immensity, omnipresence, and eternity appear vigorously described in both the Old and New Testaments as exclusive traits of the God of the Covenant. Not even in the earliest accounts of patriarchal piety is Yahweh presented as circumscribed by his places of worship. He is beyond any place. He intervenes everywhere. Nor is his life limited in duration. He exists from always and unto always. Immensity, omnipresence, and eternity are the real foundation of the faith that the God of the Covenant can always save his people, intervening on their behalf at any time and in any place.[^1]

From the perspective of systematic structure, these attributes are aspects necessarily included in the concept of *Ipsum esse subsistens*. In studying the nature of God, we structured the divine perfections according to an order accommodated to our mode of knowing—that is, seeking the ultimate root that accounts for and from which the other perfections derive. We understood that this ground is found in the fact that God is the *Ipsum esse subsistens*. We did not follow the position of Duns Scotus, who placed that ultimate foundation in *infinitas radicalis*—that is, in the inescapable exigency of infinity that distinguishes divine being from any other being. We did not consider that this was the metaphysical essence of God. The *infinitas radicalis* flows from the absolute simplicity of the divine act of being. Nevertheless, the Scotist intuition of the radical exigency of infinity as belonging to the divine essence is of great accuracy. That essential infinity demands being beyond all spatial and temporal limit.

### The Immensity of God

To affirm that God is immense is equivalent to saying that God has no measure and that, for this reason, He is in every place without ever being circumscribed by it. Two terms are used to designate this reality: immensity and omnipresence. These are two indissolubly linked concepts. Immensity is an absolute attribute, according to which God is immeasurable—and not because He has an infinite extension, but because He has no extension whatsoever. He does not realise his being by expanding spatially, but in the absolute simplicity of an act of intellect and love. Omnipresence, on the other hand, is a relative attribute, in the sense that it indicates the relation of indistance (non-distance) that God maintains with respect to the whole cosmos. God is present everywhere without being bound to any place. This relation of presence to all things is a consequence of God's immensity.

Divine immensity is described in Sacred Scripture with eloquence and constancy. Although the temple of Jerusalem is the privileged place of Yahweh's presence, not even it can contain Him. Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the temple is paradigmatic: *Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain thee; how much less this house which I have built!* (1 Kgs 8:27). Divine immensity implies that God is everywhere (cf. Job 23:8–9). God is everywhere—not only in heaven, but even in the deepest abyss. This is the doctrine that Our Lord takes for granted in his dialogue with the Samaritan woman: God is spirit, and therefore He is to be worshipped in all times and places (cf. Jn 4:20–24).

The explicit affirmation of divine immensity is present in the Athanasian Creed (*Quicumque*) and in the Fourth Lateran and First Vatican Councils.[^2] In this way, a unanimous teaching in Sacred Scripture and in the tradition of the Church is gathered up. It is impossible to believe in the God of the Covenant, Lord of time and history, without proclaiming his transcendence over everything created.

The arguments adduced by theologians to demonstrate the immensity of God are eloquent: God is in-finite to the point that He cannot be de-fined by any place or by any concept.[^3] This uncircumscribability of God found perhaps its most lucid formulations under the pen of Saint Augustine, who outlined the concept of divine immensity followed by all subsequent theological tradition.

In the *Confessions*, he recounts what his initial conception of divine immensity had been, and how he discovered that this conception was false:

> "It is true that I did not conceive of you, my God, in the shape of a human body, ever since I began to have some taste for wisdom; I always shunned this, and rejoiced to find it thus taught in the faith of our spiritual Mother, your Catholic Church [...] With all my soul I believed you to be incorruptible, inviolable, and unchangeable [...] Yet I thought that whatever was not extended throughout certain spaces, or diffused [...] was absolutely nothing [...] I imagined you as a vast Being extended through infinite spaces, penetrating everywhere through the whole mass of the world, and outside of it, in all directions, an unbounded immensity; so that the earth should contain you, the heavens contain you, and all things contain you, and all terminate in you, while you terminated nowhere [...] Thus did I reason, but it was false. For if it were so, a greater part of the earth would contain a greater part of you, and a smaller part less; and all things would be so full of you that the body of an elephant would occupy so much more of your Being than that of a small bird [...] and thus, divided into particles, you would be present to the great parts of the world in great parts, and to the small in small, which is not so."[^4]

Here is perfectly described the contradiction involved in confusing divine immensity with infinite extension. Such a conception proves as contradictory as pantheism and renders impossible God's presence to all things. Indeed, conceiving God as extended creates the insoluble problem of how two extended beings can coincide. Rather than a presence, it would be an invasion, or He would have the same relation to things as space does.[^5]

Divine immensity conceived in a spatial manner is absurd. Such an immensity would render the ubiquity of God impossible. Divine immensity is equivalent to his non-spatiality. It is above all spirituality and simplicity. It is not divided into parts. For that reason, strictly speaking, it neither diffuses nor pours itself out, nor can it become part of any material being, nor collide with it, nor be juxtaposed with it.[^6]

### The Omnipresence of God

Precisely because God does not belong to space, He can be present in all places and in all things. The very passages of Scripture that speak of the immensity of God speak of his universal presence. Indeed, the reverse is true: in Sacred Scripture, the movement is from omnipresence to immensity.

The Fathers are well aware that the ubiquity of God must correspond to a presence that transcends space. Origen notes that Celsus slanders Christian doctrine when he alleges that Christians treat God as though He possessed a body or were situated in a place.[^7] Saint Gregory of Nyssa explicitly connects the ubiquity of God with his immensity.[^8]

Presence is a relation of indistance between two beings. In every relation there must necessarily be the terms that are related and the ground or basis by which they are related. In the case before us, since this relation cannot be based on spatiality, it must be based on divine action. God's presence to the world has nothing material or quantitative about it. God is neither the measure of created being, nor is He measured by it. God is not boundless matter, but a spiritual being who transcends space. His proximity, then, does not mean spatial nearness. And yet He is closer to us than we are to ourselves.[^9]

Here is how Saint Thomas reasons it:

> "God is in all things; not, indeed, as part of their essence, nor as an accident, but as an agent is present to that upon which it acts [...] Since God is the cause of being in things, not only when they first begin to exist, but as long as they are preserved in being, just as the sun causes light in the air so long as the air remains illuminated, it follows that God must be present to whatever exists for as long as it has being, and according to the manner in which it participates in being. Now, being is what is most intimate to each thing and what penetrates it most deeply, since it is the formal principle of whatever is found in it. It is therefore necessary that God should be in all things, and innermostly in them."[^10]

The intimacy of the divine presence is a consequence of the intimacy entailed by the creative act: it is the whole being, *secundum totam suam substantiam*, that comes forth from the hands of the Creator. Precisely because of his qualitative distinction, God is present in things in the most intimate manner. He is present because the whole being of things springs from his infinite power. The absolute attribute of immensity becomes the relative attribute of omnipresence by virtue of creation and conservation.

Ever since Saint Gregory the Great, this presence has customarily been described as a presence *per essentiam*, *per potentiam*, *per praesentiam*.[^11] God is present in things because He is giving them being, because his power extends everywhere, and because all things are open before his eyes. It is an absolute immediacy, an intimacy infinitely greater than any that could occur in the closest spatial relationship.[^12]

God's presence to the cosmos is therefore a real presence, for the power with which God governs creatures is real, his knowledge is real, and the being He imparts to things is real. Precisely because things receive everything from God, they have a transcendental relation to Him that is real—the innermost reality of the creature. This relation requires us to conceive of God's presence as a substantial presence. It is God Himself who is present. If the divine action reaches immediately to each and every thing, and this action is identified with the divine substance itself, it must logically be affirmed that the divine substance—God Himself—is present to all things.[^13]

For this reason, being present everywhere is proper and exclusive to the divinity. To God alone belongs such union with created being. God alone can be in all places by Himself without any intermediary; divine action alone can extend to everything that exists without any intermediate causality. It is a causality that no one else can possess, since the creative act belongs properly and exclusively to Him who does not possess being by participation, but by essence. And since God is infinitely simple, He alone can be universally present according to his whole being.[^14] This presence is so exclusively divine that it cannot even pertain to the Humanity of the Lord.[^15]

### The World as the Glory of God

The presence of God in all things means that the cosmos constitutes a constant invitation to encounter God. The world is not a desert through which man wanders in overwhelming solitude, but a place inhabited by an intimately personal and loving presence. Sacred Scripture sings of God's presence in things and persistently exhorts us to encounter Him, precisely in the contemplation of the beauty and harmony of being: *The heavens are telling the glory of God; and the firmament proclaims his handiwork. Day to day pours forth speech, and night to night declares knowledge. There is no speech, nor are there words; their voice is not heard; yet their voice goes out through all the earth, and their words to the end of the world* (Ps 19:1–4 [Vulgate 18:2–5]).

Man must recognise God in this universal acclaim and render Him true worship (cf. Wis 13:1–9; Rom 1:18–23). Walking with eyes open to the harmony of creation leads to walking in the presence of its Creator, contemplating Him in things as their ultimate ground. The theology of creation must take fully into account God's presence in the world and the consequent goodness of created things.[^16]

This positive outlook occupies a prominent place in Christian spirituality. Spiritual authors have frequently exhorted us to contemplate God in creation. Here is how Saint John of the Cross comments on these verses of the *Spiritual Canticle*: "Pouring out a thousand graces, / He passed these groves in haste, / and glancing upon them, / with his figure alone, / left them clothed with beauty":

> "In this stanza, what is contained in substance is that God created all things with great ease and brevity, and in them left some trace of who He was—not only giving them being out of nothing, but even endowing them with innumerable graces and virtues, beautifying them with admirable order and indefectible mutual dependence, and doing all this through his Wisdom, by whom He created them, who is the Word, his only-begotten Son [...]
> 
> "As Saint Paul says, the Son of God is the radiance of his glory and the figure of his substance (Heb 1:3). It should be known, then, that with this figure alone of his Son, God looked upon all things, which was to give them their natural being, communicating to them many natural graces and gifts, making them finished and perfect [...] Looking upon them as very good (cf. Gen 1:31) was to make them very good in the Word his Son. And not only did He communicate to them natural being and grace by looking upon them, as we have said, but also with this figure alone of his Son He left them clothed with beauty, communicating to them supernatural being; which took place when He became man, exalting man in the beauty of God, and consequently all creatures with him, by having united Himself with the nature of all of them in man. Wherefore the Son of God Himself said: *Si ego exaltatus a terra fuero, omnia traham ad meipsum* [When I am lifted up from the earth, I will draw all things to myself] (Jn 12:32) [...] And thus, in this elevation of the Incarnation of his Son and the glory of his resurrection according to the flesh, the Father did not merely beautify creatures in part, but we can say that He left them completely clothed with beauty."[^17]

This passage provides an excellent example of the Christian vision of the cosmos as the trace and glory of God. It is a vision in which the theology of creation and the theology of the Incarnation are united. The trace of God is discovered in the harmony and beauty of the world, because "creatures are like a trace of God's passing, by which one traces his greatness, power, and wisdom."[^18]

This vestige leads to the knowledge of the Word, through whom and in whom all things were created (cf. Jn 1:1–3; Col 1:15–20). From the Cross, Christ draws the entire universe mysteriously to Himself, elevated to the order of redemption to such an extent that the whole creation groans in travail, awaiting the freedom of the children of God (cf. Rom 8:20–23). For the Christian, the affirmation of God's presence in the world always possesses a Christocentric dimension.

This joint vision of the mysteries of creation and redemption shines with unmistakable clarity in the teaching of Saint Josemaría Escrivá, who synthesised the Christian's spiritual attitude towards creation as "passionately loving the world":

> "I have taught this constantly with the words of Holy Scripture: the world is not evil, because it has come forth from the hands of God, because it is his creature, because Yahweh looked upon it and saw that it was good (cf. Gen 1:7ff.)." And a little further on, he adds: "A true Christian awareness—professing the resurrection of all flesh—has always naturally opposed 'disincarnation', without any fear of being accused of materialism. It is therefore legitimate to speak of a 'Christian materialism', which is boldly opposed to materialisms closed to the spirit."[^19]

### God's Presence in Man

Grounded upon the presence of immensity, there exists another mode of God's presence in man, far more intimate and personal. We refer to the presence of Trinitarian indwelling, which will be treated further on.[^20] This new mode of presence is a relation of man to each of the three divine Persons in that which is distinct to them. It cannot be based, therefore, upon the *ad extra* action of God—in which the three Persons act as one unit—but upon something belonging to the intra-Trinitarian relations, which alone distinguish the divine Persons. This relation pertains, then, to that which transforms man, divinising him to the point of making him a child of God in Christ. It is the divinisation wrought by the Holy Spirit in his invisible mission to the soul.[^21]

### The Eternity of God

In Sacred Scripture, the eternity of God is an attribute inseparable from the divine being. There is never the slightest allusion to a genesis of God. The sacred authors do not even strive to prove that God is eternal. They present Him as the One who exists before the creation of the world and will never die. He is the One who lives for ever; his fidelity endures for ever: *Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, from eternity to eternity thou art God* (Ps 90:2).

This is not a matter of mere limitless duration. God does not change because He is endowed with a life that does not age. The New Testament insists that God's salvific decree, accomplished through the sending of his Son into the world in the last days, is an eternal decree manifested in time (cf. 1 Cor 2:7; Eph 1:4; 3:9; Col 1:26). God and his decrees transcend history. He is the Alpha and the Omega (cf. Rev 1:8; 21:6), the beginning and the end (cf. Rev 21:6). It is to this profoundly vital biblical conception that theology appeals when it employs the concept of eternity or of pure act (*actus purus*) to designate God.

The Lord of history cannot be subject to history: there is in Him neither past nor future; He does not live in the recollection of the past or in expectation of the future. All is present to Him in an eternal now. Even his free interventions in history, which are temporal in their effects, are not temporal when considered in themselves. Truly God loves man with an eternal love, even though man exists only in time. Eternity thus presents itself to us as a mysterious and unfathomable reality. Saint Augustine writes:

> "Eternity is the very substance of God, which has nothing mutable; there nothing is past, nothing as though it no longer were; nothing is future, as though it did not yet exist. There nothing is except what is present."[^22]

In the Magisterium of the Church, the teaching concerning the eternity of God is found in the very same texts that affirm his immensity.[^23]

Eternity cannot be conceived, therefore, as an endless time. Nor can immensity be confused with infinite space. Immensity is not spatiality, nor is eternity temporality. Eternity implies the absence of mutation in nature or in activity. Strictly speaking, neither eternity nor time exist as things in themselves: what exist are temporal or eternal beings—that is, beings whose existence unfolds in a changing duration and can therefore be measured by time, or a Being who fully possesses Himself without a before or after and who, for this very reason, is eternal. Eternity and time are the substantives used to designate the "measures" of these two vastly different modes of being.[^24]

Boethius defined eternity as *interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*—the total, simultaneous, and perfect possession of boundless life. The accent falls upon two elements: first, having no limit in duration; second, the fact that it is a mode of existing in which there is no succession, since the whole of it exists at once.[^25] This latter element is the essential core of the eternal. The eternal is utterly devoid of beginning and end, not because it is realised through an unfolding in duration, but because it fully possesses itself in the uniformity of an immutable life. Even if one could speak of a time of infinite duration—without beginning or end—such time would not thereby become eternity, for it would still concern beings whose existence develops in a fleeting present between a past and a future. Given this fleetingness, the "now" of time is imperfect; nor is all time "now", since it also consists of past and future. The "now" of eternity is perfect, not only because it is not fleeting, but because the whole of eternity coincides with that "now".[^26]

For this reason, eternity, properly speaking, belongs exclusively to God. To Him alone pertains that ineffable mode of existence of One who fully possesses his life all at once. In treating divine immensity, it was necessary to transcend all materiality; in speaking of the eternity of God, it is necessary to transcend all temporality.[^27]

Both immensity and eternity are attributes that, taken in their strict sense, are incommunicable. Only He who is his own act of being can be adorned with these attributes. And yet, there is in man—the image and likeness of God (cf. Gen 1:26)—an innate longing for eternity. This desire is inseparable from true love, essentially bound up with the yearning that union with the beloved be for ever. Rest in the Good can be perfect only if that rest has no end; and it would have an end if man did not endure for ever. The longing for eternity is likewise revealed in the fact that man seeks to possess his life in a perpetual "now", recovering the past through memory and anticipating the future through expectation. To an eternal love God calls us: eternal because it will have no end; eternal because it will be an indefectible love, without wavering or weariness:

> "In God alone is there eternity in the proper and strict sense, because eternity follows upon immutability, and God alone is absolutely immutable. Yet, to the extent that beings derive immutability from God, to that extent do they participate in eternity."[^28]

Theology customarily designates this duration by the term *aevum* (aeviternity). And this is not merely because it will have no end, but because it entails a life of a quality vastly superior to what we now know. It is the life that springs from being elevated to the maximum perfection it is capable of receiving, both in its nature and in its activity. What is aeviternal is not susceptible to decay. The possession of its life in God is plenary and indefectible.[^29] This is what is designated by the term "eternal rest": a plenitude of life, not merely indefinite in duration, but above all in some manner infinite in its intensity and indefectibility, for it implies an ineffable participation in Trinitarian life.[^30]

Infinity in the possession of one's own life separates the eternity of God from the fleetingness of created being. Whereas God is an infinite *nunc stans* in intensity and duration, time is a *nunc fluens*. From this perspective, all versions of dynamic pantheism appear especially crude. However they may be framed, what point of similarity can there be between divine eternity as manifested in the deeds of the God of the Covenant and the evolution of a God who unfolds in step with creation? Can a "creative evolution"—even if one artificially attaches the name of God to that process—ever furnish a complete explanation within itself? A *deus fluens* is just as absurd as the pagan myths of dying and rising gods. And it certainly lacks the slightest point of contact with the teaching of Sacred Scripture. The Creator of time does not belong to time. The Augustinian formulations are luminous and eminently suited to our age.

God's transcendence over time is made clear in Saint Augustine's reply to a question that might seem childish, yet whose resolution proves illuminating: *What was God doing before the creation of the world?* The answer is elementary: there is no "before" prior to the creation of the world, because time exists only together with the existence of mutable being, since time is nothing other than the measure of the changing duration of beings. Nor is there in God any before or after:

> "If, however, anyone should wander off in fancy through those supposed times before the creation, and marvel that you, almighty God, Creator and Preserver of the universe [...] let countless ages pass before making so great a work, let him awake and realise that his wonder is directed at false things. For how could countless ages pass, which you, the author and creator of all ages, had not yet made? Or what times could exist that were not created by you? [...] Nor do you precede times temporally; otherwise you would not precede all times. But you precede all past times by the sublimity of your ever-present eternity, and you surpass all future times, because they are future, and when they come they will be past. But you are the same, and your years shall not fail (Ps 102:27). Your years neither come nor go; whereas ours come and go, that all of them may be. Your years abide all at once, because they abide; nor are those that go displaced by those that come, for they do not pass; but ours shall all be, only when none of them shall be any longer. Your years are but one day, and your day is not every day, but today, because your today does not give way to tomorrow, nor does it succeed yesterday. Your today is eternity; therefore you begot co-eternal with yourself Him to whom you said: *Today I have begotten you* (Ps 2:7). You made all times, and you are before all times; nor was there ever any time when there was no time."[^31]

Divine simplicity led us to say that God is present with his whole being (*totaliter*) to each and every being in the universe, even the smallest. This same simplicity leads us now to affirm that God is present to every moment of time according to his whole being—that is, in the infinite simplicity of his being—whereas things never coexist with Him *totaliter*, since they exist only in a continuous flux. This is an abyssal difference in which God's transcendence over all creation is reflected.

This divine transcendence over time led theology to insist that everything divine is utterly simple and eternal. As has already been seen, the fourth-century Fathers based their defence of the perfect divinity of the Son upon the fact that in God there is neither before nor after, and therefore that generation in God can take place only in an eternal today.[^32] This is clearly apparent in the Augustinian passage just cited. Everything in the Trinity is co-eternal.

Saint Augustine describes the question in addressing God: "No time can be co-eternal with you, because you abide; and if time abided, it would not be time. What then is time?
