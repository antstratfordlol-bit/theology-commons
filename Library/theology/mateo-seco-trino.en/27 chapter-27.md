## Chapter 27: Notions and Appropriations {#mateo-seco-trino.27}

The affirmation of three really distinct Persons in God would prove inoperative unless something were added concerning the characteristics that distinguish them. Indeed, from the statement of the Trinitarian mystery it follows that in the divine Persons there is something that is common and something that is proper, since they possess the same nature yet are really distinguished from one another. This requires a careful appraisal of the notes or concepts that serve for us to know the Persons insofar as they are distinct. This was perceived very early on by the great theologians of the Trinity, such as Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, Saint Augustine, or Saint John Damascene.

Yet the Trinitarian mystery exceeds the capacity of every created intellect, and consequently the notes or concepts to which we refer must be clearly contained in revelation, even if only in an implicit manner. Only God, in revealing Himself in His tripersonal reality, can indicate to us the notes by means of which we can know Him in His personal distinction. We find ourselves, therefore, engaged in the study of what Saint Basil designated as "notes of recognition".[^1]

### Human Language Regarding the Divine Persons

The question of notions and appropriations in God is situated within the realm of our knowledge and of our mode of speaking about the Persons. Such is the approach taken, for instance, by Saint Thomas, who develops this theme precisely in the question dedicated to the knowledge of the divine Persons.[^2] It is, therefore, a typically theological question. Its purpose is to conceptualise the teaching of Sacred Scripture, so as to express in our human language, as accurately as possible, the difference among the Persons, and thereby to be able explicitly to adore propriety in the Persons and unity in essence.[^3]

The necessity of setting forth the content of faith with the greatest possible precision in order to avoid misunderstandings and deviations compelled the Greek Fathers very early on to establish elementary rules for applying various names or denominations (*klēseis*) to God. This was done, among others, by Saint Athanasius, Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nyssa, Saint Gregory of Nazianzus, and Saint John Damascene.[^4]

As was to be expected, these notes of recognition came to be established in strict dependence upon the biblical expressions referring to each of the Persons: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. It is obvious that paternity belongs to the Father, filiation to the Son, and procession to the Spirit. In doing this, one does no more than translate the revealed name into abstract language in order to consider it as the property of the Person. One succeeds in this way in distinguishing the Persons by bringing their properties into the foreground. Here is an eloquent passage from Saint Basil:

> "But if one is willing to accept what is true, namely, that begotten and unbegotten are distinctive properties (...) which lead as if by the hand towards the clear and unambiguous notion of Father and Son, harm to piety will be avoided and coherence in discourse will be preserved. For properties (*idiotētes*) effect a distinction in what is common, by virtue of the characteristics that particularise them (...) For example, the divinity (*theotēs*) is common; paternity and filiation, on the other hand, are properties (*idiōmata*). By combining both elements—what is common and what is proper—the understanding of the truth is wrought within us. Thus, when we hear of an unbegotten light, we think of the Father; and when we hear of a begotten light, we think of the Son. In what pertains to light, there is no opposition between them, but in what pertains to begotten and unbegotten, they are considered under the aspect of their antithesis. For the nature of properties consists in showing otherness in the identity of substance."[^5]

Saint Basil goes on to caution that the existence of distinctive properties in God entails no composition within the substance. They cannot be conceived, therefore, as accidental realities in God. Saint Gregory of Nazianzus is more explicit in affirming not only the existence of notions in God, but in enumerating them in an orderly fashion:

> "Father, Son, and Holy Spirit have in common the divine nature (*theotēs*) and their not having been made; Son and Holy Spirit have in common that they receive their origin from the Father. It is proper to the Father to be unbegotten, to the Son to be begotten, and to the Holy Spirit to be sent."[^6]

Saint Augustine, referring to the notions proper to the Father, points out:

> "Begetter and unbegotten are two distinct concepts. For although both things are affirmed of God the Father, the former bespeaks a relation to the begotten, that is, to the Son, a truth that the Arians do not deny."[^7]

And Saint John Damascene:

> "One is the essence, one the goodness, one the power, one the will, one the energy, one the authority (...) The Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit are one single reality in all things, save for being unbegotten, being begotten, and proceeding (...) We acknowledge one only God, but we establish differences in the properties of paternity, filiation, and procession."[^8]

### The Divine Notions

In Trinitarian theology, a notion is understood as that characteristic or property whereby we distinguish one Person from another. A notion, therefore, possesses the following features: (a) it must signify a characteristic proper to the Person whom one wishes to distinguish from the others; (b) it must refer to relations of origin, since outside of these everything in God is identical; (c) it must be an abstract noun, since a Person can be distinguished by several notions; (d) it must express dignity in keeping with the concept of person; (e) it must signify in order to our knowledge, for the notion has no other purpose than that of helping us to distinguish the three Persons.[^9]

God is infinitely simple, and the divine Persons entail no complexity in Him. Nor can the notions introduce any composition into God. Saint Basil already warned of this with clarity. The notions are no more than an abstract way of speaking about the Persons, adapted to the nature of our human knowledge. It is an imperfect way of speaking, yet useful, provided it is not forgotten that this conceptualisation arises from the limitations of our knowledge and does not correspond to a composition in the divine nature. Indeed, given our discursive mode of knowing, the concrete names of Father, Son, and Spirit do not suffice to show us graphically the Persons in their mutual reference. It becomes necessary, therefore, to find abstract names to underline the opposition existing among the three Persons.[^10]

The notions are predicated of the Persons as properties that distinguish them from the others. For this very reason, they are connected with the processions, which constitute the foundation of all personal distinction in God. In order to determine the notions, it is necessary to consider the processions, bearing in mind that each procession gives rise to two terms: the one who proceeds and the one from whom He proceeds.

Accordingly, the notions are five. Three of them belong to the Father: innascibility or *agennēsia*, inasmuch as the Father does not proceed nor can proceed from another Person; paternity, inasmuch as He begets the Son; active spiration, inasmuch as from Him proceeds the Holy Spirit. To the Son belongs filiation, inasmuch as He proceeds from the Father by generation; to Him belongs also active spiration, which is uniquely one with that of the Father. To the Spirit belongs passive spiration (being spirated, *ekporeusis*).

Duns Scotus adds another notion to distinguish the Holy Spirit: infecundity (*infecunditas*), since from Him no other Person proceeds.[^11] Nevertheless, the majority of theologians, while accepting the innascibility of the Father as a notion, do not accept that infecundity should be taken as a notion. They judge that infecundity is a negative concept and does not express a perfection corresponding to the dignity of the person. The innascibility of the Father is likewise a negative term, and large sectors of theology understand it in a purely negative sense. Nevertheless, they grant it the status of a notion, arguing that innascibility does indeed indicate a perfection coherent with the dignity of the person.[^12]

### Notions and Properties

Precisely because their purpose is to facilitate our knowledge of the divine Persons, the notions are grounded upon that which really distinguishes them, namely, their relations of origin. They are, so to speak, the noetic reflection of the personal properties. There is, however, a nuanced distinction between notions and properties: notions correspond to the requirement of our intellect; properties are independent of our intellect and exist in God as that which expresses the real distinction of the Persons.

The vocabulary concerning notions and properties is by no means uniform. Nor is the conception of them held by the various theological currents uniform.[^13] In the first place, there is a marked divergence of emphasis between Greek theology and Latin theology. In the distinction of the Persons, Greek theology attributes greater importance to the divine properties than Latin theology does;[^14] Latin theology places greater relief upon relations, to the point that Saint Thomas places the entire weight of the distinction of the Persons upon the opposition of relation, since, according to him, the Persons are constituted by subsistent relations. The well-known Anselmian axiom proclaimed by the Council of Florence receives its most radical application in the Thomistic exposition.[^15] In the second place, even among the Latins there are diverse ways of conceiving notions and properties. One need only recall, for example, the differences existing among Saint Thomas Aquinas, Saint Bonaventure, and Duns Scotus.[^16]

Here is Saint Thomas's formulation:

> "There are five notions in God: innascibility, paternity, filiation, common spiration, and procession. Of these, only four are relations, for innascibility is so only by reduction. The properties are no more than four, because common spiration is not a property, since it belongs to two persons. The personal notions, that is, those constitutive of persons, are three: namely, paternity, filiation, and procession, since innascibility and common spiration are called notions of the persons."[^17]

Saint Thomas's text is consistent with his position of identifying the Persons with subsistent relation. The personal notions are no more than three: those corresponding to the opposite relations; the other two are notions of the persons, but "not personal". Saint Thomas's qualifications are the outcome of an effort to avoid, as far as possible, any difference that does not arise exclusively from the opposition of relation, with the aim of highlighting divine simplicity. Hence his insistence that innascibility indicates nothing more than non-origination, that is, taking it in a negative sense, as merely not being begotten.

Relying upon this simplicity, Saint Thomas identifies the abstract and the concrete in God. Indeed, simplicity in an infinite degree excludes all composition. For this reason, according to him, properties are identified with Persons. Here is an eloquent passage testifying to his consistency:

> "A property in God is the same as a person, because personal properties are identified with persons for the same reason that the abstract is identified with the concrete. They are, therefore, the same subsistent persons; and thus paternity is the Father Himself, filiation is the Son, and procession is the Holy Spirit. As for non-personal properties, they are identified with the persons according to that other reason of identity, namely, that whatever is attributed to God is His essence."[^18]

According to this view, properties and notions coincide with relations. It is solely a matter of different perspectives. For example, "paternity is relation, if it is considered as reference to the Son; it is property, if it is considered as characteristic of the Father; it is notion, if it is considered as cognitive principle of the first Person."[^19]

The opinions of theologians and, above all, the way in which they structure their treatment of divine notions and properties diverge considerably. It suffices to recall the approaches of Saint Bonaventure or Scotus. As Saint Thomas explicitly observes, this is a question that falls within the scope of theological explanation—never fully satisfactory—and not within the defined faith of the Church.[^20]

### Properties and Appropriations in God

Personal properties are identified with the divine Persons to such an extent that they make known to us something of their distinctiveness. Appropriation, on the other hand, is a theological procedure whereby there is specially attributed to one divine Person the common action *ad extra* or a determinate attribute that is common to all three. Appropriation can be defined as "the manifestation of the divine persons by means of essential attributes".[^21] There is, therefore, a great difference between what is proper and what is appropriated: what is proper is exclusive to one Person; what is appropriated is common to all, yet is attributed especially to one on account of its closer affinity with what is proper to that Person.

Such is the case, for example, with the divine attributes and operations. When one of these attributes is applied especially to one Person, it is said to be applied to Him by appropriation. These are attributes that by definition are common to the three Persons and are attributed especially to one, without this implying in any way that it is attributed to Him exclusively. Thus, for example, it is customary to attribute omnipotence to the Father, wisdom to the Son, and goodness to the Holy Spirit. They are attributed to each while knowing that they are common to all three. And this is done because of their special coherence—within our human mode of knowing—with the divine properties.

In order for appropriation to be sound, it must not be confused with properties. Properties reflect what a Person is in His distinction from the others; appropriations designate what, according to our mode of knowing, is especially suited to each of the Persons, without excluding the others. It is, therefore, a comparison of the divine Persons among themselves. It is worth insisting that it is solely that: a comparison. When this language is radicalised, an intolerable division is introduced into the divine simplicity. The most typical example is that of Abelard, judging by the proposition condemned at the Council of Sens: "The Father is full power, the Son is a certain power, the Holy Spirit is no power."[^22] Here appropriations and properties appear thoroughly confused.

It follows from this that all divine attributes and operations can serve the purpose of appropriation, since all offer traits that are closer to some Persons than to others. Many of these traits can be attributed to the same Person or to different Persons according to different reasons.

Theological thought is rich in procedures of this kind. The Latins, beginning with Saint Hilary and Saint Augustine, have used appropriations with notable frequency.[^23] Here are some of them:

> "In the Father is unity, in the Son equality, in the Holy Spirit the harmony of unity and equality (...) These three things are one because of the Father, equal because of the Son, and interconnected because of the Holy Spirit."[^24]

The text just cited attributes Trinitarian unity to the Father. Greek theology does the same. Saint Thomas comments:

> "Saint Augustine appropriates unity to the Father, equality to the Son, and concord or connection to the Holy Spirit. It is clear that these three things entail unity, but in different ways. Unity is something absolute that does not presuppose any other thing, and therefore it is appropriated to the Father, who does not presuppose any person, because He is principle without principle. Equality requires unity with respect to another, for equal is that which has equal magnitude with another, and for this reason equality is appropriated to the Son, who is principle from principle. Connection requires the unity of two, and therefore it is appropriated to the Holy Spirit, inasmuch as He proceeds from two."[^25]

Saint Augustine writes:

> "What the Apostle says must not be taken indiscriminately: *Of Him, and through Him, and in Him*. *Of Him* is said on account of the Father; *through Him*, on account of the Son; *in Him*, on account of the Holy Spirit (cf. Rom 11:36)."[^26]

Saint Leo the Great justifies the recourse to appropriation in this manner:

> "Although in the Trinity there exists an immutable divinity, single in substance, undivided in operations, unanimous in will, equal in omnipotence, equal in glory, nevertheless Sacred Scripture attributes to each of the Persons certain names and certain deeds that seem suited to them. In doing this it does not perturb Catholic faith, but teaches it; for the intellect does not separate what the hearing distinguishes, when the truth of the Trinity is suggested to us by means of the propriety of the word or of the work."[^27]

### The Theological Meaning of Appropriations

The text of Saint Leo the Great places the question of appropriations in its exact perspective. Without appropriations, our Trinitarian language would be gravely impoverished, to the point that faith in the Trinity would be seriously imperilled. Hence their importance in theology.

Sacred Scripture frequently employs the language of appropriations. Thus, for example, it attributes the name of God preferentially to the Father, and that of Lord to the Son: *Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ* (2 Cor 1:3); it assigns distinct effects to the Persons in the work of salvation, as in this greeting: *The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, and the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all* (2 Cor 13:14). The formulas of faith and the Creeds likewise employ the language of appropriations. Faith is confessed in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, and in Jesus Christ, His only Son, our Lord, through whom all things were made, and in the Holy Spirit, the Lord and giver of life. The same occurs in the liturgy. Prayers are addressed to the Father through the Son, in the unity of the Holy Spirit.

This universal mode of proceeding demonstrates the importance for the Christian life of a correct and rich use of appropriations in the various ways in which they can be utilised. It is not a matter of a circumstantial procedure, but one grounded in the practice of Sacred Scripture and in the limitations inherent in our mode of knowing.

Indeed, by appropriating to the Persons essential attributes such as power, wisdom, or communion—without confusing them with properties—the distinctive character of each of them is better manifested, without impairing their essential unity. For the divine attributes are rationally more accessible to us than what is proper to each Person. From these attributes we are able to explain something of what is proper to each Person.[^28]
