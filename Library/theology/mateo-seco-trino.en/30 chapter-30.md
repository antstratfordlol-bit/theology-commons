## Chapter 30: The Perfection of Divine Knowledge {#mateo-seco-trino.30}

The teaching concerning divine wisdom appears in Sacred Scripture primarily linked to the narration of God's salvific interventions. God is, above all, the God of the Covenant: He who chooses, He who saves, and therefore He who knows and loves. His promises are entirely trustworthy because He knows the future infallibly. The God who manifests Himself definitively in Christ is a personal God, a spirit (cf. Jn 4:24), who knows and loves. In contrast to pantheistic doctrines that identify God with nature and consequently conceive Him as an unconscious being evolving progressively towards self-consciousness, the Christian faith confesses Him as an infinite and eternal act of knowledge, which precedes the world and has created everything that exists by His word alone.[^1]

God possesses a most perfect and universal knowledge: the Lord is a God of knowledge (*Deus scientiarum Dominus est*, 1 Sam 2:3); with Him are wisdom and might, His is counsel and understanding (Job 12:13). God is the God of wisdom (1 Sam 2:3) and has made the heavens with wisdom and love (Ps 136:5). The scriptural passages affirming the sublime divine wisdom are exceedingly numerous and, on many occasions, are embedded within a splendid poetic vision of creation, which appears arrayed in the splendours of divine wisdom (cf., e.g., Job 28:21–27; Ps 139:13–18).

God's knowledge extends, in the first place, to Himself. He knows Himself perfectly: *The Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God. For what person knows a man's thoughts except the spirit of the man which is in him? So also no one comprehends the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God* (1 Cor 2:10–11). *All things have been delivered to me by my Father; and no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and any one to whom the Son chooses to reveal him* (Mt 11:27).

This knowledge extends to all creation. God determines the number of the stars and calls each of them by its name (Ps 147:4–5). God's providence extends to the very last detail. God knows when a little sparrow falls to the ground, and even the hairs of our head are all numbered (cf. Mt 6:25–30; 10:29–30). Furthermore, everything created proceeds from the wisdom of God, who *by his wisdom founded the earth, and by his understanding established the heavens* (Jer 10:12). God knows even the most intimate depths of man and searches the innermost recesses of his heart. No human thought is hidden from His eyes (Sir 42:15–25).

God knows the future with absolute certainty, including the future free actions of men. Upon this wisdom of God is grounded the certitude of the prophecies, which are uttered not as a forecast, but as a certain prediction of future events. Upon this divine knowledge rests the certainty of His faithfulness and the security of His providence. Moreover, this divine foreknowledge of the future is proof of the authenticity of divine manifestations (cf. Is 41:21–29). *The word of God is living and active, sharper than any two-edged sword, piercing to the division of soul and spirit, of joints and marrow, and discerning the thoughts and intentions of the heart. And before him no creature is hidden, but all are open and laid bare to the eyes of him with whom we have to do* (Heb 4:12–13). Citing this text, the First Vatican Council teaches that God also knows future things that are to come to pass through the free action of creatures.[^2]

God's knowledge of man and the world can only be understood as a warm and intimate knowledge. According to Sacred Scripture, for man, to be known by God is equivalent to being acknowledged by Him as His creature and as His child—ultimately, as something belonging to Him. The assurance of being known by God is the foundation of the hope of the just, whilst this same divine knowledge lays bare the wickedness of the sinner (cf. Ps 139:2–24; Job 34:21–32). Unfathomable is the wisdom of God—the mystery of His will—which is manifested in the path chosen for the salvation of mankind: *O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!* (Rom 11:33). When the fullness of time arrived, all this divine wisdom was concentrated in the wisdom of the Cross (cf. 1 Cor 1:17–25). Salvation history, which has as its axis the mystery of the death and resurrection of the Lord, is the supreme expression of God's infinite wisdom (cf. Rom 11:32–36).

### The Perfection of Divine Knowledge

Neither philosophical discourse nor theological discourse encounters any difficulty in showing that an infinite knowledge exists in God. The existence and perfection of this knowledge form part of the infinite perfection of God. Indeed, God would not be infinitely perfect if He did not possess Himself fully in an ineffable act of self-consciousness. Without this self-knowledge He would not even be a personal being. Nor would He be infinitely perfect if He did not possess—and certainly in an infinite degree—all the perfections we behold in created beings, knowledge among them. These arguments clearly demonstrate that an eminent knowledge must exist in God. In our human way of knowing and speaking of God, the existence of this knowledge follows from the perfect spirituality of God. St Thomas Aquinas bases his argumentation regarding the existence of knowledge in God precisely upon the spirituality of the divine being, formulating an argument of great power:

> "Beings endowed with knowledge differ from non-knowing beings in that non-knowing beings possess only their own proper form, whereas the knowing being is naturally suited to have also the form of another thing; for the form of the known is in the knower. Whence it is evident that the nature of a non-knowing being is more confined and limited, whereas the nature of knowing beings possesses greater amplitude and extension; on which account the Philosopher says that 'the soul is, in a way, all things'. Now the limitation of form comes through matter; hence we said above that forms, according as they are more immaterial, approach more closely to a kind of infinity (...) Since therefore God is at the pinnacle of immateriality, He holds the supreme degree of knowledge."[^3]

The argumentation derives its force from the use of the concept of immateriality in all its positivity. It is not merely a question of immateriality as the simple lack of matter, but of immateriality as the proper constitution of being open to universality. This is evident from the manner in which St Thomas cites here the well-known Aristotelian aphorism: the soul, precisely because in its knowledge it embraces the entire universe, is *quodammodo omnia*.[^4] The root of this universality of the soul, by which it is capable of embracing matter whilst transcending it, is found precisely in immateriality as a mode of existence. The conclusion is readily perceptible: since God stands at the summit of immateriality—that is, since God is neither divided nor limited by anything—He is likewise found at the summit of knowledge.

The sublimity of this knowledge may be enunciated in three propositions: a) God possesses an infinitely perfect knowledge; b) God knows Himself with an infinitely perfect knowledge; c) God knows everything that exists and will exist—including free future contingents—likewise with an infinitely perfect knowledge.

#### God Possesses Infinitely Perfect Knowledge

Few considerations are necessary to show that God's knowledge must be infinitely perfect. They are the same considerations that must be made when showing that in God the divine life is of infinite power and quality. Quite simply, either God is infinitely perfect, or He is not God. This perfection includes the perfect possession of Himself with an infinity that constitutes Him as a being who possesses Himself in a personal life, so to speak, of infinite quality. God would not be infinite Love were He not at the same time infinite Knowledge.

This affirmation is simple and, within a theological setting, self-evident. It is fitting, however, to enumerate the qualities of divine knowledge that follow from this statement:

##### a) God is His Own Act of Understanding

In God there is no duality between the faculty or capacity to know and the act of knowledge. In other words, God is not a most perfect intellect that knows itself most perfectly, but an infinite act of knowing (*intelligere*) that subsists eternally. Stated differently: God is a pure act of knowledge and love, without there existing in Him the slightest distinction between act and the capacity to perform that act. Divine being and divine living are identical. Thus, in Him there is no transition from potency to act, but pure and most simple act (*actus purus*). God is His own act of existing (*ipsum esse subsistens*). He is likewise His own act of understanding (*ipsum intelligere*).

##### b) Divine Knowledge is Intuitive, Immutable, Eternal, and Comprehensive

It follows that God's knowledge must possess the same qualities as the divine Being with which it is identical. God does not know by means of a structure or concatenation of acts, but in a single, most simple act (*actu simplicissimo*). With this most simple act He embraces all being. Divine knowledge cannot, therefore, be conceived as a process or as a discursive movement, but as a clear and direct intuition that simultaneously embraces all the truth that exists in being. Hence it is an immutable and eternal knowledge. It cannot be other than an intuitive knowledge. All truth is eternally present before Him who is the Truth. All reality lies open to the gaze of God down to the very foundations of its being—that is, to what is most intimate and profound in its knowability.

God's knowledge is neither superficial, nor capable of enrichment, nor threatened by any loss. God is an infinite act of perfect self-consciousness from which truth derives to all created beings.[^5] Created beings are, in effect, true because they reflect the truth and being of God. This is what is classically designated by the expression *ontological truth*: the truth of things consists in their relation to the divine intellect.[^6] Whereas the affirmations of the human intellect are true to the extent that they reflect the being of things, things are true because they are the expression of the creative idea of God, in a manner resembling how a building reflects the idea of the architect.

Divine knowledge comprehends—embracing in a single and indivisible act—the entire infinity of being. There is in that knowledge no possibility of error, nor any darkness. Neither can there be in God the shadow of doubt. Nor is it a knowledge that progresses by unravelling the consequences contained within a proposition. In God all is perfect clarity and possession. Divine knowledge is therefore, without any limit and in all depth, omniscience and infinite wisdom.

##### c) Divine Knowledge is Independent of the Being of Things

This is a consequence of God's infinite sovereignty. It implies a vast difference between the human mode of knowing and God's mode of knowing. Human knowledge entails passivity before things: the human intellect conceives a concept precisely upon being fecundated by the truth that shines forth in the being of things. This is not the case with God. In Him there can be neither dependence nor any passivity with respect to created being. So to speak, His intellect cannot be fecundated by the object known, since His knowledge cannot involve any dependence with respect to created being. For this reason, divine knowing is always creatively productive, and never receptive or reproductive of things or events.[^7]

#### God is Fully Comprehensible to Himself

The primary and principal object, so to speak, of divine knowledge is God Himself. By virtue of His absolute perfection, God is simultaneously infinitely intelligible and infinitely intelligent. Once again it is necessary to insist that, in God, essence and existence, being and act, are identical. The divine essence is identified with the infinite and eternal act of knowledge and love. God possesses Himself, therefore, with the absolute fullness of that interior dialogue which is the presence of spirit to itself through self-consciousness. This divine act of consciousness is the foundation of the Trinitarian mystery insofar as consciousness is an essential part of personal being. The revelation of the mystery of the Trinity manifests that this act of knowledge is lived, in turn, by the three divine Persons as a single act in which each Person communicates with His own personal characteristic in the common and single act of all. The subsistent and eternal act of knowing is realised simultaneously by the Father, the Son, and the Spirit in the very order of the divine processions: the Father communicates His act of knowledge to the Son, and the Father and the Son communicate it to the Holy Spirit.[^8] It is a single act of knowledge, by which God possesses Himself in a most simple and infinite act of clear consciousness and vigilant love. He possesses Himself completely: God "knows Himself with all the perfection with which He is knowable, and, consequently, comprehends Himself perfectly."[^9]

#### God Knows Everything That Exists Outside Himself

God knows the whole of being, both actual and merely possible. This affirmation belongs to faith; it is likewise accessible to human reason. God's knowledge is infinitely perfect; it must, therefore, embrace all that is knowable, both what exists and what is merely possible. There is another reason of equal weight to ground this affirmation: everything that exists outside God is a copy or reflection of His being and of His perfection. God would not know Himself fully if, in knowing Himself, He did not also know the infinite imitations of which His essence is capable.

And yet, the fact that God knows everything that exists has presented serious difficulties to certain philosophers. Two difficulties are particularly formidable: a) In the act of knowledge, the intellect behaves passively, for in the act of knowing—as occurs in created beings—the intellect is fecundated by the being of things, by their species; it is in this way that it conceives the idea or concept. However, the divine intellect cannot be subject to any passivity, nor depend upon any created thing. Therefore it cannot be fecundated by the being of things; b) To affirm the universality of divine knowledge entails affirming that He knows the future perfectly. And He certainly knows it as present, since in God, as has been said, all knowledge is intuitive, immutable, and most simple. Now, such knowledge seems to strip the future—especially the future that depends upon human free choice—of its contingency and freedom. The first difficulty springs from the proper qualities of the divine act of knowing; the second, from the proper qualities of human freedom.

The first difficulty was already considered by Aristotle and Averroës, who may be regarded as paradigms for the formulation of this problem. According to Aristotle, although the act of knowing is most noble, there exist things which, on account of their lack of nobility—their mutability and lack of consistency—are better left unknown by the divine. To know them would lead to being in dependence upon what is ephemeral and mutable, and therefore to debasing the intellect.[^10] In commenting on this passage of Aristotle, St Thomas resolves this difficulty by pointing out that it is true that no thing can impart any perfection to God—that is, that no thing can "fecundate" divine knowledge, thereby giving Him some perfection—but that it does not follow from this that God is ignorant of any of the things that exist, for God, "by understanding Himself, understands all other things."[^11]

The difficulty is real, and its resolution lies in recognising that divine knowledge of things is vastly superior to human knowledge: whereas human knowledge depends upon the being of things, divine knowledge is not dependent upon them; rather, it is things that depend upon divine knowledge. It is not that the existence of things causes knowledge in God, but rather that things exist because God knows them. Thus has Christian thought perseveringly understood it. "All His creatures, spiritual and corporal," writes St Augustine, "exist because He knows them; He does not know them because they exist. For He was not ignorant of what He willed to create, but created it because He knew it. The reverse is not the case: He does not know things because He created them."[^12]

This is equivalent to saying that God does not know things in themselves, but in Himself, inasmuch as He is the cause of all things. Pseudo-Dionysius expresses it beautifully: "God does not know existing things through their being, but knows them in Himself, for He contains and encompasses in Himself, as the cause of all things, the knowledge, knowing, and essence of all things (...) Divine Wisdom, in knowing itself, knows all other things: it knows the material immaterially, the divisible indivisibly, and the manifold simply."[^13]

In knowing things in Himself, God knows them in a most perfect manner, for the knowledge of things in their proper cause is the most perfect knowledge that can be had. It is not, therefore, a generic and abstract knowledge, but an intuitive, concrete, and proper knowledge of each individual being. Christian faith in God's providence over history and over mankind presupposes that divine knowledge of each being in its unrepeatable singularity is most perfect. Both truths are mutually implicated. The certainty that divine providence embraces each being in its singularity—as appears already in the Book of Genesis—leads to the certainty that divine knowledge extends to each individual being and event.

Impressed by Aristotle's difficulty, Averroës accepted that God knows things in a generic manner, but not with a proper and concrete knowledge.[^14] It suffices to recall the qualities inherent in divine knowledge, among them its perfection, to perceive that the knowledge God has of things must be singular and proper. Indeed, to know something in a vague and general way is an imperfect way of knowing it.

Furthermore, things do not pre-exist in God in a common or generic form, but in their concrete singularity. In addition, "God would not know Himself perfectly unless He knew all the possible modes in which others can participate in His perfection, nor would He know being itself with perfection unless He knew all modes of being. It remains, therefore, beyond doubt that God knows all things with proper knowledge, according as they are distinct from one another."[^15] At the same time, the distinction between divine knowledge and human knowledge remains clear: whereas human knowledge is measured by the being of things, divine knowledge is the measure of the being of things.

### The Manner in Which God Knows Things

It is clear that the knowledge God has of things must be most perfect and that it cannot be dependent upon things. Hence the constant affirmation in Christian thought that God knows all things in their singularity and concreteness, yet does not know them in themselves, but in Himself, in the perfection of His own being, which is infinitely participable. It is, therefore, a knowledge that is creative of everything that exists. In other words, God knows all things in an eternal, infinite, and immutable act of knowledge, with the very same single act of knowledge with which He comprehends Himself. It is a knowledge that is, by its very nature, a creative knowledge.

Yet divine knowledge embraces not only what exists, has existed, and will exist, but also the infinity of possibilities that will never become reality. God knows the infinity of all possible beings and events that will never be realised. Indeed, God would not know Himself with infinite perfection if He did not simultaneously know the infinity of possible imitations of His perfection. God knows, therefore, what exists and what, while capable of existing, will never exist, yet could exist. Hence, in speaking of divine knowledge, these distinctions are employed to express (according to our limited mode of speech) the diverse "modes" of divine knowledge:

#### a) Knowledge of Simple Intelligence (*Scientia Simplicis Intelligentiae*)

This expression designates the divine knowledge of possibles—that is, the knowledge of the infinity of possibilities that will eternally remain just that: mere possibilities. The fact that divine knowledge is the cause of all that exists does not mean that everything God knows must exist.

#### b) Knowledge of Vision (*Scientia Visionis*)

This expression serves to designate the knowledge God possesses of all that is, was, or will be. It is called the knowledge of vision because God eternally contemplates all things—past, present, and future—in an intuitive manner and, consequently, holds them always present in His eternity.[^16]

The knowledge of simple intelligence is, so to speak, purely speculative, and corresponds to the knowledge God has of Himself as the exemplary cause of all beings. The knowledge of vision is practical, adding to the knowledge of simple intelligence the divine decision whereby, from that infinity of possible imitations, He freely chooses those which He decrees shall exist. The knowledge of vision is, then, the knowledge God has of the things He has decided shall exist—that is, the knowledge God has of Himself not only as exemplary cause, but also as efficient cause of all that exists.

In the light of this distinction, it is possible to answer more concretely the question of how God knows things: merely possible things, in the knowledge of the infinite virtuality of His own being; existing things—including future ones—in His determination that they shall exist. This determination is the cause of things existing. To affirm, therefore, that God knows what exists in His decision that it shall exist is equivalent to affirming that God knows things not in themselves, but in their proper cause.

### Divine Knowledge as Cause of Things

To say that God knows things in Himself implies the affirmation that divine knowledge is the cause of things. St Augustine's formulation is eloquent: God created things because He already knew them. Here is how St Thomas formulates the question:

> "The knowledge of God is the cause of things. For the divine knowledge is to all created beings what the knowledge of the craftsman is to the things he makes. Now the craftsman's knowledge is the cause of the things made, because the craftsman operates through his intellect; wherefore the form in his intellect is the principle of his operation (...) It must be observed, however, that a natural form is not the principle of action insofar as it remains in its subject giving it being, but insofar as it has an inclination to produce the effect. The same holds true of the intelligible form, which, insofar as it is in the one who understands, is likewise not a principle of action unless there be added to it an inclination to produce an effect, which is done through the will (...) If, therefore, there is no doubt that God produces things by His intellect, since His being is His understanding, it is necessary that the divine knowledge be the cause of things insofar as it has the will conjoined to it; on which account it is customary to call it the knowledge of approbation (*scientia approbationis*)."[^17]

God knew things before creating them. This consideration is self-evident, since creation presents itself to us as the work of an intellect and not as the product of blindness. From this perspective, it is clear that God's knowledge does not depend upon things, because it is itself the cause of things—at least, the directive cause, in a manner analogous to the procedure of an architect. In the text just cited, St Thomas explains, moreover, the fundamental reason that exists for distinguishing between the knowledge of simple intelligence and the knowledge of vision. The knowledge of vision includes the determination to create—that is, the divine will.

Thus far the straightforward reading of the text: divine knowledge is the directive cause of things. Can divine knowledge, in addition, be regarded as the effective cause of things? Posed in this way, the question was extensively debated between Thomists and Molinists in a dispute spanning the entire *De auxiliis* controversy.[^18]

According to the Thomists, the knowledge of vision is not only the directive cause of things, but also their effective cause. By this second affirmation they wish to accord primacy to the role of divine knowledge in the fact of creation; they wish, moreover, to make clear the unity between intellect and will in God: creation should not be conceived as a divine idea subsequently put into practice by the will of God through an executive decree, but as an act of the intellect determined towards creation. According to the Molinists, in the creation of the world divine knowledge plays the directive role and the will plays the executive role. The knowledge of simple intelligence becomes the knowledge of vision precisely through the divine determination to create. Consequently, the divine knowledge that is the cause of the creation of the universe is the knowledge of simple intelligence, whereas the knowledge of vision corresponds to the knowledge God has, so to speak, of the creative decree. For the Thomists, by contrast, the only knowledge that is the cause of creation is the knowledge of vision.

In applying the analogy of the craftsman to God the Creator, the Molinists adhere much more closely to the human starting point of that analogy than do the Thomists. So to speak, the knowledge of the divine architect operates in a manner very similar to the knowledge of a human architect. The Molinist approach is thus far more readily intelligible than that of the Thomists. However, they introduce a sharper distinction between the acts of knowledge and will in God and, above all, introduce greater difficulty when explaining how the knowledge of vision is not dependent upon things. For if the knowledge of vision is not the immediate cause of things, in a certain sense that knowledge depends upon something distinct from itself. The diversity of solutions given to this question is important for understanding the divergent hypotheses that both schools would present when explaining the knowledge God possesses of the future.[^19]

### Divine Knowledge of the Future

The knowledge God has of things is immutable and infallible. The adjective *immutable* bears here the same meaning as when applied to the being of God: God is not immutable with the immutability of a stone, but with the immutability inherent in Him who is a most simple and infinite act of life. Pure act (*actus purus*). Given the imperfection of our knowledge, we describe as immutable—a negative term—Him who is pure act.

Yet within the roughness of this language, this term designates certain qualities of divine knowledge that pose a grave problem when speaking of the knowledge God has of free future contingents. This is the second difficulty enunciated at the beginning of this chapter: do not the immutability and infallibility of divine knowledge prove incompatible with the fallibility and mutability proper to contingent events, and especially with the freedom of human decisions?

The immutability of divine knowledge is inseparable from its all-embracing perfection. Neither can God acquire new knowledge, nor does He know future free actions in a conjectural manner, even were it through an exhaustive calculation of probabilities. God possesses an infallibly certain knowledge of all future things. Still more, God does not see things in themselves, but in Himself, for He cannot depend upon anything extra-divine. And yet, even though the knowledge God has of human free choices is infallibly certain, those choices do not cease to be free simply because they are known beforehand by God in an infallible manner. Here lies the question.

We find ourselves before one of the most mysterious questions of thought and, more specifically, of all theology. It concerns, on the one hand, the mysteriousness of the divine being and of His sublime life of knowledge and love, infinitely perfect and transcendent to all created knowledge; it concerns likewise the mysteriousness of man himself and of his destiny in his condition as an intelligent and free being; it concerns, finally, the mysterious manner in which divine providence and human freedom are conjoined. It is man's ultimate destiny and freedom on his journey towards it that are here brought before us. Once again it is necessary to recall that the theologian must be conscious that the demands which mystery presents to the human mind are not resolved by denying one part of that mystery. In the case before us, one can deny neither the proper characteristics of free human action nor the proper qualities of divine knowledge. And among those characteristics is found—as has already been seen—the fact that divine knowledge of things not only cannot depend upon them, but is, moreover, infallible. The question is, then, how the infallible divine knowledge of the future and the existence of human freedom are reconciled.

The difficulty increases if one takes into account that God not only possesses a perfect knowledge of all things, but that this knowledge—precisely on account of its perfection—is an intuitive knowledge: that is, a mode of knowing things as present, since without that presence intuition is impossible. Once again it is St Thomas who provides a paradigmatic text in responding to this question:

> "Although contingent effects are realised successively with the passage of time, God does not know them successively according to the being they have in themselves, but knows them all simultaneously, because His knowledge, like His being, is measured by eternity, which, existing all at once, encompasses all times. Consequently, whatever exists in time is present to God from eternity, not only because He has the principles and ideas of things before Him, but also because from all eternity He looks upon all things as really present to Him. Whence contingent effects are infallibly known by God as things having presence before His gaze, even though, compared with their proximate causes, they are future contingents."[^20]

The text offers a clear affirmation: God knows future contingents—that is, what has not yet occurred—and knows them with a presential quality that allows that knowledge to be infallible. There is, moreover, an important distinction: these future events will come to pass necessarily or contingently, depending upon the perspective from which they are viewed. If viewed from the standpoint of divine knowledge, those future events are already taking place. And thus, although they are contingent, they possess an aspect of necessity. To use the well-known philosophical example: although it is contingent that Socrates should run, whilst he runs, it is necessary that he be running. That is to say, he cannot be running and not running at the same time. In other words, there is nothing so contingent that it does not involve an aspect of necessity. Divine knowledge of the future is infallible for this reason: because He sees it as already present—that is, He sees it in its aspect of necessity. However, if those future contingents are viewed in the immediate causes from which they spring, it is clear that they are contingent, because those causes are contingent. Although it is necessary that Socrates be running whilst he is running—Socrates cannot run and not run at the same time—it is obvious that the fact of his running, considered in the cause from which it proceeds, remains in itself contingent.[^21]

St Thomas thus provides an initial clue to show that the infallibility of divine knowledge of the future and the contingency of that same future are compatible. The divergence between Thomists and Molinists arises upon seeking to take a step beyond this text of St Thomas, asking in what manner the future contingent is found in God's knowledge as already present. If the future contingent does not yet exist, how is it possible that God not only knows it infallibly, but knows it as present? Does the appeal to God's eternity suffice for this?

For the Thomists, the response is initially more straightforward: God knows the future contingent as present because He knows it in Himself, in His knowledge of vision, and this knowledge is the cause not only of the existence of things, but of the mode in which they exist. For many Molinists, by contrast, God knows future events as present because He is eternal, and His eternity encompasses all time. That is to say, those future events would be present to God's knowledge by virtue of His eternity considered as encompassing all time. For the Thomists, on the other hand, those future events would be present precisely because there is eternally present to God His determination that those future events should happen as such free and contingent futures. It is here that the question of the compatibility between divine knowledge and human freedom is posed with total radicality. For would not such a causality of divine knowledge over the free contingent future deprive this future of its freedom and contingency?

### Divine Foreknowledge and Human Freedom

When Thomists are asked whether their conception of divine knowledge does not seem to them incompatible with the affirmation of authentic free decisions on the part of the human will, they reply that the fact that God knows the future in His own determination that it should happen not only does not impede these actions from being free, but that these actions are free precisely because they are so contained within the divine determination. This response is nothing other than the logical application of the general affirmation that God knows the extra-divine world because that world is precisely the fruit of His knowledge.

When attempting to reconcile this affirmation with the existence of human freedom, they usually employ the following line of reasoning. When speaking of the freedom of a human act, one is designating the relation of this act to its proximate cause, which is the human will. But neither divine determination nor divine foreknowledge impedes the proper relation between the will and its free choice. Therefore they do not impede its quality as a free act. This is so because God transcends every mode of being; consequently, neither His foreknowledge nor His mode of moving human freedom violates the nature of this freedom, but rather accommodates itself perfectly to its nature. God would not be omnipotent, they insist, if He could not move each being in accordance with the nature of that being. Thus they find the fundamental reason for the compatibility between divine foreknowledge and human freedom precisely in the very infallibility of divine knowledge, which is not only the cause of things happening, but of the mode in which they happen.

The Thomist argumentation would lack the most elementary logic were it not grounded, in turn, upon the categorical affirmation of the most absolute divine transcendence—that is, upon the affirmation that God is *supra omne modum entis*, and being above every mode of being can move each being by accommodating Himself perfectly to its proper nature. According to this conception, human freedom is not only not in contradiction with the knowledge of vision, but springs from that very knowledge. Man acts freely because God has so foreseen and decreed it. The infallibility of that foresight not only does not limit human freedom, but is its ultimate cause.

On the Thomist side, the solution to the difficulty rests, then, upon the clear awareness of the infinite transcendence of God. That transcendence makes it impossible for God to enter into collision with created being. Precisely because God infinitely transcends all modes of created being, He can move each thing in accordance with its natural mode of being, without ever violating it. The Thomist theory readily accounts for the fact that divine knowledge of the future is infallible and intuitive. For only that which has being is knowable. Now, by definition, free future contingents do not yet exist either in themselves or in their immediate causes. Therefore they cannot be known in those causes. They exist only in God. For this reason, only in Him can they be known.

The difficulties usually raised against the Thomist explanation are principally these two: a) How can God foresee sin, if He cannot antecedently will it? b) How is it possible for human freedom to coexist with a prior and infallible divine determination? Is it not mere dialectic to take refuge in divine omnipotence, asserting that God would not be completely omnipotent if He could not infallibly achieve what He wills without violating the nature of things?

These grave difficulties that can be levelled against the Thomist approach lead the Molinists to reject two assertions that are key in the Bañesian position: a) that God moves prior to the act;[^22] b) that God knows the free future contingent in His determination that it should happen, a determination which would precede divine knowledge with logical priority. Where, then, does God know the free future according to the Molinists? Molina's answer is that God knows it through a knowledge situated between the knowledge of simple intelligence and the knowledge of vision. God knows the free future, then, through *middle knowledge* (*scientia media*), so called because it is situated between the other two. This middle knowledge is the key to the Molinist answer to the problem of the conjunction between divine foreknowledge and human freedom.

The Molinist hypothesis may be described as follows: Prior to the creative determination of His will,[^23] God knows through the knowledge of simple intelligence the infinite possibilities of imitation contained in His own essence. He consequently knows the infinite possibilities of action of free creatures across the infinity of possible worlds. Through middle knowledge He knows what each will *would* do in any of the possible worlds. Finally, He decides in which world to place man. In virtue of this choice, He knows through the knowledge of vision how the human will *is going* to act. It is, so to speak, a knowledge of vision that takes place in the light of middle knowledge.

The objective of the Molinist endeavour is clear: to maintain the affirmation of God's absolute foreknowledge of all futures, yet without grounding that foreknowledge exclusively upon the divine determination that they should happen. Molina's reasoning is likewise obvious: beyond mere words, the Thomist position seems to leave no room whatsoever for the spontaneity of freedom and, consequently, seems to deny it. For this reason, Molina introduces an additional term into the divisions of divine knowledge: middle knowledge (*scientia media*). Consequently, if divine knowledge is distinguished by the objects with which it deals, in Molina that knowledge is divided as follows: *knowledge of simple intelligence*, which deals with possibles, those possibles that will never cease to be merely possible; *middle knowledge*, which deals with *futuribles* (*futuribilia*)—that is, with those determinations of human freedom that would have been future had that freedom been placed in another world or in another situation; *knowledge of vision*, which deals with the actual future, including the free decisions that are to come to pass.

Middle knowledge was introduced by Molina with a single purpose: to ensure that the free future—which God knows through the knowledge of vision—is not known solely in the simple divine determination that it should happen, but also in the exhaustive knowledge of human freedom. By means of this exhaustive knowledge, God would know all that the will could do but will not do: that is, He would know all futuribles. In this way God knows what choice each person would make in the diverse worlds and circumstances in which he might be placed. In the light of this middle knowledge, God chooses the present world. And it is in that decision to choose the present world, knowing all futuribles, that He would know the future through His knowledge of vision. A question arises: how does God know intuitively, as present, those futures that have not yet occurred? Molina's answer is already familiar to us: those futures are present to God on account of His eternity. God knows those futures in their objective truth which has not yet occurred, but which is reflected as present inasmuch as God is eternal.

Grave difficulties can likewise be raised against the Molinist hypothesis. The first is that it does not sufficiently explain how the absolute sovereignty of divine knowledge is maintained in this hypothesis, for it asserts that God knows futures and futuribles in their proper and immediate cause: that is, in the exhaustive knowledge of the will from which they proceed. The most serious difficulty arises when one recognises that in a free act there is no metaphysical nexus between the will and its act, so that until that act is elicited, freedom can incline towards it or towards its contrary. How is it possible, then, to know infallibly what the will is going to decide before it decides it, if no metaphysical nexus yet exists between freedom and its act? Does not affirming that the future decision of the will can be known by exhaustively knowing that will itself and the circumstances in which it is placed amount, in the final analysis, to affirming that the will is infallibly determined by the weight of circumstances? Finally, one cannot answer coherently the question of why God chose an order in which a person becomes a sinner, rather than choosing another in which that sinner would not have sinned.

Such is the outline of the two most celebrated attempts at resolving the question of how divine foreknowledge and freedom are conjoined. These two attempts will reappear when treating of predestination, whose proper place is among the questions concerning providence; they will reappear likewise when treating of grace as divine help. For decades these two attempts polarised the attention of many theologians, who employed them whilst adding numerous nuances and variants that did not substantially alter them.[^24] It was perhaps one of the most passionate controversies in the history of theological thought: the *De auxiliis* controversy. The dispute was brought to a close by the decision of Pope Paul V. Through it, the limits of the human intellect were laid bare when contemplating the mystery of God and the mystery of man and his destiny. That controversy served to elucidate certain characteristics inherent in divine knowledge and in the essence of human freedom. Finally, it demonstrated that many avenues exist for attempting to respond to the question of how divine foreknowledge and human freedom are conjoined.

In any event, it is patent that there is a necessity to profess with all firmness the complete truth of faith concerning these mysteries: the truth of the absolute sovereignty and transcendence of God—a sovereignty and transcendence that is also manifested in His mode of knowing—and the truth of the existence of human freedom. Although the controversy passed centuries ago, it is nonetheless a question that intimately touches contemporary man. Not without reason has the Second Vatican Council pointed to man's yearning for autonomy and the fear that the existence of an infinitely wise and powerful God renders the existence of human freedom impossible as a determining cause of contemporary atheism.[^25] The Christian is urged to show that, according to the faith of the Church, human freedom is placed in no danger either by God's omnipotence or by His foreknowledge; rather, quite the reverse is true: human freedom springs, as from its native soil, from the omnipotent divine will which loves man in his concrete and wavering humanity, as an intelligent being capable of loving. To show this, it suffices to draw upon the Gospel passages in which Jesus speaks of the loving and sovereign providence of the Father, who cares even for the birds that perish in a frosty night, and of the heed God pays to the prayer that wells up from the free will of men.

### Divine Knowledge as Knowledge of Love

Given the thematic mode of knowing proper to our intellect, it may prove philosophically difficult to reconcile divine foreknowledge and human freedom. It is fitting to keep always in mind, however, that even within their mysteriousness, the affirmations of faith are explicit and radical. Above all, they are heartening to the human spirit. The knowledge God possesses of the future is the knowledge of the God of the Covenant: it is the reason why God can be faithful to His promises. It is, therefore, a knowledge of love and fidelity. God has created everything out of love and hates nothing He has made (cf. Wis 11:24). His loving knowledge extends even to what is smallest and most ephemeral: to the lilies of the field or to the sparrows sold in the market for a penny (cf. Mt 10:29). It is that unfathomable divine wisdom before which no one can boast (cf. Rom 11:33–36) and which is definitively manifested in the wisdom of the Cross (cf. 1 Cor 1:17–25). That infinite wisdom is concretised in the knowledge the Good Shepherd has of each of His sheep (cf. Jn 10:14–16).
