## Chapter 20: The Mystery of the Intimate Life of God {#mateo-seco-trino.20}

The earliest and simplest Christian enunciations of the mystery of God establish that there is but one God—that is, that there do not exist several gods, as is the case with men or with the rest of beings; they also establish that, in this one God, there are Three: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. These Three are really one and the same God. There is, therefore, in God real oneness and real otherness.

In common parlance, nature or essence is the name given to that which constitutes something in a determinate order of being; individual or person is the name given to the subject distinct from other subjects with which it may share the same nature or order of being. Hence, in order to speak of the mystery of the oneness and otherness of God, theology arrived relatively early at the distinction between the divine nature and the persons. And since the divine nature is one, theology saw clearly that the distinction of the persons cannot arise from the nature of God, but from His life. Whence, in the attempt to express the Trinitarian mystery, theology sought the foundation of the distinction of the persons in the divine operations—that is, in the intimate life of God. At the same time, while maintaining this conceptual distinction, theology does not forget that, given the divine simplicity, God's essence is His own act, and therefore in God essence and life are really identified.

Two assertions that might appear contradictory are thus conjoined: there is but one God, yet this God is not a solitary being. He is a communion of persons. That personal communion is the expression of the ineffable richness of His spiritual life. To study the mystery of the Trinity is, therefore, to study the mystery of the intimate life of God.

### The Revelation of the Trinity as Revelation of the God Who Is Love

The life of God is an ineffable and infinite act of knowledge and of love, as befits the supremely spiritual Being. The Trinity is the manner in which He who is essential Love lives: Presence and Love, Word and Gift.

Perhaps Richard of Saint Victor was the theologian who best expressed the connection between the perfection of the divine nature and the plurality of persons. According to Richard, love is the feature of the divine life that, according to our mode of speaking, grounds the Trinitarian mystery. Here is his argument:

In God, the supreme and absolutely perfect good, is found absolutely full and perfect goodness. Where goodness is found in all its fullness, supreme love must also be present. Indeed, there is nothing better than love, nor is there anything more perfect than the love of friendship. Strictly speaking, no one is ever said to possess perfect love if it is merely love of oneself. For there to be perfect love, it is necessary that love tend towards another distinct from oneself. Consequently, where there is no plurality of persons, it is impossible for love to exist in all its perfection.

Richard of Saint Victor is thinking of supreme love both in quality and in intensity. He is thinking, therefore, of the love of friendship—which is the most perfect—lived with infinite intensity. If it is said that God is an act of infinite and subsistent Love, there must exist in Him a love of infinite quality and intensity; that is, there must exist in Him a perfect love of friendship. But this requires that the beloved be really distinct from the lover and, at the same time, worthy of supreme love. This implies that the personal God would have no one to love with supreme love if there did not exist another person of equal dignity to Himself—that is, if there did not exist another divine person. For this reason—concludes Richard—in order that the fullness of charity may be present in the bosom of the divinity, it is necessary that there be divine persons of equal dignity.[^1]

We are not confronted here with any claim to "demonstrate" the mystery of the Trinity. Any attempt to rationalise the Trinitarian mystery would be folly and is doomed to failure. It is simply a matter of receiving, in adoration, the mystery delivered to us in the faith of the Church. The thought set forth above is solely a loving contemplation of the data contained in revelation, a contemplation that seeks to present to our intellect what God has spoken concerning Himself, seeking the relationships existing among those teachings. It is a matter, then, of grasping the teaching of faith with the greatest possible breadth and depth, considering for this purpose the relationship existing among the various truths.

Among these truths are the following: God is Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:16); there is but one God (cf. Deut 6:4); this God is tripersonal (cf. Mt 28:19). These three statements refer to the same infinite and supremely simple being; they are therefore related. All that theology does is to seek the nexus existing between them. This is the proper context in which to situate the reflections of Richard of Saint Victor.

The other considerations proposed by Richard regarding the existence of persons in God likewise follow the line of the essential structure of spiritual being: happiness is found in its fullness only in interpersonal communion; therefore this communion must exist in the bosom of God; glory is perfect only if it takes place in the equality of persons; therefore there must be equality of persons in God.[^2]

> "There is nothing sweeter than love, nothing more delightful in the life of the spirit, no greater joy than the delights of love, no good more pleasurable than this love. The divinity would be perpetually deprived of these delights were it to remain solitary upon the throne of its majesty."[^3]

The statement that God is love is made explicit in the statement that God is a communion of persons; and, in turn, this interpersonal communion is framed by the fact that God is essentially love. The divine persons arise from love and find their unity in love. Unity and otherness exist in God with the absolute perfection of spiritual being: there is a multiplicity of individuals without that multiplicity dividing or multiplying the substance.

Throughout the preceding chapters we have considered God as *Ipsum esse subsistens*. We began the treatment of the divine nature with the consideration of *Ipsum esse subsistens* because being is the first of all perfections, and its deepest root.[^4] The oneness of God finds its ultimate root in the fact that He alone possesses identity between essence and existence, between being and act. In God, fullness of being is identical to fullness of life. In God there is, therefore, a perfect spiritual life of knowledge and love. And since it belongs to love not only to give, but to give oneself,[^5] it is supremely coherent that God should be constituted as a community of persons, wherein communication, self-giving, and union attain full and perfect realisation. That this takes place in the Trinity we know only thanks to Jesus Christ, the revealer of the Trinitarian mystery. Nevertheless, once we have realised that the unity of God (like His very being) consists in love, it is neither absurd, nor inconceivable, nor impossible (even from the standpoint of reason) that in the agapic being of God an agapic trinity should blossom. Thus, in the primal, fontal love a threefold personal love finds its enactment: the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.[^6]

### The Trinity as Mystery in the Strict Sense

It is obvious that the Trinity is a mystery in the strict sense, for in it the fullness of God's life is expressed in its most radical form. It is not that this truth is obscure or complex; rather, its supreme simplicity and supreme luminosity transcend the powers of any created intellect. The mystery of the Trinity is, therefore, one of those mysteries that "by their very nature so exceed the created intellect that, even when handed on in revelation and accepted by faith, they remain veiled by the veil of faith itself and, as it were, shrouded in darkness."[^7]

Even after being revealed, the mystery of the Trinity continues to transcend the capacity of penetration of the created intellect, which can demonstrate neither its existence, nor its nature, nor its intrinsic possibility. It can only—and must—strive to understand the content of the revealed doctrine (to know what it is that it believes), and to make explicit the faith in the Trinity by means of certain analogies and declarations that articulate the various revealed aspects of the mystery with one another. Finally, human reason can resolve the objections of those who attack the Trinitarian mystery, exposing the incongruity of these objections and showing that the Trinitarian doctrine contains no contradictory assertions. We believe a mystery; we do not preach the absurd.

Yet the Trinitarian mystery is an authentic mystery in the strictest sense of the word. Or rather, the mystery of the Trinity is the centre and source of all other mysteries. This means that, even when accepted by faith, no one can positively demonstrate, by internal reasons, its coherence and rationality, as though, once revealed, this mystery had passed into the domain proper to philosophers.[^8] After being revealed, the Trinitarian mystery continues to transcend the created intellect in such a manner that it can never be used as an object of philosophical knowledge. All the analogies employed in studying this mystery are only that: analogies that help us understand what we say, having a foundation in reality, but demonstrating nothing. They merely hint at a distant image that structures our knowledge of the various aspects of the mystery. The simple fact of mixing both planes—that of faith and that of philosophy—is already a grave error of principle. Consider, for example, those philosophers who attempt to base themselves on the Trinitarian mystery in order to defend an evolutionary conception of the divinity.

### The Psychological Analogy

In earlier chapters, when discussing our knowledge of the divine being, analogy was treated in detail. Analogy, it was said, has as its *raison d'être* the fact that between God and the world there exists at once likeness and unlikeness. For this reason it conforms to the theological method to apply to God the perfections we see in things, following the threefold way of affirmation, negation, and eminence. This was said when considering God in the unity of His nature—that is, as creator of the world and, therefore, as efficient and exemplary cause of creatures. Is it legitimate also to employ an analogy to refer to the intimate life of God, that is, to the Trinitarian mystery?

Theology almost universally employs the psychological analogy as the warp and weft upon which to weave Trinitarian doctrine. It is designated by the adjective "psychological" because it takes as its reference the life of the human soul (*psychē*). This is not, of course, an empirical study of human psychology, but a metaphysical consideration of the life of the spirit—that is, of its acts of knowledge and love.

Many reasons explain why theology has almost always chosen this path to structure the treatise on the Trinity. The very names given by the New Testament to the divine Persons, especially to the Word, seem to point to this analogy as the best path. Indeed, to say that God has a *Logos* with Himself from before the creation of the world (cf. Jn 1:1–3) indicates that this *Logos* has some reflection in the human *logos*, that is, in the life of the human spirit. The anthropological teaching of Sacred Scripture also suggests the same path. From Genesis onward, the Bible repeats that man is made in the image and likeness of God (cf. Gen 1:26–27; Wis 2:23; Sir 17:1). It is logical, then, to take the spiritual operations of man as a starting point to refer to the Trinitarian mystery, in which the spiritual life of God is condensed.

From the earliest centuries, the Fathers used the life of the human spirit as the analogy whereby to express the Trinitarian mystery. This is particularly prominent in Saint Justin, Tertullian, Saint Gregory of Nyssa, and Saint Augustine.[^9] With Saint Gregory of Nyssa, the analogy appears already clearly structured and resolved upon as the path along which to organise the exposition of the Trinitarian mystery:

> "The doctrine of our religion knows how to find a certain distinction of the hypostases in the unity of nature. Even those who are strangers to our doctrine will consider that God is not devoid of intelligence (*logos*). Whoever confesses that God is not devoid of intelligence (that God is not *alogos*) will have to admit that God has a Word (*Logos*) (...) If he should say that he conceives the Word of God after the likeness of our word, he may then be led to a higher conception. Indeed, it is entirely necessary to believe that the Word, like everything else, corresponds to the nature [from which it proceeds] (...) In the transcendent nature, everything that is predicated of it must be proportioned to the greatness of that which is contemplated..."[^10]

This text is already familiar to us.[^11] It belongs to the *Great Catechetical Oration*, in which Gregory undertakes an orderly and methodical explanation of the contents of the Christian faith. It is one of the earliest efforts at an overarching systematisation of the entire doctrine of faith. The Nyssene's exposition is founded precisely upon the analogy existing between the life of God and the life of the human spirit. In the same way that man is not an alogical being, but possesses a *logos*, so neither is God alogical, but has a *logos*—one infinitely superior in ontological quality to the human *logos*, as corresponds to the transcendent being of God.

It is highly significant that in a text almost contemporary with this, the reason why this analogy is used is formulated reflectively:

> "If you wish to know God, know yourself first. By the constitution of your being, by what is within you, you will know God. Enter within yourself, look into your soul as into a mirror, decipher its structure, and you will see yourself as the image and likeness of God."[^12]

The psychological analogy finds in Saint Augustine its clearest and most decisive systematiser.[^13] While in Gregory of Nyssa this analogy took the spoken word as its guiding thread—so that the Word and the Spirit proceed from the Father after the likeness of the word in which thought (*logos*) and breath (*pneuma*) are found—in Saint Augustine this analogy is already an analogy of the two operations of the soul, knowledge and love, as operations proper and distinct to every spiritual being:

> "Although the human mind is not of the same nature as God, nevertheless the image of that nature, which surpasses all others in goodness, is to be sought and found in the noblest part of our nature (...) It is an image insofar as it is capable of God and can participate in God. It could not achieve so sublime a good were it not an image of God. Behold, then: the soul remembers itself, understands itself, and loves itself; this is already a trinity; we do not yet see God, but we do see an image of God."[^14]

From Saint Augustine onwards, the psychological analogy has been used almost universally by theology to refer to the mystery of the Trinity. Even Richard of Saint Victor who, as we have seen, focuses exclusively on the analogy of love, is also employing the psychological analogy, for he is considering the nature of love as it manifests itself in the operations of the soul.[^15]

The psychological analogy is of great importance. It is clearly suggested by Sacred Scripture and has been employed by the patristic theology of both East and West from the earliest centuries. Although its definitive structuring is due to Saint Augustine, this analogy was already widely used long before him. It possesses universal validity, just as the structure of the life of the spirit is universal. Its reasoning goes beyond mere metaphor; it is a genuine analogy based on the likeness existing between the deepest depths of the life of the human spirit and the being of God, who is spiritual. Its speculative value must not be minimised.

Yet the psychological analogy does not belong to the faith of the Church; it is merely an instrument in the service of theological endeavour. The Trinity is a mystery in the strict sense and is accepted by virtue of faith, not by force of reasoning. Theology comes afterwards, and attempts neither to supplant nor to rationalise faith, but to work within faith seeking understanding (*fides quaerens intellectum*). The psychological analogy is only that: an instrument that it is legitimate—and highly advantageous—to use in the service of theological reflection. It is an instrument fully compatible with the apophatic dimension of theology, since it seeks neither to rationalise faith nor to subordinate it to analogy, but to place that analogy at its service.

### The Greek and Latin Schemas

It is a commonplace to say that Greek theology and Latin theology approach the study of the Trinitarian mystery from two different perspectives: the Greeks proceed from the trinity of persons to the unity of nature, whereas the Latins proceed from the unity of nature to the trinity of persons. This commonplace synthesises—with all the simplification that this entails—two distinct ways of proceeding, in an attempt to highlight the advantages and difficulties of two ideal schemas that have never actually existed in such stark clarity, even though they do indeed represent different paths, as has already been seen in studying the history of Trinitarian theology and the question of the *Filioque*.[^16]

These are different paths that also raise different problems, yet they cannot be regarded as incompatible, or even as parallel, but rather as complementary. Still less so if we go back to the patristic era. Greek patristic theology takes as its point of departure the baptismal confession of faith and Sacred Scripture, in which the doctrine concerning God is set forth first, followed by His Trinitarian reality. It suffices to recall the example of Saint Gregory of Nyssa, who in the *Great Catechetical Oration* begins by speaking of the unity of God, in order subsequently to show that this God has a *logos* and a *pneuma*. In turn, Latin patristics—and its principal representative, Saint Augustine—likewise has as its starting point the baptismal confession of faith and Sacred Scripture; and although Saint Augustine begins his *De Trinitate* with the unity of the divine essence, he keeps the trinity of persons and the theology of the missions very much in mind from the outset. The opposition that people have sought to see between the two theologies—Greek and Latin—is largely fictitious. The historical reality is far more nuanced.[^17] In the patristic period, Greek theology and Latin theology stand very close together and do not admit of such dichotomies. Their approaches and problems are very similar.[^18] Rather, it may be said that Latin theology, in its development, follows Greek theology without contradicting it.[^19]

Patristic texts show that, in both East and West, the breach between theology and economy occurs as an indirect consequence of the teachings of the Council of Nicaea. Indeed, the explanation of the *homoousios* (*consubstantial*) brought with it the necessity of focusing theological reflection upon the relationship of the Persons to the divine substance, since it is in this relationship that their equality and unity reside. The Son is equal to the Father because He receives His substance from Him.[^20] This preferential attention relegated to second place the economy in which the Trinity is revealed. Yet this development affected Greeks and Latins alike, as the history of theological thought demonstrates.[^21]

A rigorous consideration of the Trinitarian mystery does not admit of a strict dichotomy between these schemas: whoever considers the trinity of persons must consider it within the horizon of the unity of God, and whoever considers the unity of God must consider it within the horizon of the trinity of persons. This requires that we highlight above all the harmony and complementarity existing between these two schemas. In his *De Trinitate*, Saint Augustine provides a veritable lesson in theological method: he begins from the divine unity, but already at the beginning of the second book he draws upon the theology of the missions—that is, on the distinction of the Persons—to speak of the mystery of God.[^22]

Within this convergence of distinct schemas, it is evident that Latin theology—especially from the Middle Ages onwards—places the divine unity in the foreground and is, consequently, particularly sensitive to the concern of identifying the ground upon which the distinction of persons is established. In this climate one can understand the importance attached to the opposition of relation as the sole foundation for establishing the distinction of the Persons. The most characteristic axiom is already familiar to us: in God everything is identical except where there is opposition of relation.[^23]

Yet this does not mean that it is fair to characterise Latin theology as a theology of the divine essence, as though it forgot the theology of the Persons, or as though its path were primarily philosophical.[^24] It is worth recalling the firmness with which Saint Thomas emphasises the unknowability of God and the real distinction among the Persons, and consequently the reality of the processions in God.

### Meaning of the Principal Terms Used in Trinitarian Theology

In the historical section, we have already encountered several terms that were coined as circumstances required, in order to set forth the Trinitarian mystery with the greatest possible precision. It was also seen how the progress of Trinitarian theology and the pacification of the Church depended in no small measure upon the appropriateness of the chosen terminology. The most significant period in this terminological consolidation extends from the Council of Nicaea (AD 325) to the First Council of Constantinople (AD 381), above all through the important advance brought about by the distinction between nature and hypostasis and Saint Basil's formulation: "one substance, three hypostases."[^25]

We now indicate the principal terms used in treating the Trinitarian mystery and their current meaning. These are terms whose correct use remains of great importance.[^26] Before enumerating them, let us insist once more that all these words must be applied to God analogically, as corresponds to the likeness-unlikeness existing between Him and created being.

#### a) Essence (*ousia*), Nature (*physis*), Substance (*substantia*)

These three terms serve to designate everything in God that is common to the three Persons, such that they designate the sum of the absolute attributes and predicates that are affirmed of God. They are used, therefore, to designate everything that must be said of God in the singular.[^27] Etymologically, the three terms designate the same reality, though with different nuances: essence is what a thing is; nature is what a thing has received by its birth and therefore what a thing is; substance is what stands beneath the accidents, that is, what persists. The three names designate, then, what God is. The necessity of applying them to God analogically becomes clear if one recalls that God does not have an essence distinct from His existence, that He is not born, and that He has no accidents.

#### b) Supposit or Hypostasis and Person

These are the terms used to designate the realities that are predicated of God in the plural. Supposit (*suppositum*) or hypostasis—which etymologically also signify that which is placed beneath—are defined as a subject, complete in itself, distinct from the others of the same nature, and of which are predicated all the perfections it possesses, because all rest upon it as upon their ultimate foundation. Person (*prosopon*) adds to the concept of supposit that of being a subject possessing the most perfect nature—that is, an individual subject or supposit of a rational nature.

#### c) Subsistence

In classical theology the term *personalitas* (personhood) is used in a sense distinct from its modern psychological sense, though closely related. By *personalitas* is understood that which constitutes the person as person—that is, what gives it its ultimate perfection as a personal being. The Latins locate this ultimate perfection in subsistence (*subsistentia*). The term subsistence is likewise derived from the Greek word *hypostasis* with the meaning of that which stands beneath. In this sense it is said that there exist in God three subsistences, that is, three persons, whereby the term subsistence comes to designate the same reality as hypostasis.[^28] As has already been seen, the term subsistence is also used to refer to God in His unity: *Ipsum esse subsistens*. Hence theology speaks of there being in God a single absolute subsistence (*subsistentia absoluta*) and three relative subsistences (*subsistentiae relativae*).

#### d) Subsistent Relations

The distinction between absolute subsistence and relative subsistences serves to show where unity and otherness reside in God. There is only one God, who exists in three mutually referred subsistences. The divine Persons—the three relative subsistences—are distinguished from one another solely by their opposition, that is, by the fact of being relations mutually referred to one another. They are relations that subsist as distinct from one another because the one is opposed to the other, and they are relations existing within the *Ipsum esse subsistens*. Thus, for example, the Father is referred to the Son and the Son is referred to the Father, and both references are distinguished from each other because they are opposed to each other: the Father cannot be the Son, and the Son cannot be the Father, since it is impossible at once to beget and to be begotten. Yet at the same time, both relations are subsistent with the subsistence of the *Ipsum esse subsistens*.

Relation (*schesis*, *pros ti*, *esse ad*) is the order or reference existing between two terms. For this reference to be real, it requires that the reason or foundation on which the relation is based be real as well. This foundation, in the Trinity, is the origin that one Person has from another—that is, the fact that one Person proceeds from another. The Father gives to the Son His own substance, and the Father and the Son give it to the Holy Spirit.

In God there are four relations: paternity, filiation, active spiration, and passive spiration. Nevertheless, there are only three Persons, since there are only three relations of opposition, paternity and filiation not being opposed to active spiration.

#### e) Processions

Processions (*probasis*, *probole*) designate the fact that one Person receives His origin from another. Saint Thomas defines procession as *ordo originis* (the order of origin), that is, the order that follows from the origin that one person has in another.

The Trinity is realised by way of immanent processions—that is, processions that remain within the divine being. In the case of the divine Persons, this is the most perfect immanence, for even though one Person proceeds from another, the perfect numerical identity of the divine essence is maintained. The Father not only communicates His own substance to the Son, but communicates it without any division or cleavage. When we say that the Son is consubstantial with the Father, we do not merely say that He is of an equal substance to the Father, but that He is of numerically the same substance as the Father. That is why there is only one God.

These are real processions. For a procession to be real, it is necessary that the origin be real and, therefore, that there be real actions in God. For the Father's paternity to be real, it is necessary that the act by which He begets the Son be real; or, what amounts to the same, it is necessary that the Son really proceed from the Father through a real act of generation, really receiving His being from the Father.

In God there are two immanent processions: the Son proceeds from the Father by way of generation; the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son by way of spiration (*ekporeusis*). For this reason the three divine Persons are consubstantial, that is, they possess one and the same essence.

#### f) Notions and Properties

The notions or notional properties (*idioma gnoristikon*) are those notes or characteristics by which we know and distinguish the divine Persons. They are, therefore, notes that are not common to the three divine Persons. These notions are five: innascibility, paternity, filiation, active spiration, and passive spiration.

Properties (*idiomata*) are those notions that can be formally predicated of only one Person. They are, therefore, notes exclusive to that Person. Personal properties are those properties by which the Persons are constituted in their personal being—namely, paternity, filiation, and procession (passive spiration).[^29]

#### g) Missions

Our Lord speaks of having been sent by the Father and, in turn, speaks of sending the Holy Spirit to the apostles (cf. Jn 3:17; 17:3; 20:21; 16:7). The term mission designates, then, the sending of a divine Person *ad extra* from the Trinity. Mission implies the order of origin—that is, that the Person sent proceeds from the One who sends Him.

#### h) Circuminsessio (*Perichoresis*)

By this term is designated the fact that the divine Persons, while remaining distinct from one another, exist in one another by virtue of being the one and same God.

#### i) Appropriations

Precisely because the works *ad extra* are common to the three divine Persons, appropriation is the name given to the act of attributing a particular work *ad extra* or a particular attribute—which is common to all three—to one Person in preference to the others, in light of His personal characteristics. Saint Thomas defines appropriation as "the manifestation of the divine Persons by means of essential attributes."[^30] Thus, for example, omnipotence and the work of creation are appropriated to the Father; Wisdom is attributed to the Son.

### The Trinity and the Christian Life

The entire life of the Church converges towards the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity. Towards the Trinity converge, as their ultimate reason, Christian prayer and worship, which are directed to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. This central place of the Trinitarian mystery is a consequence of the infinite majesty of God, who alone deserves the worship of latria. This central place is likewise a consequence of the structure of salvation history, which proceeds from the Most Blessed Trinity and culminates in man's participation in Trinitarian life through the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul and, when it reaches its fullness, in the beatific vision. Salvation, indeed, consists in the fact that man is constituted an adoptive child of God in the Son through the Holy Spirit. Eternal life consists in the active contemplation of the Trinitarian mystery (cf. Jn 17:3), so that man, who proceeds from the Trinity and is the image of the Trinity, returns to the Trinity by means of his acts of knowledge and love.[^31]

The importance of the Trinitarian mystery for the Christian life is perceived with particular force in the fact that Christian initiation begins with baptism, which can only be valid if conferred in the explicit Trinitarian confession according to Christ's command (cf. Mt 28:19). It is understandable that explicit faith in the mystery of the Trinity should be necessary for salvation. This necessity is commonly described as a relative necessity of means (*necessitas medii*); for in cases of impossibility, an implicit faith in the mystery suffices.[^32] The necessity of believing explicitly in the mystery of the Trinity is the same as the necessity of baptism, of which the explicit Trinitarian invocation constitutes an essential part.

The importance of faith in the mystery of the Trinity is also shown by the fact that it is impossible to believe in the mystery of Christ without at the same time believing in the mystery of the Trinity;[^33] it is impossible to participate in the mystery of Christ without entering into the realm of Trinitarian life, for in our union with Christ the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul takes place. Finally, participating in the intimate life of God through the clear vision in glory is the goal towards which our earthly journey is directed.

Hence the life of the Church and the interior life are essentially Trinitarian, and Trinitarian piety consequently forms part of man's itinerary towards God. The mystery of Christ leads to the mystery of the Triune God. Here is how Saint Josemaría describes this spiritual itinerary:

> "We started with simple and charming vocal prayers, which we learned in our childhood, and which we would never want to give up. Prayer, which began with such childlike simplicity, now broadens out into a wide, smooth-flowing stream, for it follows the pace of friendship with Him who said: *I am the way* (Jn 14:6). If we love Christ in this way, if with divine daring we take refuge in the wound that the lance opened in His side, the Master's promise will be fulfilled: *If a man love me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him, and will make our abode with him* (Jn 14:23).
> 
> "The heart then needs to distinguish and adore each of the divine Persons. In some way, it is a discovery made by the soul in the supernatural life, like the discoveries of a little child who is opening its eyes to existence. And the soul communes lovingly with the Father and with the Son and with the Holy Spirit; and it readily submits to the action of the life-giving Paraclete, who gives Himself to us unmerited: the supernatural gifts and virtues!
> 
> "We have run like the deer that yearns for running streams (cf. Ps 41:2); thirsty, our lips parched, dry. We want to drink at this spring of living water. Without anything odd, throughout the day we move in this abundant and clear spring of fresh water that leaps up to eternal life (cf. Jn 4:14). Words become superfluous, because the tongue cannot express itself; the intellect is stilled. One does not reason; one looks! And the soul breaks out again into a new song, for it feels and knows itself also lovingly looked upon by God, at all hours."[^34]
