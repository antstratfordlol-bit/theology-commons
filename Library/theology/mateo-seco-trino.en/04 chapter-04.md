## Chapter 4: The Revelation of the Trinitarian Mystery {#mateo-seco-trino.4}

God reveals Himself in Jesus Christ as Father, Son, and Spirit. We shall proceed, first of all, to set forth the New Testament doctrine concerning each of the three divine Persons, and conclude by examining those passages that contain the principal Trinitarian expressions.

### The Revelation of God the Father

The invocation of God as father is known in many religions. The divinity has frequently been called father of gods and of men. In using this term, two aspects of God's activity are emphasised: that He is the first origin of everything; and that He is goodness and loving solicitude towards all men, acting towards them as a good father does towards his children. This paternal tenderness of God is even expressed through the imagery of motherhood, which conveys with greater graphic force God's closeness and compassion: *As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you* (Is 66:13). This union of fatherhood and motherhood in God appears especially underlined in the texts that express His fatherhood towards the poor and the weak (cf. Hos 11:1–9; Jer 31:9, 20; Is 49:15; 66:13). Sacred Scripture also teaches that God transcends the human distinction of sexes. God is neither man nor woman, but the Supreme Being; His fatherhood transcends human fatherhood and motherhood and, at the same time, is the origin and measure of all other fatherhood: *from whom every fatherhood in heaven and on earth is named* (Eph 3:15).[^1]

#### God as Father in the Old Testament

The sacred authors present Israel as the firstborn of Yahweh (cf. Ex 4:22), as the people specially cared for by God to the point of calling them *my people* (cf. Deut 14:1–2; 7:8), since He has chosen them as His own possession to be the bearer of the messianic promises, that is, to be the bearer of the mysterious divine designs of salvation. Israel has been chosen precisely in order that these designs may reach fulfilment. Even creation is understood as the beginning of salvation and, therefore, as an exercise of God's fatherhood over man (cf. Deut 14:1; 32:6; Mal 2:10).

The fatherhood of Yahweh over the people is founded upon the fact of their election and deliverance (cf. Deut 7:7; 14:1–2). It rests, therefore, upon the historical commitment that Yahweh freely contracted in choosing Israel and promising them His continuous protection. Even the punishments Yahweh inflicts upon the people when they are unfaithful spring from the fatherhood of Yahweh, who corrects as a father corrects his children, and who is *slow to anger and abounding in steadfast love* (cf. Is 64:7; Jer 2:27; 31:20; Hos 11:3–8).

The idea of God as Father flows spontaneously in the prophetic texts, referred not only to the people as a whole, but also to the helpless righteous person: *As a father pities his children, so the Lord pities those who fear him. For he knows our frame; he remembers that we are dust* (Ps 103:13–14). God is especially *father of the fatherless and protector of widows* (Ps 68:5). The whole of wisdom literature is permeated by a sentiment of filial attachment to the provident and just God, which presages the revelation of the Sermon on the Mount.

The fatherhood of Yahweh assumes its strongest tones when referred to the Messiah, who is king and priest forever precisely because He is the Son of Yahweh (cf., e.g., Ps 2:7; 110:3). Christ's message surprised His hearers and, in a certain sense, was unprecedented; yet it had received a preparation in the affirmation of the fatherhood of God with respect to the people and, in a special way, with respect to the Messiah.[^2]

#### The Father Revealed by the Son

Our Lord inserts His preaching about God into the teaching on the fatherhood of God contained in the Old Testament. When Christ teaches the disciples to pray the Our Father, He is proposing the fundamental manner in which the Christian is to address God. This form of address is interwoven with the affirmation of God's providence over man and over the world (cf. Mt 6:8; 6:26–30).

Here are some affirmations of God's fatherhood taken from the Sermon on the Mount as we find it in the Gospel of Saint Matthew: *so that you may be sons of your Father* (Mt 5:45); *You, therefore, must be perfect, as your heavenly Father is perfect* (Mt 5:48); *your Father who sees in secret* (Mt 6:4); *your Father who is in secret* (Mt 6:6); *your Father knows what you need* (Mt 6:8); *Our Father* (Mt 6:9); *if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father also will forgive you* (Mt 6:14); *if you do not forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive...* (Mt 6:15); *your Father who is in secret* (Mt 6:18); *your heavenly Father feeds them [the birds of the air]* (Mt 6:26); *your heavenly Father knows that you need them all* (Mt 6:32); *how much more will your Father who is in heaven give good things to those who ask him!* (Mt 7:11).[^3]

These texts stand in clear continuity with the affirmations of the Old Testament, but at the same time God's fatherhood is underscored in them with truly striking force and immediacy. Our Lord does not merely affirm the fatherhood of God over men, but shows with what bowels of mercy He exercises this fatherhood. The parables of mercy, such as that of the Prodigal Son, are a fine exponent of Jesus' teaching regarding the infinite mercy of God towards sinful man (cf. Lk 15:11–32).

Over and above this, however, Jesus' teaching on the fatherhood of God entails a radical novelty grounded in the consciousness of His divine Sonship to the Father; for in its light, all that the Old Testament had said concerning the fatherhood of God acquires a new and definitive perspective. Jesus calls God His Father in an entirely new way which, in turn, decisively influences the manner in which the rest of us can call God Father.[^4] The sonship of men to God, which we have already seen affirmed in the Old Testament, is now the consequence and application of our being rooted in Christ, who is the eternal Son of the Father (cf. Jn 15:1–10). In consequence, the entire teaching of the Old Testament regarding the fatherhood of God appears thoroughly renewed. The Good News is not merely that God is like a Father, but that God is, in the strictest and most proper sense, the Father of Jesus, and that, in Jesus, we are truly made children of God. The Good News is the relationship of fatherhood that Jesus lives as authentically received Sonship.[^5]

This is the most essential element of the New Testament message concerning God: that God has a Son, who is eternal and is God as the Father is God. This radical novelty hinges upon what Jesus said of His divine Sonship, that is to say, upon the consciousness Jesus possessed of His being and His origin. "If we may venture to penetrate into the human consciousness of Jesus, we shall see in it the origin of Christian language about God. The incarnate Son Himself called Him *my God* simultaneously with *my Father* (Mt 27:46; Lk 23:46). Inasmuch as the Son became man, and is so forever, the Father becomes His God and our God, while remaining His Father and becoming ours (Jn 20:17)."[^6] Grounded in this consciousness of Christ's Sonship to the Father, the Apostles confess Jesus as the Word who in the beginning was with God and was God (Jn 1:1), as the image of the invisible God (Col 1:15), and as the radiant light of His glory and the exact imprint of His nature (Heb 1:3). Later, following apostolic tradition, the Church would confess at the Council of Nicaea that the Son is consubstantial with the Father, that is, one only God with Him.[^7]

When dealing with the theology of God the Father, attention is directed, therefore, to those texts in which Jesus calls God His Father and to the consciousness of Sonship to the Father that Jesus displays. These are texts in which Jesus speaks of God as His Father with a sense of complete immediacy. Already as a child Jesus says of Himself that He must be about His Father's business (Lk 2:49), and He warns that not everyone who says "Lord, Lord" will enter the kingdom of heaven, but only he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven (Mt 7:21).

Jesus feels closely identified with the Father's activity (cf. Mt 15:13; 16:17; 18:35; 20:23). Jesus feels so united to the Father that the Father has handed all power over to Him; still more, that only He knows the Father and, reciprocally, only the Father knows Him: *All things have been delivered to me by my Father; and no one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and any one to whom the Son chooses to reveal him* (Mt 11:27; cf. Lk 10:22). This is a text of capital importance, for it reveals the equality of knowledge existing between both; and since knowledge follows being, it points towards equality of nature. "It is, then, a knowledge born of the intimacy of living with God. The Son too (that is, He alone!) is initiated into the plans and secrets of God; He knows what the Father wills and what is pleasing in His sight."[^8]

This intimacy between Jesus and the Father manifests itself with unmistakable traits in the way Jesus prays: when praying He always calls God Father.[^9] Jesus addresses the Father calling Him *Abba* (Mk 14:36). Christian tradition has understood Jesus' exclamation *Abba* as the expression of His unique consciousness of Sonship to the Father. To this must be added the fact that Jesus never placed His Sonship to the Father on the same level as ours. Thus, for instance, He never called Him *our Father*, but used the expression *my Father and your Father* (cf., e.g., Jn 20:17), without ever including Himself in our adopted sonship. The same occurs with Jesus' prayer: Jesus teaches others to pray, but He always prays alone (cf. Mk 1:35; 6:46; 14:32–42; cf. Lk 3:21; 5:16; 6:12; 9:28; 11:1).

This conduct of Jesus explains why the early Christian community understood this singular expression—*Abba*—as the manifestation of a unique consciousness of His Sonship,[^10] that is, as the manifestation of His awareness of an intimate relationship with God as Son in the fullest and most proper sense.[^11]

In the Gospel of Saint John this Father–Son relationship is depicted as eternal and essential, so that Jesus of Nazareth is affirmed to possess an existence prior to His birth according to the flesh: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God* (Jn 1:1, 18); Jesus affirms that He comes from God: *Not that any one has seen the Father except him who is from God; he has seen the Father* (Jn 6:46; cf. Jn 1:18); in dispute with the Jews He says: *Before Abraham was, I am* (Jn 8:58); and in the Priestly Prayer: *And now, Father, glorify thou me in thy own presence with the glory which I had with thee before the world was made* (Jn 17:5).

This is a union between Father and Son that is not merely operative, but vital and eternal. The Son does whatever He has seen the Father do (Jn 5:19–22; 6:38); it is the Father who sends the Son (Jn 5:26–38). This union between Father and Son is of such a nature that Jesus can affirm: *I and the Father are one* (Jn 10:30). The expressions used by Jesus are crystal clear: *In that day [after the sending of the Spirit] you will know that I am in my Father* (Jn 14:20); *that they may all be one; even as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that thou hast sent me* (Jn 17:21). Upon this intimate relationship among Christians, Christ, and the Father, Christian life is built in obedience and love. Here lies the key to the First Epistle of the Apostle John.[^12]

This special fatherhood of God over Jesus is already recorded in Saint Peter's earliest discourses in Acts: *This Jesus God raised up, and of that we all are witnesses. Being therefore exalted at the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this which you see and hear* (Acts 2:32–33). It is similarly reflected in the writings of Saint Paul: God, who is Father of all men, is Father of Jesus Christ in an exclusive and unique way, for Jesus is His Son by nature and not by adoption: *When the time had fully come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law, to redeem those who were under the law* (Gal 4:4–5). *God, sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh...* (Rom 8:3). It is evident that, in this Pauline argument, the Son does not receive His Sonship upon being sent; rather, He is sent precisely because He is the Son. Jesus is God's *own Son* (Rom 8:3; 8:32), the *Son of His love* (cf. Col 1:13). He is the Son *through whom also he created the worlds*, as Heb 1:2 states in clear parallelism with Jn 1:18.

### The Revelation of God the Son

In the Christian message, the question concerning God and the question concerning Christ are inseparable. God is the Father of Christ in the strictest sense; Jesus is the Son of God with such propriety and accuracy that, being truly Son, He is of the same nature as the Father. Christology and Theology are, therefore, inseparable. In the preceding pages we have considered the most significant biblical texts concerning the fatherhood of God. Attention was directed, then, to the teachings on God the Father contained in the preaching of Jesus Christ. This preaching on God the Father—as has been observed—was carried out by Our Lord inasmuch as it was the reflection and manifestation of His own Sonship. Father and Son are correlative realities: fatherhood refers to the Son, and sonship refers to the Father. The biblical passages in which both are expressed are identical.

#### Old Testament Preludes

In the Old Testament we find certain forms of mediation between Yahweh and His people that exegetes usually consider preludes—merely preludes—to the teaching of the New Testament concerning the Person of the Son. These are mere intimations, not an anticipation or beginning of the revelation of the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity. And yet, it may be said that the revelation of Jesus' divine Sonship—even from a literary point of view—was prepared by the progressive revelation of God's fatherhood over the people, over the king, and over the Messiah; and, above all, by the personifications of Yahweh's activity.

These personifications can be considered as various forms of mediation between Yahweh and His people. The New Testament hagiographers draw upon these personifications to express their Theology of the Son: namely, the Angel of Yahweh, the Word of God, and Wisdom. Their theological importance lies fundamentally in the fact that they are presented as mediations between Yahweh and creation.[^13]

##### The Angel of Yahweh

The Angel of Yahweh appears under an individual form, with a concrete salvific mission. In the journey through the wilderness he is like Yahweh's messenger, the helper of Israel (cf. Ex 14:19; 23:20–23; 32:34; Num 20:16). The Angel of Yahweh is the guide and protector of those who fear God (cf. Gen 24:7, 40; 1 Kings 19:5–6; 2 Kings 1:3, 15). At times he appears as an avenging angel (2 Sam 24:16–17; 2 Kings 19:35–36) and judge (2 Sam 14:20; 19:27); at other times, one can no longer clearly distinguish the Angel of Yahweh from Yahweh Himself.[^14]

##### The Word of God

The Word of God is, first and foremost, the word whereby God, in revealing Himself, makes known His will concerning Israel, whom He has chosen and constituted as His own people precisely through His word. At Sinai Yahweh utters *the ten words* (Ex 34:28) addressed to Moses, who is charged with transmitting them to the people. These ten words—these ten commandments—are the immovable foundation of all the duties that flow from the Covenant. This immovability of the words is signified by their inscription upon the tablets of the Law and by the injunction to transmit them faithfully to all generations (cf. Ex 32:16; 34:1, 27). Moses' eminent standing with respect to Israel—he enjoys the highest authority throughout their history—rests precisely upon the importance of the word of God: that God chose him to speak in His name (Ex 3:1–20); upon his charge to convey the words of God to the people and to ensure that the people remain faithful in observing them (cf. Ex 34:10–35). Moses is Israel's mediator before God, for *there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom the Lord knew face to face* (Deut 34:10).

The word of the prophets is also the Word of God. That word is, primarily, the word that proceeds from heaven and is heard by the prophet (cf. Is 5:9; Ezek 10:5; Jer 1:11; 24:1). It is a word that turns the prophet into an envoy of Yahweh, making him the mouth of Yahweh (cf. Is 6:8; 50:4; Jer 1:7; 15:19; 20:9). Wherever the prophetic word is present, there Yahweh is acting, announcing, guiding His people, saving, and judging (cf. 1 Sam 3:10–14; 2 Sam 2:4–16; Jer 1:4–10). The word of Yahweh is efficacious: *For as the rain and the snow come down from heaven, and return not thither but water the earth, making it bring forth and sprout, giving seed to the sower and bread to the eater, so shall my word be that goes forth from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose, and prosper in the thing for which I sent it* (Is 55:10–11). The efficacy of the word of God extends likewise to creation. It is by His word that Yahweh created the world (cf. Gen 1:3–25; Ps 33:6–9; Wis 9:1–4).

According to Old Testament theology, the whole of divine activity in creating the world, choosing Israel, and saving mankind stands in close dependence upon the Word of God (cf., e.g., Is 9:8; 55:10–11; Jer 20:9; 23:29). The word of God not only renders the power of God and God Himself efficaciously present, but is a strong, efficacious word, replete with wisdom. In such a theological setting, one readily understands how John, relying upon a new revelation, gathered together all the features of the nature of the *dabar Yahweh* of the Old Testament, including the affirmations concerning wisdom, into a single Word, whom he identified with Christ (cf. Jn 1; 1 Jn 1:1).[^15]

##### The Wisdom of God

The wisdom of God, infinite and unfathomable, is revealed in creation (cf. Jer 10:12; Prov 3:19; 8–9; Job 28; Wis 7:22–8:1). This wisdom is a breath of the power of God, a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty; she is a reflection of eternal light, a spotless mirror of the working of God, and an image of His goodness (cf. Wis 7:25–26). These are images that will be applied directly to Christ in the New Testament (cf. Col 1:15; Heb 1:3). The hymns dedicated to wisdom (cf. Prov 1:20–33; 8; 9:1–6; Wis 6:12–25; 7:22–8:1) personify her to such an extent that some have seen in her an authentic hypostasis: *The Lord created me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old. Ages ago I was set up, at the first, before the beginning of the earth* (Prov 8:22–23). Arian exegesis would lean upon texts such as this to argue that the Word, Wisdom of God, was created as the first creature. And Saint Paul would call Christ the *power of God and the wisdom of God* (1 Cor 1:24).

#### The Divinity of Jesus

Jesus manifested His divinity little by little, in a gradual and progressive manner, through a pedagogy suited to the staunch monotheistic conviction of the people of Israel. This manifestation of His divinity is indissolubly bound up with the consciousness Jesus possessed of His Sonship to the Father. The New Testament is an eloquent witness to the divinity of Our Lord Jesus Christ; indeed, the whole of it revolves around Peter's confession—*You are the Christ, the Son of the living God* (Mt 16:16)—, that is to say, around the affirmation that Jesus is Lord (cf. Phil 2:11).

##### The Synoptic Gospels

In the Synoptic Gospels, numerous passages are found in which Jesus, assuming the title Son of Man, manifests that He possesses divine dignity and powers. Indeed, Jesus as the Son of Man attributes to Himself the power to forgive sins (cf. Mk 2:5–12). It is by using this title that Jesus claims for Himself an utterly unique dignity: He declares Himself greater than the Temple (cf. Mt 12:6), greater than kings (cf. Lk 10:24) and specifically greater than David (cf. Mt 22:43–45), greater than the prophets (cf. Mt 11:11 ff.), greater than the Law (cf. Mt 5:21 ff.), Lord of the Sabbath (cf. Mt 12:8), etc.[^16] Jesus, as the Son of Man, presents Himself as the object of that absolute and unconditioned commitment that is owed to God alone. He affirms that whoever welcomes the Son of Man is saved, whereas whoever rejects Him is lost (cf. Mt 16:24–25); He demands that His disciples give their lives for Him (cf. Mk 8:34, 38) and love Him more than their own parents and children (cf. Mt 10:37).

The Son of Man presents Himself as the Son of God equal to God when, questioned by the Sanhedrin concerning His messiahship and divine Sonship, He replies that He is the Messiah and adds: *you will see the Son of man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven* (Mk 14:62). *And they all condemned him as deserving death* (v. 64). The expression used by Jesus—*you will see the Son of man seated at the right hand of Power [God]*—gave to the affirmation of His divine Sonship the precise meaning of being God, equal to God the Father in divinity (in "Omnipotence"), and it was on this account that they pronounced Him guilty of death. This would not have occurred had the affirmation of being Son of God been understood in a generic or moral sense (as "righteous", or "chosen by God"). It should be observed, therefore, that what is decisive in this text is not merely that Jesus responds affirmatively to the question about His divinity and divine Sonship (which could have been taken in a broad sense), but that He does so by identifying Himself with Daniel's Son of Man, seated—moreover—at the right hand of the Almighty: for this reason, Caiaphas understood that Jesus was asserting that He was God, whence the accusation of blasphemy (cf. also Lk 22:66–71).[^17]

An important aspect of the Synoptic Gospels' witness concerning the divinity of Jesus is constituted by the title *Son of God*, and in general by the attribution of divine Sonship to Christ. As has already been stated, the title *son of God* can have—and indeed has—in the Old Testament a generic and moral sense (angels, the righteous, etc., are sons of God). There are in the Synoptics certain passages in which *Son of God* could be interpreted in this sense (cf. Mt 5:9; Mk 3:11). In other passages, by contrast, the expression *Son of God*, or more simply *the Son*, denotes Jesus' divine Sonship in its proper and full sense; that is to say, it denotes the true divinity of Jesus Christ.

In this regard, remarkable for their clarity are the texts in which Christ Himself addresses God in His prayer, calling Him *Father* or *my Father* (cf. Mt 6:9; 11:25–26; 26:39, 42; Mk 14:36; Lk 11:2; 22:42; 23:34, 46). It is probable that the term employed by Jesus was consistently *Abba*, and not merely when explicitly recorded as such in the Gospels. An analysis of this practice of Jesus, utterly unheard of in Jewish prayer, shows that it introduces entirely new elements into the long Jewish tradition. Jesus' religious consciousness appears to manifest that He is not a man pre-constituted prior to His election as son; rather, His entire concrete being is indissolubly bound to His relationship with the Father. He proceeds in everything from Him and, therefore, maintains with the Father that relationship of intimacy which is inconceivable in others.[^18]

The Father Himself bears witness to the Son at Jesus' Baptism (cf. Mk 1:11), calling Him *beloved Son*, which also conveys the idea of Only-Begotten Son. It is the very same voice heard at the Transfiguration of the Lord: *This is my beloved Son, with whom I am well pleased; listen to him* (Mt 17:5; cf. Mk 9:7; Lk 9:35–36). This declaration at the moment of the Transfiguration is like a confirmation of what had already been heard at the baptism.[^19]

In the Synoptic Gospels, Jesus refers to Himself as the Son who knows the Father (cf. Mt 11:27; 21:37–38) and who, as Son, is superior to the servants God had previously sent to His people (cf. Mt 21:34–36), superior even to the angels themselves (cf. Mt 24:36). Jesus distinguished His Sonship from that of His disciples, never saying *our Father* (cf. Mt 5:48; 6:8; 7:21; Lk 11:13; Jn 20:17).[^20]

##### The Pauline Epistles

In the Pauline epistles, numerous testimonies to the divinity of Jesus are likewise found. Prominent among them is the celebrated hymn of the Epistle to the Philippians, which is one of the most thoroughly analysed by modern exegesis: *Have this mind among yourselves, which is yours in Christ Jesus, who, though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form he humbled himself and became obedient unto death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name which is above every name, that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father* (Phil 2:5–11).[^21]

This Christological hymn constitutes a summary of the entire mystery of Christ: from the eternal pre-existence of the Word, who existed in the form of God prior to the Incarnation, to His glorification, exalted to the glory of the Father and constituted Universal Lord (the name which is above every name is *Kyrios*, that is, Lord); a glory attained through the *kenosis* or voluntary self-emptying.

The pre-existence of Christ in His divinity is strongly affirmed by Saint Paul in Col 1:15–17: *He is the image of the invisible God, the first-born of all creation; for in him all things were created (...) He is before all things, and in him all things hold together* (Col 1:15–17). Christ not only exists before every creature as the image of God, but exercises with respect to the whole of creation a work exclusive to God: creating and sustaining all things in being.[^22]

In the same epistle the Apostle writes that in Christ *the whole fulness of deity dwells bodily* (Col 2:9). This text seems to refer more directly to the glorification (divinisation) of Jesus' body after the Resurrection, but it also indicates that His divinity is not the simple indwelling of God in a man, but the bodily indwelling of God: in other words, the Incarnation of God.[^23]

Saint Paul customarily refers to the divinity of Jesus by using the title Lord (*Kyrios*), reserving the name God (*Theos*) for the Father (cf. 1 Cor 8:6). Nevertheless, he occasionally applies the noun *Theos* directly to Jesus Christ: *awaiting our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ* (Tit 2:13). In the Greek original there is a single article governing both *great God* and *our Saviour Jesus Christ*; that is to say, this Saviour of ours, Jesus Christ, is the great God.[^24]

Saint Paul affirms Jesus' divine Sonship with absolute clarity. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* recalls that Saint Paul comments regarding his conversion on the road to Damascus: *When he who had set me apart before I was born, and had called me through his grace, was pleased to reveal his Son to me, in order that I might preach him among the Gentiles...* (Gal 1:15–16). And immediately he began to preach Jesus in the synagogues, saying: *He is the Son of God* (Acts 9:20). "This was 'to be from the first (cf. 1 Thess 1:10) the centre of the apostolic faith' (cf. Jn 20:31) which was professed first by Peter as the Church's foundation (cf. Mt 16:16)."[^25] It is the same doctrine contained in the celebrated opening of Hebrews (Heb 1:1–3), where Christ is called the Son of God, heir of all things, through whom He created the ages, the radiance of His glory and the exact representation of the Father's substance. It is said that He upholds all things by the word of His power, and is seated at the right hand of the Majesty on high.[^26]

##### Word of God and Only-Begotten Son in Saint John

The sacred writer himself states openly that this Gospel was written to demonstrate the divinity of Christ (cf. Jn 20:31). In the Prologue (Jn 1:1–18), the fundamental contours of the mystery of Christ are outlined, which are subsequently developed throughout the Gospel, both in the narrative of Jesus' deeds and words, and in the evangelist's reflections upon them. It opens with the affirmation of the eternal pre-existence of the Word of God: *In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God* (Jn 1:1). This Word or Speech of God is God. The Greek term *Logos* may have been taken by Saint John either from Hellenic culture or from Judaism, to which, as noted earlier, the expression Word of God (*Dabar Yahweh*) was by no means foreign. In any case, it is beyond question that we are confronted with a clear distinction between the Father and the Logos. Indeed, slightly further on the Word is called the *Only-Begotten of the Father* (Jn 1:14). This Word of God became man: *And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us* (Jn 1:14); He is the One who was contemplated by the disciples of Jesus; He is the One of whom John the Baptist bore witness (cf. Jn 1:15), and from whose fulness we have all received; He is the Only-Begotten Son, who is in the bosom of the Father (Jn 1:18).

Jesus Himself claims pre-existence: *before Abraham was, I am* (Jn 8:58; cf. Jn 17:5, 24). The expression *I am* carried a very powerful resonance for the Jews, for it is the expression whereby God reveals Himself to Moses:[^27] "with this expression Jesus indicates that He is the true God."[^28] On this account, "the summit of Revelation was reached where the Word was able to make the eternal *I am* of God resound in the flesh."[^29]

Just as prior to the Incarnation it pertained to the Word to be light and life for men (cf. Jn 1:4–5), so too the incarnate Word, Jesus, declares that He is the light of the world (Jn 8:12) and that He is the Life (Jn 14:6) and the source of life: *For as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so also the Son gives life to whom he will* (Jn 5:21). In the words of Christ recorded in Saint John's Gospel, the close bond between what Christ is and what Christ does becomes evident: Christ's works manifest that He is the Son of God, equal to God.[^30]

*Only-Begotten of the Father* (Jn 1:14) is a title Saint John frequently applies to Jesus as equivalent to the expressions "the Son", "the Son of God", or "Son of the Father". Occasionally we find in the Fourth Gospel the expression *son of God* used in a broad sense (angels and the righteous are sons of God), but on numerous other occasions it undoubtedly signifies the divinity of Christ. Especially explicit are these statements: *I and the Father are one (...); the Father is in me and I am in the Father* (Jn 10:30, 38). Here Christ not only calls God Father, but affirms that He is "one with God the Father"; that is to say, He affirms that His being Son of God consists in being God.

#### The Father and the Son Are One

We have repeatedly encountered passages in the Synoptics that speak of the equality existing between the Father and the Son. Such is the case, for instance, with Mt 11:27: *No one knows the Father except the Son and any one to whom the Son chooses to reveal him*. This equality in knowledge points, as is obvious, to equality of nature. In Saint John's Gospel we likewise find this equality between Father and Son brought to the fore.

This is especially the case with the affirmation contained in Jn 10:30: *I and the Father are one*. It is a unity of wills, which is revealed to be so intimate as to state that the two are one.[^31] Jesus' declaration is so explicit that the Jews seek to stone Him as a blasphemer. When Jesus asks them why they want to stone Him, they answer: *We stone you for no good work but for blasphemy; because you, being a man, make yourself God* (Jn 10:33). Faced with this reply from the Jews, Jesus not only does not retract His previous statements, but insists that they ought to look to the works He performs, because they demonstrate His union with the Father. If the Jews had grasped the true meaning of Jesus' preaching and miracles, they would have known that *the Father is in me and I am in the Father* (Jn 10:38), for Jesus' thaumaturgic power and His manner of acting in general prove His unity with the Father.

When Jesus says that He and the Father are one, according to the context He is referring to the works He performs in the Father's name. The statement is broad and intimates a deeper and more mysterious communion, which is that of being and of nature (cf. Jn 17:11, 22). The Jews thus understand that Jesus makes Himself equal to God (Jn 10:33). The unity of Father and Son is a unity of will, of love, of knowledge, and of power. This explains the agitation of the Jews who wish to stone Him as a blasphemer.[^32]

Jesus returns to this equality with the Father at the Last Supper. *If you had known me*—He says to the disciples—*you would have known my Father also* (Jn 14:7). And addressing Philip, who has failed to grasp Jesus' answer and insists that He show them the Father, He says: *Do you not believe that I am in the Father and the Father in me?* (Jn 14:10). And He adds, with an argument identical to that of Jn 10:30: *Believe me that I am in the Father and the Father in me; or else believe me for the sake of the works themselves* (Jn 14:11). The works of the Son are the works of the Father, not merely because the Father has given the Son the power to perform them, but because the Father is ever in the Son.

Throughout His life, Jesus on various occasions manifested this equality with the Father implicitly. He demanded that absolute love be rendered to Him, He showed Himself possessed of divine powers, He affirmed of Himself that He existed before Abraham existed, and He called God His Father in an absolute and unique manner. In this context one can better perceive the full force of His assertion: *I and the Father are one* (Jn 10:30). As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states: "Only the divine identity of Jesus' person can justify so demanding an assertion as this: 'He who is not with me is against me' (Mt 12:30); and again: 'something greater than Jonah is here... something greater than Solomon is here' (Mt 12:41–42); something 'greater than the temple' (Mt 12:6); when he reminds them that David called the Messiah his Lord (cf. Mt 22:41–45); when he states: 'Before Abraham was, I am' (Jn 8:58); and even: 'I and the Father are one' (Jn 10:30)."[^33]

### The Revelation of God the Holy Spirit

In Sacred Scripture, the Holy Spirit is called Spirit of Yahweh, Spirit of God, Spirit of Jesus Christ, Holy Spirit.[^34] It has rightly been written that the Holy Spirit is the most mysterious Person of the Most Holy Trinity, for just as in man his spirit signifies what is most intimate, so the Holy Spirit expresses the invisibility of God, His profound secret, and His incomprehensibility. In the Holy Spirit we encounter the deepest mystery of Trinitarian life, just as we encounter what is most secret in man when we come to know his spirit and his soul.[^35] It is not surprising, therefore, that the Holy Spirit has so often been described as the "Great Unknown".[^36] Already Saint Thomas Aquinas pointed out that there exists a veritable poverty of vocabulary—*vocabulorum inopia*—for expressing that which pertains to Love and, consequently, for expressing that which pertains to the Holy Spirit.[^37]

Notwithstanding this, the Holy Spirit is constantly present throughout the New Testament.[^38] This is because Christian teaching on God is simultaneously Christocentric and Trinitarian:[^39] it is in the mystery of Christ that the Father is revealed to us as Father, and it is also Christ who reveals to us the Holy Spirit; at the same time, it is the Holy Spirit who makes known to us the mystery of Christ and, in consequence, the mystery of God (cf., e.g., Jn 14:26).

In the farewell discourse at the Last Supper, Jesus speaks at length of the Holy Spirit: He is the Advocate (*Parakletos*) who will be with the Apostles forever, the Spirit of truth who will be sent to the disciples by Jesus and who will guide them into all the truth (Jn 14:16–17; 16:7–13). "In the intimacy of the Upper Room one arrives—it may be said—at the summit of Trinitarian revelation. At the same time, we find ourselves before definitive events and supreme words, which at the end will be translated into the great missionary mandate addressed to the Apostles and, through them, to the Church: *Go therefore and make disciples of all nations*, a mandate that contains, in a certain sense, the Trinitarian formula of baptism: *baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit* (Mt 28:19). This formula reflects the intimate mystery of God and of His divine life, which is the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, the divine unity of the Trinity. One can read this discourse as a special preparation for this Trinitarian formula, in which is expressed the life-giving power of the sacrament that works participation in the life of the Triune God, because it confers upon man sanctifying grace as a supernatural gift. By means of it, man is called and made capable of sharing in the inscrutable life of God."[^40]

This dense passage from the Encyclical *Dominum et vivificantem* highlights the close connection between the promise of the Spirit and the affirmation of the "personality" of this same Spirit as contained in the missionary mandate. This revelation of the Spirit is framed within the economy of salvation: it is the Spirit who guides into all the truth and who sanctifies men, uniting them so intimately with God that He makes them partakers of the divine life: that life which has its source in the Father, is offered to us in the Son—through our insertion into the Son—, and is communicated to us by the Holy Spirit.

#### The Spirit of Yahweh in the Old Testament

New Testament teaching concerning the Holy Spirit recalls all that was said in the Old Testament regarding the Spirit of Yahweh. When at the Last Supper Jesus announces the sending of "another Paraclete" (cf. Jn 14:16–17), He is referring to the same Spirit who already acted in Creation (cf. Gen 1:2), who spoke through the prophets, and who, from Christ's glorification onwards, will be with the disciples to guide them into all the truth.[^41]

In Sacred Scripture, the primary meaning of spirit is wind. Wind is of vital importance in agricultural life. Hence, in the Old Testament, it is not regarded as a mere natural force, but as a power attributed to the Creator and Sustainer of life (cf. Gen 1:2; 8:1). This meaning is complemented by another closely related to it: *ruah* as breath of life, as life itself, since he who breathes lives, and ceasing to breathe is the sign of death. God is the one who breathes His breath into living beings, especially into man (Gen 2:7), in such a way that in man this breath of life appears as the breath of God. For this reason Job says: *as long as my breath is in me, and the spirit of God is in my nostrils, my lips will not speak falsehood* (Job 27:3–4; cf. 33:4; Ps 104:29).

From here one passes to the consideration of the "spirit of Yahweh" as something personal to God. It is something of Himself that God gives to men as a gift, uniting them in a special way to Himself. Thus, for instance, through His Spirit God creates a new heart in the people, giving them a heart of flesh in place of the heart of stone to which sins had reduced them (cf. Ezek 36:26–27); the Spirit of Yahweh sanctifies men, gives them wisdom and knowledge (cf. Is 59:21; Wis 1:4–5; 9:17).[^42] Moreover, certain figures—such as Joseph, Moses, Joshua, David, and Elisha—receive the Spirit as a permanent gift of God that keeps them faithful to their mission. Outstanding among them is the Messiah.[^43] Thus it was prophesied: *And the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and might, the spirit of knowledge and the fear of the Lord* (Is 11:2). According to the prophecies, the Messiah will be the bearer of the Spirit in all His fulness.

Not only is the Messiah the privileged bearer of the Spirit of Yahweh, but the messianic times themselves are characterised by a special outpouring of this Spirit upon the people. Saint Peter recalls this in his preaching on the day of Pentecost: *these men are not drunk, as you suppose, since it is only the third hour of the day; but this is what was spoken by the prophet Joel: "And in the last days it shall be, God declares, that I will pour out my Spirit upon all flesh, and your sons and your daughters shall prophesy (...) yea, and on my menservants and my maidservants in those days I will pour out my Spirit; and they shall prophesy"* (Acts 2:15–18; cf. Joel 2:28–32).

#### Principal Pneumatological Texts in the New Testament

The texts of the New Testament that speak of the Holy Spirit may be grouped into three major categories: those that speak of the Spirit of God as His charismatic power; those that speak of Him as the divine force that sanctifies believers; and those in which this power of God is more clearly described as a divine Person.

##### a) The Spirit of God as Charismatic Power

The virginal conception of Jesus is the work of the Holy Spirit, who overshadows Mary (cf. Lk 1:35). Hence it is said that *she was found to be with child of the Holy Spirit* (cf. Mt 1:18). These and similar texts have as their intellectual background all that is said in the Old Testament concerning the creative and life-giving action of the Spirit of God (cf., e.g., Gen 1:2; Ps 104:30; Wis 1:7) and Yahweh's protection over the Israelite camp (cf. Ex 13:21–22; 19:16; 24:16; 40:34–38). In recounting Jesus' conception through the work of the Spirit, it is highlighted that He is the Messiah, and that in Him culminates the entire salvific process unfolded throughout history: Jesus is conceived in an exceptional manner, as the masterpiece of God's charismatic intervention in history. For this reason, the Child begotten of Mary will be called in an exclusive and unique manner *Son of God* and *Son of the Most High* (Lk 1:32–35). Hence John says of Jesus that He *gives the Spirit without measure* (cf. Jn 3:34).

At His baptism, this Spirit manifests Himself descending upon Jesus in an equally unique and total manner, taking possession of Him for His messianic manifestation (cf. Lk 4:1). Jesus is the One who is always led by the Spirit of God (cf. Lk 4:1; Mk 1:12; Mt 4:1); the One who always acts under the impulse of the Spirit of God. Jesus baptises in the Spirit of God (cf. Mt 3:11); Saint Peter says of Him that God *anointed Jesus of Nazareth with the Holy Spirit and with power; how he went about doing good* (Acts 10:38).

Jesus draws attention to His messianic anointing by the Spirit when in the synagogue of Nazareth He applies to Himself the text of Is 61:1–2: *The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor...* (cf. Lk 4:18–30). Jesus' preaching derives its power from this anointing of the Spirit, for in it is fulfilled the prophecy of Is 42:1–2: *Behold, my servant whom I have chosen... I will put my Spirit upon him, and he shall proclaim justice to the Gentiles* (cf. Mt 12:18–21). Jesus affirms that He acts with the power of the Spirit: *if it is by the Spirit of God that I cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come upon you* (Mt 12:28). The resurrection of Jesus is likewise the work of the Father through the Holy Spirit: *If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you...* (Rom 8:11).

##### b) Sanctification, the Work of the Spirit

Already during the earthly life of the Lord, the figures most closely connected to His messianic mission receive the Spirit in a special way. Such is the case, for example, with the Baptist (cf. Lk 1:15), Zechariah (cf. Lk 1:67), Simeon (cf. Lk 2:25), Elizabeth (cf. Lk 1:41), and, above all, Holy Mary (cf. Lk 1:46–55). Upon departing this world, Jesus breathes His Spirit upon the Apostles, who are now to continue His work (cf. Jn 20:22). That is why Saint Peter, on the day of Pentecost, speaking of Christ's glorification, declares: *Being therefore exalted at the right hand of God, and having received from the Father the promise of the Holy Spirit, he has poured out this which you see and hear* (Acts 2:33). The prophecy of Joel concerning the outpouring of the Spirit in the messianic era is thus fulfilled (cf. Acts 2:18; Joel 2:28–32); the promise of the Lord at His Ascension is likewise fulfilled: *you shall receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you* (cf. Acts 1:8). The power of the Holy Spirit is frequently manifested in apostolic activity (cf., e.g., Acts 11:15–16), to such an extent that the history of the early Church could be described as the epic of the Holy Spirit, for the birth of local Churches was accompanied by the outpouring of the Spirit. Suffice it to recall how the Apostles speak at the Council of Jerusalem of the Spirit's action in the Church: *It has seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us...* (Acts 15:28). The election of Matthias (cf. Acts 1:15–26), that of Paul and Barnabas (cf. Acts 13:2), that of the deacons (cf. Acts 6:3–6), and the appointment of bishops (cf. Acts 20:28; 1 Tim 4:14; 2 Tim 1:6) are the work of the Holy Spirit. Christians are called temples of the Holy Spirit (1 Cor 3:16–17; 1 Cor 6:19); they are built together into a *dwelling place of God in the Spirit* (Eph 2:22).

The baptised have been *sanctified by the Spirit of our God* (1 Cor 6:11). This is the rebirth by water and the Spirit spoken of in Jn 3:5. Indeed, the baptised are regenerated through the action of the Holy Spirit, whom Jesus Christ *poured out upon us richly* (cf. Tit 3:4–7). It is the presence of the Spirit in the soul of the righteous person that confers that *spirit of sonship when we cry, "Abba! Father!"* (cf. Rom 8:15), for *God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us* (Rom 5:5). Consequently, it is necessary to live according to the Spirit and not according to the flesh (cf. Rom 8:9–11).

##### c) The Holy Spirit as Divine Person

In numerous New Testament passages, the Holy Spirit appears not only as a divine power operating in Jesus Christ, in the Church, or in Christians, but is furthermore described as "someone" distinct from the Father and the Son and intimately related to them.

In the Synoptics this appears clearly in a passage of key Christological and Trinitarian significance: the Baptism of Jesus. Indeed, in those accounts, the Holy Spirit, who descends in the form of a dove, is clearly distinguished from the Father and from the beloved Son (cf. Mk 1:9–11; Mt 3:13–17; Lk 3:21–22). This personal distinction appears with still greater clarity in the command to baptise *in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit* (cf. Mt 28:19).

The presence of the Holy Spirit is so constant in the Acts of the Apostles that it has been termed the Gospel of the Holy Spirit. Indeed, the book opens with the Ascension and Pentecost, and contains numerous passages speaking of the Spirit as a gift received through the laying on of hands and as the One from whom the charisms of the faithful spring. The disciples are frequently described as filled with the Holy Spirit. It is the Spirit who indicates how the expansion of the Church is to take place—for instance, telling Philip to join the chariot of the Ethiopian eunuch (Acts 8:29), or instructing Peter to go to the house of the centurion Cornelius (Acts 10:19–20); it is the Spirit who inspires the Apostles with the words they must speak when tried by the Sanhedrin (Acts 4:8). The "personality" of the Holy Spirit is clearly intimated in Acts 1:4 and Acts 2:33, in speaking of the sending of the Spirit, and in Acts 19:1–7, when those who had received John's baptism reply to Paul: *We have not even heard that there is a Holy Spirit*.

Still greater prominence belongs to those texts in which the Spirit appears as the subject of personal verbs such as to dwell, to search, to distinguish, and to will (1 Cor 2:10; 6:19; 12:11). And likewise those texts in which ternary formulas appear. Here are several: *The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of God and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all* (2 Cor 13:14); *Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of service, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of working, but it is the same God who inspires them all in every one* (1 Cor 12:4–6; cf. Eph 1:3–14).

In Saint John references are also found to the descent of the Spirit upon Jesus at His Baptism (cf. Jn 1:32–33). Jesus is *he who baptizes with the Holy Spirit*, it is said in Jn 1:33, referring perhaps to the outpouring of the Spirit that Jesus will bestow. A similar interpretation may apply to the allusion to being born of the Spirit in Jn 3:5–6.[^44]

It is in the Johannine account of the Last Supper that we encounter the most explicit texts concerning the Holy Spirit as a Person. Some of these texts have already been quoted: *I will pray the Father, and he will give you another Counselor, to be with you for ever, even the Spirit of truth, whom the world cannot receive, because it neither sees him nor knows him; you know him, for he dwells with you, and will be in you* (Jn 14:16–17). And slightly further on: *the Counselor, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name...* (Jn 14:26). He is a second envoy: as the Father sent Jesus, so now the risen Jesus will send the Spirit (cf. Jn 16:7); that is to say, He is an envoy distinct from the Son.

This distinction appears with special clarity in Jn 15:26: *When the Counselor comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, who proceeds from the Father, he will bear witness to me*. The text speaks of sending and of proceeding: it is the Son who sends—which could be understood of temporal mission alone—, whereas the Spirit proceeds from the Father, which theologians normally understand as referring to an eternal procession, that is, to the origin of the Holy Spirit. The Orthodox, who hold that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, interpret this text in the sense that the Son merely sends the Holy Spirit, without Himself being an origin of the Holy Spirit. For this reason, this passage is customary read in conjunction with another in which the Son–Spirit relationship appears with greater clarity: *When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth (...) He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and declare it to you. All that the Father has is mine; therefore I said that he will take what is mine and declare it to you* (Jn 16:13–15). Theologians note that, in order for the Son to send the Holy Spirit, it is necessary that the Spirit have a certain procession of origin with respect to Him.[^45] Indeed, Christ is here indicating that the Holy Spirit will bear witness to Him because He will take of what is His.

#### "Personality" of the Holy Spirit

As has been seen in the preceding paragraphs, the nature of the Holy Spirit as a Person in God, distinct from the Father and the Son, is progressively delineated in Sacred Scripture. His "personality"—His personal distinction with respect to the Father and the Son—is less clear to us than that of the Son. His mission is likewise less visible than that of the Son. Nevertheless, the testimony of Sacred Scripture as a whole—especially Jesus' farewell words—is sufficiently explicit to ground the Church's faith in the Holy Spirit as the third Person of the Most Holy Trinity.

The Spirit who already appears as the divine power sanctifying Mary (cf. Lk 1:35), who inaugurates Jesus' public ministry at His baptism (cf. Mk 1:9–11), and who accompanies Him constantly throughout this ministry to such a degree that to contradict Christ's work is to blaspheme against the Spirit (cf. Lk 12:10), is depicted in Acts as the One who sanctifies the Church and the faithful (cf., e.g., Acts 1:8; 2:1–13, etc.).

Especially important when discussing the "personality" of the Holy Spirit are the texts of the promise of the Spirit already cited: Jn 14:15–17; 15:26; 16:14. Other texts, read in the light of the Lord's farewell words, receive a new illumination, and many of them can be understood as referring to the Person of the Holy Spirit inasmuch as He acts with His sanctifying power in salvation history.

A strong intimation of the "personality" of the Holy Spirit is found in those passages that speak of His actions. It is said of the Holy Spirit that He dwells in the disciples (Jn 14:17); that He will be with them (Jn 14:17); that He comes (Jn 16:7–13); that He takes what belongs to Jesus (Jn 16:14); that He proceeds from the Father (Jn 15:26); that He hears (Jn 16:13); that He teaches (Jn 14:26); that He makes known (Jn 16:13); that He declares (Jn 16:13); that He glorifies Jesus (Jn 16:14); that He guides into all the truth (Jn 16:13); that He bears witness (Jn 15:26); that He convinces the world of sin (Jn 16:8); that He is given (Jn 14:16); that He is sent (Jn 14:26; 15:26; 16:7); that He intercedes for us before God (Rom 8:26); that He bears witness with our spirit (Rom 8:16); that He apportions charisms as He wills (1 Cor 12:11); that He speaks in the Old Testament Scriptures (Heb 3:7; 1 Pet 1:11; 2 Pet 1:21), etc.[^46]

This is strongly personalising language and imagery which, joined to the Trinitarian profession of faith made in baptism (cf. Mt 28:19), led the Church of the early centuries to an ever sharper comprehension of the personality of the Holy Spirit. Here is how Saint Gregory of Nazianzus summarises the path traversed by divine pedagogy in revealing the "personality" of the Holy Spirit: "The Old Testament proclaimed the Father clearly, but the Son more darkly. The New Testament plainly revealed the Son, and hinted at the divinity of the Holy Spirit. Now the Spirit Himself dwells among us, and supplies us with a clearer demonstration of Himself. For it was not safe, when the Godhead of the Father was not yet acknowledged, plainly to proclaim the Son; nor when that of the Son was not yet received to burden us further with the Holy Spirit—if we may use so bold an utterance (...) But rather that by gradual additions, and, as David says, Goings up, and advances and progress from glory to glory, the light of the Trinity might shine upon the more illuminated."[^47]

#### The Holy Spirit, Sent by the Father and the Son

As we have seen, the majority of New Testament passages speaking of the Holy Spirit do so in the context of His mission to the Apostles and the Church. In these passages, this sending is attributed at times to the Father, and at other times to the Son. In turn, the manner of this sending, of this mission, reflects the personal characteristics of the Holy Spirit. As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states: "The eternal origin of the Holy Spirit is revealed in his mission in time. The Spirit is sent to the apostles and to the Church both by the Father in the name of the Son, and by the Son in person, once he had returned to the Father (cf. Jn 14:26; 15:26; 16:14). The sending of the person of the Spirit after Jesus' glorification (cf. Jn 7:39) reveals in its fullness the mystery of the Holy Trinity."[^48]

This is seen, for example, in the passage where the sending of the Spirit is attributed to the Father: *I will pray the Father, and he will give you another Counselor, to be with you for ever, even the Spirit of truth (...). The Counselor, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name, he will teach you all things* (Jn 14:16–17, 26). The Holy Spirit, who will remain eternally with and in the Apostles, is described here as the Spirit of Truth, who leads man to the knowledge of the deep things of God—God is Truth—, and He is sent by the Father in the name of the Son.

In the following passage, by contrast, the sending of the Spirit is attributed to the Son: *When the Counselor comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father, even the Spirit of truth, who proceeds from the Father, he will bear witness to me* (Jn 15:26). This sending of the Spirit by Jesus suggests that the Holy Spirit also proceeds from Him, as stated in the text previously quoted: the Father sends the Spirit who proceeds from Him. In noting that Jesus sends the Spirit, "John reclaims the traditional truth stemming from Isaiah (Is 11:2; 61:1) according to which the Spirit is the proper gift of the Messiah, who brings justice to the islands without growing faint or discouraged, without breaking a bruised reed or quenching a smouldering wick."[^49]

*I tell you the truth: it is to your advantage that I go away, for if I do not go away, the Counselor will not come to you; but if I go, I will send him to you. And when he comes...* (Jn 16:7–8). The Son sends the Spirit, who is depicted with the attributes proper to a person. Indeed, although *pneuma* is grammatically neuter, this *pneuma* is spoken of with masculine pronouns: *And when he comes...* (*ekeinos*).[^50] The significant practice of using the masculine rather than the neuter pronoun to refer to the Holy Spirit is repeated in Jn 16:13 and 14:26.[^51]

The sending of the Spirit both by the Father and by the Son points to the truth that the Spirit proceeds not from the Father alone, but from the Father and the Son. The formulas expressing this sending, as we have just seen, are diverse; yet from them emerges the communion of the Father and the Son in the origin of the Spirit. "The Spirit will be sent by the Father in the name of Jesus. The formula indicates the perfect communion between the Father and the Son in the sending of the Spirit. The Father is, without doubt, the source of this mission: therefore the Son will send the Spirit from beside the Father (Jn 15:26). But the Son is also a principle of this sending: hence the Father will send Him in the name of the Son."[^52]

#### The Joint Mission of the Son and the Spirit

The salvation of mankind takes place in their incorporation into Christ through the Spirit. To express it in another way: salvation consists in our being made children of God in Christ by the Holy Spirit. The mission of the Holy Spirit is so intimately linked to the mystery of the Incarnation—to the mission of the Son—that on occasion both missions are characterised as a single joint mission. This is done by the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*: "When the Father sends his Word, he always sends his Breath. In their joint mission, the Son and the Holy Spirit are distinct but inseparable. To be sure, it is Christ who is seen, the visible image of the invisible God, but it is the Spirit who reveals him. Jesus is Christ, 'anointed,' because the Spirit is his anointing, and everything that occurs from the Incarnation on derives from this fullness (cf. Jn 3:34). When Christ is finally glorified (Jn 7:39), he can in turn send the Spirit from his place with the Father to those who believe in him: he communicates to them his glory (cf. Jn 17:22), that is, the Holy Spirit who glorifies him (cf. Jn 16:14). The joint mission will from then on be displayed in the children adopted by the Father in the Body of his Son: the mission of the Spirit of adoption is to unite them to Christ and make them live in him."[^53]

Commenting upon Jn 16:12–14, John Paul II writes: "Between the Holy Spirit and Christ there subsists, therefore, in the economy of salvation, an intimate relationship, whereby the Spirit acts in human history as *another Counselor*, permanently ensuring the transmission and irradiation of the Good News revealed by Jesus of Nazareth (...) The supreme and complete self-revelation of God, accomplished in Christ and attested by the preaching of the Apostles, continues to be manifested in the Church through the mission of the invisible Paraclete, the Spirit of truth. How intimately this mission is related to the mission of Christ, and how fully it is grounded upon it, consolidating and developing in history its salvific fruits, is expressed by the verb *to receive*: *he will take what is mine and declare it to you* (Jn 16:14)."[^54]

Given this close relationship of the Spirit with the Father and with the Son, in Sacred Scripture the Holy Spirit is designated at times as the Spirit of the Father or who proceeds from the Father (cf. Mt 10:20; Jn 15:26), and also as the Spirit of the Son (cf. Gal 4:6), the Spirit of Christ (cf. Rom 8:9), and the Spirit of the Lord (2 Cor 3:17). On other occasions He is designated as the Spirit of God (Rom 8:9, 14; 15:19; 1 Cor 6:11; 7:40). Finally, He receives as His proper name that of *Holy Spirit*. "The Church has received this name from the Lord and professes it in the Baptism of her new children (cf. Mt 28:19). This is the name most frequently employed in the Acts of the Apostles and in the apostolic epistles."[^55]

### Trinitarian Expressions in the New Testament

In the preceding sections, the New Testament texts speaking of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit separately have been analysed. We now turn to these same texts and to several others inasmuch as they enumerate the three divine Persons together, thereby constituting the scriptural foundation of Trinitarian theology.

#### The Baptism of Jesus

The earliest explicit texts that name the three Persons simultaneously are found in the narrative of the Lord's Baptism (Mt 3:13–17; Mk 1:9–11; Lk 3:21–22; Jn 1:32–34). In these texts, witness is borne to Jesus as the Son of God in a real and full sense, the Father is mentioned, whose voice resounds from heaven, and the Holy Spirit appears descending upon Christ. As Jesus came up from the water, the heavens were opened and the Spirit of God (Mt) or the Holy Spirit (Lk) was seen descending upon Him like a dove, and a voice from heaven said: *Thou art my beloved Son; with thee I am well pleased*. At the moment when His public preaching as Messiah is about to begin, Jesus receives this solemn declaration consecrating Him before the Baptist and onlookers as the One who would baptise with fire and with the Holy Spirit (Mt 3:11; Lk 3:16).

Here the three Persons are manifested as distinct. There is no doubt that the One who speaks and the One addressed stand in the relationship of Father and Son, while the Holy Spirit, who descends in the bodily form of a dove (Lk 3:22), is above all at the service of the proclamation of the Messiah and alludes to His anointing by the Spirit (cf. Is 11:2; Acts 10:38; Lk 4:18).[^56]

#### The Missionary Mandate of Matthew 28:19

In the Synoptic Gospels, the most explicit text concerning the Trinitarian mystery is the baptismal formula contained in Mt 28:19: *make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit*. In this text the distinction of the divine Persons appears with clarity, since the names Father, Son, and Holy Spirit each carry the definite article that personalises them. The opposition between the Father and the Son provides the key to understanding the Holy Spirit in a strictly personal sense, that is, as distinct from Father and Son. The expression *to baptize in the name* is a clear designation of personal being. And because all three Persons are designated on an equal footing, it is implied that the three are equal.[^57]

This text, of such paramount importance as an explicit synthesis of the Trinitarian mystery, is regarded today by the majority of exegetes as summarising the baptismal practice of the early Church—a practice guided by the Spirit of Jesus Christ and, therefore, authorised by Jesus. Indeed, the text represents no novelty, but synthesises the Trinitarian structure of the Synoptic tradition and of the entire New Testament; it expresses, therefore, the totality of the soteriological reality applied to us in baptism: we are made children of God in the Son through the Holy Spirit.[^58]

From the standpoint of theological content, the passages recounting Jesus' baptism and the missionary mandate illuminate one another. Both point to Christian baptism. It must furthermore be kept in mind that, in the execution of the missionary mandate, Jesus' baptism had an exemplary significance for Christian baptism. Whence, reciprocally, Christ's baptism is illuminated by the missionary mandate.

The earliest records of Christian baptism preserved in the New Testament describe it as baptising *in the name of Jesus Christ* (cf. Acts 2:38; 10:48) or *in the name of the Lord Jesus* (Acts 8:16; 19:5; cf. Rom 6:3). Between this formula and the more explicit formula of baptising *in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit* there is no essential difference, for baptism in the name of Jesus places the neophyte in an indissoluble relationship not only with the Lord Jesus Christ, but also with God and with the Holy Spirit. Consequently, the Trinitarian baptismal formula can be well understood as a "synthesis of baptismal catechesis that amplifies and enriches the original invocation of the name of Jesus by means of the ideas of divine sonship and, correlatively, of the fatherhood of God and the invocation of the Spirit. Between the baptismal formula thus understood and the Synoptic baptismal account there is a connection at the level of the history of tradition."[^59]

#### The Transfiguration

The scene of the Lord's Transfiguration also appears related to the Trinitarian theophany of baptism (Mt 17:1–13; Mk 9:1–12; Lk 9:28–36). In it, for a moment, Jesus manifests His glory while displaying His condition as Servant of Yahweh: Jesus' face and garments became dazzling as the light, Moses and Elijah appeared speaking of His departure from this world (cf. Lk 9:31), and a voice came out of the cloud, saying: *This is my Son, my Chosen; listen to him!* (Lk 9:35). This is an expression symmetrical with that found in the baptism account, denoting the union—and distinction—between Father and Son. The cloud intimates the presence of the Holy Spirit. For this reason, tradition has likewise seen in this passage a revelation of the Most Holy Trinity. As Saint Thomas writes, "the whole Trinity appeared: the Father in the voice, the Son in the man, the Holy Spirit in the shining cloud."[^60]

Between the Baptism and the Transfiguration of Jesus there is, moreover, a clear theological link: Baptism stands on the threshold of His public life; the Transfiguration on the threshold of His Pasch. By Jesus' baptism the mystery of the first regeneration was manifested, namely our baptism; by the Transfiguration our second regeneration was manifested, namely our resurrection.[^61]

#### Johannine Passages

In Saint John passages are likewise found that present a triadic conception of God, particularly those texts of the Last Supper in which Jesus speaks of sending the Spirit. This triadic conception occurs even in passages referring to the structure of faith and, therefore, in passages not directly related to the baptismal institution, which adds a fresh perspective:[^62] *But the Counselor, the Holy Spirit, whom the Father will send in my name [that of the Son]...* (Jn 14:26); *When the Counselor comes, whom I shall send to you from the Father...* (Jn 15:26).

In Jesus' Priestly Prayer, the Persons of the Father and the Son appear closely united: eternal life consists precisely in knowing Them (cf. Jn 17:3, 21), but this recognition of God's glory can only take place in the Spirit (Jn 14:16–17). "The unity between Father and Son becomes, then, through the Spirit, the foundation of the unity of believers, a unity that must be a sign for the world (Jn 17:21). The revelation of eternal love is like the gathering of the scattered flock under one shepherd (Jn 10:16). The unity, peace, and life of the world are produced, accordingly, through the revelation of the glory of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. Trinitarian doxology is the soteriology of the world."[^63]

In the Johannine epistles triadic formulas also appear: *All who keep his commandments abide in him, and he in them. And by this we know that he abides in us, by the Spirit which he has given us* (1 Jn 3:24; cf. also 1 Jn 4:11–16).

#### Pauline Formulas

So numerous are the Pauline passages in which Trinitarian faith is clearly intimated that it may be stated that Pauline thought is wholly determined by Trinitarian faith.[^64] At the same time, the express mention of the Three almost always occurs in relation to the history of human salvation.

It is in Christ—in the Son—that men have access to the Father through the Holy Spirit, for *through him we both have access in one Spirit to the Father* (Eph 2:18). Christ, indeed, is the perfect image of the invisible God (cf. Col 1:15), that is, an image of the same rank as the reality it reflects. When the world did not yet exist, the Son already was. The fact that all things were created through Christ and for Christ (cf. Col 1:16–17) is likewise expressed by the confession *Jesus is Lord* (Rom 10:9). And it is the Spirit of Jesus who gathers and sanctifies the Church, crying out in our hearts with the cry proper to that divine Sonship with which Jesus prayed: *Abba! Father!* (cf. Rom 8:14–16).

Among the numerous Pauline passages, three are customarily cited as especially significant in their triadic expression:

**2 Thess 2:13–14:** *We are bound to give thanks to God always for you, brethren beloved by the Lord, because God chose you from the beginning to be saved, through sanctification by the Spirit and belief in the truth. To this he called you through our gospel, so that you may obtain the glory of our Lord Jesus Christ.*

It is God the Father who has chosen the saints from eternity. This refers to the Christian vocation. The goal of that vocation is identification with the Lord, sharing in His glory. The Spirit is manifestly the Holy Spirit, who brings about sanctification, that is, identification with Jesus Christ.

**2 Cor 13:14:** *The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of God and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all.*

This is the conclusion of the Epistle and the culmination of the exhortations with which Paul takes his leave. It is a formula plainly employed in the liturgy. Grace is a gift that comes from Christ; love comes from God, who has so loved the world that He gave His only Son along with every good; these divine gifts are distributed to us by the Holy Spirit, who is the gift that accompanies this grace and produces it. This formula distinguishes the three divine Persons and, at the same time, intimates their equality, in the sense that each contributes to the common work of human salvation. Christ is distinguished from the Father and from the Holy Spirit; all three stand on the same line as the origin and source of the Christian's sanctification.[^65]

**1 Cor 12:4–6:** *Now there are varieties of gifts, but the same Spirit; and there are varieties of service, but the same Lord; and there are varieties of working, but it is the same God who inspires them all in every one.*

This text attains its deepest dimension when read in the context of 2 Cor 13:14, cited above. Spirit, Lord, and God appear juxtaposed, and to Them are attributed the charisms, ministries, and workings received by the community. Saint Paul is speaking of the diversity of gifts and charisms, and of the unity of all in God. It is debated whether these three terms (gifts, services, workings) signify distinct realities or whether the three nouns refer to a single reality considered under diverse aspects to relate it to the Trinity. In any case, it seems clear that Saint Paul mentions the three divine Persons here as one more manifestation of the triadic formulation so habitual in his writings.[^66]

**Eph 4:4–6:** *There is one body and one Spirit, just as you were called to the one hope that belongs to your call, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of us all, who is above all and through all and in all.*

This text is parallel to 1 Cor 12:4–6, in which Saint Paul, mindful of the diversity of charisms existing in the community, underlines the unity that must prevail, arguing for it on the basis of the unity of God. According to this reasoning, the unifying principle of the ecclesial community is God the Father of all Himself, who mapped out the plan of redemption unfolded throughout history, reaching its culmination with the sending of His Son—the Lord—and the outpouring of the Spirit. The allusion to the oneness of baptism further accentuates the Trinitarian dimension of the text, which is embedded in an epistle whose opening is likewise Trinitarian.[^67] The enumeration of the three Persons follows the same order as 1 Cor 12:4–6: first, the Holy Spirit, who is like the soul enlivening the ecclesial Body; then one Lord, head of this Body and therefore principle of unity; finally, God the Father, to whom the plan of salvation itself is attributed and who is consequently the supreme unifying principle in the Trinitarian salvific schema.[^68]

**Gal 4:6:** *And because you are sons, God has sent the Spirit of his Son into our hearts, crying, "Abba! Father!"*

The text highlights our divine sonship in Christ, which is brought about by the Father sending the Spirit of His Son into our hearts. The three Persons appear here in their joint intervention in the work of salvation and in their distinction from one another.

* * *

In summary: Throughout our survey of the most explicit texts concerning the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity, it has become evident that, in their affirmations about God, the New Testament hagiographers think of the one God of Old Testament revelation, and yet the Christian conception of God contains a radical novelty with respect to the Old Testament understanding. What is new in the New Testament conception of God does not represent an evolution of the concept of God, but emerges by virtue of a new revelation. In Jesus of Nazareth, God speaks and acts in the world in so direct a manner that Jesus is called the Son of God in the proper and strong sense (cf. Heb 1:1–3). Jesus thereby introduces an entirely new feature into the Old Testament divine image: He calls God His Father and invites us—united to Him—likewise to call Him Father in a new way. Jesus Himself, at the end of His earthly life, promises the Spirit of truth who proceeds from the Father, and whom He will send from the Father. Moreover, Jesus commands His Apostles to make disciples and baptise them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit (cf. Mt 28:19). The new existence of the Christian rests, therefore, upon a mysterious event that, in the faith of the early Church, was already articulated in a triadic formula: faith in the Father, in the Son, and in the Holy Spirit, one and only God.[^69]

The preaching of Our Lord Jesus Christ, as reflected in the Synoptic Gospels, has as its object faith in and love for the heavenly Father. The Kingdom of God is first and foremost the kingdom of a God who is Father: Father of men, and Father also of Jesus Christ in an exceptional and unique manner.[^70] This fatherhood of God occupies the centre of Christian reflection on God. As has been noted, "in early Christian language Christ is rarely called God directly. Even when Jesus is associated with the Father in His work properly so-called, the name of God is reserved for the Father. Christ is the Lord, which in certain contexts is another way of saying that He is God."[^71] Here is an eloquent Pauline text: *for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist* (1 Cor 8:6).

The Incarnation of the Son of God reveals that God is the eternal Father.[^72] It is in this natural Sonship of the Son to the Father that the mystery of God's intimate life is manifested, which is, in the first place, the mystery of the Father, who is *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*. Jesus Christ is God because He is the natural Son of the Father, that is, He is God because He is true Son of the Father. And at the same time, it is in this eternal Sonship that the fatherhood of the Father is revealed to us, who is Father precisely in begetting the Son. This gives priority to the Father, not of time—God is eternal—nor of causality—in God there is nothing caused—, but of pure origin. "The Father is par excellence the principle, the origin of that interior, infinite dynamism which is the very essence of the Godhead. A dynamism not of causality, but of communication."[^73]
