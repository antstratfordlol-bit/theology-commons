## Chapter 28: The 'Perichoresis' or 'Circuminsessio' {#mateo-seco-trino.28}

The study of the processions and relations leads us towards the consideration of the oneness of God, which is manifested in the mutual immanence of the divine Persons: the *circuminsessio* or *perichoresis*. Indeed, the Persons are distinguished solely by their opposition of relation; in all else they are one and the same God, a single substance, a single essence.

The Trinitarian confession of faith therefore implies the confession of the mutual immanence of the Persons and of their real distinction. They are three Persons in a numerically identical nature. There is no confusion of the Persons with one another, but unity of essence; yet this unity of essence entails that there is no division among the Persons—that is, the fact that they in-exist in one another. The same truth, albeit conversely, is affirmed in the Christological confession: in Christ there are two natures united in a single Person, without confusion, yet without separation—that is, without watertight compartments.[^1]

To speak of the unity of the divine Persons is equivalent to affirming that one Person exists in the other two. It cannot be otherwise, if it is maintained in earnest that the Three are one and the same God. It is not that the divine Persons have the same substance, but that they *are* the same substance; it is not that they participate in that substance, but that each is, at the same time, the whole of the most simple divine substance. And since the divine substance is nothing other than God's infinite and most simple act of being, the mutual immanence of the Persons signifies nothing less than the most perfect communion of the act of being and of life—the most simple act of knowledge and of love—in which He who is essentially Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:16) unfolds.

Our Lord described His union with the Father in the most vigorous words and expressions to be found in human language: *The Father and I are one* (Jn 10:30); *The Father is in me and I am in the Father* (Jn 10:38); *Philip (...) he who has seen me has seen the Father; how can you say, "Show us the Father"? Believe me that I am in the Father and the Father in me* (Jn 14:9, 11); *That they may all be one, even as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee* (Jn 17:21). There is unity—*immansio*, *inhabitatio*—between the Father and the Son in being and in acting. Something similar, albeit in a less explicit manner, is said of the Holy Spirit: *For God has revealed to us through the Spirit, for the Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God. For what person knows a man's thoughts except the spirit of the man which is in him?* (1 Cor 2:10–11).

### The Concept of Perichoresis

Our Lord's words concerning His unity with the Father must be taken in all their radicality; in them He affirms not only His indissoluble moral unity with the Father, but His authentic identity in the act of being: He and the Father are identical in substance. The otherness (*alteritas*) that follows from the relation of begetter-begotten takes place without separation or division between them. In begetting the Son, the Father does not give Him a part of His substance, but gives Him the whole substance that He Himself is. The Holy Spirit, the bond of love between Father and Son, dwells in the depths of God, that is, in the innermost depths of divine intimacy.

Theology attempts to express this profound unity by means of the concept of mutual in-existence or in-dwelling. The Greeks employ the term *perichoresis*; the Latins, *circuminsessio*, mutual indwelling (*inhabitatio*), or mutual immanence (*immanentia*).

Etymologically, *perichoreo* signifies circular or rotational motion in the same place. It therefore implies sameness and differentiation. It is primarily a dynamic concept, which also possesses a static meaning. It was first utilised to designate the unity of the two natures in Christ. The theological problem was to safeguard noetically the affirmation that Jesus Christ is one single Lord. This unity takes place in Someone who possesses two abysmally different natures: the divine nature and the human nature, and who acts through both. Both natures constitute a single Lord because they are united to such a degree that it can be affirmed of them that, without ceasing to be distinct, the two natures indwell one another in an ineffable *perichoresis*, by virtue of the single Subject upon whom they rest.

It was exceedingly difficult to express this reality. Nevertheless, the Fathers lacked neither faith, nor theological audacity, nor linguistic precision to express it in the least inaccurate manner possible. The expressions employed are countless: from the nuptials of the Word with human nature, to the entire range of terms used to refer to the blending of liquids: *krasis*, *anakrasis*, *synkrasis*. The four adverbs of the Council of Chalcedon are a clear exponent of the effort to defend simultaneously the unity and the difference of the natures in Christ.[^2]

The concept of *perichoresis* was employed with the intention of highlighting the intimate unity of Christ. It is used to ground the communication of idioms (*communicatio idiomatum*), that is, to express the foundation whereby human attributes and divine attributes are predicated of the being and activity of Jesus Christ. A *perichoresis* occurs between the natures because there exists between both natures a *krasis* or *synkrasis*, an intimate union. In this sense it was used by St Gregory of Nazianzus († 390), St Maximus the Confessor († 662), Pseudo-Cyril, and St John Damascene (ca. 750).[^3]

The Damascene is the first to have employed the concept of *perichoresis* in Trinitarian theology. By that time the concept was already well delineated through its extensive usage in Christology:

> "The permanence and dwelling of one of the three Persons in the other signifies that they are inseparable, that they cannot be separated, and that they possess an interpenetration without confusion. They do not merge and blend with one another, but are mutually joined. That is to say, the Son is in the Father and in the Spirit, and the Spirit is in the Father and in the Son, and the Father is in the Son and in the Spirit, without any fusion, mingling, or confusion taking place. The motion is one and identical, since the impulse and movement of the three Persons is unique, something that cannot be observed in created nature."[^4]

Here we find gathered that conceptual acuity which was required to refute Nestorianism and Monophysitism: unity without confusion; distinction without division. Many of the expressions employed by the Damascene evoke those used by the Council of Chalcedon. The Damascene echoes, moreover, the primitive dynamic meaning of the term, a dynamism that proves singularly suited to show that the mutual indwelling of the Persons is not something static, but something infinitely vital.

The Latin Fathers likewise employed similar expressions to formulate this question, although lacking a technical term that might impart agility to their formulations. Such a term would only be found in Scholasticism as a translation of the Greek term *perichoresis*;[^5] yet the doctrinal content is already present in St Hilary:

> "What is in the Father is also in the Son (...); the one is derived from the other, and the two are one single thing (...). They are mutually in one another (*in se invicem*)."[^6]

> "Each of the other; and not both one, but one in the other."[^7]

> "God in God, because He is God of God."[^8]

St Augustine expounds this matter immediately after treating the question of the divine properties. Having pointed out the distinction of Persons, he returns to the unity existing among them:

> "In corporeal things, a single entity is not the same as three together, and two are more than one. In the supreme Trinity, by contrast, one person is equal to the other two, and two are not greater than a single one of them, and in themselves they are infinite. And each of them is in each of the others, and all in one, and one in all, and all in all, and all are one."[^9]

And in a more dynamic sense, he writes:

> "In these three, because the mind knows itself and loves itself, the triad constituted by the mind, love, and knowledge is preserved without any mixture, even though each of these things is its own being and is, with respect to the others, in each of the others, wholly and as a whole, whether each of them is in the other two, or any two are in one of them, so that all is in all."[^10]

St Bonaventure uses the term *circuminsessio* to refer to this doctrine that is unanimous throughout Christianity. The doctrine of *circuminsessio* in being posits distinction and unity simultaneously: "only in God is there the highest unity with distinction, so that this distinction without confusion and this unity without separation are possible."[^11]

As can be seen, in Scholasticism as well, the point of reference for *circuminsessio* continues to be its original Christological usage, among other reasons because Trinitarian discourse seeks the very same objective as Christological discourse: to manifest unity in distinction.

### The Mutual Immanence of the Persons

All Trinitarian concepts converge upon this intimate and indissoluble unity among the divine Persons. In a mysterious manner, without being confused, Father, Son, and Spirit are in one another, for they are one and the same God. The interpenetration of the three divine Persons is nothing other than the consequence of the oneness of substance existing among them, just as the *perichoresis* existing between Christ's human and divine natures is the consequence of the unity of the one and identical Christ.[^12]

This mutual indwelling takes place under every aspect in which the divine Persons may be considered; it occurs whether they are considered in their essence—the one divine essence, which is an infinite act of knowledge and of love—or whether they are considered in their processions or in their relations. St Thomas formulated this truth with admirable clarity, taking as his guiding thread the unity between the Father and the Son:

> "In the Father and the Son three things must be considered: the essence, the relations, and the origin; and according to each of these, the Son is in the Father and the Father in the Son. As regards the essence, the Father is in the Son, because the Father is His essence and communicates His essence to the Son without any change (...) If we consider the relations, we know that either of two relatively opposed terms enters into the concept of the other (...) According to origin, it is likewise clear that the procession of the intelligible word does not terminate outside, and similarly what is spoken in the word is contained within it.
> 
> "And the same reasons are applicable when speaking of the Holy Spirit."[^13]

The doctrine of *circuminsessio* is explicitly taught by the Council of Florence, which presents it as the expression of the unity of the Trinity:

> "These three Persons are one God and not three gods: because the three possess one and the same substance, a single essence, a single nature, a single divinity, a single immensity, a single eternity (...) Because of this unity the Father is wholly in the Son and wholly in the Holy Spirit; the Son is wholly in the Father and wholly in the Holy Spirit; and the Holy Spirit is wholly in the Father and in the Son."[^14]

The one God is the three Persons, and at the same time each of the Persons is wholly God. So to speak, there is no more divinity in the three Persons together than in one alone, since there is no perfection in one that is not in the other. The only difference is that one Person is not the other; but this difference lies exclusively in the opposition of relation, not in the essence or substance.

### Importance of the Perichoresis in Trinitarian Theology

The affirmation of the mutual indwelling of the divine Persons belongs to the faith of the Church. It is clearly present in Sacred Scripture, and is taught, among others, by the Eleventh Council of Toledo,[^15] the Fourth Lateran Council,[^16] and the Council of Florence.[^17]

Theological reasoning leads without any doubt to the same conclusion: if the Persons are the same divine essence—the whole divine essence—it is necessary that they be in one another, since they are distinguished from one another solely by the relation of opposition, which introduces no division whatsoever into the substance. The Three possess the same substance in an undivided manner. The *perichoresis* is nothing other than the expression of the unity of substance in God.

Furthermore, the *perichoresis* demonstrates the infinite perfection of generation and spiration in God. In begetting the Son, the Father communicates to Him His own substance with such perfection that the Son possesses the very same substance as the Father in numerical identity. The same holds true for the procession of the Spirit.

Were the *perichoresis* to be denied, it would be impossible to affirm in earnest the unity of the divine essence. One could at most defend a generic equality among the Persons. But such generic equality is neither possible in God, nor is it sufficient for believing coherently in the mystery of the Trinity. St Thomas was well aware of this. For this reason, he resists speaking of one absolute existence in God and three relative ones. That way of speaking seemed to him incompatible with the radicality with which the divine *perichoresis* must be taken. Since there is but one single essence—and in God essence and act of being (*esse*) are identical—there is only one single existence (*esse*).

As has been repeated on numerous occasions, Latin theologians typically take as their starting point the unity of the divine substance or essence, and from there proceed to the study of the Persons. The *perichoresis* is treated at the conclusion of this long road as a consequence of the unity of substance. In the Greek structuring of Trinitarian theology, the *perichoresis* is of even greater importance, since the Greeks generally do not begin from the unity of essence or substance, but from the threefold personal reality: the Father, unoriginate principle (*principium sine principio* / *anarchos arche*), communicates His very substance to the Son and to the Holy Spirit. Hence they treat the *perichoresis* not merely as a necessary consequence of the unity of substance already explicitly affirmed at the outset, but as one of the most viable pathways for establishing that unity of substance. The path is thus traversed in reverse order: the *perichoresis* is affirmed first—the scriptural texts being sufficiently explicit—and from there one ascends to the affirmation of the unity of the divine essence.[^18]

The *circuminsessio* is an expression of the oneness of the divine substance. It is, so to speak, its inseparable splendour. Hence, in an orderly conceptualisation, it ought not to be considered in dependence upon the divine immensity, but upon the unity of substance. According to human discourse, the mutual immanence of the Persons does not occur because the divine substance is uncircumscribable, but because the three Persons possess one and the same substance.[^19]

The concept of *perichoresis* is pivotal in theology, for by means of it the joint signification of unity and diversity is facilitated: a unity that takes place without mingling or confusion. These characteristics rendered its use exceptionally fruitful in Christology. From there it was transferred into the Trinitarian domain, where it proves of paramount importance for expressing the mystery of the divine unity and Trinity. The Persons are one and the same God, indwelling one another, yet without any mixture or confusion existing between them.

The doctrine of the *perichoresis* likewise has much to say in the anthropological sphere—in the dialogical realisation of the human person—and in the ecclesiological sphere. Not in vain is man the image of God, and the Church the icon of the Trinity. It was Our Lord Himself who directed our gaze towards this mysterious relation between Trinitarian unity and the unity of Christians: *That they may all be one; even as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be in us, so that the world may believe that thou hast sent me* (Jn 17:21).
