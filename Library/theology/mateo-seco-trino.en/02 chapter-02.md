## Chapter 2: The Features of the God of the Covenant {#mateo-seco-trino.2}

If God exists and is a personal being, He must necessarily—according to our mode of understanding—possess His own proper characteristics that distinguish Him from other beings. Sacred Scripture offers certain traits of what God is like, traits that set Him apart from all created reality. Nevertheless, one finds in Scripture neither a systematic enumeration of these divine characteristics, attributes, or perfections, nor are these attributes presented as the outcome of personal deduction. They are disclosed gradually, to the extent that God's interventions in history are recounted, for it is precisely in this measure that God makes Himself known.

It should be borne in mind that, given the infinite divine perfection and simplicity, God's attributes are not distinct from the divine nature. Yet man possesses only an analogical knowledge of God: *for from the greatness and beauty of created things their original author, by way of analogy, is seen* (Wis 13:5); consequently, man has no other path for speaking of what He is like save by enumerating His perfections. This is likewise what occurs in Sacred Scripture, wherein God speaks to men in human language.

Now, in the created sphere, the attributes or properties of creatures designate qualities inhering in them as in a subject. Thus one can say that man *has* life, wisdom, love. Those selfsame perfections are found in God in an entirely different manner: they do not reside in Him as in a subject; rather, God *is* those perfections: God is Life, God is Love, God is Wisdom. Saint Augustine expressed it in this way: *(Deus) quod habet hoc est*—God is that which He has.[^1] These divine properties or attributes spoken of in Sacred Scripture are really identical with the divine essence—God is not only good, but is goodness itself—and, given the simplicity of God, they are likewise identical with one another: God's holiness is His justice; His justice is also His mercy. This does not mean, however, that when speaking of God our concepts are identical with one another; for although these perfections are really identified in God, when we speak of them we employ human concepts, which are limited, and since they are limited, these perfections thus conceived cannot be identical with the divine being, either in our subjective thought and language or in their objective signification, which in turn is conditioned from our human vantage point. For this reason, the affirmation that concerning God "we cannot know what He is, but rather what He is not" is universal.[^2]

This is why it is essential to observe the law of analogy, lest we turn the divine attributes into formulas purporting to express adequately what God is. "Indeed, it is legitimate to say *God is infinite*, but the infinite reality of God is only analogous to mathematical infinity: it is not the same boundlessness of numbers or mathematical magnitudes; it is legitimate to say that *God is eternal*, but the eternity of God is not equivalent to an immensely long span of time that will never end (...) For this reason it is dangerous to conceive theological realities without taking into account analogy, with its three moments: the affirmation of qualities in God; the removal (*remotio*) of all that is creaturely—limited or imperfect—; and eminence (*eminentia*), an act of the intellect whereby we declare the human terms designating divine qualities to be open and without bounds."[^3]

### The Omnipotence of God

The omnipotence of Yahweh is highlighted from the opening verses of the Book of Genesis. Indeed, already in the creation of the world God's power over all things is splendidly manifested, for everything that exists outside Himself has been created by Him, and everything that exists is conserved by Him in being (cf. Gen 1; Ps 19; 33:6; 104; Job 38–40). God has created the universe by His omnipotent word alone (Ps 33:6); this word never returns to Him empty, but always accomplishes its purpose (Is 55:11). It is God who governs the world and preserves it, for He "brings out their host by number, calling them all by name; by the greatness of his might, and because he is strong in power, not one is missing" (Is 40:26).

The omnipotence of Yahweh is displayed with particular force in the manner in which He delivers the chosen people. God always—and effortlessly—vanquishes the enemies of Israel: *The Lord is a warrior; the Lord is his name* (Ex 15:3); *Sing to the Lord, for he has triumphed gloriously; the horse and his rider he has thrown into the sea* (Ex 15:1). When God intervenes on behalf of His people, everything yields before His will; the earth trembles, and even the elements—the sea, the clouds, the sun—are placed at His service: *Lord, when thou didst go forth from Seir, when thou didst march from the region of Edom, the earth trembled, and the heavens dropped, yea, the clouds dropped water. The mountains quaked before the Lord, yon Sinai before the Lord, the God of Israel* (Judg 5:4–5). For the Bible, the miracles of the Exodus—for example, the manna, the quails, the water gushing from the rock, the victories over their enemies (cf. Ex 16–17)—stand as clear proofs of divine omnipotence. The sapiential and prophetic literature will reflect unceasingly upon this power of God, exalting its boundless character.

There is no place beyond the reach of God's power, even the very abyss, Sheol itself. This is a crucial clarification, placing the whole of biblical teaching at the antipodes of dualism. There is nothing—not even the powers of evil—that escapes the power of God: *The shades tremble beneath the waters and the inhabitants thereof. Sheol is naked before God, and Abaddon has no covering. He stretches out the north over the void, and hangs the earth upon nothing (...) By his wind the heavens were made fair; his hand pierced the fleeing serpent. Lo, these are but the outskirts of his ways; and how small a whisper do we hear of him! But the thunder of his power who can understand?* (Job 26:5–14). Divine omnipotence is made manifest in all that God does and embraces everything: creation, the governance of peoples, judgements upon men—in other words, all that pertains to nature and history. It is a power exercised with wisdom, ordering all things *fortiter et suaviter*, with strength and gentleness (cf. Wis 8:1). It is God who, by His power, makes Sarah fruitful (Gen 18:14), and who subsequently will grant Holy Mary a virginal motherhood (Lk 1:37); He gives life and death, health and disease: *See now that I, even I, am he, and there is no god beside me; I kill and I make alive; I wound and I heal; and there is none that can deliver out of my hand* (Deut 32:39; cf. Hos 13:14; Is 66:9). Even the hearts of men are in His hands: *O Lord, Lord, Almighty King, for all things are in your power, and there is none that can resist you if you will to save Israel. For you have made heaven and earth, and every wondrous thing under heaven. You are Lord of all, and there is none that can resist your majesty* (Esth 13:9–11 [Vulg.]).

In the Old Testament, divine omnipotence is designated especially by figures representing the power of the word God utters, both in regard to creation (Gen 1:1–2; Ps 148:5) and in regard to history (Is 55:11); by the power of Yahweh's arm or hand, which extends to all things (cf. Ex 15:6; 32:11; Ps 44:2–4; Is 53:1); and by the breath or spirit of Yahweh that reaches everywhere (cf. Gen 1:2), by which heaven and earth were made (cf. Ps 33:6), and with which He overwhelmed the Egyptians (Ex 15:10).

In the New Testament, total divine omnipotence continues to be affirmed, gathering up this entire doctrinal legacy. Nothing is impossible with God (cf. Lk 1:37). His power is manifested above all in the healing of the sick and the sanctification of sinners (cf. Mt 19:26; Mk 14:36; 10:27), wrought by Christ with the authority He has received from His Father (cf. Jn 5:19–22). That power is displayed in a manner that appears as weakness, in accordance with the self-emptying (*kenosis*) spoken of in Philippians 2:7. Christ accomplishes redemption likewise by the power of His word (cf. Mk 2:1–12). Man is in the hands of God like clay in the hands of the potter. No one can resist the will of God (Rom 9:14–23).

### The Eternity and Fidelity of God

In biblical thought, God is the One who never dies—that is, the One who is not subject to the process of decay inherent in material creatures. He is the Creator of the cosmos, not a part of it: *Art thou not from everlasting, O Lord my God, my Holy One? We shall not die* [or: *thou diest not*] (Hab 1:12). Whereas in the world surrounding Israel the gods are caught up in the cycle of birth and death, coming into being and perishing are simply impossible categories within the Old Testament testimony concerning God. On this point, biblical doctrine stands in radical contradiction to all theogonic myths, and especially to the myth of the dying god.[^4]

Boethius defined eternity as *interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*—the total, simultaneous, and perfect possession of boundless life.[^5] As is obvious, such a notion is abstract for the sacred writers, who never formulated the eternity of God in these terms or from this perspective. The biblical concept of eternity must be deduced negatively, by abstracting from temporal limits (that is, by affirming that it has no bound in time), since positively, as an eternal present, it is not conceived in those categories. The God of the Hebrews held sway over the horizon of time and was not subject to temporality. As Schnackenburg writes, the Hebrews "do not imagine eternity as another mode of being, but rather prolong the line of time indefinitely backwards and forwards. Everything falls within generation and corruption, except the divinity. Thereby they oppose all environmental conceptions that assume the gods had a beginning."[^6]

It is evident that, in Sacred Scripture, God is above all the living God (cf. Deut 5:26; 2 Kings 19:4; Ps 42:2). The expression *living God* carries diverse connotations depending on the context: at times it denotes the exuberant vitality of Yahweh in contrast to idols—dead, impotent, and lifeless gods (cf. Jer 16:20; Is 44:18; Ps 135:15–17; Wis 15:17); at other times it designates divine assistance extended to the devout who place their trust in Him (cf. Ps 42:2; 84:2). Existence is congenital to that mysterious Being whom the Hebrews named *El*, *Elohim*, *Yahweh*. Precisely because existence is inseparable from the divinity, the biblical authors never raise the question of God's origin, taking His pre-existence prior to creation as self-evident. Thus in the account of creation: it is assumed that Yahweh is prior to all things and above all things. He is the everlasting God (cf. Gen 21:33), for whom time does not count: *Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever thou hadst formed the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting thou art God (...) For a thousand years in thy sight are but as yesterday when it is past, or as a watch in the night* (Ps 90:2–4). The years of God endure throughout all generations (Ps 102:24).

The prophets underscore this divine attribute when pointing to God's fidelity to His promises. Indeed, such fidelity would be impossible were Yahweh a god who could cease to exist. *The eternal God—it is said already in Deuteronomy—is your dwelling place, and underneath are the everlasting arms* (Deut 33:27). *Who has performed and done this, calling the generations from the beginning? I, the Lord, the first, and with the last; I am He* (Is 41:4). *I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god* (Is 44:6; 48:12). Affirmations concerning the eternity of God are numerous and unambiguous in the prophets and the sapiential books (cf., e.g., Is 26:4; 33:14; 40:28; Dan 12:7; Ps 9:7; 10:16; 29:10; 92:8). Yahweh is the true God; He is the living God and the everlasting King (Jer 10:10). It has been observed that in this passage Yahweh is characterised as eternal in direct relation to His life. "His vital fullness is, therefore, the internal foundation of His eternity. All the generations of Israel swear by Yahweh, the living God (cf. Judg 8:19; 1 Sam 14:39), thus acknowledging that His vitality is the ultimate and highest element of His essence. That life cannot be diminished in any way, not even by sin (cf. Job 7:20; 35:6). It is so enormously rich that neither can it increase by means of man's righteousness (Job 35:6) (...) From this aspect of the vitality of God independent of the world, the Old Testament contemplates what dogmatics intends by *actus purus*."[^7]

The absolute vitality of God is thus brought into high relief, in such a way that whatever is subsequently said concerning divine immutability can never be confused with the *apatheia* of much of Greek thought. Nevertheless, as is obvious, the eternity of God, His fidelity to the Covenant, and kindred traits speak of permanence, and therefore in this sense point towards a certain immutability in God: *Of old thou didst lay the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the work of thy hands. They will perish, but thou dost endure; they will all wear out like a garment (...) but thou art the same, and thy years have no end* (Ps 102:25–27). Divine wisdom, *remaining in herself, renews all things* (Wis 7:27). Precisely because God has neither beginning nor end, He is likewise not susceptible of change in His being. He endures forever. Eternity, indeed, understood as fullness of life, is bound up with the concept of immutability—not insofar as this concept designates a mere absence of change, but insofar as it is understood within the fullness of life. It is a life that suffers no diminution, and because it is ever full, neither has it undergone increase.

Divine fidelity implies a certain immutability of the will: the word of God stands forever (Is 40:8), and He never recalls His word (Is 31:2). *The heavens will vanish like smoke, the earth will wear out like a garment, and they who dwell in it will die like gnats; but my salvation will be for ever, and my deliverance will never be ended* (Is 51:6). These passages and many others like them—which theologians would later apply to the immutability of the divine will—coexist in the Old Testament alongside others in which repentance and changes of attitude are attributed to God. Thus, for instance, He alters His decision to exterminate the people of Israel as punishment for the worship of the golden calf in response to the intercession of Moses (Ex 32:10–14), or to inflict other punishments (cf. Am 7:3, 6; Hos 11:8–9). These are texts intended to highlight the infinite mercy of Yahweh and the power of prayer and intercession over the heart of God. The Hebrews, intimately convinced of the efficacy of prayer and divine mercy, did not even set themselves to reconcile these experiential truths with divine immutability. Nevertheless, "the problem did not entirely escape Hosea, since he seeks to explain the incomprehensible mercy of Yahweh towards His guilty people by the holiness of Yahweh—that is, by that which differentiates God from man and places Him above all created reality. The prophet clearly perceived the mystery of God: the mystery of One who, being wholly other and exalted above all that is the object of human knowledge, can unite within Himself attributes that seem irreconcilable."[^8]

These anthropomorphisms and anthropopathisms in describing sentiments of love and wrath in God "are even particularly apt within their kind for making us see what God has willed to reveal to us beyond all speculation concerning *actus purus*, inscribing it in a certain sense in the human heart: His indestructible and inexhaustible vitality, which internally possesses a supreme degree of dynamism and externally governs all things."[^9] In any case, these passages must receive the same attention as those others that speak of the immutability of God's will: *God is not man, that he should lie, or a son of man, that he should repent* (Num 23:19); the counsel of Yahweh stands forever (Sir 42:21–22).

### The Omnipresence and Wisdom of God

In Sacred Scripture, divine omnipotence is accompanied by other attributes without which it would not be real and true: omniscience—that is, the knowledge of all things—and the immensity, or uncircumscribable nature, and omnipresence of God. Indeed, only He who knows all things, who is everywhere present, and who cannot be circumscribed by anything truly possesses power over all things. Implicitly, the presence of these attributes in God is affirmed in those scriptural passages that proclaim the divine transcendence and omnipotence; for the concept of the One God, Lord of the cosmos and of history, carries with it the affirmation of His omniscience and His universal presence.

In our manner of speaking, the omnipresence of God stands in close relation to His transcendence over the world and over matter—that is, to His spirituality. Only a being that is not confined to a locality by a material structure can be present in all places simultaneously. Given the concrete mentality of the Israelites, the notion of God's spirituality was not easily grasped, and popular religiosity tended to localise God in particular places. Hence, as with so many other divine attributes, in the history of the chosen people, alongside the clear teaching of Sacred Scripture concerning divine omnipresence, there appear traces of a rudimentary popular piety that tended to link God to specific sites.

Yet it must be borne in mind that, already from the Book of Genesis, when describing the power of God, He is presented as intervening in the diverse places mentioned in the narrative, thereby showing that His power extends over them all: God calls Abraham from Ur of the Chaldees, leads him to the land of Canaan, and protects him in Egypt (Gen 11–12); He compels Pharaoh to set free the tribes of Israel (Ex 6–7); He intervenes with a mighty hand at the Red Sea (Ex 15:1–2); He accompanies the people through the long sojourn in the wilderness. Thus, from the very first manifestations in Sacred Scripture, Yahweh appears as a God who is not exclusively bound to a concrete location; rather, although present in particular places—for instance, at Shechem (Gen 12:6), Beersheba (Gen 21:33), or Hebron (Gen 18:1)—He is constrained by none, but transcends them all. Yahweh was never a local deity in the sense of being circumscribed within a determined place; and if He was specially venerated at certain sanctuaries, this was in remembrance of the revelations wherewith, according to tradition, He had honoured the ancestors of Israel in those places.[^10]

This explains why the Temple of Jerusalem appears as the pre-eminent locus of God's presence: Yahweh has His dwelling in Zion, His holy mountain (Ps 46:4; Am 1:2; Is 2:2; 11:9; 48:2), in the Temple of Jerusalem (Is 2:3; 64:11; Jer 7:10). The special presence of God in the midst of His people was the greatest pride and supreme confidence of Israel, imparting meaning to its unity and its ardour for the Promised Land.[^11] At the same time, in texts belonging to the same period, God's presence in heaven is underlined (Ps 2:4; 11:4; 68:4; Am 9:6; Is 63:15) in such a way that, by affirming that He is there, it is simultaneously asserted that He is everywhere, employing poetic expressions that convey the omnipresence of God: *Heaven is my throne and the earth is my footstool* (Is 66:1; cf. Jer 23:24).

In biblical thought, this simultaneous presence of God in all things is bound up with His character as Creator:

> "Yahweh is truly the Lord of all cosmic realms, and is therefore not bound to the cosmos in any respect. The very celestial powers active in the stars—regarded in the ancient world as the supreme deities—lose their divine status so completely in Old Testament revelation and become so thoroughly subject to Yahweh that the author of Genesis 1 simply designates the sun and the moon as 'lights', without employing their proper names which carried reminiscences of those divinities (...) This transcendence of Yahweh has its ultimate roots in His absolute character as Creator vis-à-vis all entities, which have not arisen by emanation from His essence, but have entered into existence as His word, condensed as it were, out of the primordial power of His creative will (Gen 1:1 ff.; Is 42:5; 45:18; Ps 33:6, 9; 148:5)."[^12]

The immensity of God is explicitly proclaimed in Solomon's prayer at the dedication of the Temple: *Behold, heaven and the highest heaven cannot contain thee; how much less this house which I have built!* (1 Kings 8:27). Heaven and earth are full of God's glory (cf. Is 6:3); it is God Himself who fills heaven and earth (cf. Jer 23:24); God is higher than heaven, deeper than Sheol, longer than the earth, and broader than the sea (cf. Job 11:8–9). Divine immensity is exquisitely expressed in the Psalmist's words: *Whither shall I go from thy Spirit? Or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend to heaven, thou art there! If I make my bed in Sheol, thou art there! If I take the wings of the morning and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea, even there thy hand shall lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me* (Ps 139:7–10). In His dialogue with the Samaritan woman, Our Lord replies to her question regarding the proper place of worship by recalling this doctrine already familiar in Israel: God is to be worshipped in every place (cf. Jn 4:20–24). As can be gathered from the texts cited, this divine presence is no mere spatial juxtaposition, but the presence proper to a personal Being who knows, loves, and intervenes.

The affirmation of divine omniscience is even more explicit than that of omnipresence. From the most ancient texts it is taught that God knows the future (cf., e.g., Gen 25:22; Ex 18:15; Num 27:21; Josh 9:14; Judg 1:1; 18:5; 1 Sam 9:9; 10:22). God knows everything that happens everywhere: *The eyes of the Lord are in every place, keeping watch on the evil and the good* (Prov 15:3). Nothing occurring upon earth is hidden from Yahweh (Job 38:2–41; Zech 4:10). The eyes of Yahweh penetrate even into Sheol (Prov 15:11; Job 26:6) and into the hearts of men (Prov 15:11; Ps 11:4; 33:15). God knows human thoughts (Ps 94:11), secret intentions (Ps 139:1–4), and hidden plans (Ps 44:21; 139:2). God's knowledge searches the reins and the heart of man (Jer 11:20; 17:10). As was the case with omnipresence, divine omniscience is likewise expressed in the Bible in close connection with His creative action, for He who fashioned the human heart scrutinises all its deeds (cf. Ps 33:15; 7:9). Thus the Psalmist prays: *For thou didst form my inward parts, thou didst knit me together in my mother's womb (...) thou knowest me right well; my frame was not hidden from thee, when I was being made in secret (...) Thy eyes beheld my unformed substance; in thy book were written, every one of them, the days that were formed for me, when as yet there was none of them* (Ps 139:13–16). And God says to Jeremiah at the moment of his vocation: *Before I formed you in the womb I knew you* (Jer 1:5).

Special emphasis is placed on the affirmation that God has known from all eternity all future events, both those concerning individual men (Ps 139:15–16) and those concerning whole peoples (Am 3:7; Is 5:19; 14:26; 19:17). In fact, the prophetic charism is grounded in this divine foreknowledge: the prophet knows these events because God has made them known to him, since God alone knows the future in and of Himself: *Who told this long ago? Who declared it of old? Was it not I, the Lord? And there is no other god besides me* (Is 45:21; cf. 41:23).

God, therefore, possesses wisdom in all its fullness. The Book of Genesis already refers to this divine attribute when presupposing Him to possess the knowledge of good and evil (Gen 2:17; 3:5). Wisdom is one of the divine perfections unceasingly praised in Sacred Scripture (cf. Is 28:29; 31:2; 40:13–14; Dan 2:20–23; Job 9:4; 11:6–8). It is an unsearchable wisdom (Is 40:28; Job 11:6–9; Ps 92:5; 139:17–18), which God has received from no one (Is 40:13–14), and which surpasses all human calculation: *For my thoughts are not your thoughts, neither are your ways my ways, says the Lord. For as the heavens are higher than the earth, so are my ways higher than your ways and my thoughts than your thoughts* (Is 55:8–9).

In the New Testament, this insight that the wisdom of God is infinitely exalted above the wisdom of men receives a radical formulation in the theology of the Cross. The wisdom of God was not received by human wisdom (cf. Rom 1:21–23); for this reason human wisdom has been confounded, and the wisdom of God is now manifested on the Cross of Christ in a manner at once manifest and incomprehensible: folly to the Greeks and a stumbling block to the Jews, yet the power and salvation of God to those who believe (cf. 1 Cor 1:19–24; 2 Cor 1:12).[^13]

The Old Testament insists that the wisdom of God is present in all His works: in the Law (Deut 4:5–6; Ps 19:7), in creation, and in the governance of the world (Gen 1:10, 31; Job 36–37; Ps 8; Prov 3:19–20; Wis 7:22–27). If men attain wisdom, it is because they receive it from God (Job 32:8; Wis 7:7; 9:17). In the sapiential literature, the wisdom of Yahweh—which accompanies Him in all His works as the creative and ordering attribute of the universe—is described with traits so vivid and personal, with such an unmistakable personal profile, that this poetic personification appears at times to point towards a true hypostasis, thereby foreshadowing the personification of the *Logos* in Saint John.

### The Sapiential Texts on the Wisdom and the Word of God

Precisely because of the difficulty experienced in handling abstract concepts, the Jewish people tended to personalise certain divine attributes. This occurs particularly in several passages of Sacred Scripture with the expressions *wisdom of God* and *word of God*. Sound reasons underlay this distinctive personalisation of wisdom and the word: God accomplishes all things accompanied by His wisdom; the word of God is unfailingly efficacious, for He need only utter it and the world springs forth from nothingness. The word appears, then, as creating all things.

In the sapiential books there are hymns dedicated to wisdom that describe it as something divine, and in which its personification is so accentuated as to suggest wisdom as an independent entity.

Thus, for example, in Proverbs 8–9 wisdom is presented under a twofold aspect. It appears as a lady who calls out to men, exhorting them to justice, goodness, and prudence. It is a wisdom possessing a marked ethical character: the personification of the art of self-governance and of the governance of peoples. Its beginning is the fear of the Lord (Sir 1:14). The personification of wisdom is here so striking that one finds passages such as these: *The Lord created me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old. Ages ago I was set up, at the first, before the beginning of the earth. When there were no depths I was brought forth, when there were no springs abounding with water (...) When he established the heavens, I was there (...) I was beside him, like a master workman; and I was daily his delight, rejoicing before him always* (Prov 8:22–31). In this passage, the wisdom spoken of is the wisdom of God, which serves Him as master builder in the construction of the world. That wisdom enjoys such autonomy as to be Yahweh's delight, playing before Him across the face of the earth. A little further on, Wisdom is said to have built her house, inviting all: *Come, eat of my bread and drink of the wine I have mixed* (Prov 9:1–6).

In Sirach 1:1–10, wisdom is described as issuing from God and present in all the works of creation. It is the first among creatures: *All wisdom comes from the Lord (...) Wisdom was created before all things (...) The Lord himself created wisdom; he saw her and apportioned her (...) he poured her out upon all his works (...) and supplied her to those who love him.*

In Wisdom 7:21–27 one finds the most magnificent personification of wisdom. She is a spirit endowed with splendid qualities that enable her to penetrate and pervade all things; she is a breath of the power of God, a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty, a reflection of eternal light; remaining within herself, she renews all things: *I learned both what is secret and what is manifest, for wisdom, the fashioner of all things, taught me. For in her there is a spirit that is intelligent, holy, unique, manifold, subtle (...), all-powerful, overseeing all, and penetrating through all spirits (...) For she is a breath of the power of God, and a pure emanation of the glory of the Almighty (...) For she is a reflection of eternal light (...) Though she is but one, she can do all things (...) in every generation she passes into holy souls and makes them friends of God* (Wis 7:21–27). The personification of wisdom is unmistakable here. The traits with which she is depicted—sharing in creation and the governance of the world, sanctifying souls by making men friends of God—have always led this passage to be understood as a preparation for Trinitarian doctrine. Indeed, this passage forms the backdrop of several Johannine and Pauline texts (cf. Jn 1; Col 1:15), wherein Christ is spoken of as the Wisdom of God and the Spirit of Christ as the sanctifying Spirit.

Yet we are dealing here with nothing more than a poetic personification, which would be utilised in the New Testament as a vehicle for expressing Trinitarian doctrine. An attentive examination of each text reveals that these are literary personifications of a divine action, manifestation, or attribute, without their being understood in the strict sense of a person or subsistent reality. And yet, they are personifications open to being understood with a consistency extending beyond mere poetic imagery:

> "Rather, one must recognise in this wisdom, already within the Old Testament conception of the later period, a certain degree of autonomy, albeit in close proximity to and dependence upon God. Naturally, there can be no question of attributing to it a hypostasis in the sense that appears only later in the concept of the Word of God."[^14]

This personification of wisdom to the point of granting it a certain autonomy must therefore be regarded merely as a preparation for the revelation of the Trinitarian mystery, and not as an initial revelation. It is a preparation, not the inception of the revelation of this mystery, which takes place exclusively in the New Testament.[^15] But it is a genuine preparation; for if the whole Old Testament is preparation for the New, how could one fail to recognize that this preparation occurred at the very centre of Sacred Scripture—namely, in the Trinitarian mystery of the One God?

An identical role is played by the biblical theme of the Word wherewith God has revealed Himself in history. The word uttered by God is no empty sound, but an operative and efficacious reality. It is by His word that God creates the world and governs the phenomena of nature (cf., e.g., Gen 1:3; Ps 107:25; 147:15–18). It is by His word that God reveals Himself to Israel (Is 9:8; Ps 46:6; 106:9). It is a word possessing power in itself, and hence frequently appearing personified, with its own course of action: *For as the rain and the snow come down from heaven, and return not thither but water the earth, making it bring forth and sprout (...) so shall my word be that goes forth from my mouth; it shall not return to me empty, but it shall accomplish that which I purpose, and prosper in the thing for which I sent it* (Is 55:10–11).

The Word of God is portrayed not merely as a message intelligible to men, but as a dynamic and mighty reality that unfailingly achieves the divine designs. It is a reality present at the origin of the world's creation, at the election of the people of Israel, and in everything that unfolds in history. It is a reality proclaiming both the transcendence of God and His nearness to His people: He is present through His Word, which calls, teaches, rebukes, and forgives. It is a living word which, as such, acts with power (cf., e.g., Is 9:8; 55:10–11; Ps 107:20; 119:81). In the Old Testament we witness:

> "a hypostatisation of the Word carried through with resolve, although not quite to the degree seen with Wisdom (...) Thus John, supported by a new revelation, was able to gather together all the features of the nature of the Old Testament *dabar Yahweh*, including the affirmations concerning Wisdom, in the single Word whom he identified with Christ (cf. Jn 1; 1 Jn 1:1)."[^16]

The personification of the Wisdom and the Word of God gives rise to their being frequently depicted as mediations between God and the world—as beings belonging to the divine sphere through whom God acts. For this reason it may be said that:

> "the revelation of the Trinity in the New Testament was prepared by the affirmation and lived awareness of God's transcendence, and by the mysterious mediations intended to unite this transcendence with God's presence to His people; mediations attributed to beings endowed with personality, whose personality is not distinct from that of Yahweh, and which prepared the mind of the believer to acknowledge distinct Persons in God. This revelation was not the revelation of God's personality, for God had already revealed Himself as a personal being. It was the revelation of the mysterious mode in which God is personal."[^17]

These personifications already intimate the richness of Yahweh's intimate life and serve as an anticipation of New Testament revelation, wherein the living and personal God of history reveals that His most simple unity is lived in the tripersonal fullness of God the Father, God the Son, and God the Holy Spirit. As has been written:

> "The Old Testament image of the living God, the God of history, is not a concluded and closed image, but one open to the definitive revelation of God. It is only the shadow of the good things to come (Heb 10:1)."[^18]

### The Moral Attributes of God

Among the moral attributes whereby the Old Testament describes God, those of truth, justice, fidelity, and love stand out. Later, in the New Testament, it will be affirmed roundly that *God is love* (1 Jn 4:8, 16) as the trait summarising all divine perfections. Indeed, all these descriptions of God's goodness appear so intertwined that it is difficult to assign to each an adequately distinct and demarcated meaning.

Thus, for example, the truth of God frequently denotes His fidelity and loyalty. God is true because He never lies; He is ever faithful to His covenant. Fidelity, in turn, is habitually linked to goodness, and it is stressed that in God's acting His covenantal fidelity is always joined with His benevolence towards men. Goodness is regularly paired with fidelity and, above all, with mercy.[^19]

### The Truth and Fidelity of God

In the Old Testament, truth and fidelity appear with virtually interchangeable meanings (cf. Ps 25:10; 26:3; 40:10–11; 57:3, 10; 36:5; 88:11; 89:1–2), consistently indicating firmness, stability, reliability, and that a person is what he ought to be. In this sense, the veracity and fidelity of God correspond to the fact that God, the supreme Sovereign, is ever faithful to what He ought to be. This is a foundational Old Testament concept when speaking of divine being, since the whole theology of the Old Testament revolves around the ideas of election and covenant. Hence Yahweh's fidelity and His veracity are highlighted together. Yahweh is the One who is ever faithful and true, the One who unfailingly fulfills what He has promised. God, indeed, is not only wise and just, but is a God of history, who has committed Himself to the history of Israel in view of a mysterious salvific design for the whole of humanity. He has promised a Saviour, who constitutes the very *raison d'être* of the Jewish people, chosen as bearer of the promise.

Hence *'emet* (truth, fidelity, firmness) is so intimately linked with *hesed* (goodness, loving-kindness, grace). Yahweh is *rich in hesed and 'emet* (cf. Ex 34:6), in mercy and truth; He possesses goodness, truth—that is, veracity and constancy—in the highest degree; in a word, fidelity. His fidelity (*'emet*) is a buckler and shield (Ps 91:4)—that is, a secure protection. Yahweh is a trusty guide (Ps 43:3); all His paths are mercy and truth (Ps 25:10)—that is, His entire way of acting is inspired by benevolence and fidelity to His promises.[^20] The reality most contrary to Yahweh is injustice, unfaithfulness, and falsehood. Yahweh is truth: all His ways are mercy and truth (Tob 3:2; Ps 25:10); mercy and truth go before His face (Ps 89:14); the works of His hands are truth and justice (Ps 111:7); the truth of the Lord endures forever (Ps 117:2). When it is said of Our Lord that He is *full of grace and truth* (cf. Jn 1:14), or when He declares of Himself that He is *the way, the truth, and the life* (cf. Jn 14:6), it is evident that these statements refer to attributes of Yahweh specially highlighted by the Old Testament.

Precisely because these concepts all turn upon the reality of Yahweh's covenant and promise, the ideas of veracity, fidelity, and mercy are indissolubly joined to the concept of justice—that is, to the idea of salvation; for in the religious perspective of the covenant, fidelity is linked to the panoramic vista of justice or salvation in the divine plan. These plans have at their very centre the promise of the Saviour.

### The Divine Justice

The justice of God (*sedeq*) is conformity in action with what ought to be. From patriarchal times onward, the Bible portrays the God of the chosen people not as a blind, uncontrolled force, but as an omnipotent power that acts always according to equity and justice. God is just in every respect, for *shall not the Judge of all the earth do right?* (Gen 18:25). In biblical thought, the concept of the just always possesses a marked religious significance, since Yahweh is the source of all right and law. To comprehend this in its proper dimensions, one must keep in view the concept of covenant central to the whole Bible: Yahweh is bound to Israel by a concrete covenant, and this entails juridical consequences and reciprocal demands.

Because He is the Just One—the One who always respects the covenant, who is ever true and faithful—Yahweh is the source of all right, and consequently the One who traces the line of what is just according to His own standards. The divine will is the supreme rule for those who believe in the God of Israel; and on the other hand, the divine will is ever in concordance with the attributes of justice and mercy.

Yahweh is the God of Israel who, bound by the covenant, executes justice for Israel against her enemies and establishes justice among the members of the people of Israel. For this reason, the Israelites view their victories over enemies as acts of God's justice (cf. 1 Sam 12:7; Mic 6:5; 2 Sam 18:31). At the same time, they conceive God as the avenger of all who suffer oppression within the people of Israel itself (cf. Hos 2:19; 10:12), for Yahweh punishes those who transgress His Law (Am 5:24; Is 5:16). Against all injustice, God is the champion of the weak—such as the poor (Ps 140:12), the fatherless, and the widow (cf. Ex 22:22; Deut 10:18; 27:19).

One of the distinctive marks of the future Messianic King, upon whom the Spirit of Yahweh will rest, is to exercise justice holily and establish righteousness: *There shall come forth a shoot from the stump of Jesse (...) And the Spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him (...) he shall not judge by what his eyes see, or decide by what his ears hear; but with righteousness he shall judge the poor, and decide with equity for the meek of the earth (...) Righteousness shall be the girdle of his waist, and faithfulness the girdle of his loins* (Is 11:1–5). And in the New Testament, the Messiah establishes the doctrine of the Beatitudes as the fundamental law of His Kingdom—that is, an affirmation of full justice, embracing mercy and the forgiveness of enemies (cf. Mt 5:20–48). It is a justice surpassing that of the scribes and Pharisees, carrying with it the call to be perfect as our heavenly Father is perfect (cf. Mt 5:48).

Hence the concept of justice applied to God is intimately connected with that of holiness. Here is a description of this concept in its rich variety of nuances: Justice

> "is attributed to the Holy One, who commits no iniquity; who leaves neither the wicked unpunished nor the good unacknowledged; to the merciful God, slow to anger, who desires not the death of the sinner, but that he repent and live; to the loving God, who seeks the salvation of His people and communicates His righteousness to the sinner, justifying him."[^21]

Hence too, in the New Testament, the term justice (*dikaiosyne*) is simultaneously a divine attribute and a gift bestowed by God upon men—a grace granted in Christ to those who believe in Him: *But now the righteousness of God has been manifested apart from law, although the law and the prophets bear witness to it, the righteousness of God through faith in Jesus Christ for all who believe. For there is no distinction; since all have sinned and fall short of the glory of God, they are justified by his grace as a gift* (Rom 3:21–24; cf. also 1 Cor 1:30; 2 Cor 5:21).

Upon reading the Old Testament for the first time, one may be taken aback by the manner in which God is sometimes said to exercise justice, above all against the enemies of Israel. Yahweh clearly acts with partiality on behalf of His chosen ones: on behalf of Abraham (Gen 12:10–20; 26:6–10), Jacob (Gen 27), the Israelite tribes who plundered the Egyptians (Ex 3:21–22; 12:35), and He commands the total destruction of the Canaanites (Deut 7:1–11, 16). This command of total destruction (*herem*) must be understood in the light of ancient law, which regarded the annihilation of cities as standard practice and frequently required it as obligatory, at least in the context of sacred war.

Alongside these considerations, a crucial factor must be borne in mind: the peculiar Hebrew mode of speaking that attributes to God what is proper to secondary causes, without distinguishing between what God commands, what God wills, and what God permits. In the case before us, it is customarily pointed out that:

> "the Hebrews were convinced that they were waging holy war against Yahweh's enemies and acting as instruments of His vengeance. The sacred authors express this conviction in the form of a command given by Yahweh to destroy the vanquished. They do so all the more freely because they pay little heed to secondary causes and attribute all events directly to God as First Cause (e.g., Am 1:4; 4:7–10), including fortuitous accidents (Ex 21:13). This is likewise the reason why they say that God incites to evil (2 Sam 16:10; 24:1; 1 Sam 26:19) and that, on the other hand, He will not leave unpunished the hardened heart of Pharaoh (Ex 4:21; 7:3) or that of His people (Is 6:10)."[^22]

Moreover, the fact that an individual is singled out as chosen by God does not imply that God approves of everything he does. One need only recall the paradigmatic case of King David. To this must be added the fact that God did not reveal the fullness of the Law at the outset; that revelation was made only by Jesus Christ. For this reason we witness throughout Israel's history a process of progressive awakening to what is good and what is evil:

> "In both the dogmatic and moral spheres, the process of development was gradual, and God respected the prevailing mentality, condescending to many practices that He could not positively suggest or command (...) Not everything done by the patriarchs and other figures chosen as instruments in salvation history is acceptable within the spiritual and moral framework of the Gospel. Jesus Himself remarks that Moses permitted the bill of divorce *propter duritiam cordis eorum* (Mt 19:8)."[^23]

### The Mercy and Love of God

In the Bible, the notion of a God *merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness* (Ex 34:6) frequently accompanies the attribute of justice, thereby making plain that divine justice is first and foremost holiness, and that God's primordial relation with mankind—and especially with the chosen people—is a relation of mercy.

> "The Israelite knows that he can count upon Yahweh's assistance and goodness; he invokes it with confidence, yet does not claim it as something owed (Ps 6:4; 25:6–7; 119:149; 143:12); he knows too that to obtain it he is bound personally to fulfil the conditions of the covenant, that is, to observe the precepts of his God (Ex 20:6; Deut 7:12; 1 Kings 8:23; Hos 10:12, etc.). In any event, whenever Israel breaches the covenant, Yahweh does not withdraw His *hesed*, because He is a merciful and compassionate God, longsuffering, rich in loving-kindness (*hesed*) and fidelity (*'emet*), keeping steadfast love for thousands, forgiving iniquity, transgression, and sin (Ex 34:6–7; Num 14:18; Ps 86:15; 103:8; Joel 2:13; Jon 4:2; Neh 9:17)."[^24]

God does not desire the death of the wicked, but that he turn from his way and live (cf. Ezek 33:11). When Our Lord, in the parable of the Prodigal Son (Lk 15:11–32), reveals the depths of God's paternal compassion towards the sinner, He brings to its consummate expression the truth already clearly affirmed in the Old Testament concerning divine mercy. The mercy of God is displayed especially towards the poor, the afflicted, and the weak (Ps 69:16; 79:8; 103:13; 119:77). Here too, when Jesus of Nazareth points out that the central criterion of the Universal Judgement will be the corporal and spiritual works of mercy, He stands firmly embedded in the long doctrinal tradition of the Old Testament (cf. Mt 25:31–46).

This mercy is efficacious and intrinsically bound to the concept of the covenant. For this reason, the sentiments of Yahweh:

> "bear the imprint of familial affection. One must never lose sight of this context of the community of the *berit*, or covenant, which governs all the religious expressions of the sacred writers. Yahweh is not an abstract deity with concrete attributes, but a Being who always manifests Himself within the historical context of the chosen people's life (...) Only later, in the light of prophetic and sapiential reflection, will the God of all nations and of the cosmos appear; and then His attributes and sentiments will assume a universal scope transcending the familial sphere of historical Israel."[^25]

Divine mercy, as is evident, stands in intimate relation to the love God bears towards His people, towards mankind, and towards creation. This love of God is already seen in Genesis directed towards the first man (Gen 2:8; 3:21), Noah and his family (Gen 7:1, 16), and the patriarchs (Gen 12:2–3; 15:1–18). As is well known, in the Old Testament, within the covenantal context, God's love is depicted above all as the love of a husband for his wife, or as the love of a father for his son. From the prophets onwards—especially Hosea, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and Isaiah—Yahweh's covenant with the people is portrayed as a spousal covenant; consequently, the sins of the people are depicted as unfaithfulness to that bridal bond (cf., e.g., Hos 2–3; Jer 2:1–36; Ezek 16:1–52; Is 40:1–6).

God is likewise the Father of the people, whom He treats with fatherly care (cf. Ex 4:22; Deut 14:1; 32:5). The love of God springs from His divine fatherhood (cf. Hos 11:1–9). Among all nations, Israel is His firstborn (cf. Ex 4:22; Jer 31:9). At times this love is described with the tenderness of maternal affection: *Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should have no compassion on the son of her womb? Even these may forget, yet I will not forget you* (Is 49:15). In direct continuity with these insights, Our Lord will explain to Nicodemus that *God so loved the world that he gave his only Son* (Jn 3:16).
