## Chapter 26: The Theology of the Divine Persons {#mateo-seco-trino.26}

The tradition of the Church has resolutely employed the name and concept of *person* to designate the three subsistent relations in God—that is, to refer to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. This usage is not without its difficulties, given that our starting point for forming the concept of person is none other than human persons, who are complete, incommunicable, and numerically distinct substances. How, then, can the notion of person be applied to the mystery of the Three of the Trinity, who are numerically one single God?

It is obvious that the divine reality transcends all conceptualisation and all language, and that it consequently demands a judicious use of analogy when employing the concept of person. Yet given the divine transcendence, the question arises whether, even with a correct use of analogy, it is possible to apply the concept of person to God in order to refer to the Trinitarian mystery. Notwithstanding the difficulties raised by the use of this concept in Trinitarian theology, theologians consistently employ it to designate the three subsistents of the Trinity. In doing so, they simply adhere to centuries of theological tradition. That tradition itself provides a prime example of how the proper use of concepts and vocabulary is of capital importance in Trinitarian theology.[^1]

### The Concept of Person in Theological Tradition

As was seen in the second part of this work, the distinction between substance and person was key to a correct exposition of the Trinitarian faith in both the Greek and Latin spheres.[^2] This was, so to speak, one of the most significant contributions of the Cappadocian Fathers. Let us recall the principal milestones along that long journey in search of unequivocal expressions, which culminated in the fourth century with the distinction between nature and person, and with the acceptance of a common language shared by Latins and Greeks.

Trinitarian reality imposed itself upon Christian thought from the very first community. Leading to this, among other factors, were the baptismal confession of faith and the clarity of the missionary mandate (cf. Mt 28:19): in God there is unity and trinity. It is a Trinity that intervenes as such within salvation history, to the point that Saint John can affirm that our hands have touched the Word of life (cf. 1 Jn 1:1–3). Hence the concepts and expressions used by the Fathers from the beginning to speak of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit belong to the realm of the personal. They refer, in effect, to objective and subsistent realities, distinct from one another, which act with intelligence and freedom.

Language fluctuated during the early centuries. Nor did Greek philosophy offer a clear definition of person. Perhaps one of the most accurate descriptions is the Aristotelian statement that the person is the subsistence constituted by the soul as the entelechy of the body.[^3] In one way or another, the conjunction of intelligence and subsistence that Aristotle establishes in his description of personal being would remain present in medieval theological systematisation.

The Greek terms employed in the early centuries to designate the person were *ousia* and *hypostasis*, which on numerous occasions appeared as synonyms. They are treated as such, for instance, at the Council of Nicaea (AD 325).[^4] This identification between *ousia* and *hypostasis* persisted until the middle of the fourth century. Nevertheless, it must be kept in mind that the term *hypostasis* was always used to designate a being that exists as a concrete and real subsistent. Even amidst the linguistic fluctuation and imprecision of these centuries, when speaking of the Trinitarian mystery the Fathers frequently employed the expression *three hypostases* and avoided speaking of *three ousiai* in God, as though gradually the term *hypostasis* were singling itself out above all others as the most apt for designating the concept of person. This was the case even in Origen and Saint Athanasius, who understood *ousia* and *hypostasis* as virtually synonymous terms.[^5] The Fathers were well aware that, above and beyond terms, what matters are concepts, as Saint Gregory Thaumaturgus observes, for the Trinitarian mystery is not a matter of words, but of real hypostases.[^6]

The Latins likewise employed a similar conceptualisation to distinguish unity and trinity in God. Tertullian introduced the term *persona* into Latin theology. To him is owed the classic formula *tres personae, una substantia*.[^7] Novatian followed him in this distinction.[^8] Hippolytus of Rome used the corresponding Greek term—*prosopon*—to refer to the concept of person, because he took as his starting point the *essentia* or *substantia* common to the three divine Persons; for this reason, despite using it, he was able to avert modalism with reasonable success.[^9] The fact is that both the term *persona* and the term *prosopon* presented a serious difficulty for their adoption in Trinitarian theology: their philological origin. Originally, both terms served to designate the mask used by actors in the theatre and, consequently, seemed to favour a modalist understanding of the divine Persons, as though they were merely different guises under which the divinity presents Himself before mankind.

Language gravely hindered mutual understanding with the Greeks, who feared that the original sense of the term *prosopon* or *persona* would entail falling into Sabellianism. Equal difficulties were encountered by the Latins in translating *hypostasis* as *person*. Led by the grammatical meaning of the term *hypostasis*—"that which stands beneath"—the Latins tended to identify it with the ideas of substance or essence, and therefore rejected speaking of the existence of three hypostases in God, fearing that this implied three substances.[^10]

Saint Jerome and Pope Saint Damasus in the West, and Saint Athanasius and the Cappadocians in the East, carried out extensive and fruitful work to overcome these terminological misunderstandings. Saint Augustine was likewise understanding towards these difficulties of vocabulary.[^11] Reconciliation was accomplished first in the East through the work of Saint Athanasius at the Council of Alexandria in 362, when Saint Basil's formula *mia ousia, treis hypostaseis* was accepted. This formula consecrated the conceptual distinction between substance and person and, above all, refined the theological meaning of the terms *ousia* and *hypostasis*. Saint Gregory of Nazianzus was the chief architect of agreement on this question, not least by accepting that the names *hypostasis* and *prosopon* could be used interchangeably to designate the Persons.[^12]

Perhaps nothing is more eloquent on this subject than the Oration delivered by Saint Gregory of Nazianzus at the First Council of Constantinople (AD 381), in one of the most decisive interventions on behalf of terminological convergence:

> "A single nature in Three; that is God (...) Let us not be Sabellians by defending the one against the three through a confusion that abolishes distinction. Let us not be Arians by defending the three against the one (...) We believe in the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, consubstantial, equal in glory (...) We know the One in the unity of the *ousia* and in the inseparability of adoration; we confess the Three in the hypostases or in the persons, as some prefer to say, for it is necessary to put an end to this ridiculous strife stirred up among brethren, as though our religion consisted in words and not in realities.
> 
> "Indeed, what do you mean, you who advocate the formula *three hypostases*? Do you perchance use this word to designate three *ousiai*? I am certain you would protest loudly against anyone who thought so, for you confess that the *ousia* of the Three is one and the same. And what do you mean when you use the name *persons*? Do you perhaps imagine the One as a strange compound, like a man with three faces? By no means! You too would protest with loud cries, saying: 'May he who harbours such thoughts never see the face of God!' What, then, do hypostases mean for us, and persons for you? (...) By this we mean that the Three are distinct, not in their nature, but in their properties (*idiotetes*). Tell me, then: is it not possible to say the same thing, even while using different terms?"[^13]

The *Quicumque* Creed would capture with Latin clarity this thought already elucidated in the East.[^14] Beyond terminological disputes, the concept of person imposes itself as necessary for speaking of the Trinity. The fundamental reason is that we designate with the name *hypostasis* or *person* intelligent and free beings, distinct from one another, who act with freedom and intelligence. Now, to attribute creation to the Father, redemption to the Son, and sanctification to the Holy Spirit requires conceiving them as three subsistents distinct from one another who act with freedom.

### Difficulties in Applying the Notion of Person to God

From the events just recalled onwards, the course of theological thought has been unanimous in using the concept of person to speak of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.

The use of this concept in Trinitarian theology appears eminently fitting; nevertheless, it is not devoid of risks.[^15] This has been emphasised above all by two twentieth-century theologians: Karl Barth and Karl Rahner, who show themselves reticent when it comes to applying the concept of person to God. It has been observed that in these hesitations, especially regarding Barth's position, the influence of Hegel is noticeable. For Hegel, God is not a personal being, but the Absolute Spirit, whereas person is precisely the particular manifestation of spirit, its necessarily limited individualisation. Personality would thus be the constitutive form of the human spirit as a finite spirit, and consequently could not be applied to God, who is infinite spirit, since the concept of person would have meaning only within finitude.[^16]

Barth's difficulties in applying the concept of person to God depend upon his assertion that personality and finitude are inseparable. They are also linked to his well-known rejection of the analogy of being (*analogia entis*) in theological enquiry—an analogy that is indispensable for presenting the Trinitarian mystery. Indeed, without understanding the concept of person as something abysmally distinct from and superior to its concretisation in human finitude, it is impossible to apply it to God. But if the concept of person is not applied to God, how can one express that the Three of the Trinity possess themselves and give themselves in Trinitarian relations? Barth proposes replacing the expression *three persons* with that of *three modes of being* (*Seinsweise*)—a formula that either verges dangerously on Sabellianism or is nothing more than a circumlocution to express the very reality designated by the term *person*.[^17]

Saint Augustine had already perceived this problem, arguing that the use of the name *person* to designate the Three is due to the poverty of our language—to the fact that we are unable to find a better word. Here is how he formulates the issue:

> "And just as for Him it is identical to be and to be God, great, good, so in Him being and person are identified. Why, then, do we not call these three realities one person, as we say one essence and one God, but rather affirm the existence of three persons, given that we do not say three gods or three essences? Perhaps because, since we have confessed that they are three, it pleases us to employ a word that expresses the Trinity, so as not to remain silent when we are asked what these three are."[^18]

Karl Rahner likewise experiences grave misgivings regarding the advisability of using the term *person* in Trinitarian theology. His reasons stem fundamentally from the meaning assigned to it by modern philosophy, a meaning quite different from that which it received in the patristic era. This was a position that Rahner developed gradually over time. Here is a brief summary of that trajectory:[^19]

In *Theological Investigations*, he accepts the use of *person* as the customary term in the Magisterium of the Church, but cautions that, from Descartes onwards, this concept aligns more closely with the Cartesian outlook than with that of Boethius.[^20] In his celebrated Trinitarian treatise in *Mysterium Salutis*, he proposes that, given the new meaning the term *person* has acquired, equivalent expressions to the classical concept might be used, such as "distinct personal relations" or "three distinct modes of subsistence".[^21] In *Sacramentum Mundi*, he radicalises his position to the point of asserting that the concept of person must be used "with great caution in Trinitarian theology, if one seeks right understanding and not confusion".[^22]

The difficulties raised against applying the concept of person underscore the need to employ this concept with extreme delicacy. Yet they do not justify its abandonment, especially when the formulations proposed to replace it prove even more unsatisfactory.[^23]

Moreover, there are numerous positive reasons for retaining this usage. The word *person* is the term employed by the Ecumenical Councils and by the Magisterium of the Church in their exposition of Trinitarian and Christological doctrine. The word *person* is the best term to designate the divine *Thou* whom we address when we invoke the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, or to designate the *Thou* of Jesus. The theologian has, in addition, another powerful reason: the experience of the two millennia that have preceded us, and the new meaning the term *person* has received through being enriched by theological use.

As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* observes, for the formulation of the dogma of the Trinity, the Church had to develop a terminology of her own with the help of notions of philosophical origin: *substance*, *person*, and so forth. Yet in doing so, she did not subject faith to human wisdom, but rather imparted "a new, surprising meaning to these terms, which were thenceforth destined also to signify an ineffable Mystery."[^24] The new theological meaning with which these terms have been enriched through centuries of usage renders them uniquely suited for expounding Christian doctrine, for their long history makes them far more accessible to the Christian people than speculative paraphrases.

### The Analogical Concept of Person

The concept of person must therefore be used in Trinitarian theology analogically: that is, keeping in mind that the distance between God and man is infinite and, consequently, that this distance must also be infinite in what is most radical in both—their personal reality. When speaking of three Persons in God, the term *person* designates a reality that is realised in God in a manner infinitely superior to the way it is realised in human nature, yet which is realised truly and properly:

> "Person signifies that which is most perfect in all nature—namely, what subsists in a rational nature. Now, since every perfection must be attributed to God, inasmuch as the divine essence contains within itself every perfection, it is fitting that the name *person* should be applied to God. Nevertheless, it does not belong to God in the same way as to creatures, but in a more excellent manner, just as other names that were assigned to creatures and are attributed to God."[^25]

The concept of person is applied to God according to the laws of analogy, but it is found in Him properly and not merely metaphorically. In effect, by the name *person* we designate intentionally a pure perfection: namely, being as subsisting in itself, as possessing itself in plenitude. If the concept of person involves imperfection in man, this is due to the limitation of human being, not to the concept of person itself; as such, the concept of person means only that the one to whom it applies possesses himself and gives himself in all fullness. Since God is infinitely perfect, His personal being is of infinite perfection in terms of self-possession and intra-Trinitarian self-giving.

The concept of person, when applied to God, exhibits a profound difference from the concept of person applied to finite beings. In created beings, by the name *person* we designate the essence or substance in so far as it subsists in itself—that is, we designate something absolute—whereas in God, by the name *person* we designate not His substance, but the relations; although, as is obvious given the divine simplicity, what is relational in God is identified with the divine substance and is distinguished from it solely by a distinction of reason.[^26]

The concept of person involves two essential notes: substantiality and incommunicability. These two notes are found in God in a manner very different from how they are found in creatures. Whereas in created beings the distinction and incommunicability proper to the person arise from its substance, in God they arise exclusively from relative opposition. These relations, in turn, are not lacking in subsistence, since they are identified with the divine essence; they are also substantial and therefore unite the two essential notes constitutive of the concept of person.[^27]

In God, the word *person* designates *in recto* not the substance, but the relation; whereas in human beings the name *person* designates *in recto* the substance, even though, as perfect substances—that is, as intelligent and free beings—they achieve their full realisation only within interpersonal relation.

In God, relations not only distinguish the Persons from one another, but constitute them:

> "According to this, it is true that the word *person* signifies relation directly (*in recto*) and the essence indirectly (*in obliquo*); yet relation not inasmuch as it is relation, but inasmuch as it is signified after the manner of a hypostasis."[^28]

### Various Definitions of Person

Although there is near-universal agreement regarding the application of the concept of person to God, the difficulties to which we have referred have prompted theologians to formulate numerous definitions of person in order to refine its essential notes. The most prominent definitions are those of Boethius, Richard of Saint Victor, and Saint Thomas Aquinas. All three have the Trinitarian mystery explicitly in view in their definitions.

#### The Definitions of Boethius and Richard of Saint Victor

Severinus Boethius († 525) defined the person as an individual substance of a rational nature: *rationalis naturae individua substantia*.[^29] Substantiality, individuality, and relationality (rationality) appear united in this definition. Primordial importance is given to substance. Substantiality and individuality designate what is most evident in the human person: persons are concrete beings, subsisting in themselves, perfectly differentiated from all other beings and persons. Each person is identical only to himself, subsists in himself, and cannot be confused with another.

The intellectuality of the person is the most perfect way in which substance can possess itself: with consciousness or presence of self to self. Intellectuality lies at the root of the possibility of personal communication; that is, it lies at the root of the possibility of supreme self-giving, in consciousness and freedom. In order for this self-giving to be constant, it must take place without the person losing himself, without dissolving in the act of giving. In the Boethian definition, *individual substance* indicates the root of one's own individuality, whereas *rational nature* indicates the possibility of personal communication—that is, of the *communio personarum*.

In God, the two elements of the Boethian definition are clearly verified: subsistence and communion. The Persons are distinct because their origin is distinct and their relation is distinct, yet they possess the highest possible degree of communion: the living unity of the one divinity.[^30]

Richard of Saint Victor († 1173) encountered difficulties in applying the Boethian definition of person to God. He considered that this concept could not be applied to God in the precise terms Boethius had formulated. Richard, who begins from the unity of the divine nature, understands that God is an individual substance of a rational nature, and yet one cannot say of that nature that it is a person. Furthermore, Richard notes, the emphasis in the concept of person applied to God must be placed not so much on the substance—which is one and the same for the three Persons—as on what is proper and incommunicable to each Person: paternity, filiation, and *ekporeusis* or spiration. Hence he defines the person as an "incommunicable existence proper to the divine nature" (*divinae naturae incommunicabilis existentia*).[^31]

The qualifying clause "proper to the divine nature" highlights the difficulty Richard encounters in applying his own definition to God. He specifies that it is an existence proper to the divine nature: that is, the definition of person cannot apply to any other.

With this definition, Richard aims at the identity of each Person with Himself and, consequently, at His distinction from the others. This is the sense in which he uses the word *incommunicable*. *Incommunicable* simply designates that which makes it possible to say that the Father is not the Son or that the Son is not the Father; *incommunicable* designates *in recto* that upon which the otherness existing among the divine Persons is grounded—an otherness that is necessary precisely in order that interpersonal self-giving may take place.[^32]

It will be recalled that Richard's thought on the mystery of the Trinity is based upon his conception of God as essential love, and upon the necessity that genuine love exist in God so that beatitude and glory may be found in Him. Now—continuing with Richard's reasoning—if there were no otherness in God, true love could not exist in Him. Without this polarity between one person and another, there could be no absolute and infinite, personal and gratuitous communication between them, nor that self-giving proper to charity which constitutes the marvellous richness of intelligent being.

In this light one must understand what Richard means by "incommunicable existence". It may be said that, for him, person designates the mode of existing indispensable for love to occur: a mode of existing that simultaneously distinguishes and unites. It distinguishes in so far as this mode of existing has its own distinctive characteristics:

> "I think that the name *person* designates not a thing, but a subject. Indeed, what does the name *person* convey, if not one who is distinct from everything else by that which is proper to him?"[^33]

#### The Definition of Saint Thomas

Saint Thomas inclines towards the Boethian definition, but modifies it in an important manner. Bearing in mind the Trinitarian mystery, he defines the person as *subsistens in rationali natura*—"that which subsists in a rational nature".[^34]

At bottom, this formulation says the same thing as Boethius's; but Thomas, by replacing the Boethian term *substantia*—which is very close to *nature*—with that of *subsistentia*, which aligns more closely with the incommunicable mode of existing, directs attention towards that which is the subject or bearer of the nature or substance, in so far as it subsists as distinct and incommunicable.[^35]

Indeed, to speak of three individual substances existing in God is a dangerous step towards tritheism. If, by contrast, one speaks of three *subsistentiae*, this signifies that the divine nature or substance, numerically one, is possessed by three subjects who subsist in three relatively distinct modes of subsistence.[^36]

In the *De potentia*, Saint Thomas defines the divine persons as a distinct subsistent in the divine nature: *distinctum subsistens in natura divina*.[^37] This definition evokes the importance attributed by Richard of Saint Victor to distinction among subsistents as the most prominent feature of personality. In the *Summa Theologiae*, he defines the divine person as relation in so far as it is subsistent:

> "It is one thing to enquire into the meaning of the term *person* in general, and another to enquire into the meaning of *divine person*. Now *person* in general signifies, as has been said, an individual substance of a rational nature; and *individual* signifies that which is indistinct in itself, but distinct from others. Consequently, *person*, in whatever nature it may be, signifies that which is distinct in that nature (...) But in God there can be no distinction other than that which arises from relations of origin (...) Moreover, relation in God is not like an accident inhering in a subject, but is the very divine essence; wherefore it is subsistent, since the divine essence subsists (...) Consequently, *person* signifies relation inasmuch as it is subsistent."[^38]

The concept of person employed by Saint Thomas, while remaining very close to that of Boethius, has as its distinctive hallmark the importance assigned to the concept of subsistence or subsistent. By this he seeks not only to avert the clear dangers entailed by the Boethian definition when it prioritises substance as constitutive of the person, but also points to the fact that, in God, the Persons are constituted precisely by subsistent relation.[^39]

By relying upon the concept of subsistence rather than upon that of substance, Saint Thomas improves Boethius's definition by incorporating something of Richard of Saint Victor's insight. What constitutes the person for Saint Thomas, rather than sheer individuality, is subsistence—the *distinctum subsistens*. Nevertheless, his difference from Richard's definition is readily perceptible: Thomas will never use the element of *incommunicable existence* as the constitutive feature of the divine person. Thomistic subsistence means something very different from Richard's *existentia incommunicabilis*, for according to Saint Thomas there are not three incommunicable existences in God—which would lead dangerously to the conception of three incommunicable acts of existing—but a single existence realised in three relations which, because they are rooted in the divine nature, subsist in themselves.

### Divine Person and Subsistent Relation

Since in God the person is the distinct subsistent, and in God there is nothing distinct apart from the relation of opposition, it follows, according to Saint Thomas, that the divine Persons are nothing other than relations inasmuch as they are subsistent. It may be said that these relations are distinguished by being opposed, and that they are persons—with all that this concept entails of substantiality—inasmuch as they subsist in the divine essence with which they are identified. Here is how Saint Thomas reasons:

> "Relation in God is not an accidental reality belonging to a subject, but is the divine essence itself. And therefore it is a subsistent relation, just as the divine essence is subsistent. Hence, just as the Godhead is God, so divine paternity is God the Father, who is a divine person. Consequently, a divine person signifies relation inasmuch as it is subsistent. This is to signify relation after the manner of substance, which is a hypostasis subsisting in the divine nature; although that which subsists in the divine nature is not a reality distinct from the divine nature."[^40]

This identification between subsistent relation and person reflects the oneness of the divine substance. Hence the concept of person in God refers directly to the relation and indirectly to the divine essence in which the Father, the Son, and the Spirit are identified, who are one single God:

> "According to this, it is true that the word *person* signifies relation directly (*in recto*) and the essence indirectly (*in obliquo*); yet relation not inasmuch as it is relation, but inasmuch as it is signified after the manner of a hypostasis."[^41]

The subsistent relations constitute, distinguish, and refer the divine Persons to one another. As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states: "The real distinction of the persons from one another, because it does not divide the divine unity, resides solely in the relationships which relate them to one another."[^42]

In Trinitarian theology, the concept of subsistent relation as equivalent to person highlights the intimate divine life, the *communio personarum* as relation. Each Person subsists in so far as He is identified with the divine essence and is distinguished from the Others exclusively in so far as He refers to them. The Trinitarian mystery is a mystery of oneness and otherness, of distinction and interior donation—a mystery of intimate communion of knowledge and love. The Persons are subsistent relations: they are relations that are constituted precisely by their mutual reference.

The Thomistic identification between divine person and subsistent relation averts the danger of conceiving the divine substance as something common "individualised" by the diverse relations, which would then inevitably appear as modifications supervening upon the divine substance.

When it is said that the relations are identified with the divine substance, it is meant that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are the same God—not that each is a modified part of that God, but that each is absolutely and completely God. They are differentiated solely by the opposition of relation. The Eleventh Council of Toledo had already expressed this matter as follows:

> "Thus we confess and believe that each Person particularly is fully God; and the Three one single God. Their single, undivided, and equal divinity, majesty, and power is neither diminished in each one nor augmented in the three; for there is nothing less when each Person is individually called God, nor is there anything more when the three Persons are together called one God."[^43]

In like manner, defining the divine Persons as subsistent relations prevents conceiving them as though they were already constituted prior to the relation—that is, as though the relations emanated from already constituted hypostases and were merely the expression of a pre-existing difference between them. For Saint Thomas, the difficulty is that, if the distinction among the Persons did not stem from relations, it would have to stem from the substance, in which case there would not be a total identification of each hypostasis with the divine essence.[^44]

Saint Bonaventure takes a different view. For him, as for Alexander of Hales, the relations are not constitutive of the Persons, but only their characteristics. The Persons are distinct by reason of their properties of origin. The relations do nothing more than manifest these properties. Duns Scotus follows this line: according to him, the Son and the Spirit are constituted and distinguished by their respective modes of proceeding, and not by their relation of opposition.[^45]

### Trinity of Persons and Unity of Essence

From the perspective of the theology of the divine Persons, one can better address the very core of the Trinitarian mystery: how unity and trinity coexist in God without any contradiction. Neither does the confession of the Trinity diminish faith in the one God, nor does the affirmation of the one God lead us to conceive Him as solitary, or to regard the Persons as mere modes of being or appearing of God. The definition of person as subsistent relation, by placing the accent upon relationality, helps us grasp the affirmation of the oneness of the divine substance in all its radicality.

Indeed, to subsist exclusively as relation is to be entirely *for the other*. This means that the divine Persons find their very identity precisely in their mutual reference. For this reason, the most crucial task in the treatise on the Trinity does not consist in applying to God a concept of person slavishly copied from the human person, but in knowing how to employ analogy. Were this not done, one would fall into an unchristian anthropomorphism. In Trinitarian theology, the core of the concept of person emerges from the constitutive importance that relation possesses within it as total self-giving to the other. In truth, the divine person is nothing other than relation that subsists; apart from the opposition of relation, all is one in the three Persons.

The essential Johannine definition of God—"God is love" (1 Jn 4:16)—is fulfilled in Trinitarian life in a manner that transcends all knowledge. God is not merely a being full of love; He is Love itself, constituted as a tripersonal being precisely through His eternal self-giving, through His mutual relations of knowledge and love. He is subsistent Love. It can be said with utter rigour that the intra-Trinitarian life is nothing other than a life in infinite and eternal donation, to the extent that the structure of the Persons is essentially and prior to all else relation, the giving of self.

Throughout history, the person has been considered from a threefold perspective: the ontological, as subsistence; the psychological, as self-consciousness; and the dialogical, as communion and donation. These perspectives are not contradictory, but complementary. In the divine Persons, subsistence, knowledge, and self-giving are held together in perfect harmony.[^46]
