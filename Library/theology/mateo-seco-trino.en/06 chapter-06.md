## Chapter 6: Anti-Trinitarian Heresies {#mateo-seco-trino.6}

As was seen in the preceding chapter, the Holy Fathers had to confront the objections that Jewish monotheism and pagan polytheism raised against Trinitarian doctrine. But misunderstandings of the radical novelty of the God of Jesus Christ were not found only outside the Church; within her also divisions and heresies arose. These heresies were, in fact, so important a stimulus for formulating with precision the Trinitarian doctrine contained in the earliest narrative theology that, without knowledge of them, it is impossible to grasp patristic affirmations in their proper dimensions. For in order to preserve the identity of the faith, it was necessary to counter the "rationalisation" to which the heresies attempted to subject Christian doctrine. Yet, in turn, this process of rationalisation could only be effectively confronted by employing a conceptual precision that compelled the Fathers to maintain great rigour of language.

The problem was always the same: how to combine divine unity with the trinity of Persons. The rationalising temptations were ever poised between these two poles: sacrificing the trinity of God to His unity, or sacrificing unity to trinity. Throughout history, the most frequent attempts to rationalise the mystery have been those that sacrifice the trinity of Persons to the unity of God. This is readily understandable: for Christianity—as has been seen from the Creeds—the oneness of God is the first and fundamental dogma. The fact that the existence of three Persons in God is affirmed does not in any way diminish the confession of His oneness.

### Monarchianism

Monarchianism denies the trinity of Persons in God by the method of affirming solely the Person of the Father. The objective of Monarchianism is clear: to defend Christianity against the accusation of ditheism for affirming that Christ is God equal to the Father. The paths chosen to deny this plurality of Persons in God have been two: either to deny that Christ is truly God, or to deny that He is a subsistent really distinct from the Father. There are, therefore, two distinct strands of Monarchianism.

The first strand makes Christ a divinised man, that is to say, a man adopted by God as son with such power that it "may be said" that he is God, though he is not so in reality; he is nothing more than a divinised man. For this reason it is called adoptionist Monarchianism.[^1] It is also called dynamic modalism, since it claims that Jesus is the adoptive son of God in the sense that in Him dwells in a special manner the divine power, the *dynamis* of God.

The second strand does indeed say that Christ is God, but denies that He is really distinct from the Father; He would only be one of the modes in which God has revealed Himself to us. Hence the name modalist Monarchianism. Some of these Monarchians, to make even more forceful their assertion that Christ is merely a mode in which God has revealed Himself to us, go so far as to affirm that it can even be said that the Father suffered on the Cross. Hence the moniker Patripassianists.

It was Tertullian who characterised these currents with the name Monarchianism, succeeding in grouping them around their most distinctive feature: the affirmation that there exists only one God, who is at the same time a single monarch, a single person who is now called Father, or Son, or Spirit. But this fatherhood is not real, for it is not affirmed that the Word is distinct from the Father: "Either he is Father or he is Son," Tertullian argues, "for day is not the same as night, nor is the Father the same as the Son, so that both should be at once one and the other, which is what these utterly shallow Monarchians want."[^2]

Nevertheless, the word *monarchy*—not the term *Monarchianism*—and the concept included in the term monarchy possess a correct meaning, which would be highlighted with the passage of time. There is an authentic monarchy in the Trinity in the sense that the Father is the Father, that is, the One who begets the Son and spirates the Holy Spirit. For this reason He is the sole first principle, fountainhead and origin of the entire Trinity, *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*. There is, then, an authentic monarchy in God, and so the Fathers unanimously affirm.[^3]

#### Adoptionist Monarchianism

One may take as the earliest datum concerning the existence of adoptionism that which Saint Irenaeus provides, around the year 180, regarding the Ebionites. According to the Ebionites, Christ, although He is the "chosen one of God" and the true prophet, is nothing more than *nudus homo*, a mere man.[^4] Some Ebionites say that Jesus was begotten by Mary and Joseph; others, that He was born of a virgin; all deny His pre-existence prior to the Incarnation.[^5] They are assumed to have been Jewish converts to Christianity who lived in accordance with the Jewish law.[^6] In this context, when Jesus is presented as the Son of God, it is taken for granted that this is an adoptive sonship.

Eusebius of Caesarea († ca. 339) regards Theodotus of Byzantium, also called "the leather-worker", as the first champion of dynamic Monarchianism. According to Theodotus, Christ was only an ordinary man, born of the Virgin Mary.[^7] Saint Hippolytus adds that, for Theodotus, Jesus was endowed with the divine *dynamis* when the Spirit descended upon Him at His baptism. This *dynamis* supposedly turned Christ into a superior man, but nothing more. Some of the Theodotians say that Christ received His divinisation at the Resurrection.[^8] Theodotus was condemned by Pope Saint Victor († ca. 198). Among his followers are numbered Artemon († ca. 235) and Paul of Samosata († ca. 272).[^9]

Paul of Samosata is undoubtedly the most celebrated among the adoptionists. He was bishop of Antioch between the years 260 and 268. His doctrine is known to us only very fragmentarily and solely through his adversaries. According to Eusebius, Paul of Samosata taught that Christ is not truly God, but simply and purely man.[^10] According to Saint Athanasius, Paul denied that Christ was the eternal Son of the Father.[^11] At the same time, he affirmed that the Logos was nothing more than an impersonal divine property and power that had been bestowed upon Christ to guide Him.[^12] The Son and the Spirit, according to Paul of Samosata, were only divine powers, identified with a single divine Person: the Father. Paul employed the term *homoousios* to signify that the Logos is not a distinct hypostasis from the Father, but forms with Him an undifferentiated essence.[^13] For this reason, at the Synod of Antioch in the year 269, the term *homoousios* was rejected as a concept applicable to the Logos. Later, at the Council of Nicaea, the term *homoousios* would be key to confessing the perfect equality of nature of the Son with the Father, but now with a different meaning.

#### Modalist Monarchianism

The modalist form of Monarchianism was the most widespread and, certainly, is the best known.[^14] Numbered among its principal exponents are Noetus, Praxeas, and Sabellius. The modalist Monarchians, designated Sabellians in the East from the third century onward owing to the influence Sabellius exerted upon the development of this heresy, wished to recognise only a unipersonal God. Desiring to affirm, at the same time, that Christ is God, they sought a way to preserve this oneness of person in God by saying that Christ is not a subsistent distinct from the Father, but a distinct mode of manifestation of the Father.[^15] The initiator of this tendency was Noetus, bishop of Smyrna, who around the year 180 preached the identity of the Son with the Father. This identity is such that, for example, Noetus affirms that it is the Father who suffers on the Cross. Hence these modalist Monarchians are also called Patripassianists.[^16]

The doctrine of Praxeas is known above all through the refutation made of it by Tertullian around the year 213.[^17] This refutation is based upon the incompatibility existing between modalism and Christian doctrine concerning salvation: in order to defend the monarchy, they accuse Catholics of being polytheists and—Tertullian shrewdly remarks—"they take fright at the economy of salvation."[^18]

The modalism of Noetus and Praxeas referred expressly to the Father and the Son, without mentioning the Holy Spirit. With Sabellius, condemned by Pope Saint Callistus around the year 220, modalism reached its most complete formulation, explicitly including the Holy Spirit. According to Sabellius, God manifested Himself first in the person of the Father as creator, then in the person of the Son as redeemer, and finally in the person of the Holy Spirit as giver of life and sanctifier. In all these interventions, God would always be the same person, who had manifested Himself in diverse forms.[^19] To designate this threefold mode of appearance, Sabellius used the word *prosōpon* in its etymological sense: mask. In manifesting Himself, God had put on the mask—*prosōpon*—first of Father, then of Son, and finally of Holy Spirit. Sabellius spoke, then, of a trinity in God, but this trinity was not real in itself, but a mere manifestative appearance.[^20]

Modalism is directly opposed to the Trinitarian dimension of salvation as we confess it in baptism. This is seen with particular clarity in Sabellius. For him there is no simultaneous Trinity, but a successive one: the Father manifests Himself as Father in creating, as Son in dying, as Spirit in sanctifying. For modalism, so to speak, there exists only an economic Trinity; to its manifestation in history there corresponds no immanent Trinity, no Trinity in itself. But the faith of the Church proclaims salvation in baptism as due simultaneously to the Father, the Son, and the Spirit, to the point that, as Origen says, salvation does not take place if the Trinity is not complete.[^21] Indeed, it is not possible to be united to the Father without being grafted into Christ; nor is it possible to be united to Christ except through the sanctifying action of the Spirit. The saving activity of the three Persons must, therefore, be simultaneous.[^22]

The Magisterium reacted to these denials of the Trinity less rapidly than the theologians, perhaps because these formulations scarcely reached the ordinary faithful.[^23] Pope Zephyrinus (ca. 198–217) rejected Patripassianism, affirming that "it is not the Father who died, but the Son."[^24] The most important intervention was the excommunication of Sabellius by Pope Saint Callistus (ca. 217–222). With this excommunication the active period of ancient modalism came to an end.[^25] Pope Saint Dionysius (ca. 260–268) wrote to his namesake Dionysius of Alexandria, rejecting the doctrine of Sabellius, which asserts "that the Son is the Father and vice versa."[^26] The First Council of Constantinople (a. 381) included the rejection of Sabellianism in a general formula: "To anathematise every heresy, and in particular that of the Eunomians or Anomoeans, that of the Arians or Eudoxians, and that of the Semi-Arians or Pneumatomachians, that of the Sabellians, Marcellians, that of the Photinians, and that of the Apollinarians."[^27] A similar formula was likewise employed by the Roman Council of the year 382, as recorded in the *Tomus Damasi*: "We anathematise also those who follow the error of Sabellius, saying that the Father is the same as the Son."[^28]

### Gnostic Theogonies and Dualism

The most widespread and powerful hostile currents against which the Fathers had to struggle in the early centuries were the Gnostic sects. The world of gnosis was a motley assortment of figures, religious ideas, and speculations which, on the question of the mystery of God, stood in frontal opposition to Trinitarian doctrine and to the economy of salvation as preached by the Church.

Gnosis already existed prior to the appearance of Christianity, and into it had flowed oriental doctrines, Jewish elements, and Hellenic thought, forming an amalgam that proved alluring to many intellectuals of the second to fourth centuries.[^29] From oriental religions, Gnosticism took the irreconcilable dualism between the world and God, between spirit and matter, and the theory of the origin of evil as a degradation that begins within the very bosom of the divinity; from Hellenism it received the taste for speculation and dialectic.[^30]

The name by which this entire variegated world is designated is Gnosticism. This name is used because it describes the feature most common to it: the importance attached to *gnosis*, to knowledge, to self-consciousness. Indeed, all these sects agree in placing man's salvation in knowledge, or rather, in self-knowledge. Man is saved, according to them, by becoming aware of his kinship with the divinity and, therefore, that he is superior to this world.[^31] It is necessary to distinguish clearly between gnosis and Gnosticism, as did Saint Irenaeus, Clement of Alexandria, and Origen, who always defended the existence of an authentic Christian gnosis.[^32]

Gnosticism is simply a pathological manifestation of gnosis. The Gnostic is convinced that he is saved because he believes he possesses a perfect knowledge of who he is. It is a knowledge received through the illumination of an interior experience. This experience procures salvation for the Gnostic, since in it he discovers that he is not of this world, but of divine origin. He thus awakens from the slumber in which matter holds him plunged.

The Gnostics agree in affirming that within the interior of the divinity an emanation process (*probolē*) of aeons has taken place, which constitutes a degradation of the divine within the very interior of the divinity itself. At times they enumerate up to thirty aeons. Gnostic theogony has nothing to do with Trinitarian doctrine, which can never be understood as a theogony. It is said that the Son and the Spirit proceed from the Father, but not that they emanate from Him and, still less, that they entail an immanent degradation of the divine substance.

Alongside this theogony, Gnosticism professes a dualism that sets God and the world in frontal opposition. The world is not created by God, but is the aberrant fruit of this internal degradation of the divinity. It is the byproduct of Sophia's sin. The maker of the world is not God, but a demiurge who, moreover, is foolish and blind. God is completely alien to the world, to the extent that one cannot enter into contact with Him except through intermediary beings, increasingly distant from the divinity.

It is the whole of Christian teaching concerning the mystery of God and of salvation that the Gnostics subvert. Specifically, they subvert Christian teaching on the nature of God, the mystery of the Trinity, the creation of the world, God's relation to the world, man as the image of God, the work of the Redeemer, and the salvation of man.[^33] With regard to the Trinitarian mystery, two were the principal truths the Fathers had to defend against the Gnostics: the equality of the three divine Persons, and that they are only three.[^34]

This equality among the divine Persons demanded the rejection of all dualism, whether duality is conceived as existing within the bosom of God, or thought of as two distinct gods, even if one is more powerful than the other. They reject both the dualism that attributes the creation of the world to a being distinct from the one God, and that which attributes the salvation of man to a God distinct from the Creator.

The first type of dualism occurs especially among the Gnostics and their speculations concerning the demiurge. Cerinthus (first century), for instance, said that "the world was not created by the primary God, but by a power situated at a great distance from the supreme power."[^35] An example of another type of dualism is found in Marcion († 160),[^36] who said that the God of the Old Testament—the Creator—was evil and incompatible with the God of the New Testament, the Father of Jesus Christ, who is good and merciful. Against him the Fathers—and especially Tertullian—insisted upon the unity of the two Testaments.[^37] For Christian faith demands the rejection of every form of dualism, since the affirmation that there exists one only God belongs to Trinitarian doctrine, and, in consequence, the affirmation that the Saviour and the Creator constitute one and the same God.

Very diverse judgements have been passed upon Gnosticism throughout history, among many other reasons because its boundaries are not precise. All authors agree that many of its masters—for example, Valentinus, Basilides, Heracleon—were endowed with great speculative ability. Some have described them as the first Christian theologians, holding them responsible at the same time for the Hellenisation of Christianity;[^38] the Fathers—Saint Irenaeus, Saint Hippolytus, Saint Epiphanius—did not consider them Christians, since they judged their thought to be totally incompatible with Christian doctrine; recent authors have observed that the Gnostics succeeded in expressing certain aspects of Christian doctrine in a more satisfactory manner than their orthodox contemporaries.[^39]

Although some expressions of the Gnostics are Christian, the fundamental lines of their thought are radically incompatible with the Christian faith. Pre-eminent among these lines are their theogony and their dualism, which links with their contempt for the flesh, leading them to deny an authentic incarnation of the Word. Docetism—namely, the affirmation that the Lord's body was merely an appearance—is general among the Gnostics. Their dualism prevented them from taking salvation history seriously and, therefore, from grasping that this history is essentially Trinitarian, being a communication of the intimate life of God to man.[^40] This misunderstanding is inseparable from their theogony: the saviour is nothing more than an intermediary being between God and the world.[^41]

### Arian Subordinationism

To affirm that the Father is the fountainhead and origin of the entire Trinity is equivalent to affirming the existence of an order within God. There is a priority of the Father, precisely inasmuch as He is the source of the divinity of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. In this sense it may be said that the Father is greater, for from Him the other two divine Persons proceed. To deny this would mean denying that the Father truly begets. All that the Son has He receives from the Father. Indeed, we call the Father the first Person, the Son the second, and the Holy Spirit the third. Nevertheless, this internal order of the Trinity—which derives from the order of procession—cannot signify a subordination in the ontological realm. The Son is equal to the Father in everything, for He is God of God. To deny this would be equivalent to denying perfect sonship and, in consequence, would be equivalent to denying the Father's perfect fatherhood. One may speak, then, of order in the Trinity, and in this sense one may speak of subordination; but one cannot speak of ontological subordination, as if the Person of the Son were ontologically inferior to that of the Father.

Subordinationism is the affirmation of an ontological subordination within the Trinity. As has already been seen, the term subordinationism is sometimes applied to presentations of the Trinitarian mystery elaborated by certain authors who, while remaining substantially orthodox, use expressions that seem to imply an ontological subordination. It is necessary to distinguish between a real subordinationism and a verbal subordinationism. This verbal subordinationism is nothing other than an imperfect expression of the order existing within the bosom of the Trinity. This is seen in many expressions of the ante-Nicene Fathers, who never hesitated to affirm the divinity of the three Persons.[^42] Bearing in mind that theological terminology was not yet perfectly developed, these expressions must not be taken in a heretical sense, but only as what they are: imperfect expressions.[^43]

Subordinationism properly so called is that which affirms an ontological subordination among the divine Persons. The clearest example is that of Arius († 336), whose heresy filled the whole of the fourth century. Arius denied the divinity of the Word, conceiving Him to be of an inferior nature to the Father. He said that the Word was *poiēma*, that is, a made thing, a creature. To assert this, Arius relied upon phrases of Scripture such as *The Father is greater than I* (Jn 14:28), or upon the application to the Logos of Old Testament texts concerning Wisdom: *From the beginning, and before the ages, he created me* (Sir 24:9 [Vulg. Sir 24:14]; Prov 8:22).[^44] This was equivalent not only to denying the eternal existence of the Son, but also the eternity of the Father's fatherhood.

Arius's first clash with his bishop, Alexander of Alexandria, took place at a meeting of the entire clergy in the middle of church. Arius accused Alexander of having taught erroneous doctrines concerning the Son's filiation. Alexander asked him not to teach that there was a moment when the Word began to exist. Arius did not obey his bishop. Here is how Alexander describes Arius's position and his own position in a letter addressed to all bishops: "To our beloved and most esteemed colleagues of the Catholic Church everywhere: (...) Here is what they [Arius, Achillas, etc.] say, fabricating inventions against the Scriptures: God has not always been Father, but once God was not Father. The Word of God did not always exist, but came into existence out of nothing. For God made out of nothing him who did not exist. Therefore once he did not exist. So the Son is a creature and a made thing (...) We, the bishops of Egypt and Libya, assembled to the number of one hundred, have anathematised the partisans of Arius who had the impudence to utter such statements, and their followers (...)"[^45] Alexander, naturally, sets forth in this letter an extensive scriptural dossier in support of the natural and eternal filiation of the Word, in which the texts most commonly adduced to speak of the Word's perfect divinity figure prominently: Jn 1:1–3; 1:18; 10:15; 10:30; 10:38; 14:9; 2 Cor 4:4.

He certainly does not exaggerate the gravity of Arius's assertions. This is seen in a fragment of the *Thalia*, written by Arius after the year 320: "God was not always Father; but once God was alone without being Father, and later He became Father. The Son did not always exist; for all things having been made out of nothing, and all things being creatures and works, the Word of God also was made out of nothing, and once he did not exist; nor did he exist before being made, but he too had a beginning when he was created. For God was alone, and the Word and Wisdom did not yet exist. Later, when He wished to create us, He then made one and called him Word and Wisdom and Son, in order to create us through him."[^46]

The paragraph is a fine specimen of Arius's thought and of his relationship to earlier theological tradition. Arius is clear: the Word is not eternal. There was a moment when the Word did not exist and, therefore, when the Father was not Father. What is more, the Word was not begotten, but was made; He is a creature. Further still, He was made by the Father with a view to the creation of the world.[^47] There are manifested in Arius's doctrine in extreme form the hazards of the distinction between *logos endiathetos* and *logos prophorikos*. The Word appears in dependence upon the creative will of the Father and, in consequence, in dependence upon the creation of the world. The very existence of the Word depends upon the Father's decision to create the world. Arius frames the Father–Son relationship exclusively in the "economic" realm, without realising that the relations between Father and Son are, prior to that, immanent relations within the bosom of the Godhead.[^48]

The Arian misunderstanding concerning the Trinitarian mystery is also bound up with a Christological error. When speaking of the Incarnation, Arius incorrectly follows the *Logos–sarx* framework so characteristic of the Alexandrians: the Word is united directly to the flesh of Christ, taking the place of a human soul. According to this monophysite conception long antedating Eutyches, the Word would have suffered in His very nature as Word the humiliations and pains of the Passion, something incompatible with the immutability and impassibility proper to God. In consequence, the assertion that the Word was not God, but a creature, became inevitable.[^49]

The Christological problem influenced the Trinitarian error. But the primary cause of Arius's error is found in his conception of the nature of the divinity and in the denial that generation in God is possible. God is eternal, Arius argues. He must, therefore, be unbegotten (*agennētos*), for that which is begotten is posterior to the begetter and, in consequence, is not eternal. Therefore a begotten being cannot be God in the strict sense. Nor can two unbegotten beings exist, because then two gods would exist. Arius is left, then, with no other option than to say that the Word is an *ad extra* production of the Father, that is to say, a creature. And that is what he does.

This means denying that an authentic fatherhood exists in God. For Arius, generation in God is impossible not only on the part of the Son, but above all on the part of the Father. When speaking of generation in God, Arius shows himself incapable of transcending material generation. In consequence, generation within the bosom of the divinity would entail the division of God Himself. One can understand why he finds it utterly unacceptable to say that the Son is begotten of the substance of the Father.[^50] Thus, the names of Father and Son would not in fact be names that can be applied in their proper sense to God.

Saint Athanasius has preserved for us this synthesis of Arius's thought, entitling it *The Blasphemies of Arius*:

> The true God, in that which He is (in His substance), is ineffable to all. He alone has none equal to Him or like Him, nor of equal glory. We call Him unbegotten (*agennētos*) on account of Him who is begotten in His nature (...) He who has no beginning set up the Son as the beginning of things made, and adopted Him as His own Son, having begotten Him. This One has nothing proper to God in that which is proper to Him according to hypostasis, for He is not equal to Him, much less consubstantial (...) There is, then, a triad, but not of equal honour. Their hypostases are not commingled with one another (...) According to His substance, the Father is foreign to the Son, because He exists without beginning (...) The Father was God when the Son did not yet exist (...) Even the Son has not seen His own substance, since, being Son, He exists in reality by the will of the Father. Who can say that He who is from the Father can know and comprehend Him who has begotten Him? It is evident that He who has a beginning is not capable of knowing and comprehending, as He is in Himself, Him who has no beginning.[^51]

Many problems are interwoven in this text of Arius, which exhibits a highly elaborate line of thought. Everything converges towards the same point: the Son is a creature who has not always existed. The Father is beyond all things, and is unknowable even to the Son, for an abyssal ontological difference exists between the Father and the Son. This is equivalent to affirming that the Son is an intermediary being between God and creation. The Arian affinity with Platonic thought and its theory of the demiurge is readily apparent. The Son, confessed in the baptismal profession of faith as equal to the Father, has become in Arian theory the created mediator of creation, a being belonging to an intermediate sphere, as if He were a created sun illuminating the entire universe.[^52]

This subordinationism was the harshest Trinitarian and Christological heresy the Church had to endure. So the history of the turbulent fourth century testifies. The "success" and vigour of Arianism are explained by the fact that it presented a faith "rationalised" to the taste of contemporary ambient culture, and by the perfect symbiosis existing between Arius's position and a large part of Greek thought. Another factor in this success was that Arius, who moved within an Origenist environment, prolonged in an unacceptable manner certain statements of Origen. Such is the case, for example, with the expression *deuteros theos* (second God) applied to the Word, which Arius interpreted as a second-class God.[^53]

Arius denies the Trinity, therefore, by way of denying the equality of the Persons. He affirms that there are three Persons in God, but, in fact, only one is true God: the Father, who is the sole unbegotten, unoriginate, unborn One. The Word, being begotten by the Father, has received being and, therefore, cannot be God. When Arius calls Him God, he understands that He is God "by grace", not by nature. The ultimate reason for this position lies in the fact that Arius denies that an immanent procession can occur in God. Every procession in God would be, therefore, an *actio ad extra*,[^54] and in consequence authentic fatherhood and authentic sonship are impossible in God.[^55]

### The Pneumatomachians

The subordination of the Logos, which is the strictly Arian thesis, was followed in the second half of the fourth century by the affirmation of the subordination of the Holy Spirit, and with it the explicit denial of His divinity. Those who denied it are known by the name of Pneumatomachians—fighters against the Spirit—and also as Macedonians. Macedonius was bishop of Constantinople from 342 to 359. Only after his death was the paternity of this Trinitarian error attributed to him.[^56]

The dispute with Arius had centred upon the question of the perfect divinity of the Word. This question had so absorbed men's minds that, at that time, the divinity of the Holy Spirit was not explicitly raised. It is significant that the Nicene Creed confined itself to this simple mention of the Spirit: "And in the Holy Spirit", without further specification. Faith in the divinity of the Holy Spirit was the peaceful possession of the Church, which, however, did not sufficiently specify His "personality" as distinct from that of the Father and of the Son.

It was logical that if the Arians denied the divinity of the Son, they would deny *a fortiori* the divinity of the Holy Spirit, since He is always mentioned after the Son. And indeed they did deny it, though not explicitly. But awareness that a new heresy had arisen—the heresy against the Spirit—was gained only when those who denied the divinity of the Spirit belonged to the opponents of Arius: they confessed the divinity of the Son, but denied the divinity of the Holy Spirit. Technically, then, this heresy, whose structure of thought is so close to Arius, arose precisely among his adversaries.

The earliest news of this heresy reaches us through Saint Athanasius, who wrote to Serapion around the year 360: "You write to me in distress that some who are undoubtedly opposed to Arius on account of his blasphemy against the Son of God entertain hostile thoughts against the Holy Spirit: they claim that he is not only a creature, but one of the ministering spirits, differing from the angels only in degree."[^57]

Saint Athanasius warns that the denial of the divinity of the Holy Spirit subverts the entire Trinitarian dogma: "In like manner as the Arians, by denying the Son, deny also the Father, so these men, by denying the Holy Spirit, deny also the Son. The two factions have divided the insurrection against the truth between them, so that the one, fighting against the Son, and the other, against the Holy Spirit, arrive at the same blasphemy against the Trinity."[^58]

The problem is perfectly defined: these figures accept the divinity of the Son, but say that the Holy Spirit is a creature. In doing so, they subvert the Trinitarian mystery just as much as if they denied the divinity of the Son. Sozomen attributes this same doctrine to Macedonius.[^59] It is impossible to know what Macedonius's doctrine was exactly, for no work of his setting forth his thought has come down to us. What is certain is that this heresy was spreading throughout the East. The Council of 362 held at Alexandria explicitly affirmed the equality of the Holy Spirit with the Father and with the Son. And Saint Basil, in his *Against Eunomius*, written before 370, was already arguing against this error.[^60]

Historically, the struggle against the Holy Spirit found an initial foundation in Arianism, above all in the radical Arianism of Aetius and Eunomius. This is well demonstrated by Saint Basil when dealing with the Holy Spirit in the three books he wrote against Eunomius. The Arians defended the unity of God by having recourse to ontological subordination. It was logical to extend this subordination to the Holy Spirit: just as the Son is subordinate to the Father, so the Spirit is subordinate to the Son.

The Tropici employed a second reason based upon the mode of procession of the Holy Spirit. The Tropici were the Egyptian Christians spoken of by Saint Athanasius in the letter to Serapion cited above. Saint Athanasius calls them Tropici on account of their manner of arguing from the figures of speech or turns of phrase (*tropoi*) employed by Sacred Scripture. These men defended the *homoousios* of Nicaea, understanding it as the reason for the divinity of the Son: the Son is consubstantial with the Father because He is begotten by Him. But they added: the Holy Spirit is begotten neither by the Father nor by the Son; therefore He cannot be consubstantial with them.[^61]

Finally, the Macedonians found a third argument against the divinity of the Holy Spirit in the fact that the work of creation is not attributed to Him, but only that of sanctification.

The struggle against the Holy Spirit on the part of the Pneumatomachians manifested itself with particular clarity in their hesitations concerning the doxology. Their reticence was the occasion for Saint Basil to write his treatise *On the Holy Spirit*. The Pneumatomachians objected to the Spirit appearing alongside the Father and the Son in coordinated form in the doxology; they would accept only a subordinate form. According to them, one ought not to say "Glory to the Father and to the Son together with the Holy Spirit", but rather "Glory to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit", so that the glory of the Holy Spirit would not be celebrated jointly with that of the Father and of the Son.[^62] Saint Basil rightly argued that this doxology is correct, for it corresponds to the theology contained in the baptismal mandate, which places the three divine Persons in equality of power and honour.[^63]

Unlike the Arians, the Pneumatomachians wrote no major work, and their argumentation is rather meagre. This lack of intellectual vigour explains in part their rapid decline and their disappearance within a short time. The decisive cause of their short lifespan was in all probability the great personality, the spiritual vigour, and the speculative power of the Fathers who, confronting them, defended the divinity of the Holy Spirit, forging a body of thought that would receive its definitive endorsement at the Council of Constantinople (a. 381), as we shall see in the next chapter.

For this reason it is fair to say that the historical and doctrinal importance of the Pneumatomachians lies solely in the fact that, by their denial of the divinity of the Spirit, they compelled the express confession of the "personality" and consubstantiality of the Spirit with the Father and the Son, thereby closing the Trinitarian question properly so called with the formulation of the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed.[^64]
