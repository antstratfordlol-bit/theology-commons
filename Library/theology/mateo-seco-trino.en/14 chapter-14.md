## Chapter 14: Human Knowledge of God {#mateo-seco-trino.14}

In the preceding chapter, questions concerning human knowledge of the existence of God were examined. Now a further step is taken: once his existence has been affirmed, an attempt is made to say something regarding the manner in which we know what God is.

This question already finds a first answer, albeit at a very elementary level, in the very affirmation of God's existence. Indeed, it is impossible to affirm that a thing exists without knowing something of its proper nature.[^1] The question of the existence of God carries with it an implicit pre-understanding of what God is, that is to say, a certain concept of God. It is a concept in which all cultures converge and which is indissolubly bound up with the ideas of infinity, greatness, goodness, and supreme being.

The term to which the ways used to affirm the existence of God lead makes this initial pre-understanding somewhat more explicit. Each argument in favour of the existence of God gives us knowledge of some of his perfections. God is the uncaused cause, the necessary being, infinite perfection, supreme intelligence. These are truly imperfect notions, yet they say something real about the divine nature. Although in shadows, from rational argumentation in favour of the existence of God some divine attributes can be glimpsed. Nevertheless, God as such is not designated in philosophical argumentation by a concrete, personal name. He is only perceived as the cause and source of all things, as the foundational Being of all that exists.

Very different is the biblical approach. Here, God is first and foremost a personal being who intervenes in history: He is the God of the Covenant, the God of revelation, the God who became man in Jesus Christ. Biblical language concerning God constitutes a testimony that evokes in the believer the desire to know more deeply who God is, meditating upon his word and his actions. God becomes intimately personal and near.

Yet once again it must be said that both ways of knowing God—that of reason and that of faith—remain at a great distance from what God really is. God is beyond all knowledge, all image, and all word. This is true also of the God who has revealed himself to us in Christ. As Thomas Aquinas writes, echoing a unanimous tradition, of God "we cannot know what He is, but rather what He is not."[^2] Led by this great truth, the Holy Fathers insisted on the ways of negation, affirmation, and eminence as complementary and necessary paths for speaking of God. Subsequent theology focused its efforts on the analysis of analogy as the key question for being able to think and say something about God. In both cases, what is under discussion is the scope and the limits—above all the limits—of human discourse concerning God.

### The Ways of Affirmation, Negation, and Eminence

The way of affirmation (*thésis*) consists in affirming that all the perfections existing in created things exist in God. This way is grounded in the consideration that God is the cause of all things and must therefore possess in himself all the perfections found in them. It is necessary, then, to affirm that in God there are truth, goodness, beauty, life, etc., which in diverse degrees shine forth in creatures.

This *via affirmationis* must be re-dimensioned by the *via negationis*. The way of negation (*aphaíresis*), proceeding from divine omni-perfection, denies that any kind of imperfection or limitation exists in God. This way radically rectifies the preceding path, for the perfections we perceive in beings can only be predicated of God to the extent and in the manner that they imply no imperfection whatever. God has no limitation nor any contradiction in himself. It is not, therefore, an absolute denial of the existence of these perfections in God, but only the denial of limit and imperfection in God, which is the only way to affirm the existence of these perfections in God in an infinite degree.[^3]

In consistency with this, it is necessary finally to employ the *via eminentiae* (*hyperochē*), which considers that all perfections exist in God in an eminent manner, that is to say, in an infinite degree. It is the other side of the *via negationis*. Once limitation is denied, positive indication is made towards the infinity for which no adequate human concepts exist. As we shall see further on, pure perfections are all really found in God and in an infinite degree. This transcendence over every limited concept accounts for why it has always been said that we do not know of God what He is, but rather what He is not. Saint Gregory of Nyssa expressed this precisely when affirming that, even in accepting divine revelation, we know God only in darkness: "In this consists the true knowledge of what we seek: in seeing in not seeing, for what we seek transcends all knowledge, totally surrounded by incomprehensibility as by a darkness."[^4]

The three ways mutually complement one another, so that it is necessary to employ all three together. Here is how Saint John Damascene expresses it, synthesising the long path traversed by Patristic theology: "Since He is the cause of all things and contains in himself the concepts and causes of all beings, He is also named after everything that exists, even according to opposites, such as light and darkness, water and fire; we must realise that God is not essentially these things. He is supra-essential and nameless. Nevertheless, being the cause of all that exists, He is named after all that is caused."[^5]

### Analogical Knowledge

Even when using the *via eminentiae* correctly, it is necessary to frame thought and language about God within what is called an analogical perspective. The principle of analogy underlines that the statements attained by the *via eminentiae* are not adequate definitions—that is, affirmations corresponding adequately to the reality of God—but rather expressions that manifest simultaneously the relations of similarity and dissimilarity between God and what is said of Him.[^6] Precisely because He infinitely transcends all creation, that dissimilarity is greater than the similarity.

In the human mode of knowing, analogy is a key instrument for acquiring new knowledge and for speaking of God, both by the light of natural reason and by the light of faith. The word analogy signifies similarity, correspondence: from something known, we proceed to know something new, but which bears a certain likeness to what we already know. And we know it precisely in this similarity-dissimilarity. Much of the expressive power of literature lies precisely in the capacity to establish analogies among things.

Analogy has an initial starting point in language itself. It is termed *analogia nominum*, the analogy of names. We can use the same word by attributing it to diverse realities. This occurs, for example, with the word *fox*: it can be attributed to the animal known by this name, or to a man, such as Herod (cf. Lk 13:32). These are very different subjects which, nonetheless, resemble each other in something: in cunning for prey. In this likeness lies the analogy. And from here one moves to the analogy of concepts. In analogy the same word is attributed to several subjects in a sense partly diverse and partly identical. It is, then, a reality situated between univocity and equivocity. Univocity occurs between those names or realities that are identical; equivocity between those that resemble each other in nothing; analogy between those that are partly the same and partly different. It is clear that one cannot speak of God and the world univocally: this would eliminate their essential difference. Nor can one speak equivocally: this would be equivalent to denying divine causality over the world and the fact that man is the image and likeness of God. The way, then, to speak of God and the world can be none other than that of analogy.

Theology is thus urged to use analogical language, both in addressing natural reason and in meditating upon revelation. But it is worth emphasising that the analogy used in theology—even the analogy of being, to which we are primarily referring at present—does not coincide exactly with Aristotelian analogy, although it draws much from it.[^7] Indeed, the very concept of analogy depends on the difference within which the diverse terms to which it applies are framed. The essential difference between the analogy of being as found in Aristotle's thought and the analogy of being used in Christian thought—for example, in Saint Thomas—lies in the "new" relationship between the world and God perceived from faith in creation. The truth of creation *ex nihilo* reveals, on the one hand, the certainty of a close resemblance between God and the world: the certainty arising from the affirmation that everything proceeds from God *secundum totam suam substantiam*; the truth of creation also implies an abyssal dissimilarity: that which stems from the fact that the world does not proceed from God by emanation, but by creation *ex nihilo*—that is, it does not proceed from the divine substance, but from nothingness. To affirm creation *ex nihilo* implies, therefore, affirming that God is not part of this world—He is not an element of it, even as its unmoved mover—but is outside and far above it. The dissimilarity existing between God and the world is consequently, in a certain manner, infinite. Yet God, without being part of the world, imprints his trace upon it insofar as He is its creator. The intelligibility of the world points precisely towards the intelligence of its creator. God did not make the world from pre-existing eternal matter; rather, everything in the world proceeds from God.

On the other hand, the God of faith, the God who has spoken through Jesus Christ, has used human language to speak of himself. This means—with the assurance given by faith—that human language is valid for saying something of God and, therefore, the validity of the analogy of being, since the language used in Revelation—like all human language—involves the interplay between similarity and dissimilarity proper to the ascent in knowledge: that is to say, it implies the analogy of being.

Yet precisely because of this, alongside the analogy of being it becomes necessary to speak of an analogy of faith (*analogia fidei*). We refer to the way in which analogy is used in Revelation. It is a new analogy, created by God himself in the fact of Revelation. This analogy rests upon the analogy of being and enriches it; for, in utilising its same elements, He has endowed them not only with new vigour, but has immensely enriched them with new meanings. The analogy of faith not only confirms the analogy of being but transcends it, since the realities to which it refers transcend the realities known by the mere analogy of being. So to speak, the new relationship of God with men established with revelation, above all with his definitive word in Christ, "creates a new analogy that transcends the mere confirmation of the *analogia entis*."[^8] Such is the case, for example, with the revelation of the mystery of the Trinity, or with the revelation of the personal being of Christ. The same occurs with the analogy of the bread or the vine. The word of God, in referring to this analogy, creates it anew in a certain sense, for it creates a new relationship. The bread and the vine receive a new relationship because they are "apt" to receive that new relationship; yet at the same time, that new relationship absolutely transcends them. For this reason it is said that the *analogia fidei* is grounded in the *analogia entis* and, at the same time, transcends it.[^9]

The analogy of being and the analogy of faith are not opposed to each other, just as the knowledge of faith and natural knowledge are not opposed, but mutually complement each other. Nor are they distinguished in a dualistic manner, as though natural knowledge came first and knowledge of faith came afterwards. In the journey towards God both forms of knowledge are indissolubly linked, to the point that the philosophical moment is found within the theological moment itself, and cannot be reduced to a merely propaedeutic foundation for faith.[^10]

Analogical language seeks to express the relation of similarity-dissimilarity existing between God and creatures. It is a language that both unites and distinguishes. This occurs, for example, when being is applied to God and to creatures. God exists and creatures exist. Nevertheless, the mode of existing is so diverse that, at the same time as it is affirmed that both exist, of the creature it is said that it *has* existence, whereas of God it is said that He *is* the pure act of existing (*actus purus essendi*). Analogy points, then, to similarity, but highlighting likewise the dissimilarity.

Philosophers distinguish two kinds of analogy: the analogy of attribution and the analogy of proportionality. The basis of this distinction is found in the diverse ways in which the relation between the analogue terms is verified. There are names that we apply to things similar in their form, and others that we apply because of the similarity of relation that these things bear to one another. Thus, for example, the vision of the human eye bears a similarity of form with the vision of the mind. This is the analogy of attribution; between colours and the human eye there is a relationship similar to that existing between being and human intelligence. Here lies the analogy of proportionality.[^11]

Extrinsic analogy of attribution occurs when the same name is applied to several subjects, but what the term signifies is properly realised in only one of them. A typical example of this is the word *health* applied to an animal, to food, or to air: to the first it is attributed properly; to the others improperly, that is, only insofar as food and air are causes of health. Intrinsic analogy of attribution occurs when what is signified by the name applied to diverse things is properly found in all of them. Thus, for example, the name of *being* is applied both to substance and to accident: both are beings and are so really, albeit in a very diverse manner. The analogy of attribution always entails an order or priority in the relation established, that is to say, an *analogatum princeps* (primary analogue) and a secondary analogue. In the previous example, being is attributed primarily to substance, which is the primary analogue, and secondarily to accident.

The analogy of proportionality is established between relations. In this type of analogy attention is paid to the similarity existing between two or more relations. At least four terms are therefore required. Thus, for example, A : B :: C : D. The similarity is established between the relation of the terms considered two by two—the relation of A to B and of C to D—and not between the terms themselves. This comparison between relations helps greatly in being able to say something of God, both from the analogy of being and from the analogy of faith. A typical example is that of divine fatherhood: God is with respect to men what a father is with respect to his child. In this case, the analogy is established between both relations of fatherhood: that of men with their children and that of God with men.

Once again we must turn our gaze to the structure of human intelligence. Our knowledge always begins from the senses. We can only know God through creatures—even the God of Revelation, who can only speak to us through human events and words. However revealed the words of Jesus Christ may be, they do not cease to be human and created words, incapable in themselves of adequately signifying the divine reality. For this reason, everything said of God—even in faith—must be said in an analogical manner.[^12]

To God both the analogy of attribution and that of proportionality can be applied. Both help us to know something of Him. At the foundation of any application of analogy lies the reality of creation, that is, of the relation between the creature and God. It is in this realm that intrinsic analogy of attribution is understood: the perfections shining forth in created being can be attributed to God, because created being proceeds from God as from its efficient and exemplary cause. Being, truth, and goodness are really attributed to God and to creatures, though in a vastly diverse manner; but these perfections are properly and really in both. Upon this analogy of attribution is grounded the analogy of proportionality. The father-child relation can be applied to God and man, generating new knowledge concerning God, because in both terms—God and man—are found, though in diverse degree and manner, the essential elements of the relation of fatherhood.[^13]

Hitherto, in speaking of analogy and its application to knowledge of the divine nature, the analogy of being has been in the foreground, based upon the fact of creation and what this truth entails when considering God-world relations. But God, too, in revealing himself to mankind, has utilised human language, and inevitably has utilised analogy. It suffices to recall the beautiful parables of Our Lord Jesus Christ. This use of analogy, while confirming its validity for saying something about God, enriches analogy itself. Human words—without ceasing to be human—contain a divine mystery, constitute a special sign of the mystery, and are the very presence of that mystery.

This occurs pre-eminently in the revelation of the Trinitarian mystery. God is Trinity; He is unique, but not solitary. The words used for his revelation indicate the human realities through which something can be glimpsed: Father, Son, and Spirit are not abstract ciphers without any contact with the realities of which men speak. The fact that these words were chosen gives them in themselves a special value and a new meaning. Not an equivocal meaning with respect to the former, but a new meaning, not uprooted from its prior meaning. The Son is Son and Word. The terms used to speak to us of Him were not chosen by God at random. For this reason, the acceptance of analogy is an internal moment of the acceptance of revelation, including also the analogy of being, which is an internal moment of the new analogy established in the language of the revealed texts.

Between God and creation there is greater dissimilarity than similarity. Analogical language—including revelation—is therefore a language that reveals and conceals at the same time. Knowledge of God, as highlighted by the Greek Patristic tradition, takes place in darkness. God is always a hidden God (*Deus absconditus*). Nothing is more exemplary in this regard than the mystery of the Cross, the culminating event of divine revelation. The event as such is divine word, revelation of God. In it the mystery of God is manifested, while at the same time hiding in self-emptying (*kenosis*). He speaks to us through human words and events. As has been observed when comparing the analogy of being with the analogy found within faith: "the function of the analogy of being is to direct us towards God, who is beyond the world: analogy in this way shows how religious language must be purified of its anthropomorphism to be set in the direction of that which can be said. The analogy of faith is directed, on the other hand, to letting God enter into language. The analogy of faith unabashedly accepts the need for a certain anthropomorphism."[^14] It is the consequence of accepting that God himself has become the Son of Man.

### Apophatic Theology

The awareness that God is beyond all concept and all word has led numerous authors to emphasize divine transcendence in a form customarily termed apophatic theology or negative theology. This is a theology that seeks to highlight, above all, the inadequacy of any word or thought to refer to the divine nature. God is always beyond all expression.[^15] It is, then, a theological path that proceeds by means of negations in order to draw near to God, transcending every concept.[^16]

In doing so, theologians look to numerous passages of Scripture in which the invisibility and ineffability of God are brought into relief. No one can see the face of God (cf. Ex 33:20–23; 1 Tim 6:16). Negative theology is present in the New Testament, wherein, while affirming that we have seen the glory of the Only-begotten (cf. Jn 1:14), it continues to insist on speaking of God through privative attributes: God is *aóratos*, invisible (Rom 1:20); *árrētos*, ineffable (2 Cor 12:4); *anexereúnētos*, unsearchable (Rom 11:33); *aprósitos*, unapproachable (1 Tim 6:16).

This way of doing theology—which is so often inseparable from mystical theology—confronted us with special clarity when describing fourth-century Greek theology. In this regard, the texts of the Cappadocians are eloquent. It suffices to recall the beautiful passages devoted by Gregory of Nyssa to describing how God can only be known in darkness, knowing in not knowing. Even any revelation God makes entails making clear that He is not enclosed within it. God always transcends every word, even that of revelation:

> "Speaking of God, if it concerns his essence, it is time to be silent. If it concerns his works, knowledge of Him can reach us: it is time to speak of his omnipotence, recounting his works and explaining his actions, and using words within this limit. But in what goes beyond this, the creature must not overstep the boundaries of its condition, but must be content with knowing itself."[^17]

For this reason the deepest knowledge of God to which man can attain is wrapped in sacred silence. In affirming this, Saint Gregory of Nyssa refers not only to the knowledge of God attained by the sole powers of natural reason, but also to the knowledge of God that comes from faith and the illumination of the Holy Spirit. The word of God, too, is a "darkness"—a mystery—into which the soul enters guided by faith. What is more, it is precisely an abyss of mystery.[^18] Mystical life, too, is an abyss of mystery. It is precisely here, at the summit of mystical ascent, that man discovers that God is always beyond. Thus it happens to Moses: "When Moses advances in knowledge, he confesses that he sees God in darkness, that is, he knows now that the Divinity, by its very nature, is that which surpasses all knowledge and all comprehension."[^19] Hence true knowledge of God consists "in seeing in not seeing, for what we seek transcends all knowledge, totally surrounded by incomprehensibility as by a darkness."[^20]

Gregory of Nyssa uses paradoxical language to describe human knowledge of God: it is a seeing in not seeing, seeing amidst luminous darkness, seeing in an ever-growing ecstasy, seeing amidst sober intoxication, seeing amidst vertigo.[^21] We reach a point where negative theology and mystical life intertwine, both swept away by the awareness of divine transcendence and ineffability. It is a way of doing theology that was always present among the Greeks and, in particular, in the so-called School of Alexandria, and which found its most beautiful and definitive expressions in Gregory of Nyssa and Saint John Damascene.[^22]

The Latins, too, frequently allude to divine transcendence, warning that God is always beyond:

> "We wish to seek Him in order to find Him, we wish to seek Him because we have already found Him. If we wish to seek Him to find Him, it is because He is hidden; if we wish to seek Him because we have found Him, it is because He is incomprehensible."[^23]

One of the most radical formulations of negative theology is found in Pseudo-Dionysius (beginning of the sixth century), who exerted a great influence on subsequent theology, both Eastern and Western. Here is the advice he gives to whoever wishes to surrender to mystical contemplation:

> "Renounce the senses, intellectual operations, all that is sensible and all that is intelligible. Strip yourself of all things that are and even of those that are not. Set aside your understanding and strive to ascend as far as you can until you are united with Him who is beyond all being and all knowing."[^24]

The reasoning is clear: if the mysteries of God are beyond all knowledge, one can only reach them by transcending all knowledge. Hence the name *negative theology*: it is not that knowledge of God is denied; rather, what is demanded is the renunciation of mastering God, of rationalising Him. Here is the prayer with which Pseudo-Dionysius begins his *Mystical Theology*:

> "Supra-essential Trinity, more than divine and more than good! Guardian of the divine wisdom of Christians, guide us beyond not knowing and light, unto the highest peak of the mystical Scriptures. There the mysteries of the Word of God are simple, absolute, immutable in the more than luminous darkness of the silence that reveals secrets. They overflow with dazzling light amidst the blackest darkness. Absolutely intangible and invisible, the mysteries of most radiant splendour flood our dazzled souls."[^25]

We stand before a young theology, conscious of the greatness of God. The possibility of knowing God is not denied; rather, his transcendence is reaffirmed by exalting the role of the *via negativa* for authentic knowledge of divine intimacy, since "every negation falls short before the transcendence of Him who is absolutely simple and stripped of all limitation."[^26] Pseudo-Dionysius is exhorting with these words to a learned ignorance (*docta ignorantia*), not to scepticism. Indeed, he takes for granted that precisely in this darkness something of God is truly known. He is known, but He is not comprehended. It is a luminous obscurity:

> "May we also enter into this more than luminous darkness! Let us renounce all vision and knowledge in order to see and know the invisible and unknowable: Him who is beyond all vision and all knowledge!"[^27]

Dionysian theology powerfully influenced both speculative theology and mystical theology. It may be said that the whole of Byzantine theology stands in the shadow of Dionysian thought, modulated first by Saint Maximus the Confessor († 662) and later by Saint John Damascene († ca. 749), by Symeon the New Theologian († 1022), and by Saint Gregory Palamas († 1359).[^28] Negative theology, however, must not be confused with the Neoplatonic approach. For the Neoplatonists, the unapproachability of God is due to man's present condition, especially his corporeality; for negative theology, this unapproachability is due to God's absolute transcendence over every created being.[^29] It is not an anthropological question, but a divine reality.

In the West, the thought of Pseudo-Dionysius was introduced by John Scotus Eriugena († ca. 877), who translated the Dionysian corpus into Latin. Nicholas of Cusa († 1464) is customarily cited as one of the authors most influenced by Pseudo-Dionysius.[^30] According to Nicholas of Cusa, God is the Other; and knowledge of Him is beyond opposites; negative theology is superior to affirmative theology.[^31] But one must not forget that the influence of Pseudo-Dionysius—precisely because of his authority and because he points to an undeniable reality, divine transcendence—is not confined to these authors, but reaches many important Latin theologians, even if, strictly speaking, they are not classed among the authors belonging to negative theology. Some of these authors, such as Saint Albert the Great and Saint Thomas Aquinas, dedicated extensive commentaries to Pseudo-Dionysius's *De divinis nominibus*. The Thomistic assertion that God is known only as unknown belongs precisely to these commentaries. In line with Pseudo-Dionysius is also the reminder that of God we know not what He is, but what He is not.[^32] The influence of negative theology is also very strong in important authors of Western spirituality. This is seen, for example, in Saint John of the Cross, who during his studies assiduously read Pseudo-Dionysius.[^33] His conception of the mystical life as an ascent amidst the dark night, transcending all knowledge, evidently evokes this rich current of thought. It suffices to recall these verses of the Mystical Doctor in which he gathers the rich current of apophatic spirituality: "I entered in—I knew not where— / and remained unknowing, / transcending all science."[^34]

### The Noetic Transcendence of God

The transcendence of God in Being is accompanied—as the other side of the same coin—by transcendence over all created knowledge. This is so, fundamentally, by virtue of the nature of God and the nature of all created being. It is not due, as the Neoplatonists thought, to the situation in which man finds himself in this world, to his relationship with matter, such that it would suffice to strip oneself of it to attain knowledge of the divine Being.[^35] The noetic transcendence of God is rooted in the transcendence of the divine being. Only an uncreated concept—an uncreated and eternal image, living and subsistent, like God himself—can adequately express God. Both divine being and divine life—which are identical—are beyond every created word or concept. For this reason, the divine being is radically and essentially incomprehensible and ineffable. He is essentially mysterious. Neither men nor angels can comprehend Him. As Thomas Aquinas writes, "the vision of the divine essence infinitely exceeds every created substance."[^36] As has already been seen, both the passages of Sacred Scripture and the patristic texts affirming the ineffability of God are numerous and clear. Clear also is the constant teaching of the Magisterium regarding divine incomprehensibility and ineffability.[^37] Negative theology is, therefore, perfectly justified.

And yet, alongside the biblical passages affirming absolute divine transcendence, there are other texts speaking with equal clarity of an intuitive and face-to-face vision of God in heaven. The saints see God as He is (cf. 1 Jn 3:2). It is a full and direct knowledge: *For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall know fully, even as I have been fully known* (1 Cor 13:12). We find ourselves, then, before a paradox: the God who is above all and who transcends all, while remaining transcendent, truly communicates himself to man in clear vision.[^38] Man—who can in no way comprehend God—can, nevertheless, know Him in the only manner in which this is possible: by means of the intuitive and face-to-face vision. To this extent—unspeakable and ineffable—does the divinisation of man reach: without ceasing to be man, he is elevated to the point of seeing God with the eyes of the soul.

It is understandable that some theologians have sought to overcome this paradox by distinguishing in God between essence and energies. Even in heaven, God's essence would remain invisible and unknowable, whereas divine energies—like a divine radiance—would indeed be within the reach of divinised man. This distinction already appeared in the controversy with Eunomius and, later, in Pseudo-Dionysius. In Saint Gregory Palamas it acquires nuances distinct from the Dionysian ones and becomes essential, precisely because it is used to resolve the aporia between God's transcendence and human knowledge.[^39] According to this distinction, in the beatific vision the divine essence would not be seen immediately in itself, but in the divine energies, which at times seem identified with the grace of adoption.[^40]

This distinction, however, does not seem to resolve anything, precisely because of the absolute transcendence and simplicity of God. Indeed, if the energies truly manifest the divine essence, they themselves are the divine essence; and if they are not the divine essence—but created gifts that divinise man—then they are incapable of directly manifesting the divine essence, and, consequently, scriptural texts speaking of seeing God face to face must be interpreted in a metaphorical sense, which is unacceptable.[^41]

Catholic theology has always understood these passages—and other parallel ones—in a strong sense: the righteous will contemplate God face to face; they will contemplate Him in his intra-Trinitarian intimacy.[^42] It is, then, an extremely close personal union between man and God, a dialogue of knowledge and love in which the three divine Persons are known and loved immediately in themselves. This implies, given the divine simplicity, that the divine Persons must communicate themselves directly to man, themselves, without any objective mediation, for there is nothing outside God that can express Him as He is.[^43]

The immediate vision of the divine essence by man becomes much more momentous if one takes into account an anthropological perspective of primary importance. In this vision, fulfillment—while transcending it—is given to the desire for happiness that dwells in the human heart. Saint Thomas Aquinas has recourse to this intimate structure of man to resolve the objections of those who think that the beatific vision is impossible. Here is his argument:

> "This opinion is not acceptable [that of those who say the beatific vision is impossible], because, since man's ultimate happiness consists in the highest of his operations, which is that of the intellect, if the intellect can never see the divine essence, it follows either that man would never attain his happiness, or that his happiness consists in something other than God, which is contrary to faith; for the ultimate happiness of the rational creature consists in that which is the principle of its being, since a thing is perfect to the extent that it is united with its principle."[^44]

We stand before a paradoxical argument: on the one hand, it is taken for granted that God transcends all creation; on the other hand, reference is made to a desire to see Him, a desire inscribed in human nature itself. A desire that divine providence cannot leave void.

The existence of this desire is a constant affirmation in Christian doctrine, as highlighted in Saint Augustine's well-known exclamation: "You made us, Lord, for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."[^45] Throughout history, diverse solutions have been sought by theologians to this paradox—namely, to the fact that man desires with all his strength something that totally surpasses him, such as the vision of God, to the point that, if he does not attain it, he would remain definitively frustrated.[^46] On the one hand, it is clear that man's sole end consists in this supreme and intimate union with God. From here stems, in the final analysis, the desire to see Him. Indeed, if the vision of God is man's end, it is logical that man should yearn for it, even if confusedly. On the other hand, it is also clear that this union with God infinitely transcends any created faculty; therefore, one must accept that man has inscribed in his nature the desire for something that infinitely transcends him. It is one more paradox of this paradoxical being that is man.

The solution to this paradox must be found along the path already indicated by Saint Augustine: in the fact that the heart of man is made for God; that is to say, in the affirmation that the vocation to this intimate dialogue with the Most Blessed Trinity is the ultimate reason for human existence. There has never been a moment in which man has not been addressed by this divine call, for the vocation to intimate life with God is the ultimate reason for human existence. This is the reason for the in a certain sense infinite capacity of the human heart, to the point that it can find rest in none other than God. And this is the reason for the desire of which Saint Thomas speaks here.[^47]

### The Question of the Divine Names

The noetic transcendence of God leads to posing the question of how we can speak of Him. Indeed, we speak of things to the extent that we know them, since names and propositions are by nature signs that we use to express concepts. But it has already been said that there is no created concept capable of expressing the divine being. Neither, then, will there exist any name that can express the being of the Divinity. That is why God is said to be ineffable (*árrētos*) and, at the same time, is called the One of many names, the One of all names.[^48] To God, indeed, can be applied the names of all perfections.

Sacred Scripture attaches supreme importance to the name of God. This is evident in numerous passages, especially in the revelation of the name Yahweh (cf. Ex 3:14). So important is the divine name that the second petition of the Lord's Prayer is precisely *hallowed be thy name* (Mt 6:9), and constant, humble invocation of the name of God forms an essential part of Christian piety. On the one hand, then, God is ineffable and beyond all names; on the other, He can be named with names that, while not expressing Him perfectly, do indicate something of what He is.

Arriving at this point, it is necessary to make precise distinctions that are classic in theological thought. First, distinguishing between the reality signified by the terms with which we designate God (*res significata*) and the mode in which they signify it (*modus significandi*). Secondly, one must distinguish among the perfections we apply to God: some are pure perfections—that is, their concept involves no imperfection, such as existence, goodness, beauty, etc.; others are impure (or mixed) perfections—that is, their concept involves some imperfection, such as walking, eating, etc. Finally, it is necessary to distinguish the level of knowledge in which we move: whether it is the level of natural knowledge or that of revealed knowledge.

In any case, it must be said that it is impossible to designate God with a name that truly expresses the divine essence in the mode proper to it. That name would indeed have to be simultaneously simple and contain within itself all perfections. This name in fact is none other than God himself. For this reason it is frequent among theologians to affirm that not even in heaven will a proper name be found to designate the divine essence.[^49]

And yet it is possible for man to designate both the attributes and the divine essence with names that truly and properly say something of what God is. Although no name adequately expresses the divine reality, it can express something true of this reality; although the names of all perfections can be applied to God, these names are not synonyms; rather, each designates a distinct aspect of God's infinite perfection. Although in God all perfections are identified, what we designate with the word *justice* is not the same as what we designate with the word *mercy*. Between these concepts there is a genuine distinction of reason.[^50]

If the names we apply to God are not synonyms, it follows that their correct application to God requires certain nuances according to the names in question. Here are the most important, referring to the natural knowledge of God:

a) Certain names are applied to God with all propriety as regards the perfection signified, not as regards the mode of signifying it. These are the terms designating pure perfections, such as wisdom, freedom, love. These perfections, precisely because they entail no imperfection in their concept, are really found in God, but they are not found as we conceive them, since it is impossible for us to conceive anything in an infinite manner.[^51]

b) The names applied to God properly are not synonyms, since, although they designate the same most simple reality—God—they do not designate it under the same aspect. "God is one in reality and multiple in concepts, because our intellect conceives Him with the same multiplicity with which things represent Him."[^52]

c) The names applied to God and to creatures must always be applied in an analogical manner, for this analogy corresponds to the distinct mode and degree in which these perfections exist in God and in creatures.

d) The names designating impure or mixed perfections can only be applied to God metaphorically and always within analogy, since these terms can properly be applied only to creatures, and therefore, in applying them to God, the meaning they have when applied to creatures is always included.[^53]

If we turn now to the names applied to God by Revelation, it is important to keep in mind the following distinctions:

a) Essential names and personal names. Essential names directly designate the perfections of the divine essence, whereas personal names designate the Persons. This distinction is found, obviously, in the New Testament and throughout the theological tradition, for it is inherent to confession of the Trinitarian mystery. The question confronted us with special force in the Eunomian controversy. Essential names are common to the three divine Persons, whereas personal names belong properly to each of them. Thus, the name *Unbegotten* belongs properly to the Father alone, and that of *Son* belongs only to the Word, whereas the names *Holy*, *Immortal*, *Eternal*, etc. belong equally to the three Persons.

b) Proper names and appropriated names. This concerns the diverse way in which names can be applied to the divine Persons. Proper names are those applied to each Person as proper to Him, without being applicable to the others. To the first Person are applied the names Father, Principle, and Unbegotten; to the second Person, those of Son, Word, and Image of the Father; and to the third, the names Holy Spirit, Love, and Gift. Some of these names, in turn, can be used in an essential sense or in a personal sense. Such is the case with the name Love, used at times to designate the divine essence or the Person of the Holy Spirit. Appropriated names are those that, while belonging to the three divine Persons, are attributed especially to one of them because of the special affinity that this perfection has with that Person. Thus, for example, omnipotence is attributed especially to the Father, wisdom to the Son, and Love to the Holy Spirit.[^54]

Among the divine names contained in Sacred Scripture, theologians customarily attach special importance to that of the Sinaitic revelation: *I am who I am* (Ex 3:14), to the point of considering it the most proper of essential names: "He who is," writes Thomas Aquinas, "is the most proper of all the names of God (...) This name does not signify a determined form, but being itself; and since the being of God is his very essence, and this belongs to none other than Him, this will undoubtedly be, among all, the name that designates Him with greatest propriety, for beings take their name from their form."[^55] It is, as the Catechism of the Catholic Church states:

> "A Divine Name that is mysterious just as God is Mystery. It is at once a Name revealed and something like the refusal of a name, and by that very fact it better expresses God as what He is, infinitely above all that we can understand or say: He is the 'hidden God' (Is 45:15), his name is ineffable (cf. Judg 13:18)."[^56]
