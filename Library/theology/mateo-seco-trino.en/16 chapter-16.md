## Chapter 16: God's Transcendence Over the Material Universe {#mateo-seco-trino.16}

From the very first words of Sacred Scripture, divine transcendence over the whole created world is affirmed. Yahweh is the Creator and the Lord of all. He is before all things and is above all things. He is supratemporal and supracosmic. He is a *mysterium tremendum et fascinosum*, infinitely surpassing man. Even when God draws definitively near to man in Christ, He remains the holy and sublime God, transcendent yet close. This divine transcendence always and explicitly accompanies the personality of the Christian God, both in Sacred Scripture and in the great tradition of the Church. The confession of the Incarnation has nothing to do with any of the forms of pantheism that will encounter us, above all, in certain thinkers of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The Christian God is an intimately personal God, who has a concrete name (cf. Ex 3:14), who establishes his Covenant with man, who becomes man, but who never enters into composition with the world.[^1]

According to our mode of knowing, this transcendence of God over the cosmos appears, first and foremost, as the attribute that accompanies his fullness of being. He is beyond created entities because He does not possess being by participation, but is Being by essence. He has not arisen from nothingness, but is existence itself. The *ipsum esse subsistens* has no defect—no *potentialitas*—in his full self-possession.

### The Simplicity of God

Here once again we encounter the condition of our human knowledge, whose point of departure is what enters through the senses. From this perspective, it is evident that complexity is equated with richness and with a higher quality of being. In our world, a higher level in the degrees of being corresponds to a greater complexity in composition. Therefore, in treating this question it is necessary to bear in mind that the simplicity of which we speak is not opposed to richness, plurality, or multiplicity, but to composition. We do not say that God is not fullness of life, or that He does not possess a life infinitely rich in thought and love; the only thing we say is that God is not the result of any composition. God is not composed of parts, He is neither vulnerable nor divisible; nor is He a totality composed of distinct metaphysical principles—such as essence and existence, substance and accident—but a unity that contains within itself all perfection, a pure and infinite act of being.[^2] Divine simplicity expresses the metaphysical unity of God.

This simplicity has been solemnly affirmed by the Magisterium of the Church, thus gathering up the teaching of Sacred Scripture and a unanimous theological tradition. God is *natura simplex omnino*, says the Fourth Lateran Council;[^3] God is *una ... omnino simplex et incommutabilis substantia spiritualis*, teaches the First Vatican Council, having specifically in view the various pantheisms found in nineteenth-century Western culture.[^4]

Yet how is it possible to speak in human language, which is composite, of Him who is infinitely simple? The best course is to begin by employing the *via negativa*, that is, by removing from God all composition, in order subsequently to attempt to say something globally concerning his simplicity. This is the path followed with special rigour by Saint Thomas Aquinas. Here is his itinerary:

a) God has no quantitative composition, that is, God is not a body. Everything corporeal is composed of parts that complement one another in a whole, and which are therefore inferior to the totality. But in God there is nothing more perfect or less perfect, for there is nothing limited. Hence there can be no parts composing a whole. If God were a body, precisely through being extended, He would be divisible and would entail some potentiality—among others, that proper to motion—and therefore could not be pure act;

b) For this same reason, neither can there be in God any composition of matter and form, since that composition entails limitation and potentiality, divisibility, and essential imperfection;

c) For this same reason, in God essence and nature are identified. God possesses nothing, but is everything that He possesses. There is in Him nothing accidental. The distinction existing among the three divine Persons cannot be conceived as something deriving from a distinction between substance and accidents. The divine relations cannot be conceived as though they were accidental modes of the being of God;

d) There can be no composition of essence and existence in God, for every essence that enters into composition with existence behaves as a potency that is "fulfilled" or actualised by its act;[^5]

e) Nor can God enter into composition as part of anything—as though one could envisage a whole formed by God and the world—because God, precisely on account of his perfection, cannot be perfected by anything. Indeed, following the axiom that the whole is greater than its parts, the sum of God and the world would be more perfect than God alone, which would be equivalent to saying that there exists in God the potentiality for a greater perfection: the perfection that would derive from the sum of the world and God.[^6]

The line of argument we have been setting forth reduces to absurdity the possibility of a God who might be composed of anything. Neither is pantheism reasonable—insofar as one can still speak of theism—nor is it reasonable to speak of God in a manner that implies composition, as if God were a sum of his components. As will be seen later in the consideration of the Trinitarian mystery: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are God, but they do not form parts of God. God is not the sum of the three divine Persons. The divine unity is supremely simple.

For this reason, the whole divinity is in the Father, the whole is in the Son, and the whole is in the Holy Spirit. Father, Son, and Holy Spirit cannot be conceived as specific differences within that divinity, but as perfect communion in the one and same essence. The trinity of Persons does not shatter the utterly simple unity of the divine essence: Trinitarian confession encompasses not only the affirmation of the trinity of Persons, but also the unyielding affirmation of essential unity. This means that the divine essence does not multiply with the Persons. In begetting the Son, the Father communicates to the Son his whole essence, without division and without multiplication. The essence of the Son is numerically the same as that of the Father.

This is the very same consideration adduced when demonstrating the unreasonableness of polytheism. Subsistent Being is absolutely simple, and for that very reason He is unique.[^7] The Holy Fathers were keenly aware that only by upholding the absolute simplicity of God could they advance deeper into the Trinitarian mystery while avoiding the danger of tritheism.[^8]

### The Spirituality of God

Divine simplicity leads us to the consideration of God as spirit. Our Lord also used this term when speaking of God: *God is spirit, and those who worship him must worship in spirit and truth* (Jn 4:24).

The concept of spirit and the terms used to designate this reality contain a wealth of nuances, all converging in pointing towards a life superior to material life. Spirit and life are intimately connected. In the Old Testament, the term spirit (*ruah*) is used first and foremost to designate the divine breath that causes man to live. In the New Testament, that same term is used not only to designate the life of God, but also to designate the third Person of the Most Holy Trinity.[^9] Here we use the concept of spirituality to refer to the positive aspect of divine immateriality, that is to say, to refer to God's transcendence over matter.[^10]

To say that God is spirit carries with it the affirmation that He is not a body. As has already been noted, corporeality is not formally in God. It is in Him only virtually. Consequently, neither can sex be understood as being formally in God. Sex is in God only virtually. And this follows from the very definition of sex, which is corporeality and division within a genus. Masculinity and femininity are two modes of the realisation of the human. The mode in which God is realised is infinitely simple, without any division whatsoever. God cannot, therefore, be conceived as masculine or as feminine. God is beyond sex. In sex are reflected the infinite perfection and fruitfulness of the divine life, just as the perfection of God is reflected in the human body. But it can neither be said that God is a body, nor that He is male or female.[^11]

Nor can it be conceived that, within the bosom of the Trinity, one sex could be applied to a particular Person and another to another. As has already been said, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not three parts of the one and same Divinity; rather, all three are one and the same God, without the slightest division of nature existing among them.[^12]

#### Divine Immutability

When referred to God, the immutability of which we speak is the immutability of indefectible Love, in which there is no capacity whatsoever for change, because He is essentially and eternally a single, infinite act of love. This immutability therefore expresses fullness of life; and if we consider it in its relationship to created being, this immutability signifies the free and eternal self-determination of God. It can be translated as essential fidelity to his salvific plan and to his promises. It is an immutability that harmonises perfectly with divine intervention in the history of salvation and with the mystery of the Incarnation.[^13]

Here in particular it is necessary to recall what has been said regarding human language concerning God. Precisely because our knowledge has the sensible world as its point of departure, the mutability or immutability with which we are acquainted is that proper to the corporeal world. In applying these concepts to God, it is necessary not only to purify them of their materiality and to employ analogy, but also to bear in mind the requirements entailed by an infinitely perfect life. In God there can be no change implying increase or decrease, whether in his entitative or operative attributes. He cannot realise Himself in a process-like manner, that is, by means of evolution.

The Holy Fathers frequently affirmed divine immutability in order to defend the Christian message against the hazards of pantheism and polytheism, so prevalent in the culture of their day. In affirming divine immutability, the Holy Fathers kept closely in mind all that Sacred Scripture says concerning God's action in history and, above all, concerning the mystery of the Incarnation.[^14] Nor were they speaking of an immutability that would preclude God's free interventions in salvation history.

The divine immutability of which the Holy Fathers speak does not have, therefore, the same dimensions as the immutability of which the Greek philosophers speak. An abyssal difference exists between the concept of God used by pagan philosophers and the God of the Bible. The Greek Absolute is not perfectly transcendent to the world, whereas the God of the Covenant is. The affirmation of creation establishes an abyssal difference between the two concepts. To this difference is added the affirmation of the various divine interventions in history and, especially, the affirmation that God has become man.[^15]

Saint Thomas considers divine immutability in its metaphysical dimension. He who is not in potency can acquire nothing. Pure Act is not realised through a process, but in full possession.[^16] For this reason, the Trinity cannot be conceived as a process, nor the generation of the Son as though it were the outcome of the Father's capacity to beget reaching its culmination.

Divine immutability—we were saying—is compatible with God's free interventions in history. Considered within the bosom of the Divinity, those interventions are nothing other than an eternal decree of God, whose effects unfold in time. In that eternal decree, God chose us in his Son before the foundation of the world (Eph 1:4).[^17] Saint Augustine formulated this beautifully when he said: God rests while working, and works while resting.[^18]

#### Impassibility: The Absence of Passions

The perfection of the divine life implies that it cannot be conceived as an ebb and flow of feelings and passions. Properly speaking, there are in God no changes of "mood", but indefectible love.

Always within the bounds of analogy, everything that belongs to spiritual life must be predicated of God; affections that imply no imperfection whatsoever are found in God formally. Such is the case with love, gladness, and joy. Insofar as an affection implies imperfection in its concept, it can be predicated of God only virtually. Such is the case, for example, with hatred, wrath, or suffering. These "affections" cannot be formally in God, although they are present in what they contain of perfection, that is, insofar as they are implicit in the essence of love. Hatred is the rejection of what opposes love; sorrow is love mysteriously suffering with the pain of the beloved. This is the logical consequence of defining God as infinite Love: love and hatred cannot exist formally in God at the same time.[^19]

God cannot be conceived as an infinite man, endowed with infinite passions, but as a Being infinitely transcendent to man. And this transcendence is displayed in the way his thoughts surpass our thoughts and his love is beyond our manner of loving.

And yet, precisely because intellect and love are formally in God, his "affective" life is customarily expressed in anthropomorphic language. A prime example of this is Sacred Scripture, which portrays the life of God by showing it to be intimately near, akin to human affections. God grows angry, repents, is moved to pity, longs. God is the jealous spouse, the father who yearns for the return of his son. Saint Augustine comments:

> "If Scripture did not use such modes of expression, it could not make itself so familiar to men of every kind, whom it wishes to help. But in this way it terrifies the proud, spurs on the indifferent, incites the seeker, and nourishes those who penetrate its spirit. It achieves this only by first descending and, as it were, humbling itself to the level of those who are below."[^20]

#### Incorruption and Immortality

The affirmation that God transcends corporeal life makes it impossible to conceive Him as immersed in the processes of birth and death so proper to the material world. Both among the neighbouring peoples of Israel and throughout the pagan world, it was common to imagine gods subject to cycles of resurrection and death. This conception is radically opposed to the manifestations of the God of the Covenant. From the very outset, God appears adorned with indefectible life. God is the Creator of the world, and his vitality is independent of the world, precisely because He infinitely transcends it.[^21]

This is the doctrine that the Fathers would unanimously uphold. They felt a special revulsion towards pagan depictions of deities who rise and die, reproducing within themselves, on a gigantic scale, the processes of nature. As has already been seen, the Fathers used this argument—especially against Arius and Eunomius—to demonstrate the divinity of the Word: since in God there is no corruption, the generation of the Word cannot be conceived in a univocal manner with human generation, but must be eternal, without before or after.[^22]

### Pantheistic Conceptions

Whoever lacks a clear concept of creation encounters a particular difficulty in affirming a clean distinction between God and the world. Hence, in religious representations of God that do not depend on Sacred Scripture, God is in some way entangled with the world and its happenings. He is conceived in one way or another as forming part of the world. Hence also, in almost all religions pantheistic tendencies occasionally appear.[^23] The same is true of philosophical thought, both ancient and contemporary: only the concept of creation *ex nihilo* radically precludes pantheistic temptations. Even the Aristotelian prime unmoved mover, lacking a clear concept of creation, does not seem capable of entirely transcending the being it moves.

Pantheism consists essentially in identifying God and the world. The whole (*to pan*) is seen as forming a divine unity. Whence the name pantheism. This identification between God and the world can be carried out fundamentally from two perspectives: either by identifying the creature with God (divinising the creature), or by identifying God with the creature (mundanising or secularising God). God may be understood as the sole reality and the world exclusively as an epiphenomenon of his, or the world may be conceived as an emanation of God—that is, as something divine that has gradually degraded and in which, therefore, divine things exist, even though in a certain sense they are diverse from God.

In the divinisation of the world, the creature is usually confused with God through its transformation into the Divinity. These forms of pantheism are termed mystical pantheism. God is said to externalise Himself in the multiplicity of worldly beings, and to embrace God means to "lose oneself" in the soul of the world or in its deepest core upon which the totality depends. This entails "blending" or merging with it, transformation into it. Hinduism and Buddhism are prime examples of the "mysticism" that such pantheism can inspire.[^24] Among Christian authors, Meister Eckhart (c. 1260–1327) and Miguel de Molinos (1628–1696) are commonly cited as exponents of similar tendencies, in conceiving that the human soul can be transformed into God to the point of being identified with the Divinity.

The most common form of pantheism is that which confuses God with the world. This pantheism can be total or partial. According to total pantheism, divine being is identified with the entire being of the creature; that is to say, everything that exists is divine.[^25] This pantheism is subdivided in turn into monistic and pluralistic pantheism. According to monistic pantheism, the world and God constitute a single substance. According to pluralistic pantheism, creatures are substances emanated from the divine being. It is called emanationist pantheism. This form of pantheism usually coincides with the conception of this emanation as a process necessary to the divine substance. God would then be not a being in full self-possession, but a process launched in search of his own perfection.

The distinction between pantheism as it manifests itself in certain religions and pantheism formulated philosophically—that is, pantheism as a system of thought—proves very useful. Pantheism, in its extreme degree, does not belong to religion, but to a mixture of ideologies and indefinite sentiments that no longer belongs to the sphere of religion—which is adoration and prayer—but to an irreligious terrain, very close to atheism.[^26] Indeed, if the distinction between God and the world is entirely denied, religion becomes impossible, since there is no one left to venerate. Hence, in certain pantheistic attitudes, even though they present themselves under a religious mantle, it proves impossible to find any genuine religious attitude.

Pantheism becomes more radical in its philosophical version, especially when it turns into a system. Indeed, in philosophy it is inevitable that questions directly related to pantheism arise, since one of its key themes is the explanation of the one and the many. This is the ancient theme of Parmenides, Heraclitus, and Empedocles. Here is Parmenides' well-known argument: a simple thing cannot be diversified by itself, but only by means of something distinct; now, since being is simple and nothing exists outside of it, it cannot be diversified by anything, and therefore all that exists constitutes a single being.[^27] This pantheism is also found in the Stoics and Neoplatonists. For the Stoics, the *logos* is the principle or reason of the world and of its evolution. This *logos* is the foundation of the world, but this foundation is within the world itself. It is not a personal and free *logos*, but the internal order pulsating within matter itself.[^28]

In the Middle Ages and the Renaissance, pantheistic approaches re-emerged, though infrequently, under the influence primarily of Neoplatonism. The principal authors are al-Farabi (870–950) among the Arabs, Avicebron (Ibn Gabirol, 1020–1070) among the Jews, and John Scotus Eriugena († c. 877) and Nicholas of Cusa (1401–1464) among Christians.

Modern pantheism begins with Giordano Bruno (1548–1600),[^29] and finds its foremost exponent in Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677). According to Spinoza, God is an absolute and infinite substance (*Deus, sive substantia, sive natura*), which unfolds by means of immanent causality. Spinoza arrives at this assertion from his definition of substance. Substance is customarily defined as being in itself (*ens in se*), in contrast to being that inheres in another (*ens in alio*), that is, in contrast to accidents. Spinoza, on the other hand, understands it as "that which is in itself and is conceived through itself; that is, that whose concept does not require the concept of another thing for its formation."[^30] This means that no effect can be a substance. From this moment, substance and created being are incompatible. Indeed, every created being, by its very definition, refers to its creator as to the one in whom its concept finds its complete explanation. Hence Spinoza admits the existence of only a single substance, which he calls God. This is a substance that manifests itself in an infinity of attributes. From these attributes derive the finite modes of things as manifestations of the divine substance: they are the *natura naturata*, in which *natura naturans* manifests itself. This pantheism cannot fail to recall the problem of Parmenides. God is nothing other than the being of nature.

In German Idealism, pantheism emerges with very diverse nuances. Among the most characteristic authors are J. G. Fichte (1762–1814), G. W. F. Hegel (1770–1831), and F. W. J. Schelling (1775–1854). Fichte opts for the absolute Ego as the subject, object, and end of all knowledge and action; Hegel identifies God, the Idea, with the dynamism of nature; Schelling derives everything from a God who becomes and is realised in the world.[^31]

Hegel's thought that without the world God is not God is emblematic.[^32] This is so because the world is conceived as the necessary unfolding of a drama internal to the divinity. God is conceived as a gigantic, necessary process that is realised precisely in becoming, alienating Himself and returning to Himself.[^33]

Given the importance that philosophical pantheism acquired in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Europe, the First Vatican Council devoted careful attention to it. After professing faith in "one true and living God, creator and lord of heaven and earth", it affirms his transcendence over the world:

> "...who, being one singular spiritual substance, absolutely simple and unchangeable, must be proclaimed distinct from the world in reality and essence [...] and ineffably exalted above all things that exist or can be conceived outside of Himself."[^34]

The Council thus teaches the distinction between God and the world, relying on these three reasons: a) God is a single spiritual substance, that is, God cannot be multiplied so as to give rise to many beings, and therefore is "realised" only in his unity; b) God is absolutely simple, and therefore cannot form part of that which is essentially complex; c) God is immutable, and therefore cannot be confused with that in which change belongs to its innermost structure. And it concludes: hence God is distinct from the world not only conceptually, but really; He is distinct not only numerically, but essentially.[^35]

The pantheistic conception of God is utterly incompatible with the Christian God. The latter, in effect, is always intimately personal and transcendent to the world. His presence implies no mixture or confusion with creation. He can even be born in Bethlehem or die on the Cross, but He is never confused with the elements of the world. Such confusion is metaphysically impossible.

All pantheism presents a debased image of God and—though it may seem surprising at first—a debased image of man. Indeed, the God of pantheism is entangled in becoming; He is enslaved to the cycles of birth and death. He is no longer the lord of history, but its slave, the one who suffers it, perhaps seeking his own recovery through his own alienation. But a God confused with nature or with history can no longer save man either from the slavery of nature or from the tyranny of history. Pantheism degrades the image of man in the very same respect in which it degrades the image of God. Man too is absorbed into the cycles of nature or of history. Recent vicissitudes of European thought, with the dissolution of the individual into the species, bear eloquent witness to this. That dissolution was not an accidental phenomenon, but the inevitable consequence of the "depersonalisation" of God that occurred in philosophical pantheism.

Nor philosophically can it be said that pantheism is reasonable, for it denies an evident phenomenon: the distinction and multiplicity of beings. For this reason, it is frequently pointed out that philosophical pantheism is the result of an illegitimate process of mental abstraction.[^36] Pantheism perceives that all that exists is interrelated, as in a great symphony. A marvellous harmony and an evident order exist in the world. But pantheism oversteps this observation by establishing that this harmony is due to the substantial unity of things, taking account neither of the clear multiplicity of things nor of their imperfection.

For this reason, pantheism can present itself as truly seductive. It responds, on the one hand, to the evident interrelatedness among beings, and, on the other, to the intuition that there is something divine in all things. The unity and numinosity of the world can exert a powerful spell on certain cultures. Indeed, everything comes from God, and the divine perfections are reflected in all beings. But from this to confusing God with what is a reflection of his glory is an illogical inference. The presence of God in things and in the interiority of man—God is more intimate to us than we are to ourselves—had been repeatedly affirmed by patristic theology. Yet this divine immanence never led the Fathers to confuse God and the world. The reason is that God is present to the world not because the world emanates from Him, but because the world proceeds from Him as from its sole and total efficient cause. God has brought forth the world by drawing it out of nothingness, that is, producing it *secundum totam suam substantiam*. Over against the vague and sentimental yearning for union with the soul of the world present in certain religions of pantheistic spirit, Christianity proposes a personal encounter with that intimately close God who is everywhere present and who has come out to meet us in the human face of Jesus of Nazareth.
