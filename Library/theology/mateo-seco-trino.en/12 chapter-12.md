## Chapter 12: Questions Regarding God in Recent Centuries {#mateo-seco-trino.12}

In the preceding chapter, when mentioning the principal interventions of the Magisterium concerning the mystery of God, passing reference was made to the most salient questions that have emerged in recent centuries regarding the mystery of God in both his unity and his trinity. It was noted, on the one hand, that with the teachings of the Council of Florence the fundamental Trinitarian affirmations were rendered explicit; on the other hand, the latest interventions of the Magisterium—above all those of the Second Vatican Council and the Trinitarian encyclicals of John Paul II—bear witness to the great enrichment and fruitful perspective in which Trinitarian doctrine is situated in contemporary theology. The neglect of the Trinitarian mystery that took place in the modern era has been followed in our own century—especially since Vatican II—by a recovery of the great themes of Trinitarian theology and spirituality and of their indissoluble bond with the history of salvation.

The interventions of the Magisterium, especially those of the First Vatican Council regarding the capacity of reason to attain to God and those of Vatican II on atheism, likewise testify that the gravest objections against Christian doctrine concerning the mystery of God stem from the philosophical sphere. During these centuries one witnesses a drift in a large part of philosophical thought that begins with the defence of pantheism and deism, only to become polarised later, after a phase of agnosticism, in the open and militant denial of the existence of God.

The richness and variety of approaches and situations that arise in these recent centuries are inexhaustible. Any attempt to enumerate them brings with it a sense of dissatisfaction, not so much because of the data and issues chosen, as because of those that must inevitably be omitted. For our part, we shall confine ourselves to sketching the history of this epoch in a few brushstrokes that serve, above all, to assess the great Trinitarian progress represented by the present theological situation. May this outline serve as an invitation to study in greater depth the questions, problems, solutions, and perspectives that occupy the centre of contemporary theological enquiry.

### From Thomas Aquinas to Luther

Following the splendour attained in the West by Trinitarian doctrine with Saint Thomas Aquinas and Saint Bonaventure, there ensued a long and slow period of decline, due above all to its polarisation around philosophical questions and the neglect of more specifically theological themes and perspectives. Theologians gradually distanced themselves from the consideration of the mystery of God in his salvific interventions, focussing instead on a static contemplation detached from the economy of salvation. The very necessity of defending the faith against philosophical assaults sealed this polarisation. Consideration of the Trinitarian mystery remained living and operative, so to speak, only in spiritual authors, such as Saint Teresa of Jesus or Saint John of the Cross. Although unreserved faith was accorded to Sacred Scripture, its reading failed to fructify theological endeavour in any real sense, distracted as it was by other concerns. Outstanding among the authors of this period are John Duns Scotus († 1308) and William of Ockham († 1349). Scotus opened the way to nominalism; Ockham is the most prominent among the nominalists.[^1] This nominalism was to exert a decisive influence upon Luther, above all through Gabriel Biel († 1495).

#### John Duns Scotus

John Duns Scotus is undoubtedly the most vigorous thinker of this period, and his doctrine is good proof that Latin theology, even in the Trinitarian domain, offers a great diversity of positions. We have already seen the diversity of viewpoints existing, for instance, among Saint Anselm, Richard of Saint Victor, Saint Bonaventure, and Saint Thomas Aquinas. Scotus poses many of these same questions and resolves them in an original manner.

He accords primary importance to the concept of infinity: every being, in its limitation, points to infinity.[^2] The infinity of the First Being is demonstrated by the very same arguments that demonstrate his existence. Here is a characteristic argument of Scotus: "He who has active power in himself possesses being in himself; but he who has something of himself possesses it in all the fullness that can belong to it, for nothing exists in a limited manner in anything unless it has received it from some agent that determines it to possess it in a certain degree (...) From the fact that the first mover moves of itself and consequently is of itself, it follows that He is of an infinite intensive power, given that the total fullness of active power cannot exist without intensive infinity."[^3] The notion of infinity thus becomes the most perfect notion we can form of the divinity.

Whereas in Thomas Aquinas aseity or *ipsum esse subsistens* constitutes the deepest core of our concept of God, for Scotus the essential, distinctive, and primordial mark of the divine being is constituted by infinity. This is, therefore, a constitutive or fundamental infinity, prior to any other concept. Scotist infinity is not, then, a merely negative concept, but a positive and foundational one.[^4] It is difficult to understand infinity as such as the primary notion of the divinity. Thomists would say that this requirement of infinity in God flows precisely from the fact that He is *ipsum esse subsistens*; infinity cannot, therefore, be the primary concept in our conception of God. Yet according to Scotus, reason alone attains only to a confused concept of God as the supreme being, without reaching a grasp of his essence.[^5] It is evident that infinity constitutes one of the key themes for understanding Scotist thought, both with regard to the nature of God and to his Trinitarian mystery.[^6]

In the Trinitarian sphere, Scotus likewise maintains several distinctive viewpoints. There are only two processions in God: the procession of the Word and that of the Holy Spirit; the Word proceeds by way of intellect, and the Holy Spirit by way of love. Scotus states that the procession of the Word takes place *per modum naturae*, whereas that of the Spirit takes place *per modum voluntatis*. The reason is straightforward: intellection is determined by the object known, whereas the act of willing is free, that is, it is not determined like the act of the intellect. Since in God everything is infinite—Scotus argues—there can be only one Word and one Spirit, for both constitute the infinite term of those two divine processions.

When treating the divine intellect in its relation to the generation of the Word, Scotus distinguishes two acts: that of simple intellection and that of diction (*dictio*). The first consists in the knowledge whereby God holds himself present to himself; that is to say, the knowledge whereby God has memory of himself; the second, which is only logically posterior, is the *dictio*, the act of knowledge whereby the Word is spoken by the Father—that is, proceeds from the fruitful memory of the Father.[^7] Aided precisely by this distinction between intellection and diction, Scotus underlines that, in the generation of the Word, there also exists that essential love which follows upon the essential knowledge that God has of himself.[^8] In consistency with this, the Subtle Doctor also distinguishes two acts of love in God: the first follows upon the essential knowledge that God has of himself; the second receives the name of spiration and is common to the Father and the Word.[^9]

Scotus adheres to the notion of person used by Richard of Saint Victor, placing its principal core in incommunicability. As opposed to the position of Saint Thomas, who locates the notion of the divine person in the subsistent relation, Scotus inclines towards the view that the divine person is not constituted solely by relation, but also implies something non-relative. This bears directly upon his position regarding the arguments used by Latin theology to affirm the *Filioque*. As has already been seen, one of the customary arguments is that if the Spirit did not proceed also from the Son, he would not be distinguished from Him, since there would be no relation of opposition between the two. For Scotus, by contrast, precisely because person in God is not defined solely as a subsistent relation, the Son and the Spirit would be really distinct from each other even if the Spirit did not proceed from Him. The Son and the Spirit are constituted and distinguished by their respective modes of proceeding: the Word *via naturae*, and the Spirit *via voluntatis*,[^10] and not by their relation of opposition.

#### The Reformation and its Repercussions on Questions Regarding God

The crisis of the Reformation arose over problems apparently far removed from the questions proper to the treatise on the One and Triune God. The Reformers, in fact, gave primary attention to the justification of man by the grace of Christ, that is, to questions of supernatural anthropology. Yet the questions they tackled, by virtue of the radical nature of their premises, ended up impinging directly upon the consideration of the mystery of God as well.

Indeed, Luther's stance before human reason inherently carried with it, in the short term, the rejection of all natural theology. Nevertheless, this rejection is not reflexively present in him. According to Luther, man is incapable of attaining a saving knowledge of God by means of his reason, since reason is intrinsically corrupted,[^11] although this does not mean that the human mind cannot theoretically know the existence of God. But this knowledge is not saving.[^12] The contrast with the entire previous theological approach is clear: in contrast to a Thomas Aquinas, for example, who begins his study of God by setting out the arguments whereby natural reason can demonstrate his existence, Luther has recourse to *sola fides*, rejecting any cooperation of human reason. This rejection stems not only from his conviction that man is intrinsically corrupt, but also from a rupture—less visible at first glance, but of equally grave consequences—between the God of creation and the God of salvation.

Luther seeks exclusively the *Deus salutaris*, the saving God; he is not interested in what God is in himself, but in what He is for us. According to him, in the search for this saving God natural reason is destined to fail. Strictly speaking, neither Luther nor Calvin denies the natural knowledge of God; what both deny is the value of this knowledge for salvation. Hence, alongside non-saving knowledge of God, the Reformers posited revelation as the sole pathway to salvation.[^13] But this rupture between the natural and the supernatural and the negative assessment of natural reason would inevitably lead many thinkers influenced by these premises to an outright rejection of all natural theology—regarding it as idolatry—and to a radically anti-metaphysical discourse in their reflection upon the mystery of God.

Initially, the Trinitarian doctrine of the Reformers did not appear affected by these premises; rather, it might have seemed favoured, since a return to biblical reflection alongside a turning away from philosophical questions ought to have brought about a closer approximation to the Christian God. The early Reformers maintained the confession of Trinitarian faith. Good proof of this is Calvin's rejection of the anti-Trinitarian theories of Michael Servetus.

And yet, already in these early moments there appears a certain reserve regarding the Trinitarian mystery, evidenced by the silencing or rejection of those Creeds that define Trinitarian doctrine in greater detail (such as the *Quicumque* Creed), as well as a certain insularity within the mystery of Christ, who appears detached from his Trinitarian dimension.[^14] Attention to the economic Trinity took place to the neglect of the immanent Trinity, and the adoption of an anti-metaphysical stance led to the rejection of the most important concepts of Trinitarian theology since the fourth century. (Thus, for example, Luther described the distinction between nature and person as frivolous and useless). The rejection of these concepts placed Luther's Trinitarian theology in a position prior to that of Saint Basil. On the other hand, perhaps owing to the special attention paid to Scripture, pneumatology acquired great vigour in Luther, both in describing the relations between the Spirit and Christ, and in describing the relations between the Spirit and the Church, and between the Spirit and the Christian believer.[^15]

Regarding the characteristics of the Spirit, Luther departed sharply from the Augustinian conception: the Spirit is not conceived as the bond of love between the Father and the Son. For this reason, he is not understood as love either. Charity—Luther believed—belongs to the essence of God and is common to the three Persons; consequently, it cannot properly be appropriated to the Spirit.[^16]

The role of the Holy Spirit in the work of salvation is closely bound up with the Gospel and with faith in our Lord Jesus Christ. The Holy Spirit is the one who introduces us into the Church through preaching. It is the Holy Spirit who places faith in our hearts. Here is how he expresses it in his commentary on the *Magnificat* regarding the action of the Holy Spirit in the Virgin: "To understand this holy canticle aright, it is necessary to point out that the Virgin Mary speaks after having had a personal experience whereby the Holy Spirit enlightened and taught her. For no one can understand God and his word unless he has been immediately illuminated by the Holy Spirit."[^17]

Yves Congar observes that, although for Luther Scripture explains itself and leads to the recognition of Christ, for this recognition the testimony of the Spirit in the soul is necessary. This thought, which is already found in the Holy Fathers, albeit in a different context, found a deeper formulation in Calvin and Calvinism: "whereas for Luther the principle that enables one to discern whether a Scripture is apostolic and inspired is that it speaks of Christ, in Calvin the interior testimony of the Holy Spirit enables one to discern what is the word of God (and consequently inspired) and what is not."[^18]

#### Anti-Trinitarian Doctrines

The fundamental premises of the Reformation could lead—and indeed did lead—to the denial of Trinitarian doctrine. The accentuation of what God is for us and the disinterest in what God is in himself carried with it the danger of downplaying the formulations of faith regarding the Trinitarian mystery; the rejection of philosophy in study brought the danger of undervaluing the clarificatory work of the Fathers, especially the immense speculative task accomplished by the Greek patristic tradition of the fourth century.

Michael Servetus (1511–1553) was one of the first to reject the Trinitarian mystery openly. Servetus's doctrine takes us back to the most primitive modalism. The oneness of God is equated with the oneness of person. This unity is conceived in a pantheistic manner: God himself is the essence of all things, and all things are in God. This God has revealed himself through his word and his spirit. Word and Spirit are merely modes in which God has revealed himself, not two really distinct persons existing in Him. Servetus's position is usually described as a blend of Sabellianism and Platonic mysticism, for his anti-Trinitarianism is characterised above all by philosophical speculation and rational critique.[^19]

Among those who denied the Trinitarian mystery, a prominent place is occupied by the Socinians.[^20] Their rejection of the Trinitarian mystery was likewise due to a narrow rationalism which, at the same time, underestimated the powers of reason. For them, reason is incapable of discovering the existence of God. Knowledge of the existence of God requires revelation. Now, revelation clearly states that there is only one God; consequently, faith in the Trinity is contrary to the Bible. It will be futile—they argue—to search Sacred Scripture for the application of the term 'person' to the Word or the Spirit. Reason, in turn, refuses to admit that there can be more than one person in a single being. Nor can it be accepted that Jesus Christ is God.[^21]

The denial of the Trinitarian mystery has been present in Protestant thought, though not in a generalised form, from the very beginnings of the Reformation. It is almost always a simple denial of the mystery, without adding any new speculation to the heresies already known to us. Anti-Trinitarian ideas concentrated upon the denial of a plurality of persons in God. Hence the name Unitarianism by which they are usually designated, especially in their Anglo-Saxon branch, first in England and later in North America.

The Unitarian approach allied itself, above all from the seventeenth century onwards, with deism and rationalism. The existence of a God who underlies everything that exists was accepted, but no longer the deeply personal God revealed in Christ. "This God is the generic reason of the world, but he is not the Father of Jesus. That is why one cannot speak of the Trinity: it is meaningless to seek the existence of an intra-divine dialogue of love. Logically, Jesus loses his character as Son of God and is converted into a mere moral or rational exemplar for human beings."[^22]

We find ourselves here before one of the typical paradoxes of European theological thought. Starting from the endeavour to return to the Bible and the rejection of natural reason, one ends up dissolving the very heart of Christianity—the God of Jesus Christ—into the vagueness of a philosophical deism in which nothing Christian remains. It is not surprising that Unitarian churches have frequently become mere philanthropic institutions.

### Philosophical Approaches

#### Philosophical Interpretations of the Trinity

The history of Western thought bears witness to the fact that the mystery of the Trinity has also attracted the attention of philosophers, as an affirmation capable of being understood and interpreted philosophically—sometimes to defend the Trinitarian faith, at other times to find inspiration for their own philosophical work.

Thus, for instance, G. W. Leibniz (1646–1716) not only strove to demonstrate the existence of God in his *Essays on Theodicy*, but also endeavoured to show against the Socinians that, from a rational standpoint, there is no contradiction in affirming that there is a trinity of persons in the one and same God. Leibniz did not attempt to prove the mystery of the Trinity; he was content merely to demonstrate its possibility.[^23]

The most representative philosophical deployments of Trinitarian doctrine are those of Fichte (1762–1814), Schelling (1775–1854), and Hegel (1770–1831). These are interpretations inspired by the Trinitarian mystery in order to elaborate a comprehensive understanding of being and the world. In one way or another, these interpretations are linked to pantheism and evolutionism. The best known and most influential is that of Hegel, who has sometimes been described as the philosopher of the Trinity.[^24]

This is not the place to undertake a detailed examination of Hegelian thought. On the one hand, Hegel's system is a construction of great theoretical coherence, the exposition of which would require many pages and the expertise of a specialist; on the other hand, in this historical survey, we are concerned only to show how Christian doctrine on the mystery of the Trinity has been taken up in recent centuries as a constitutive element of philosophical thought. And in this respect, Hegel is outstanding; this is, moreover, one of the reasons why Hegelian thought continues to hold such an appeal for certain theologians.

As for Schelling, so too for Hegel all the phenomena of our universe are the evolution of one single infinite substance. Hegel identifies this substance with the Idea and places Trinitarian doctrine at the service of its explanation: the Idea, in its development, produces everything that exists. For this reason we noted that these interpretations are in some way linked to pantheism. Indeed, although Hegel defended himself against the charge of pantheism, his system offers no clear demarcation between the reality of God and the reality of the world. The world appears to spring forth not from a creation *ex nihilo*, but from the necessary unfolding of the Idea.[^25]

Hegel's position may be summarised as follows: the Idea in itself is the realm of the Father, of God considered as an abstract idea prior to the world; however, this Idea tends to objectify itself, to go out of itself; the second moment, that of the development of the Idea in the world, corresponds to the Son; finally, just as the Idea must not remain abstract but must give itself an objective content, so too the world is destined to be once again permeated by the Idea; hence the Idea and the world tend to meet and identify with each other; this synthesis corresponds to the Holy Spirit.[^26]

It is not easy to define Hegel's Trinitarian thought with precision, and above all to determine what he understands by the Holy Spirit, for this depends on the priority given to some Hegelian texts over others. In any case, it is clear that for Hegel God is spirit, and is conceived as absolute subjectivity to which belongs, at once, the act of differentiating itself from itself and returning to itself in the very act by which it differentiates itself. As Álvarez Gómez writes, referring principally to the passages of the *Phenomenology of Spirit*: "God is here conceived in the element of pure thought, as the pure thinking of himself. Such thinking is not possible without the thought-object, that is, without differentiating oneself from oneself. But since it is thinking that thinks itself as thought, the very act of differentiating itself from itself as thought essentially returns to itself. Therefore, the difference, in the very act and with the very intensity with which it is posited, is equally overcome. What the Holy Spirit strictly expresses is thus nothing other than the unity of the thinker and the thought, of knowing and the known, of the Father and the Son."[^27]

Many Hegelian texts seem close to modalism and subordinatianism. Nevertheless, Hegel seems at times to speak of the existence of distinct persons in God. In any event, the Hegelian philosophy of spirit and his concept of person are replete with suggestive insights, which should be kept in mind even in the study of the Trinitarian mystery. Thus, for example, his concept of person as "the supreme intensity of being-in-itself" and his warning that only in dialogue and in love are personality and subjectivity realised open up perspectives for expressing what Richard of Saint Victor had already articulated, albeit within quite a different horizon: that infinite Love seems to require a plurality of persons, since love exists only in interpersonal relation. The central importance that Hegel accorded in his philosophising to Trinitarian doctrine helped certain thinkers—and some theologians as well—to rediscover the central importance of the Trinitarian mystery.

However, one cannot say that Hegel's Trinitarian conception coincides with the Christian conception. His entire description of the dialectic of the Spirit leads to the assertion that the end of the process is more perfect than the beginning, whereas for Christian doctrine fullness is already present at the beginning, since the Father is *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*. The First Vatican Council had this issue in view when rejecting the assertion that God is the universal or indeterminate being which, by determining itself, constitutes the universality of things, distinguishing them into genera, species, and individuals.[^28]

As Walter Kasper notes, following an observation of Scheeben: "although theology has much to learn from modern philosophy, especially from Hegel, it must oppose it at the decisive point: the Trinity cannot be necessarily derived either from the concept of absolute spirit or from the concept of love. The Trinity is a *mysterium stricte dictum*. No one knows the Son except the Father, and no one knows the Father except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal him (Mt 11:27)."[^29]

#### Questions Surrounding the Rational Knowability of God

The philosophical questions raised in recent centuries regarding the existence and nature of God merit separate treatment, for they are numerous and varied, and must be analysed within their proper domain, which is strictly philosophical. God—whether accepted or rejected—occupies a pre-eminent place in philosophical speculation, to the point where it is impossible to analyse the history of philosophy in these past centuries without entering into the study of the stance adopted by various authors towards the existence and nature of God. A good example of this is seen in the most representative philosophers from Leibniz to Kant, from Hegel to Marx. This is so by the very nature of philosophical enquiry: philosophy cannot dispense with the question of God, since it strives to attain knowledge of ultimate causes and therefore strives to answer the question concerning the origin of being. This is so, likewise, because the influence of theological thought remains truly substantial in both Protestant and Catholic environments. Kant and Hegel are incomprehensible without bearing in mind some of the most cherished theses of the Reformation.

The formulation of the mystery of God in these centuries has been extraordinarily complex. Alongside the acceptance of a Supreme Being in harmony with the great philosophical tradition of preceding centuries, the rejection of God as a personal and absolute Being, the transcendent cause of everything that exists, gained ground with increasing force. The manner in which this rejection occurred was also diverse and constitutes a long trajectory in which the coherent development of premises originally of theological provenance becomes manifest: premises that run from pessimism regarding the powers of reason to attain God to the image of that God presented by extreme nominalism as an arbitrary being set in opposition to man and the world. Deism, pantheism, agnosticism, and atheism maintain explicable links with one another and appear as stages in a protracted process culminating in the open anti-theism of our own century.[^30]

It is hardly surprising that theologians attempted repeatedly to counter the theoretical difficulties posed by philosophy, in a dialogue that was at times fruitful, at times led to untenable positions, and often distracted from properly theological questions. Added to this situation is the fact that with Francisco Suárez († 1617) the treatise on the One God was separated from that on the Triune God. This separation facilitated the philosophical consideration of God, but diminished the energy available for his theological treatment and, above all, weakened the presentation of the radical novelty of the Christian God. The God spoken of in theological treatises of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries is almost exclusively the God conceived by philosophers, without giving sufficient prominence to the God who is definitively manifested in Christ, even in the mystery of his unity.

One of the most universal and constantly debated questions during these centuries concerns the capacity of reason to attain some knowledge of God. The era was complex, and positions varied widely: ranging from semi-rationalism and ontologism to fideism, traditionalism, and agnosticism.

We find ourselves, therefore, in a field clearly dominated by epistemological questions. This epistemological predominance has its roots in the theological problems already familiar to us and in philosophical attitudes that find their paradigm in Descartes. From the theological perspective, it is clear that pessimism regarding man's natural capacity to attain truth and goodness was bound so to diminish the rational possibilities of accessing the knowledge of God as to end by declaring them non-existent; from the philosophical perspective, it is evident that the turn inaugurated by Descartes was bound to lead to a constant search for the critical foundation upon which to ground discourse about God. This critical enquiry was bound to attach great importance to the discussion concerning the capacity of reason to establish the existence of God—that is, to methodological and epistemological questions.

Semi-rationalism, short-lived as it was, arose in circles close to Kantianism and Hegelianism. It was a theological tendency that attributed excessive importance to human reason, both for the presentation and for the demonstration of revealed truths.[^31] Its best-known exponents were Georg Hermes (1775–1831) and Anton Günther (1783–1871).[^32] Both strove to provide a rational foundation for Christian faith. Hermes, close to Kant, focussed on showing the reasonableness of the act of faith; Günther sought above all to show the reasonableness of the content of that same faith. Their position regarding the mystery of the Trinity may be summarised as follows: the Trinity can be known only through revelation; but once revealed, reason can demonstrate its internal coherence to the point of being able to offer a convincing explanation of the mystery. The convergence with Hegelian theses is obvious.[^33]

Underlying this approach is a rationalistic conception of faith: to believe is to possess certitude regarding what we believe, not on the authority of God who reveals, but because of the clarity with which the proposition believed presents itself to the intellect. This is so because, after an initial acceptance of the mystery, human reason can attain to an understanding of it. This implies a denial that there are mysteries in the strict sense—that is, mysteries which, even after being revealed, remain above our intellect. Consequently, the mystery of the Trinity, once revealed, could be demonstrated through philosophical reasoning.

Along the same lines of confidence in reason, though with very different roots and in a wholly different climate, stands ontologism. The name ontologism is due to Vincenzo Gioberti (1801–1852), who thus designated this current of thought within which he situated himself.[^34] Gioberti used the term ontologism in opposition to Cartesian philosophy, which he labelled psychologism. Psychologism—so Gioberti argued—is characterised by deducing the intelligible from the sensible, and ontology from psychology. By contrast, according to ontologism, the sensible does not supply the initial data for knowledge; rather, these data are supplied by the idea.[^35]

It is here that the question touching our subject arises, for the ontologists posit the immediate knowledge of God as the origin of all other knowledge. This amounts to saying that we possess an a priori and immediate knowledge of God, such that God appears not only as the efficient cause of all being, but also as the logical ground of all our knowledge.[^36] God would thus be not only the *primum ontologicum*, but also the *primum logicum*—that is, the first being known, in whom we know all other things. There would thus be a close similarity between our mode of knowing and the mode in which God knows the world. As will be seen at length later on, God knows all created things in himself, in his own essence. According to the ontologists, we too would know those things—that is, the created world—in the divine essence. This affirmation presupposes that we know the divine essence directly.

From the theological standpoint, the fundamental problem of ontologism lies precisely in the affirmation of this immediate knowledge of God. Such knowledge appears to demand such parity between the human mind and God that some went so far as to classify ontologism among pantheistic tendencies. This prompted them to soften their assertions, restricting immediate knowledge of the divine essence to the attributes reflected in creation. In any case, the hallmark of ontologism as a philosophical system consists in the thesis that "God, in some way or in some of his attributes, is at once the *medium quo* and the *objectum quod* of intuition."[^37]

At the opposite pole stands agnosticism. The agnostic—at least in theory—does not deny the existence of God; he simply denies that God's existence can be known. Outwardly the agnostic confines himself to suspending judgement on the existence of God: he does not know. This means that, in daily life, the acceptance or rejection of God is irrelevant. But that which is completely irrelevant lies very close to non-existence. Hence agnosticism and atheism almost always appear as synonymous. Yet if we hold to its etymological origin, agnostic means simply one who is ignorant of the existence of God.

Agnosticism in the strict sense exerted powerful influence during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. In many philosophical circles dependent upon the Cartesian turn, there was a widespread conviction that the intellect is incapable of transcending the phenomenal. Now, God is the most spiritual reality of all. Hence the scepticism that exists regarding all knowledge transcending the sensible was applied to the knowledge of God, and this attitude crystallised paradigmatically in the denial of the possibility of any rational knowledge of God. From this position two paths opened up, both of which have been trodden: one, fideism—that is, surrender to a blind faith lacking grounds of credibility, or seeking God by means of feeling, regarded as a faculty opposed to the intellect; the other path consists in descending towards an increasingly explicit atheism, an atheism that in Ludwig Feuerbach († 1872), Karl Marx (1818–1883), and Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) turned into a militant anti-theism.

Perhaps no one has elaborated a thought so paradigmatic of the approach to God through feeling as Friedrich Schleiermacher (1768–1834). As a philosopher of religion, he is a typical representative of the Romanticism of his age, and his influence was considerable in the development of a line of thought moving towards agnosticism. A great admirer of Spinoza and therefore close to his pantheism, he understood religious faith as the feeling or intuition of the universe or of the infinite.[^38] This intuition concerning the world is rather a metaphysics of the universe than the knowledge of a personal being transcending creation. Religion is thus reduced to a feeling of dependence in relation to the whole, to the universe. There is no personal relationship with a personal God. In practice this is already philosophical agnosticism, even though Schleiermacher was personally convinced of God's existence. Precisely for this reason, he posited the existence of God as a postulate of feeling—that is, as something that becomes evident not through the paths of the intellect, but through other avenues.

Yet these avenues neither originate in a true personal relationship with God nor lead to a personal being; rather, they share in the evanescence proper to feeling. And although Schleiermacher regarded Christianity as the most perfect of religions, he reduced it to a mere expression of the subjectivity of feeling itself. The truth and content of Jesus' words are relegated to a secondary plane, for what in fact occupies the pre-eminent place in Schleiermacher's concept of religion is the feeling of dependence upon the infinite. This feeling is, by definition, non-conceptualisable. Consequently, the dogmas of faith are no longer dogmas that must be believed, but forms of reflection upon this determinate and concrete species of the feeling of absolute dependence which is Christianity.[^39]

The term agnosticism was coined by Thomas Henry Huxley (1825–1895). It designates the spiritual attitude of one who suspends judgement regarding the existence and nature of the Absolute. The suspension of this judgement, grounded as it obviously is in a fundamental scepticism regarding the powers of reason, is above all a spiritual attitude that assumes widely differing forms and degrees. Hence agnosticism is one of the most diffuse and least easily classified phenomena of contemporary culture.[^40] Numerous philosophical positions—even conflicting ones—maintain this spiritual attitude designated by the name of agnosticism. Cornelio Fabro observes that "Huxley chose the term agnosticism so as to have an *ism* with which to present his thought among his colleagues of the Metaphysical Society: by this new term he wished to indicate that religious concern, on the plane of life, is as foreign and indifferent as music and painting can be—and indeed are, for many people."[^41]

In a certain sense, agnosticism has already made its choice regarding God by declaring that religious concern is alien or indifferent to life. This means that God—if He exists—is indifferent to the meaning of the world and of man. It is hardly surprising that the difference between atheists and agnostics is often considered minimal.[^42] In truth, to state that the question of God is irrelevant to human existence is tantamount to asserting that God is neither the origin nor the ultimate end of man. For this type of agnostic, life rests upon itself, without further pretensions to ultimate grounds. And it rests upon itself because the agnostic has chosen to live in superficiality.[^43]

Nevertheless, the difference between agnosticism and atheism appears more significant when atheism understands itself as militant atheism—that is, when atheism (which initially consists in the lack of belief in God) becomes the atheist's own faith: the belief that the denial of God is the beginning of human liberation. It is the difference that exists between indifference and militancy. Needless to say, from agnosticism one passes with ease to militant atheism—for example, to Marxist atheism.[^44]

The history of these recent centuries contains abundant manifestations of this militant atheism. Although it finds vigorous philosophical formulations, it is above all a far-reaching existential stance. Henri de Lubac described it as a "positive, organic, constructive" atheism.[^45] It is an atheism conceived as emancipation from all authority superior to man, utilising this emancipation as the foundation for building a new humanism: a humanism in which man constitutes himself as a god for man.

The Second Vatican Council characterised atheism as one of "the most serious problems of our time" and described it as closely related to the desire for human autonomy: "Those who profess this atheism maintain that the essence of freedom consists in this: that man is an end unto himself, the sole maker and creator of his own history. They claim that this cannot be reconciled with the affirmation of a Lord who is author and purpose of all things, or at least that this affirmation makes such freedom completely superfluous. The sense of power which modern technical progress lends to man may also foster this doctrine."[^46]

We find ourselves, therefore, before a tragic misunderstanding: man, created in the image and likeness of God, who finds the fullness of his humanity precisely in union with God, comes to believe that the building up of his own humanity depends upon the death of the idea of God.[^47] Hence the ambition to construct a radically atheist humanism. The vicissitudes and outcomes of this project form an essential part of the tremendous upheavals of our century. Yet the misunderstanding remains as a challenge for contemporary theology: to show that the dignity and aspirations of man find their fulfilment in union with God.

In contemporary theology there is widespread agreement that the adequate answer to the contemporary challenge lies primarily in the full presentation of the Christian God.[^48] Paradoxical though it may seem at first sight, the response to contemporary atheism lies in the clear and complete presentation of the God of Jesus Christ—that is, in the preaching of the One and Triune God, in whose image and likeness man has been created. Although, naturally, philosophical argumentation and natural theology cannot be neglected, the convincing response to the problem of contemporary atheism is not found primarily in philosophy or in an apologetic stance, but in the straightforward presentation of the Christian God in all his radicality and novelty.

The answer to atheism is found, therefore, in the Cross of Jesus, wherein the face of God is definitively revealed. Trinitarian theology, which must always remain the central concern of theological enquiry, is today also the key issue in the new evangelisation. As Kasper writes, "confronted with the radical questioning of the Christian faith, a timid, general, and vague theism is no longer of any use, but only a resolute testimony to the living God of history, who manifested himself concretely through Jesus Christ in the Holy Spirit."[^49] This means that theology must be authentic *theo-logy*, so to speak: a theology which, precisely because of its Christocentric theocentrism, is capable of demonstrating the anthropological relevance of its statements to the point where it can convincingly explain that man's greatness lies in his loving union with God, who is essentially Trinitarian love.

### New Perspectives in Trinitarian Theology

The "recovery" of Trinitarian theology, which received a powerful impulse at the Second Vatican Council, had been taking shape for several decades.[^50] One of the earliest symptoms of this recovery was precisely the return to a unified consideration of the mystery of God. To Michael Schmaus belongs the credit for having initiated this return, by opening his dogmatics with a volume devoted to the Trinity of God, in which issues previously studied separately under the treatises *De Deo Uno* and *De Deo Trino* were addressed with an overarching vision.[^51]

Justice demands that we recall that Karl Barth stands at the beginnings of this "recovery", precisely because of his emphasis that revelation is an initiative of grace and that, outside Jesus, no access to God is possible. The rejection of natural theology, so characteristic of Barth's approach and of which more will be said later, exerted a positive influence here, by way of counter-reaction, in a resolute focus on the Trinitarian mystery as the centre of discourse about God—that is, in the recovery of the Christian God. Barth's well-known position regarding philosophy, and more specifically the analogy of being (*analogia entis*), led him to propose the elimination of the concept of person from Trinitarian theology, judging that this concept, given the new meaning it has acquired in contemporary culture, induces confusion. He proposed instead returning to the expression *modes of being* or *modes of existence* (*tropoi hypárxeos*), used also by the Cappadocians.[^52] The expression *modes of existence* is not inaccurate, and was indeed employed both by the Cappadocians and by Saint Thomas Aquinas and Saint Bonaventure. Nevertheless, when presented as a substitute for the term 'person', it appears inadequate.[^53]

Among the theologians who have contributed most to bringing the mystery of the Trinity to the forefront as the centre of theological enquiry is Karl Rahner (1904–1984). Famous is the passage in which he criticises the isolation suffered by the doctrine of the Trinity in popular piety and textbook theology: "Christians, despite their orthodox profession of faith in the Trinity, are almost exclusively monotheists in their religious existence. We might venture to suggest that if we had to drop the doctrine of the Trinity one day because it had been discovered to be false, the major part of religious literature could remain virtually unchanged."[^54]

Rahner's observation spurred theologians to pursue the task of "recovering" the centrality of the Trinitarian mystery for theological reflection. Indeed, rather than a criticism of ordinary Christians, Rahner's objection exposed the lacunae and omissions in the work of theologians. Yet Rahner did not merely point out these shortcomings; he identified their causes and proposed avenues of solution, both in coherence with his highly personal transcendental philosophy.

According to Rahner, the causes of this neglect of the Trinitarian mystery are due, in particular, to the overuse of the theological axiom that the *opera ad extra* of the Trinity are common to the three Persons, for this overuse causes God to be regarded almost always merely as one. In fact, Rahner attempts to dispense with this axiom as far as possible in order to highlight what is proper to each Person in his intervention in the history of salvation. To accentuate this focus on the economic Trinity, Rahner breaks with the Western tradition of beginning the treatise on the Trinity from the unity of the divine essence, which is the Augustinian way of beginning.[^55]

In this context, one can understand the intent of what Rahner terms "the fundamental axiom on the unity of the economic and immanent Trinity". This axiom is formulated thus: "The economic Trinity is the immanent Trinity, and vice versa."[^56] This is perhaps Rahner's most distinctive and widely known assertion in Trinitarian theology. It is also the most debated on account of its ambiguity.[^57] Naturally, no one doubts that in the economic Trinity the inner nature of the divine Persons is manifested: the Son reveals his eternal sonship in his action, and our adoptive sonship in Christ is not unrelated to the fact that He is the Second Person of the Trinity; the same holds true for the Spirit as sanctifier and christifier. The ambiguity of the Rahnerian axiom lies precisely in the potential identification between the immanent Trinity and its intervention in the history of salvation, for this identification seems to pay insufficient regard to the fact that God's intervention in history is a free intervention, in which the Trinitarian mystery is manifested, but only manifested.[^58]

Rahner also proposes dispensing with the term 'person' in Trinitarian theology—a proposal we have already seen formulated by Karl Barth. Rahner fears that to affirm the existence of three Persons in the Trinity today invites people to conceive the Trinity as three centres of activity, as three spiritual subjectivities, thereby lapsing into tritheism. In response to Rahner's proposal, it has been pointed out that to discard the term 'person' runs a grave risk of modalism.[^59] Moreover, one may ask whether it is genuinely inevitable that the affirmation of three centres of spiritual activity in God must be understood in a tritheistic sense. The answer may be formulated as follows: this expression is not equivalent to affirming three consciousnesses in God, which would be the same as affirming three natures in God—and that would indeed be tritheism. The affirmation of three centres of consciousness in God signifies rather three *I*s, reciprocally related to one another, in the classical sense of three subsisting relations.[^60]

In any case, it is evident that the "recovery" of the Trinitarian mystery as the centre of theological thought is now a universal reality. This recovery, furthermore, places the consideration of the Trinity in close relation to Christology, ecclesiology, and the history of salvation. In this way, the radical novelty of the Christian confession of the One and Triune God revealed in Christ is displayed in all its clarity.

The God revealed in Christ is triune in his unity and one in his trinity. The Trinitarian confession of faith is not concerned solely with the existence of three Persons in God, but—with equal firmness—with the fact that these three Persons constitute one and the same God. The unity of God—with the attributes so beautifully described in Sacred Scripture—belongs to the confession of the Trinitarian mystery just as essentially as the confession of the trinity of persons. Contemporary theology has decided almost universally in favour of presenting these two aspects within a single treatise. This is a methodological choice springing from a deeper theological concern: to speak of God theologically.

The Trinitarian confession stands in close dependence upon the Christological confession. The manifestation of God in Christ is the sole foundation of Christian discourse about God. In this perspective, it must be recognised that the renewal of Christological studies has exerted a decisive influence upon the renewal of Trinitarian doctrine. It all rests upon the prayer and conduct of Jesus, upon the way in which He lives his sonship towards the Father, his *Abba*. This is the reason why the Church grasped the Trinity of God from the earliest moments and confessed it unequivocally in baptism. As Staglianò writes, "it is clear that the renewal of Christology enormously influences Trinitarian doctrine. The theological hermeneutic of Jesus Christ implies the delineation of a concrete monotheism—the Trinitarian one—which has very little or nothing to do with the delicate, generic, and vague theism typical of the Enlightenment, which was ultimately responsible for modern atheism. If man today poses the problem of God, Trinitarian doctrine must be presented as the Christian response."[^61]

This conscious Christological orientation of the treatise on God is now commonplace. In some treatises this choice is evident even in the title of the work as a programme to be pursued. Walter Kasper's *The God of Jesus Christ* is well known for its vigorous thought, but there are countless works that frame the treatment of the Trinity Christocentrically. Frequently this perspective is focussed on the historical life of Jesus, and especially on his death upon the Cross.

In the Lutheran tradition of the *Theologia Crucis*, the most representative theologian of this current is Jürgen Moltmann. He is also the most radical and clear exponent of a theology of the cross in close connection with the Hegelian conception.[^62] For Moltmann, the cross of Christ is not only the place where the face of God is revealed, but a constitutive moment of the Trinity itself. The mystery of the cross is thus regarded as an intra-Trinitarian event.[^63] Many criticisms may be directed at these assertions of Moltmann, so close to Hegel;[^64] yet this attempt to elaborate a Trinitarian doctrine on the basis of the cross, even if it may be deemed flawed in many respects,[^65] points to a genuinely suggestive path for a more balanced approach. This is the path pursued, among others, by Ghislain Lafont.[^66] Lafont takes as his starting point the fact that the Pasch of Jesus constitutes the supreme manifestation of the Trinity as a mystery of communion, reciprocal self-giving, and love.[^67]

This is a justified and truly fruitful perspective. The salvific history of Jesus is indeed the supreme and ultimate locus of the manifestation of intra-Trinitarian love. This leads to the consideration of Trinitarian reality in its relation to the history of salvation. The Trinity stands at the origin of salvation history, is implicated in it through the Incarnation of the Word, and stands at the end of that history as the ultimate goal of human happiness. Outstanding among the efforts to present Trinitarian doctrine in its relation to the history of salvation are the chapters devoted to Trinitarian doctrine in *Mysterium Salutis*,[^68] and the work of Bruno Forte, especially his *The Trinity as History*.[^69] Here is how Forte describes his aim, in which the history of Jesus, the history of salvation, and mystery appear interwoven: "This book speaks of the Trinity by speaking of history, and speaks of history by speaking of the Trinity. The history it recounts is above all that of the paschal event of the death and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth, whom God raised from the dead and established with power according to the Spirit of holiness as Lord and Christ (cf. Rom 1:4); in this history another history emerges, that of the One who in the Paschal event revealed himself as Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8, 16), delivering his beloved Son over to death and reconciling his Son, and in Him the world, to himself in the power of the Spirit of unity and love. The narrative of the paschal event thus opens into the narrative of the Trinity as the eternal event of love, as the history of eternal love."[^70]

The Christocentric perspective in which all theological endeavour—and most especially reflection on the mystery of God—is situated invites us to consider God in his relationship with human history, and in particular with the history of Jesus. This approach avoids becoming lost in philosophical speculations and in a vague and ineffective theism. It is, at the same time, the most direct path to encountering that Trinitarian God who comes to meet man in the Humanity of his Son. As Peter confessed before the Sanhedrin, there is salvation in no one else, for there is no other name under heaven given among men by which we must be saved (Acts 4:12).

#### Anthropology, Christology, Pneumatology

Trinitarian theology is experiencing years of great fruitfulness, which hold promise of even greater harvest. It is an authentic theological renewal that gathers up the major strides made by Christian thought throughout this century, finding an essential point of reference in the Second Vatican Council. It likewise gathers the fruits of a deeper and more unified consideration in fields of great consequence, such as anthropology, Christology, and pneumatology.

Mention must be made, first of all, of the influence exerted by the renewed concept of theological enquiry, and in particular the revitalisation brought about by the attention given to Sacred Scripture and the prominence acquired by historical studies. Abandoning a misapplied Aristotelianism that led to theology being conceived as a science concerned above all with systematic structuring, theologians have returned to giving preferential attention to historical events and, more specifically, to revelation as a historical event. The formula used by Vatican II to describe the central task of theologians—"to renew theological disciplines through a livelier contact with the mystery of Christ and the history of salvation"[^71]—has brought with it a new way of seeing things that has directly affected the renewal of the treatise on God. Some of these repercussions have already been noted in the preceding section.

For this new concept of theology implied, as has been seen, a close rapprochement between systematic theology and biblical studies. It implied above all a new approach, with attention focussed not on systematising concepts, but on allowing oneself to be permeated by biblical wisdom, language, and perspectives. This led immediately to according great importance to the history of salvation. In the treatise before us, this entails attaching far greater weight to what God has said of himself than to what human beings can discover of Him through the efforts of their reason.

It is understandable, therefore, that contemporary theology should shun philosophical theism as a condition for speaking more freely of the Christian God. Hence the treatises written in the second half of this century insist strongly upon the radical novelty of the Christian God, upon his biblical characteristics, upon his Trinitarian reality, and above all upon his character as a personal being, in his relation to history and in his free self-communication to man. God's freedom in his historical actions attains its definitive perspective when considering his action and self-giving in Christ.

In this primacy accorded to the economy in contemporary theological consideration, God is spoken of from Christ. The perspective is therefore economic-salvific; consequently, the theology of divine missions acquires primary importance. Jesus' assertion of his unity with the Father does not merely imply his divinity; it also signifies receiving in the Son, the Image of the invisible God (cf. Col 1:15), the self-revelation of the Father. As has been aptly formulated, "the divinity of Christ implies the Christianity of God."[^72]

Within this perspective, a greater unity is achieved in considering the economic and immanent aspects of the Trinity. In the preceding sections we set out the main positions of theologians regarding how this consideration is articulated. In this field, serious disagreements with Protestant thought had previously arisen. What God is for us seemed entirely to absorb the *in se* of God. Karl Barth's effort to place the Trinitarian mystery at the centre of theological consideration has borne great fruit in Protestant theology, to the extent that one may speak of "ascending" once again from the economic Trinity to the immanent Trinity.

Differences between Catholics and Protestants remain, however, on a theme of great importance: the value of human language and rational discourse when treating of God. The contemporary situation seems to require special attention to the God of history in order to confront a history without God. On this point there is broad convergence in theological writings on the mystery of God. Yet in speaking of God, even from this perspective, it is inevitable that appeal should also be made to human common sense—that is, to an elementary rational knowledge of God. It is here, regarding the validity of this knowledge, that divergences persist, primarily on account of differing views on supernatural anthropology.

Contemporary theology vigorously underlines the relationship of man, created in the image of God, to the Trinitarian mystery. Both the concept of person and the concept of inner-divine life are presented in a livelier analogy with the nature of spirit and the demands of love. Here the rich store of insights present in contemporary anthropological thought—especially regarding the dialogical dimension of the person—is harvested and applied to the ever-suggestive analogies that find their principal basis in Augustinian doctrine. Authors such as Saint Augustine, Richard of Saint Victor, and Saint Bonaventure are re-read with fresh light.

Christological studies exert a powerful influence upon this renewal of the treatment of the mystery of God. Indeed, the more one contemplates the words and actions of Jesus, the better one perceives the unmistakable "personality" of the God of Sacred Scripture and of his interpersonal mystery. Jesus' *Abba* is an open highway for treating the mystery of God.

One of the most noteworthy elements contributing to the revitalisation of Trinitarian theology is the importance acquired by pneumatology in recent decades. As has already been highlighted, pneumatology constituted the question where the greatest divergence between East and West occurred in Trinitarian matters. The remarkable development of pneumatology in the West in recent decades and the warm attention paid to Eastern authors have breathed fresh life into the doctrine on the mystery of God.

Contemporary theological reflection presents a return to theological lines of the earliest Christian flavour. We refer to a conception in which the Trinitarian mystery occupies the centre of theological thought. On this point there is a notable convergence among the various Christian confessions.[^73]

#### The Christian God and the New Evangelisation

Day by day, attention is focussed upon Christ and upon the new evangelisation demanded by our times. This new evangelisation cannot consist in anything other than proposing anew, with renewed youthfulness and authenticity, the very message that gathered the Church together from its first moments: to speak of Jesus as the Son of God; to speak of God as the Father of Jesus. The "recovery" of the centrality of the Trinitarian mystery, and the Christocentric and historical perspective in which consideration of the mystery of God is situated, will avert pitfalls and facilitate this task.

It will avoid the pitfall of dissipating energy when speaking of God as though He were an abstract idea or possibility whose existence is under debate. Christian faith is above all witness to the living God of history who has manifested himself in Christ through the Holy Spirit. The Christian confession of God is essentially Trinitarian, for faith in the Triune God is the foundational structure sustaining all faith in God and in Jesus Christ. Indeed, the fact of New Testament revelation consists substantially in this: that Jesus revealed God as his Father and, through union with Him, as the Father of all men.

To speak of God, there is nothing better than to do so with the words with which He spoke of Himself. "Christianity begins with the Incarnation of the Word. Here it is not only man who seeks God, but God who comes in Person to speak to man of Himself and to show him the path by which he may reach Him (...) In Christ religion is no longer a 'blind search for God' (cf. Acts 17:27) but the *response of faith* to God who reveals Himself: a response in which man speaks to God as his Creator and Father, a response made possible by that one Man who is at the same time the Word consubstantial with the Father, in whom God speaks to each individual and each individual is enabled to respond to God."[^74]
