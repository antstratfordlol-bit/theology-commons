## Chapter 24: The Procession of the Holy Spirit {#mateo-seco-trino.24}

The second immanent procession in God is that of the Holy Spirit. Of Him Sacred Scripture simply says that He proceeds (*ekporeuetai*), without further specifying the manner of this procession. He is designated by many names, all of them in relation to Spirit, Love, and Gift. Theology is unanimous in linking the procession of the Holy Spirit with the procession of Love. Yet the "personality" of the Holy Spirit remains the least known, to the extent that He has been called the "Unknown God"[^1] and the "Great Unknown".[^2] There is a clear pneumatological deficit in the piety of many Christians. But it is not only this. Theology likewise encounters greater difficulties in developing the doctrine on the Holy Spirit than in articulating the teaching concerning the other two divine Persons. Saint Thomas laments a veritable poverty of vocabulary for expounding the theology of the Holy Spirit, a poverty due to the fact that the relation of love is less known to us than the relation of intellect.[^3]

The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* begins its exposition of the article on the Holy Spirit by also referring to this discreet hiddenness of the Spirit in His self-revelation:

> "No one comprehends the thoughts of God except the Spirit of God (1 Cor 2:11). Now God's Spirit, who reveals God, makes known to us Christ, His Word, His living Utterance, yet does not speak of Himself. The Spirit who spoke through the prophets makes us hear the Father's Word. But we do not hear the Spirit Himself. We know Him only in the movement by which He reveals the Word to us and disposes us to welcome Him in faith. The Spirit of truth that 'unveils' Christ does not speak on His own (Jn 16:13). Such properly divine self-effacement explains why the world cannot receive Him, because it neither sees Him nor knows Him, while those who believe in Christ know Him because He abides with them (Jn 14:17)."[^4]

Notwithstanding this discreet penumbra of revelation and the evident lack of vocabulary, Sacred Scripture, Tradition, and the life of the Church provide ample witness for a theology of the Spirit capable of invigorating a pneumatological spirituality. We come to know the Spirit precisely by allowing ourselves to be led by Him towards Christ. After pointing out the Spirit's "hiddenness", the *Catechism* continues by enumerating where He can be encountered:

> "The Church, a communion living in the faith of the apostles which she transmits, is the place where we know the Holy Spirit:
> - in the Scriptures He inspired;
> - in the Tradition, to which the Church Fathers are always timely witnesses;
> - in the Church's Magisterium, which He assists;
> - in the sacramental liturgy, through its words and symbols, in which the Holy Spirit puts us into communion with Christ;
> - in prayer, wherein He intercedes for us;
> - in the charisms and ministries for building up the Church;
> - in the signs of apostolic and missionary life;
> - in the witness of saints through whom He manifests His holiness and continues the work of salvation."[^5]

This is an invitation to know the Spirit in His sanctifying action. The mysteriousness of the Holy Spirit belongs, so to speak, to His distinctive mode of existing within the Trinity. He expresses the mystery of the intimacy of God in a manner analogous to the way the spirit of man designates what is deepest within man. In studying Him, therefore, one must proceed with exquisite veneration for the mystery, without attempting to rationalise it. As W. Kasper notes, the starting point for the consideration of the Holy Spirit cannot be philosophical speculations—whether of a Neoplatonic or an idealist stamp—but solely reflection upon the action of the Spirit in history, as attested in Scripture and Tradition. "Hence the foundation of a theology of the Holy Spirit does not consist in analogies drawn from the psychic life of the human spirit. Such analogies are familiar principally to the Latin tradition since Augustine; in them, the Son appears as the fruit of knowledge through the inner word, and the Spirit as the fruit of the will and of the loving union between the Father and the Son."[^6]

It was already pointed out in the preceding chapter that the analogy of the human word had its limitations—and very clear ones—when applied to the theology of the Son: the three divine Persons are identified with God's act of intellect, yet only the Father speaks the Word. The same holds true for the Holy Spirit: God is essentially Love, but only the Holy Spirit is personal love. In turn, the Holy Spirit is personal Love in God, but He does not spirate another Holy Spirit.

The analogy of the Trinitarian mystery with the life of the human spirit is sound and assists in casting a discreet light upon the mystery. Yet it is only that: an analogy in whose concrete application, as we shall see, there is likewise no unanimity among theologians. Thus, for example, there is a clear divergence between Latins and Greeks, especially regarding the application to the Holy Spirit of the analogy with the human spirit; even within the broad lines of Latin theology, serious differences persist regarding the manner of applying the analogy of love to the Spirit.

We know the Holy Spirit through His sanctifying work, that is, through His intervention in salvation history and, above all, through His relation to Christ. Christ is the fruit of the Holy Spirit and, in turn, sends His Spirit upon the disciples. The Spirit of Christ is the one who unites us to Christ and, in uniting us to Him, veiledly discloses His own being. Perhaps for this reason, although the revelation of the divinity of the Holy Spirit is clear in the New Testament,[^7] the doctrine concerning the Holy Spirit was developed explicitly only after the definitive clarification of the theology of the Word, and in dependence upon it.

### Names, Titles, and Symbols of the Holy Spirit

Many are the names and appellations that the Holy Spirit receives both in Sacred Scripture and in theological tradition. These names discreetly introduce us to the knowledge of His mode of being in the Trinity. When Saint Thomas lamented the poverty of vocabulary for elaborating a theology of the Holy Spirit, he was referring not to these names, but to the lack of proper names that could be applied to the Spirit exclusively, as happens with the name Word, which applies exclusively to the second Person. There are no names that can be attributed exclusively to the Holy Spirit. Even the three that are applied to Him most frequently—Spirit, Love, Gift—are, at the same time, names applied to the divine essence. God Himself is spirit and love (cf. e.g. Jn 4:24 and 1 Jn 4:8).

The name with which Our Lord designates Him is precisely that of Holy Spirit. This name is, so to speak, His most proper name. It is, for example, the name by which we invoke Him in the celebration of baptism (cf. Mt 28:19), or with which Saint Paul designates Him when speaking of His indwelling in the soul of the just (cf. Rom 5:5). Although the names "spirit" and "holy" are frequently applied to the Divinity, the conjunction of these two names is used only to designate the third Person.[^8] It is a name—comments Saint Augustine—eminently suited to be applied to the third Person, who is the bond of union between Father and Son, because it contains that which is common to them: spirituality and holiness.[^9]

To refer to the third Person, Our Lord also employs the name Spirit accompanied by other appellations: He calls Him the Paraclete, the Counsellor or Comforter (Jn 14:16, 26; 15:26; 16:7) and the Spirit of Truth (Jn 16:13).[^10] In the New Testament, the third Person is also designated as the Spirit of the promise (Gal 3:14; Eph 1:13), the Spirit of adoption (Rom 8:15; Gal 4:6), the Spirit of Christ (Rom 8:9), the Spirit of the Lord (2 Cor 3:17), the Spirit of God (Rom 8:9, 14; 15:19; 1 Cor 6:11; 7:40), and the Spirit of glory (1 Pet 4:14).[^11]

The name Love likewise belongs as a proper name to the Holy Spirit, who proceeds by way of love. In this case, the word Love is equivalent to the word Word in so far as it designates the Person who is the *terminus ad quem* of the procession. Just as Word designates what is understood as present in the one who understands, so the word Love designates the beloved in so far as present in the lover.[^12] The same happens with the name Gift. This term designates the "aptitude for being given". By reason of His origin—love—the Spirit is the Person in whom God Himself is given to the rational creature, or to put it another way, in giving Him, God gives Himself. God gives us His love through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us (cf. Rom 5:5).

The Holy Spirit receives the titles of Lord and Giver of life. This is seen with particular universality in the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed. These are strictly divine titles, already found clearly attributed to the Holy Spirit in the works of Saint Athanasius and the Cappadocians. "Lord" signifies that He does not belong to the servile world of creatures. "Life-giver" implies the power to give life, which He possesses with the Father and the Son.[^13]

The Holy Spirit is present everywhere in Christian life. His mission to the Church and to souls is the pathway whereby the Most Blessed Trinity gives Itself to us, introducing us into Its intimate life. Hence the mention of the Spirit and the symbols by which He is kept present are exceedingly abundant in the liturgy and in the life of the Church. Here are some of these symbols according to the enumeration of the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*:

**Water.** In Baptism, water serves to signify our new birth through the action of the Holy Spirit. We have been baptised in one Spirit and given to drink of one Spirit (1 Cor 12:13). The Spirit is also the living water welling up from Christ crucified (cf. Jn 19:34; 1 Jn 5:8) and welling up within us unto eternal life (cf. Jn 4:10–14; 7:38; 1 Cor 10:4; Rev 21:6; 22:17).

**Anointing.** Anointing with oil is symbolic of the Holy Spirit, to the point of having become synonymous with Him (cf. 1 Jn 2:20, 27; 2 Cor 1:21). To grasp its full force, one must return to the Anointing of Jesus by the Spirit. "Christ" signifies "Anointed" by the Spirit of God. Jesus is the Anointed of God in a unique manner: the humanity assumed by the Son is wholly "anointed by the Holy Spirit". Jesus is constituted "Christ" by the Holy Spirit (cf. Lk 4:18–19; Is 61:1). The Virgin conceives Christ by the work of the Holy Spirit (cf. Lk 1:35; Mt 1:20). The Spirit is He with whom Christ is filled (cf. Lk 4:1) and whose power emanates from Christ in His healings and salvific actions (cf. Lk 6:19; 8:46). It is the Holy Spirit who raises Jesus from the dead (cf. Rom 1:4; 8:11).

**Fire.** Fire symbolises the transforming energy of the Holy Spirit. John the Baptist, who precedes the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah (Lk 1:17), proclaims Christ as the one who will baptise in the Holy Spirit and fire (Lk 3:16). The Holy Spirit rested upon the disciples on the morning of Pentecost in the form of tongues of fire (Acts 2:3–4). Spiritual tradition has preserved this symbolism of fire as one of the most expressive of the action of the Holy Spirit in the soul.

**The Cloud and the Light.** These two symbols are inseparable in the manifestations of the Holy Spirit. From the theophanies of the Old Testament, the Cloud—now dark, now luminous—reveals the living and saving God, casting a veil over the transcendence of His Glory. These figures find their full realization in Christ. The Holy Spirit descends upon the Virgin Mary and "overshadows" her so that she may conceive and bear Jesus (Lk 1:35); at the Transfiguration, a cloud came and overshadowed Jesus, and a voice came out of the cloud, saying: "This is my Son, my Chosen; listen to him" (Lk 9:34–35).

**The Seal.** The seal is a symbol closely related to anointing. God has set His seal upon Christ (cf. Jn 6:27) and also marks us with His seal in Him (2 Cor 1:22; Eph 1:13; 4:30). The image of the seal indicates the indelible character of the Anointing of the Holy Spirit in the sacraments of Baptism, Confirmation, and Holy Orders.

**The Hand.** Jesus heals the sick by laying hands upon them (cf. Mk 6:5; 8:23). The Apostles will do the same in His name (cf. Mk 16:18; Acts 5:12; 14:3). The Holy Spirit is imparted through the laying on of the Apostles' hands (cf. Acts 8:17–19; 13:3; 19:6). The Church has preserved this sign of the outpouring of the Holy Spirit in its sacramental epicleses.

**The Finger.** Jesus casts out demons by the finger of God (cf. Lk 11:20). In the Old Testament, the Law was written on tablets of stone by the finger of God (Ex 31:18); the letter of Christ entrusted to the Apostles is written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God, not on tablets of stone, but on tablets of human hearts (2 Cor 3:3). For this reason, the Church invokes the Holy Spirit in the hymn *Veni Creator* as *digitus paternae dexterae*, finger of the Father's right hand.

**The Dove.** This symbol evokes, first of all, the dove released by Noah at the end of the flood (cf. Gen 8:8–12). But above all it evokes the Holy Spirit who, at the Baptism of Jesus, descends upon Him in the form of a dove (cf. Mt 3:16 and par.). The symbol of the dove to suggest the Holy Spirit is traditional in Christian iconography.[^14]

### The Procession of the Holy Spirit According to the Operation of the Will

There are two central statements belonging to faith concerning the procession of the Holy Spirit: (a) that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father; (b) that this procession is not a generation, but a spiration. This emerges clearly from the texts of Sacred Scripture, which affirm the procession of the Spirit with respect to the Father, but at no point liken this procession to a generation. The Creeds of the Church explicitly confess these two affirmations. Following the affirmation of the divinity of the Holy Spirit by the Council of Constantinople (AD 381) as Lord and Giver of life, theology proceeded to distinguish His mode of proceeding as distinct from the generation of the Son. Thus, for example, the *Quicumque* Creed affirms that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, and that He is neither made, nor created, nor begotten.[^15]

Historically, the explicit articulation of the doctrine of the Holy Spirit took place whilst confronting the difficulties raised by heresies. Once the personality of the Spirit was established and the various forms of modalism rejected, it became necessary to defend the divinity of the Holy Spirit against the Pneumatomachians. We have already seen the struggle of the Cappadocians, whose theological enterprise culminated at the First Council of Constantinople in the clear affirmation of the divinity of the Holy Spirit. The Pneumatomachians insisted upon their denial of the divinity of the Holy Spirit by arguing that, if the Spirit proceeded from the Father, then the Father would have two sons. Hence the subsequent care on the part of confessions of faith to distinguish the procession of the Holy Spirit from the procession of the Son, who is the Only-Begotten.

The explanation of this second procession is oriented, especially among the Latin Fathers, towards the proper characteristics of spirit, holiness, and love, thereby completing the analogy of the Trinitarian mystery with the human spirit: the act of knowledge is followed by the act of love.[^16] The Holy Spirit would proceed, therefore, from the Father as love proceeds from the act of loving. There were many reasons for orienting thought in this direction, among them those that stem from the role of the Spirit in the incarnation of the Word and in the work of salvation. Holiness consists in love. The sanctifying work of the Spirit leads to nothing other than Love. Hence the Holy Spirit is conceived as proceeding from the will.

Classic and decisive for subsequent theology is this text of Saint Augustine:

> "The Holy Spirit also subsists in this unity and equality of substance. Whether He be called the unity of both, or their holiness, or love, or also unity because He is charity, or charity because He is holiness (...) the Holy Spirit is something common to both the Father and the Son, whatever that may be. But this communion is consubstantial and co-eternal. If anyone wishes to designate it as friendship, let him do so; but I judge the name charity to be more fitting. And this charity must be substantial, because God is substance, and God is charity, as Scripture says. And just as He is one substance with the Father and the Son, so is He one greatness, one goodness, one holiness, and whatever else exists in the divine essence. For with God, to exist is the same as to be good, great, etc."[^17]

This orientation of Saint Augustine would exert a decisive influence upon the West. There were many reasons for this in addition to his authority: the names that Scripture applies to the Holy Spirit—all of them revolving around love—the necessity of distinguishing the procession of the Spirit from that of the Only-Begotten, and the analogy with the life of the human spirit. Saint Augustine presents the Holy Spirit as the bond of unity between the Father and the Son. For the Greeks, by contrast, the bond of union in the Trinity is the Son.[^18]

Saint Thomas Aquinas will give a new turn to the Augustinian approach, centring the analogy even more deeply upon the life of the spirit, which unfolds in acts of knowledge and of love:

> "In intellectual nature, actions of this kind are those of the intellect and of the will. By the action of the intellect the word is produced, and through the operation of the will there is also in us another procession, which is the procession of love, whereby the beloved is in the lover, just as by the conception of the word the thing spoken is in the one who understands. Hence, in addition to the procession of the word, another procession is admitted in God, which is the procession of love."[^19]

Saint Thomas's approach is clear. The Word is Son, because the utterance of the word proceeds by an action that constitutes a true intellectual generation; the Holy Spirit is God, because He proceeds in an immanent manner in God, and God is supremely simple. The Holy Spirit is God, not by being begotten, but by being divine. The procession of the Spirit is not a generation but a spiration, as befits the nature of love, whose term is union with the beloved.

In any case, it must be kept in mind that spiration, like generation, is an eternal act. Spiration cannot be conceived as an act that brings the Holy Spirit from non-being into being: the Holy Spirit is eternal and uncreated, just as the Father and the Son are. The Father and the Son spirate the Holy Spirit as a single principle. Spiration is nothing accidental in them; rather, it constitutes them—just as paternity and filiation do—in the Persons they are.

Theology distinguishes between active spiration and passive spiration. This is another unfortunate consequence of the poverty of vocabulary. The Father and the Son spirate the Spirit, and therefore the most logical way to designate this act is by the name of active spiration. Considered from the standpoint of the Holy Spirit, spiration is called passive, since the Spirit is the *terminus ad quem* of active spiration. Nevertheless, this spiration implies no imperfection or potentiality, just as the generation of the Word implies no imperfection or potentiality with respect to the Father.

### The Holy Spirit as the Mutual Love of the Father and the Son

How are we to conceive of this love in God? And above all, how are we to conceive of the term of His spiration, who is the Holy Spirit? Saint Augustine, as we have begun to see, conceives Him as the bond of union between the Father and the Son. The Spirit would proceed, then, as the mutual love between the Father and the Son. This is clearly seen in this text, in which Saint Augustine argues that the name Love properly belongs to the Holy Spirit:

> "And if the charity whereby the Father loves the Son and the Son loves the Father ineffably manifests the communion of both, what could be more fitting than that He should properly be called charity who is the Spirit common to both?"[^20]

According to this, the Holy Spirit is the mutual love between the Father and the Son which, given the simplicity of the divine being in which nothing accidental can find a place, is subsistent love. This conception, obviously, smooths the path towards the *Filioque*, for in it the Holy Spirit appears as the common Spirit. It is the conception found, for instance, in the Eleventh Council of Toledo and in numerous Encyclicals.[^21] Similar statements are found in the *Credo of the People of God*: "We believe in the Holy Spirit, the uncreated Person who proceeds from the Father and the Son as their eternal Love."[^22] The same consideration is found in John Paul II:

> "In His intimate life, God is love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8, 16), essential love, common to the three divine Persons. The Holy Spirit is personal love as the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. For this reason He searches even the depths of God (1 Cor 2:10), as uncreated Love-Gift. It can be said that in the Holy Spirit the intimate life of the Triune God becomes entirely gift, the exchange of mutual love between the divine Persons, and that through the Holy Spirit God exists as gift. The Holy Spirit is therefore the personal expression of this self-giving, of this being-love."[^23]

The affirmation that the Holy Spirit is Love and proceeds from the Father and the Son as Love is universally accepted by theologians.[^24] Divergence arises when one asks how to understand the statement that the Holy Spirit is the bond of union between the Father and the Son, or what amounts to the same, how to distinguish in God between essential Love, belonging equally to the three divine Persons, and Love as a hypostatic property.

The most notable divergence arises between the positions of Saint Anselm and Richard of Saint Victor. As we already know,[^25] Richard establishes his entire Trinitarian doctrine upon the analogy of love. God is infinitely perfect love, and perfect love demands equality and distinction. There must be more than one Person in God, so that this Person may love with otherness. Yet neither is love between two persons perfect; a third is required, in order to be the common object of the love of the two lovers. The Holy Spirit is then presented not as the bond uniting the Father and the Son, but as the *condilectus*—the co-beloved friend. He is not the love existing between the two, but the common friend.[^26] Saint Anselm, on the other hand, more faithful to the Augustinian framework, directs the analogy towards the life of the spirit: love follows knowledge as its necessary fruit; the Holy Spirit is the love that follows the divine knowledge which begets the Word. Saint Thomas brought this orientation to its most consummate formulation in both the *Summa contra Gentiles* and the *Summa Theologiae*:[^27]

> "The Holy Spirit is said to be the bond of the Father and the Son in so far as He is Love; because since the Father loves Himself and the Son with one single love, and conversely, in the Holy Spirit, in so far as He is Love, there is included the relation of the Father to the Son, and conversely, as the relation of the lover to the beloved. But with this singularity: that by the very fact that the Father and the Son love each other, it is necessary that this mutual Love, which is the Holy Spirit, proceed from both. Consequently, by reason of His origin, the Spirit is not an intermediate reality, but the third Person in the Trinity. On the other hand, according to the aforesaid relation, He is the bond that mediates between the two and proceeds from both."[^28]

Following the statements of Saint Augustine, Saint Thomas maintains the conception of the Spirit as mutual love and as the bond between the Father and the Son. However, Saint Thomas does not locate the most important aspect of the analogy of love with the Holy Spirit here, but rather in the intimate structure of the life of the spirit. Witness this well-known passage from *Contra Gentiles*:

> "God must first and foremost love His own goodness and love Himself. Now, since it has been demonstrated that the beloved must necessarily be in the will of the lover in some way, and that God loves Himself, God will unfailingly be in His own will as the beloved is in the lover. But the beloved is in the lover according to the mode of being loved; and since loving is a certain willing, and God's willing is His essence, it follows that the being of God in His will, taken as love, is not an accidental being as in us, but essential. Therefore, God, considered as existing in His will, must necessarily be truly and substantially God."[^29]

The parallelism with the theology of the Word is evident: God, in so far as known by Himself, subsists in Himself as the Word; God, in so far as loved, exists in Himself as the Holy Spirit. Thomas expresses this thought frequently.[^30] This approach might appear excessively essentialist, since it seems focused upon the spiritual perfection of the divine essence. Y. Congar has pointed out that this is not so. For Saint Thomas, "everything that is active in God belongs to the Persons: *actiones sunt suppositorum*. Essential self-knowledge and self-love do not exist except hypostatised in personal subjects who are distinguished solely by the opposition of the relations that constitute them."[^31] They are subsistent relations, for they receive that subsistence from the divine substance with which they are identified.[^32]

The conception of the Holy Spirit as love is joined in Thomas Aquinas with the affirmation that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son. The Easterners are right when they say that the doctrine of the *Filioque* is embedded in the Latin Trinitarian conception. After so many years of reflection it has become an important part of its theological thinking. Indeed, in the Latin tradition, the Holy Spirit appears not only as love, but as the mutual love between the Father and the Son and as the bond that unites them:

> "The mutual Love of the Father and the Son, distinct from each of the lovers as the pure relation of union in Love, possesses thereby all the substantial reality of each of the lovers. Thus this Love is God: God is Love in such a way that He is, in the Person of the Spirit, personal Love proceeding from the encounter of the Father and the Son. In this sense, the Holy Spirit is, in all strictness, the Gift of God—not merely the readiness in God to give Himself, but eternal and hypostatic self-giving: that is, existing the one for the other in a manner that Love alone can achieve."[^33]

### The Holy Spirit as Proceeding from the Father and the Son

The procession of the Spirit constitutes one of the gravest points of divergence between the Catholic Church and the Orthodox Church.[^34] In this divergence, in turn, two diverse Trinitarian conceptions crystallise, which need not be incompatible. The apostolic faith regarding the Holy Spirit was confessed irreversibly in the Creed of the First Council of Constantinople, where, after professing Him as "Lord and Giver of life", it is stated that He proceeds from the Father, and receives "the same worship and glory" with the Father and the Son. The Latin tradition would subsequently confess that the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son (*Filioque*). This affirmation also belongs to the faith of the Church.[^35] It is implicit in the *ex Patre* of Constantinople and, of course, does not contradict it.[^36]

When the Latin Church affirms the *Filioque*, it does so as a profound comprehension of the *ex Patre* of Constantinople and, of course, never in contradiction with it. John Paul II remarked on an especially solemn occasion:

> "No confession of faith peculiar to a particular liturgical tradition can contradict this fundamental expression of Trinitarian faith, taught and professed by the Church of all times (...). On the Catholic side there exists a resolute will to clarify the traditional doctrine of the *Filioque*, present in the liturgical version of the Latin Creed, so that its full harmony may be made evident with what the Ecumenical Council confesses in the Creed: the Father as the source of the Trinity, the sole origin of the Son and of the Holy Spirit."[^37]

A few months later and in this same spirit, the Pontifical Council for Promoting Christian Unity published a Clarification entitled *The Procession of the Holy Spirit in the Greek and Latin Traditions*, which constitutes a remarkable effort to eliminate misunderstandings between East and West on this question.[^38] Naturally, the document seeks to address the objections against the *Filioque* coming from the Orthodox side, and it can be regarded as a sound synthesis of contemporary theological positions.

By the *Filioque* it is not denied that the Father is truly *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*, but rather this is reaffirmed. Indeed, the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, but the Father is Father only in so far as He begets the Son. To affirm that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father is equivalent to saying that He proceeds from the Father in so far as He has a Son. The Son is included in the mention of the Father. The very equality between the Father and the Son—the Son is the perfect Image of the Father—seems to indicate that the Son is one with the Father in the spiration of Love. Finally, given the unity existing among the three Persons, the Holy Spirit also characterises the generation of the Son, for the Father eternally begets the Son as the One who eternally spirates the Spirit. This is the reflection made by the *Clarification*:

> "Likewise, even though in the Trinitarian order the Holy Spirit is consecutive to the relation between the Father and the Son—because He derives His origin from the Father in so far as He is Father of the Only-Begotten—the relation between the Father and the Son itself attains its Trinitarian perfection in the Spirit. Just as the Father is characterised as Father of the Son whom He generates, the Spirit—who has His origin in the Father—characterises Him in a Trinitarian manner in His relation to the Son and characterises in a Trinitarian manner the Son in His relation to the Father: in the fullness of the Trinitarian mystery, they are Father and Son in the Holy Spirit."[^39]

For centuries Eastern Christians have been warning Westerners of the dangers incurred when one seeks to "rationalise" the mystery. They perceive this rationalisation, above all, in the fact that Westerners structure their Trinitarian treatises on the basis of the unity of the divine essence and with the primary concern of showing that the trinity of Persons does not destroy the unity of that essence. According to them, it is obvious that in this approach to the Trinitarian mystery, the primary concern is to safeguard the divine attributes so firmly argued in natural theology, especially that of unity. In particular, the axiom *in Deo omnia sunt idem ubi non obviat relationis oppositio*—an axiom that constitutes a standard argument in the defence of the *Filioque*—is said to have arisen from this rationalising zeal. The excessive emphasis on the unity of the essence is said to have made the *Filioque* necessary in order that the Holy Spirit might be distinguished from the Son.

It is clear that mutual understanding between the two traditions on the question of the *Filioque* cannot proceed from a "rationalisation" of the mystery, nor from a more or less facile dialectic which both sides could skilfully deploy, but rather from the humble acceptance of the Word of God. The data provided by Sacred Scripture are very concrete in the sobriety of their statements: the Spirit proceeds (*ekporeuetai*) from the Father (cf. Jn 15:26); the Spirit takes what is the Son's and discloses what the Son has received from the Father (cf. Jn 16:13–15); Jesus is the bearer of the Spirit, for the Spirit is upon Him (cf. Lk 4:18); the Son gives and sends the Spirit (cf. Jn 15:26; 20:22–23).

A "Trinitarian" reading of these texts is necessary: that is, a reading undertaken in the light of the whole Trinitarian mystery, considering each of the divine Persons in His mutual interrelation with the other two. The Father is not Father in the abstract, but the Father of the Only Son, such that His paternity, so to speak, bears fruit in the Son and, at the same time, is "characterised" by the Son. The Holy Spirit, who has His origin in the Father, "characterises" Him in a Trinitarian manner in His paternity, that is, in His relation to the Son, and also "characterises" in a Trinitarian manner the filiation of the Son.

With this simultaneous consideration of the processions, the dangers of a pneumatological subordinationism are avoided. History testifies that the *Filioque* was introduced into the Creed to prevent the subordinationism of the Son with respect to the Father, that is, to avert the perils of Arianism. But Orthodox theologians point out that the *Filioque* in turn carries the danger of conceiving the Holy Spirit as inferior to the Father and the Son, as an "appendix" added to the Father-Son relation. This danger becomes a reality, for example, when the Father-Son relation is conceived as something finished and closed in upon itself, that is, without a Trinitarian reading of the mystery that keeps in mind the doctrine of *circumincessio* (perichoresis). The Holy Spirit would then appear as an "addition" to the relation between Father and Son. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* provides a fine example of the effort to integrate both traditions, understanding them as complementary and presenting them within the framework of an overall consideration of the Trinitarian mystery.

The *Catechism* offers an important clarification regarding the doctrine of the Second Council of Lyons when it affirms that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from a single principle.[^40] The purpose of this statement, according to the *Catechism*, is to safeguard the monarchy of the Father, the sole principle of the whole Trinity. For this reason it is said that the Father, "as the Father of the only Son, is with the Son 'the single principle from which the Holy Spirit proceeds'".[^41] It would be an abusive interpretation of this statement if this *unum principium* were interpreted as though the Holy Spirit proceeded from the Father and the Son in so far as they share in the common essence. The oneness of the spirating principle must therefore be understood in the sense that the Spirit proceeds from the Father in such a way that the Son participates in the spiration as forming a single personal principle with Him.[^42] The Son is united to the Father in spiration precisely in so far as He is Son and Image: that is, in so far as He is receiving His whole being from the Father.

With these clarifications, the *Catechism* makes a significant correction to certain scholastic readings of the *unum principium*. Already the Fourth Lateran Council had specified that the processions belong to the divine Persons, not to the essence. Now, the *Catechism* continues, the Person of the Father is in Himself total reference to the Son. Hence the correctness with which it notes that it is the Father as Father who spirates the Holy Spirit, with the clear intention that the *Filioque* be read in the personalist context of the Father's paternity, and not in the context of the Son's communion with the Father in receiving His identical substance.[^43]

This means that not every interpretation of the *Filioque* should be taken as valid. On the one hand, the affirmation of the *Filioque* cannot mean that the Holy Spirit proceeds from Father and Son as from two principles, but as from a single principle, for otherwise the monarchy of the Father would be gravely damaged; on the other hand, this *unum principium* must be understood in a manner that gives no ground for thinking that the Spirit proceeds from the essence of the Father and the Son, but from their personal being. The danger is not purely imaginary, and several historical examples can be adduced.[^44] Hence the insistence that the Spirit proceeds from the Father as Father, and from the Son in so far as the Son is inseparable from the paternity of the Father.

In this perspective it is easy to show the agreement of both traditions when speaking of the Trinitarian order (*taxis*): the Son is the second Person and the Spirit is the third. The Son always precedes the Holy Spirit, and this order cannot be reversed. There is a theological reason for this: the Son characterises the Father as Father.[^45]

Following the affirmation that all the terms of personal relation characterise one another mutually, it is logical to ask how the Holy Spirit characterises the Father-Son relation. We read in the *Clarification*:

> "Just as the Father is characterised as Father of the Son whom He generates, the Spirit—who has His origin in the Father—characterises Him in a Trinitarian manner in His relation to the Son and characterises in a Trinitarian manner the Son in His relation to the Father: in the fullness of the Trinitarian mystery, they are Father, Son, and Holy Spirit."

At times generation and spiration have been considered separately, removed from their Trinitarian context, as if between the two processions there were, so to speak, no point of contact, or as if the Father-Son relation were not "characterised" by the Holy Spirit who is co-eternal with the Father and the Son. At times the generation of the Son has been considered without any reference to spiration, and spiration as a procession without any reference to generation. The present theological climate invites us to overcome these approaches. Here is the path charted by the *Clarification*:

> "The Father generates the Son only by spirating (in Greek, *proballein*) through Him the Holy Spirit, and the Son is generated by the Father only in so far as the spiration (in Greek, *probole*) passes through Him. The Father is Father of the only-begotten Son only in so far as, through Him and across Him, He is origin of the Holy Spirit."[^46]

Thus the fruit is gathered from pondering deeply one of the Trinitarian axioms in which both traditions fully agree: the Father is *fons et origo totius Trinitatis*. If the Father is the source of Love made person, that same Love—the Spirit—must characterise the relation between the Father as source of love and His Son as the beloved Son. The eternal Word of the Father is not just any word, but, as the Word of Him who is the source of Love, must Himself be a word through which love flows.

Finally, theology has always noted that the missions of the divine Persons are a reflection of the intra-Trinitarian relations. The relation of the Spirit to Jesus leads towards knowledge of the relation of the Word and the Spirit. It is Jesus who sends the Spirit, but Jesus Himself was anointed as Messiah by the Spirit. At the same time, the Spirit introduces men into the mystery of Jesus, because He is the Spirit of Jesus. A profound theology of the missions will do much to overcome "Filioquism" without thereby separating the procession of the Spirit from the generation of the Son. This is also how major Orthodox theologians view the matter.[^47]

This implies giving special prominence to the mission of the Spirit as the place wherein to recognise His eternal procession. It entails, therefore, a determined effort to unravel the richness contained in the theology of the missions. This includes, consequently, giving special prominence to the pneumatological dimension of Christology. An inspiring task is thus proposed: to elaborate with greater depth a Christology that brings to light the manifold bonds uniting Jesus and the Spirit, and from that vantage point to illuminate pneumatology. The Greek Fathers spoke frequently of the resting of the Spirit upon the Son. This "resting", which reflects the Trinitarian truth of *circumincessio*, assumes primary importance in the Trinitarian consideration of the Spirit.

If from the mission of the Spirit we can argue His procession from the Word, it will also be legitimate to deduce something of the relation of the Word to the Spirit when considering the action of the Spirit upon Jesus. The Spirit anoints Jesus as Messiah and eternal Priest. The Spirit directs the whole humanity of Jesus towards the Father. This anointing manifests that Love characterises paternity and eternal filiation in God, precisely by proceeding as Person-Love in the simultaneity of God's eternal today. One cannot speak of paternity and filiation without considering the Holy Spirit together with them. The Father spirates the Spirit in and through His Word. In turn, the Spirit characterises the Father and the Son as Source and Word of Love.

### The Holy Spirit as Gift

Sacred Scripture designates the Holy Spirit by the name Gift, and presents Him as the fountainhead of all the gifts that God bestows upon men. The Holy Spirit is the Gift in which God gives Himself. He is the gift of God (cf. Jn 4:10; Lk 11:13; Acts 8:20) and the source of all charisms (cf. 1 Cor 12:7–13; Eph 4:7–12). The Spirit, poured into our hearts, is the one who causes us to cry out: "Abba, Father!" (cf. Rom 8:14–17).

The Holy Spirit is, above all, the gift of the Father to Jesus, who is the bearer of the Spirit in fullness (cf. Jn 3:34–35; 1:16). The entire life of Jesus, from His virginal conception (cf. Lk 1:35) to His messianic anointing (cf. Lk 4:18), His priesthood (cf. Heb 9:14), His resurrection (cf. Rom 1:3–4), and His action in the Church (cf. Jn 20:22), is the work of the Spirit. Jesus is essentially the bearer of the Spirit; from His fullness we have all received (cf. Jn 1:16; 17:19; Heb 10:8–14; Rom 15:16).

The name Gift belongs to the Holy Spirit as a proper name. He is the gift of God *par excellence*, whether understood with the active nuance of giving (*dorea*) or as that which is given (*doron*); whether understood as intra-Trinitarian self-giving, or as the Gift of God to mankind. The Spirit is the Gift of God *par excellence*.[^48] The Fathers, especially the Latins, influenced by Saint Hilary and Saint Augustine, have highlighted this essential aspect of Gift, which stands out as the personal property of the Holy Spirit:

> "He [Christ] commanded them to baptise in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, that is, confessing the Author, the Only-Begotten, and the Gift. There is but one Author of all things. For there is only one God, the Father from whom are all things, and one only-begotten Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom are all things, and one Spirit, the Gift, in all things."[^49]

Saint Augustine uses the name Gift as a proper name of the Holy Spirit, joining it, as was logical in his Trinitarian schema, with the name Love. The underlying thought is a fine example of Saint Augustine's genius: God is love; there is no gift greater than love. Hence there is no greater self-giving nor greater gift than that which God makes of Himself. The Holy Spirit is mutual love and self-giving, the bond between Father and Son; He is also the gift bestowed upon men. The Holy Spirit is not only the gift that God makes of Himself to men, but also the gift that disposes men to receive that divine self-communication. The giving of the Spirit is in close connection with His intra-Trinitarian procession:[^50]

> "[The Holy Spirit] has relation to the Father and the Son, for He is the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. But the relation is not apparent in the name [Holy Spirit]; it is manifested when He is called the Gift of God. And He is the Gift of the Father and of the Son (...). When we say the gift of the giver and the giver of the gift, we express a mutual and formal relation. Therefore the Holy Spirit is, as it were, an ineffable communion of the Father and the Son (...). And to express in a name this concord and mutual communication, the Holy Spirit is called the Gift of both."[^51]

The Holy Spirit, in so far as He is Gift, is the pivot of the union of men with God, the author of the unity of the Church and of Christian love. Charity springs from the giving of the Spirit.[^52] The divinisation of the Christian is the work of the Spirit, and is so profound and radical that God alone can bring it about. Saint Basil grounded his defence of the divinity of the Holy Spirit precisely upon the divinising work He carries out in our souls when He is sent to them. The Spirit, who is common gift, communion, and bond between Father and Son, is sent to men to deify them by uniting them with the Most Blessed Trinity.

The name Gift is, then, proper to the Holy Spirit. It belongs to Him since He is spirated as love. What characterises a gift is precisely its gratuitousness, that is, the communication of goodness to another not out of self-interest, but out of love. The Holy Spirit is personally love and gift. The term gift is nothing other than an making explicit of the term love, since gift is nothing but love in so far as it tends to express itself in self-giving. Here is the straightforward exposition of Saint Thomas:

> "The word Gift, taken personally in God, is the proper name of the Holy Spirit (...). It must be noted that, properly speaking, a gift, according to the Philosopher, is an unrequited giving (...) and therefore implies a gratuitous donation. The reason why the donation is gratuitous is love, for when we give something to someone freely, it is because we will good to him. What we first give him, therefore, is love, whereby we will good to him. Hence it is clear that love has the nature of the first gift, through which all gratuitous gifts are bestowed. Therefore, since the Holy Spirit proceeds as Love, He proceeds also as the first gift; and for this reason Saint Augustine says that through the Gift which is the Holy Spirit, many particular gifts are distributed to the members of Christ."[^53]

God gives Himself to men in the Holy Spirit. It is the Holy Spirit who introduces us into the mystery of Christ, so that man's itinerary towards God can be described as a journey towards the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit: our spirit, enlightened by the Spirit, fixes its gaze upon the Son and, in Him, as in an image, contemplates the Father.[^54] Testimonies concerning the Christian life and how to live in the Spirit could be multiplied indefinitely. Suffice it to cite, by way of illustration, this rich text of John Paul II:

> "It can be said that in the Holy Spirit the intimate life of the Triune God becomes entirely gift, the exchange of mutual love between the divine Persons, and that through the Holy Spirit God exists as gift. The Holy Spirit is therefore the personal expression of this self-giving, of this being-love. He is Person-Love. He is Person-Gift (...). At the same time, the Holy Spirit, consubstantial with the Father and the Son in divinity, is love and gift (uncreated) from which derives as from a fountainhead (*fons vivus*) every bestowal upon creatures (created gift): the donation of grace to men throughout the entire economy of salvation. As the Apostle Paul writes: 'God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us' (Rom 5:5)."[^55]

The consideration of the Holy Spirit in His character as personal Gift in whom God freely communicates Himself to mankind is perhaps the most appropriate locus for perceiving the close relations that obtain between the immanent Trinity and the economic Trinity.[^56] The Holy Spirit is, so to speak, the point of junction between the two. Indeed, it is impossible to speak of the Holy Spirit without there immediately coming to mind the consideration of His gifts or His role in the christification of the Christian, to such an extent that a theology of the Holy Spirit proves impossible if one does not attend to His mission, to His self-giving to the Church and to mankind.
