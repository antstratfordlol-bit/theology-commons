## Chapter 13: Faith and Reason Before the Existence of God {#mateo-seco-trino.13}

We begin the systematic part of our study on God with a theme devoted to knowledge of the existence of God. This knowledge forms an essential part of man's journey towards God, for "whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him" (Heb 11:6). Man's "encounter" with God is a personal encounter that includes acts of intellect and love.

We pause first upon the questions concerning the rational knowledge of the existence of God. These are questions upon which—as has been seen in our study of the theological tradition—men have reflected deeply throughout history. We then enter into that other knowledge of God which man acquires through faith. This is a new mode of knowing, superior to natural knowledge yet closely bound to it. The existence of God thus becomes both an object of faith and an object of rational enquiry. This rational enquiry is embedded in the very heart of the theological task, famously defined by Saint Anselm as *fides quaerens intellectum*—faith seeking understanding.

At this point we shall pause to consider the differences and implications existing between both modes of knowing the existence of God.

The journey towards God is a pilgrimage involving the whole man, not only his intellect, but his entire personal being, also and pre-eminently his heart. Yet in this process, man's intellect plays an outstanding role. The search for God is the most comprehensive, demanding, and universal of all human journeys. To speak of man's journey towards God—that is, of his walk towards the "encounter" with God—implies, moreover, speaking of another journey: the journey of God towards man. For this mutual "encounter" God called man forth from nothingness; for this "encounter" he stamped his imprint upon creation and, above all, within the very interior of man. For this same "encounter" God revealed himself across the course of history.

Both journeys are, so to speak, complementary. Man would not seek God had he not already been sought by Him. This assertion bears decisively upon one of the fundamental truths of theological anthropology: man's capacity for God (*capacitas Dei*). That natural capacity—implanted in the innermost depths of human nature—is already a primary fruit of God's search for man. It is, at the same time, the guarantee that God makes himself accessible to man. Saint Augustine expressed it beautifully in one of his most celebrated phrases: "Thou hast made us for Thyself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in Thee."[^1]

God has made himself accessible to man, among many other reasons, because he has made him capable of attaining to Him. Theology must vigorously affirm this human capacity to reach God. A substantial part of this chapter is dedicated to analysing this capacity, which characterises the human being as a person.[^2]

The chapter concludes by highlighting the transcendence of God over all human knowledge, whether philosophical knowledge or the knowledge of faith. God is ever beyond all knowledge, all images, and all words. God cannot be known except as unknown.[^3] Our knowledge of God is always, in the deepest sense, an encounter with mystery.

### The Non-Self-Evidence of God's Existence

The affirmation of man's capacity to "discover" the existence of God does not imply that knowledge of God is immediately self-evident to man in his present mode of knowing. By his natural reason alone, man is capable of attaining to the existence of God, but only after an arduous journey that is not exempt from difficulties and, frequently, from errors.

That God exists is a proposition self-evident in itself (*per se nota in se*), but it is not self-evident to us (*per se nota quoad nos*). It is self-evident in itself because in God essence and existence are identical. He is the purest and most simple act of being (*actus purissimus et simplicissimus essendi*). God is not a contingent being. For this reason, whoever were to see the divine essence would immediately see that God is the necessary Being, that is to say, that he cannot not exist.

Yet no man on this earth sees the divine essence. Consequently, the existence of God is not self-evident to man; rather, it is necessary to deduce it starting from the existence of the things we know directly and immediately. Human knowledge takes the sensible world as its starting point. This alone is directly and intuitively accessible to it. From this point onwards, there remains to it only the arduous path of rational discourse, not that of intuition.

Ontologism held that the existence of God is self-evident to the human intellect. As has already been seen, according to the ontologists man would know God immediately, and through Him would know all other beings.[^4] It is worth recalling this proposition as a synthesis of this position: "Immediate knowledge of God, at least habitual (unconscious), is essential to the human intellect: in such wise that without it, it can know nothing. He is, in effect, the light of the intellect."[^5]

God would thus be the *primum logicum* of the human intellect: that which is known first and through which everything else is known. It is clear that God, being the *primum ontologicum*, causes truth in all beings: beings are true because they correspond to the divine idea. It is their conformity to that creative idea that endows them with ontological truth and intelligibility. Every being, by the very fact of being, is true. Yet from this it does not follow that the human intellect, in order to know it, must begin from an intuition of the divine essence. The human intellect begins from sensible knowledge.

Ontologism is opposed to the teaching of Sacred Scripture, according to which God is never directly known by man. The scriptural texts are exceedingly abundant: "Man shall not see me and live" (Exod 33:20); "No one has ever seen God" (Jn 1:18); "For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face" (1 Cor 13:12). From the philosophical standpoint, ontologism confuses the intuition of being in general with the intuition of God. This confusion unacceptably blurs divine transcendence over everything created, including the human intellect.

This does not mean that, on many occasions, the existence of God does not seem self-evident to those who have faith. When one believes firmly, the existence of God seems obvious. It is, so to speak, a manifestation of how faith illumines reason. This occurs especially among thirteenth-century theologians, who maintained that the existence of God is a truth so accessible to human intelligence that no one can doubt it. This is also the conviction we have seen expressed by Saint Augustine: no one in his right mind can doubt the existence of God.[^6]

Saint Thomas decisively denies that it is self-evident to us that God exists, and explains the opinion of those who think that man can perceive God's existence with self-evidence as follows: "This opinion arises partly from the custom of hearing the name of God invoked from the very beginning. Custom, and especially that which stems from childhood, acquires the force of nature; wherefore it happens that we admit as connatural and self-evident the ideas with which we have been imbued from infancy."[^7]

### The Affirmation of God from the World

If the existence of God is not self-evident to human intelligence, it follows that man's access to God entails an itinerary that, under certain circumstances, can prove arduous. This itinerary is a rich and complex vital process in which numerous and diverse factors intervene. The whole man is engaged in this process. God is also engaged. It is an interior process in which there intervenes, above all, the grace of God, with which human intelligence, will, and freedom must cooperate.[^8] The rational "discovery" of the existence of God forms an integral part of this itinerary. It is a "discovery" that renders reasonable the assent of faith, but which does not turn the existence of God into a self-evident datum even through philosophical argument, however coherent and rigorous that argument may be in itself.[^9]

It is nevertheless an authentic argument that addresses man and engages his intellectual honesty, since its point of arrival—the existence of God—is presented to him with sufficient clarity.

It belongs to the faith of the Church to affirm that man can arrive at the knowledge of the existence of God from created things. The First Vatican Council addressed the matter directly, in order to counter fideism and traditionalism:

> "God, the beginning and end of all things, can be known with certainty from the things that were created through the natural light of human reason; 'for the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made' (cf. Rom 1:20)."[^10]

The Council cites Romans 1:20, thereby framing the intent and scope of its assertion: the same scope and intent that we have found in the passages of Wisdom 13:1–9 and Romans 1:20. Both texts bear witness to a constant conviction throughout Sacred Scripture: man can know God's existence by lending an ear to creation, for indeed the heavens declare the glory of God (cf. Ps 19:1 [Ps 18:2 Vg]) with a force that renders man accountable for his rejection of God.

The Council affirms that human intelligence is capable of knowing the existence of God. This is primarily an anthropological affirmation, that is, a defence of the natural capacity of human intelligence—even in this condition of sin—to attain to the existence of God.[^11] It is significant that this affirmation is placed precisely where Revelation is treated, and not where God is treated as creator of all things,[^12] thereby pointing to man's capacity to grasp revelation, both in its natural and in its supernatural dimension.

With this definition, Vatican I brought to culmination the effort made during preceding decades to defend the capacity of the human intellect against fideism and traditionalism.[^13]

Indeed, in 1840 L. E. Bautain had been required to subscribe to the affirmation that "reasoning can prove with certainty the existence of God and the infinity of his perfections,"[^14] and in 1844 to promise "never to teach that it is impossible to give a true demonstration of the existence of God by the sole light of right reason";[^15] in 1855 A. Bonetty was required to sign a similar proposition.[^16] Pessimistic anthropological theses were thus discarded, presenting against them the anthropological exemplar of the Augustinian tradition: man as *capax entis, capax Dei*.[^17]

The First Vatican Council situates its teaching in the same line, yet within a different horizon. Whereas with Bautain and Bonetty the language used was that of the *demonstrability* of the existence of God, in the First Vatican Council the discussion centres exclusively on *knowability* (*cognoscibilitas*), not demonstrability. We are far from a rationalist approach: there is no claim that man can demonstrate the existence of God in the manner in which the natural sciences can demonstrate their conclusions; it is simply affirmed that man possesses the capacity to arrive at the knowledge of the existence of God. The possibility of the natural knowledge of God is affirmed, but the question is left open as to whether this natural knowledge actually occurs in man's journey towards God as an autonomous reality, or forms part of a more complex whole from which it cannot be separated or distinguished.

It should be emphasised that Vatican I is not treating of the manner in which man de facto arrives at the knowledge of God. It limits itself to affirming man's natural capacity, rejecting the view that man labours under an absolute impossibility of knowing God. For this reason, the teaching of Vatican I is entirely compatible with the view that man's access to God "needs to be aided by teaching and education, which guide him from without, and by grace, which guides him from within—which is as much as to say within a living religious community, in the torrent of religious tradition."[^18]

The intention of Vatican I was to show that faith is an homage congruent with human reason (*obsequium rationabile*) and not an assertion marginal or contrary to reason. It is, therefore, simply an affirmation of the radical openness of the intellect to God. It is a transcendental theological affirmation, for without this radical openness of reason to God it would be impossible for faith to be a reasonable act. Vatican I highlights the reasonableness and intellectual honesty of faith, and consequently warns of man's responsibility in his faith and in his unbelief.[^19] On the other hand, the Council balances this optimism by recalling the limits of human reason in man's present historical condition, affirming the appropriateness of revelation so that all men may know easily, with firm certainty, and with no admixture of error those things concerning the divine realm, even though reason could attain to them of itself.[^20]

### The Proofs of the Existence of God

The existence of God is not a "question" belonging to man's "aesthetic needs"; rather, it constitutes a fundamental question for him. It is the most important question in human thought, for the search for God is, at the same time, the search for the foundation of all contingent being and the search for the meaning of one's own existence.[^21] Man, who spontaneously tends towards truth and goodness, sooner or later feels the impulse to pose the question of God and to seek the proofs or arguments on which to support, in a reflective and conscious manner, his conviction or his "inkling" of the existence of God.

Since man knows only from created beings, only in them will he find the starting point to attain rationally to God. The contemplation of created things thus constitutes the first step—an indispensable step—to attaining knowledge of the existence of God. Starting from what is created, and considering its contingent, limited, and caused being, one ascends to the existence of a being that is neither limited, nor contingent, nor caused, but is the cause, the beginning, and the end of all things.

This path corresponds, therefore, to the intellect in its essential activity: to read within things (*intus-legere*), discovering, by transcending what is phenomenal, the source from which all things flow. Hence the journey towards God can be expressed through two convergent metaphors: as an ascent from created things to God; and as a deepening into the being of things—especially within man himself—to find the Being upon which they rest as their ultimate and transcendent ground. The whole of creation is an intelligible love letter addressed to the human intellect so that, reading it in its depth, it may discover the presence of Him who makes himself present within it.

The proofs of the existence of God formulated throughout history are exceedingly numerous. Among them, special importance attaches to the Five Ways employed by Saint Thomas Aquinas in the *Summa contra Gentiles*[^22] and in the *Summa Theologiae*.[^23] These ways take as their starting point a feature of created being immediately perceptible to universal human experience: movement (change), efficient causality, the contingency of beings, the perfections found in things, and the finality inherent in all beings. These characteristics of beings, known immediately through one's own experience, are considered from a metaphysical perspective. And precisely in analysing the metaphysical dependence of some beings upon others, bearing in mind that an infinite regress in this dependence is impossible, one arrives at a first necessary Being who is, at the same time, infinite, uncaused, the principle and end of all things—a Being whose existence depends upon nothing.

All the proofs of the existence of God adduced by Saint Thomas rest upon the principle of causality and, in synthesis, state the following: whatever happens has a cause; every contingent being, even if it were to have existed *ab aeterno*, requires a cause; whatever is without being of itself depends upon a cause that is of itself. In other words, that which participates in existence depends ultimately upon a cause that does not participate in existence, but is Existence itself, Being by essence (*Esse per essentiam*).[^24]

To understand the proofs adduced by Saint Thomas in their precise scope, it is necessary to bear in mind that the complete explanation of caused or contingent being is not to be found in the preceding series of contingent causes that may have gone before it (in principle, it is not denied that such a series could be infinite), but in a cause that is not contingent, upon which those causes are in that very moment depending in the final analysis.[^25] Suffice it to recall that Saint Thomas himself, who elaborates these proofs, holds that it is not possible to demonstrate by reason that this world was not created *ab aeterno*. That this world is not eternal—according to Saint Thomas—can be known only by faith.[^26]

The mental course followed by Saint Thomas in these Five Ways is straightforward and appeals to common sense:

We see beings that are in motion; but if motion—both bodily and spiritual—does not have within itself the ultimate explanation of its existence, the existence of a First Mover unmoved by anything else is necessary, who is at the same time the complete explanation of motion.

We see that there are beings caused by causes that likewise do not have within themselves the complete explanation of their causality, for they cause only insofar as they are caused; now if there are efficient causes that are necessarily subordinated, it is necessary that there be a First Cause that depends on none.

If there are contingent beings—that is, beings that can not-exist—and which therefore do not have within themselves the ultimate explanation of their existence, it is necessary that there exist a necessary Being who possesses existence of himself and can impart it to others. Indeed, if there had been a single moment in which nothing existed, nothing would exist forever. If only contingent beings existed, their existence would lack a complete *raison d'être*—that is to say, it would have no sufficient reason.

We see that one and the same perfection is participated in diverse degrees by diverse beings. So it is with the transcendental perfections: being, truth, goodness, nobility. Now, "more" and "less" are predicated of a thing by comparison with something that is the maximum in that order. If, then, there are in the world beings more or less perfect, it is because they participate in diverse degrees in the same perfection, but they themselves are not that perfection; they participate in being, but they are not Being. That is, they receive it from another. In ascending towards the fountainhead whence that perfection flows, it is necessary to arrive at a supreme perfection that is, in turn, unparticipated, and the reason for its existence in all other beings.

If there are in the world, even among beings devoid of reason, activities that manifestly tend towards an end, it is because they possess that finality inscribed within themselves by an Intelligence that orders all things. It is like an arrow directed at the target. The intelligibility of its trajectory points towards the intelligent archer who directed it towards the target.[^27]

In formulating his "proofs" of the existence of God, Saint Thomas orientates himself exclusively towards the cosmological realm and rests exclusively upon the principle of causality. Saint Augustine, by contrast, ventures into the interior of man: into the demands of his intellect and of his heart. There is, furthermore, another difference between Saint Augustine and Saint Thomas: Saint Thomas adduces the ways understanding that by them one can prove the existence of God;[^28] Saint Augustine adduces his arguments seeking only to describe the itinerary of the mind to God. Saint Thomas is powerfully drawn to Aristotle and is meticulous in his defence of theology as a science in the full sense, that is, in its demonstrative capacity; Saint Augustine stands closer to Platonic philosophy with its appeal to the interiority of man, within whom truth dwells.[^29]

The Augustinian pattern of man's itinerary towards God is always virtually the same: to move from what is exterior to what is interior in man, and from what is interior in man to that which transcends him. The *raison d'être* of this schema lies in the strength and efficacy with which Saint Augustine regards man as the image of God. God is more intimate to man than man is to himself, and at the same time transcends him.[^30] This being so, in the laws of his thinking and of his loving—in the demands of his heart—in his yearning for infinite truth and goodness, are found the call and the traces of a Being who is Truth and Love. Every fragment of truth discovered by man, every desire of love, bears witness to the existence of Truth and Love.

Saint Augustine argues repeatedly that truth appears before the mind as something universal, independent of the very existence of man: whether man exists or does not exist, it will always be true that seven and three are ten. For this very reason, any true judgement points constantly towards an immutable and eternal Being. In the Augustinian universe, God is not reached by following the path of a reasoning based on the principle of causality; the existence of God becomes present to the human spirit as reflected in the truth existing within man.

In what is true, the unchangeable Good manifests itself to us. This is so because this eternal and immutable Good is the ontological and epistemological foundation of every good and every truth. Commenting on the journey towards God traced by Saint Augustine, A. Trapé writes: "Man recognises himself when he intuits himself as an existing, thinking, and loving being. He can therefore ascend to God by three ways: the way of being, that of truth, and that of love. The second is the most frequent, and is for that reason the most typical of Augustinianism, because Saint Augustine loves to begin from the most unshakable truths, such as the consciousness of one's own knowing and willing, in order to reduce scepticism to silence."[^31]

All paths to approach God take created being as their starting point: the world and man. These are *a posteriori* paths, subsequent to the contemplation of the world. Becoming, contingency, and the order and beauty of the cosmos lead man towards the recognition of the existence of God, that is to say, towards the recognition of supreme Being, Harmony, and Beauty; his openness to truth, his sense of moral good—a good that transcends him—and his aspiration to the infinite lead man to interrogate himself regarding the existence of God and are, at the same time, signs that he bears within himself a "seed of eternity irreducible to mere matter," which directs him to the existence of God.[^32] Wonder before being was the beginning of philosophy, and wonder before being and beauty is likewise the beginning of the pilgrimage towards God.[^33]

### The Goal of These Paths

The Five Ways of Saint Thomas converge upon a Supreme Being, each highlighting some divine attribute. The First Mover must be its own activity and, since action follows being (*agere sequitur esse*), the First Mover must be Being itself. The First Cause, being uncaused, must have within itself the explanation of its own existence; now this cause could not have caused itself, and consequently it does not have an existence received from any cause, but is itself existence. The necessary Being likewise implies existence as an essential attribute, for he must not merely have existence, but must be Existence itself. The Supreme Being, wholly perfect, cannot possess any limited perfection and therefore cannot have anything received; consequently, neither can he have existence as received, but must himself be existence. God cannot have beauty or life; He is Beauty and Life. The First Intelligence, ordering all things, cannot in turn be ordered towards truth and goodness as towards something distinct from itself, but must itself be Truth and Goodness.

We find ourselves, then, before the so-called cosmological argument in its fullest exposition by Thomas Aquinas. This argument is nothing other than the search for the foundation of the actual existence of contingent being, which, by its very definition, could not be existing.

It is, therefore, the most ancient argument, whose rudimentary formulations are already present at the beginnings of Western thought and acquire constant validity in patristic exposition, especially in Saint Augustine and Saint John Damascene. At bottom, it is already the answer to the contemporary question of why there is being rather than nothingness.[^34] "The fundamental question of philosophy is: why is there something rather than nothing? Thomas Aquinas addresses this dimension of the question principally in the Third Way: the demonstration from contingency. Indeed, the contingency of reality implies its radical questionability. Everything that is, once was not and will not be; everything that is, could not-be. Everything that is, therefore, floats over the abyss of non-being; it is permeated and dominated by nothingness. This nothingness is not a reality of any kind, perhaps tenuous and vague; nothingness is nothing. It is a mere concept that we use to express the problematic nature of being [...] Being, despite its fragility, does not succumb to nothingness, and before it we are confronted with the miracle of being [...] The cosmological demonstration of God seeks at bottom only to bring to light this primal knowledge: it is an explication of wonder before the miracle of being."[^35]

Alongside this line of cosmological argumentation—from the order and contingency of the world—the anthropological argumentation is also customary: the demonstration of the existence of God from the internal reality of the human spirit, that is, from its acts of knowledge and love. For this reason, the Holy Fathers, as we have seen, spoke of the knowledge of God as being so "connatural" to man that it could be said that the idea of God was innate to man.[^36] Saint Augustine employs this argumentative path with consummate perfection: within his very spirit man finds a truth of which he cannot doubt and which speaks to him eloquently of God:[^37] "Do not wish to pour thyself outdoors; go back into thyself, for in the inner man dwells truth; and if thou findest that thy nature is mutable, transcend thyself, but remember that, in soaring above the heights of thy being, thou risest above thy soul endowed with reason. Direct thy steps, therefore, thither whence the very light of reason is kindled."[^38]

These arguments demonstrate the existence of God with sufficient force to make it reasonable to accept revelation through faith. They do not belong to the genus of physical or mathematical-scientific demonstration, but to that of metaphysical demonstration. Yet God—whose existence is the terminus of these arguments—is beyond physics and mathematics. He is likewise beyond all these arguments. God is beyond everything that can be conceived with finite categories. The "proofs" of the existence of God are only a reasoned invitation to faith; they constitute a rational appeal to human freedom and a proof of the intellectual honesty of faith.[^39] As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* points out, they are called "proofs" of the existence of God "not in the sense of the proofs sought by the natural sciences, but in the sense of 'converging and convincing arguments', which allow us to attain certainty."[^40] True certainties of a moral kind; not evidences of a physical kind.

### The Ontological Argument

In denying that the existence of God is self-evident to man, the possibility of knowing God *a priori* has been rejected. The proofs of the existence of God that we have studied hitherto have—as has been seen—a common starting point: created things. They are, therefore, *a posteriori* proofs of the existence of God. Is an *a simultaneo* proof of the existence of God possible? An *a simultaneo* proof of the existence of God would be one that could demonstrate the existence of God as being essentially contained within the notion of God. According to this, the proof of his existence would already be given to us in the very concept of God.

We have seen Saint Thomas Aquinas reject this form of argumentation, since, according to him, the proposition *God exists*, while self-evident in itself and to the blessed who contemplate the divine essence, is not self-evident to any mortal. We have also seen the various ontologist tendencies defend the view that the human intellect is capable—on this earth—of a direct intuition of the divine essence, that is to say, is capable of knowing the existence of God *a priori*. According to the ontologists, human knowledge of God would be immediate and innate.[^41]

The Anselmian argument proceeds from different grounds and is situated within a very different intellectual atmosphere: that of the conviction of the uniqueness inherent in the noetic content of the concept of God. The radical critique that accuses it of an illicit transition from the ideal order to the real order does not appear to have grasped the authentic meaning of the argument.[^42] In any case, it is clear that Saint Thomas, and with him the majority of theologians, in dealing with the proofs of the existence of God, accept none other than *a posteriori* proofs.[^43]

It may be said that the Anselmian argument, without being an apodictic proof of the existence of God, when read within the great Augustinian tradition—as was done by Alexander of Hales, Saint Bonaventure, and Duns Scotus—possesses great depth. It is no mere dialectical sleight of hand.

### The Natural Knowledge of God: The Value of Analogy

From all that has been said it follows that, at least in theory, a twofold modality exists in man's knowledge of God: one through his natural intellect; the other through faith. In practice, it is impossible to separate these two modes of knowing with precision, for they are intimately intertwined in the unity of the person who thinks and believes.

This twofold mode of access to the knowledge of God corresponds to the twofold manifestation that God has made of himself: one through natural revelation; the other through supernatural revelation. It corresponds likewise to man's capacity to discover God from created things and to his capacity to welcome the word of God in the obedience of faith, that is, to receive his word, thereby rendering unto God a reasonable service (*obsequium rationabile*; cf. Rom 12:1).

The fact that man is capable of attaining to the knowledge of God by the natural light of his reason does not imply that, in its exercise, he is not also aided by the grace of God. Neither does man exist in a state of pure nature, nor in his journey towards God is he ever deprived of divine assistance. That is why we observed that, in practice, it proves impossible to delimit one knowledge from the other with exactitude.

To affirm the capacity of reason to attain to God through created things is equivalent to affirming the noetic and theological value of analogy. Analogy, indeed, is the sole pathway whereby natural reason can attain—amidst shadows and negations—to consider something of God.

The Book of Wisdom (Wis 13:5 [cf. Wis 13:1–9]) accords fundamental importance to the mind's journey towards God by means of analogy. Analogy, indeed, ensures that our speech about God is neither mere metaphorical speech nor equivocal speech,[^44] but speech in which, respecting the nature of things, something is truly said about God. It is a mental process that involves three steps: 1) the attribution to God of the perfections we contemplate in this world; 2) the denial that these perfections exist in God in a limited manner; 3) the affirmation that these perfections are found in God really, but in so eminent a manner that their true nature lies beyond all our thoughts. Regarding God, as Saint Thomas said, "negations are true, affirmations are insufficient."[^45]

The analogy of being (*analogia entis*) is a consequence of the fact of creation; in it is manifested the unity of all that exists inasmuch as it proceeds from a single Creator. There is an authentic analogy between the perfections existing in creatures and those existing in God precisely because the perfections of creatures have been caused by God. We know that all perfections are in God; but we also know that they proceed from him as the effect proceeds from its cause, according to a mode of being that is necessarily deficient. "To affirm of God the perfections of creatures, but according to a mode that eludes us, is thus to remain between what is purely univocal and what is purely equivocal. Being signs and effects of God, the perfections of things are not what God himself is, but God himself is, in an infinitely higher mode, what things are. Consequently, to speak of God by analogy is to say, in each case, that God is a certain perfection in the highest degree."[^46]

Here is how Saint Thomas formulates this question: "We know God from the perfections that, proceeding from Him, beings possess, perfections which are in God in a far superior manner to that which they have in creatures. But it happens that our intellect knows them as they are in creatures, and as it knows them, so it expresses them by means of the word. Consequently, in the names we attribute to God, two things must be taken into account: the perfections signified, such as goodness, life, etc., and the mode of signifying them. By reason of what they signify, these names belong to God in a proper sense, and with greater propriety than to creatures; but as to their mode of signifying, they do not apply to God in a proper sense, for their mode of signifying is that which belongs to creatures."[^47]

### The Knowledge of Faith

The existence of God is also and above all an object of faith. We believe firmly that God exists. We believe by the virtue of faith, that is, relying on the authority of God who reveals himself. Man's "encounter" with God takes place precisely in the existential sphere of faith. The first and primary affirmation of the Christian faith is precisely the affirmation of the existence of one almighty God, Creator of heaven and earth, Father of Our Lord Jesus Christ.[^48] To believe in God is a most firm assent that engages the whole man in his relationship with God.

All the Creeds of the Church begin with this first and fundamental affirmation: *I believe in one God*. This is the one God, Creator of heaven and earth, who revealed himself to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and who in these last days has definitively revealed himself in Christ. For this reason, the God spoken of in the Creed is always, from the very first moment, the Trinitarian God.

This affirmation already places the knowledge of God provided by faith in a sphere far superior to that of simple natural reason. Faith and reason adhere to the same God; reason reaches Him through reasoning, following the traces of God found in creation and within man himself; it encounters Him as First Cause, as Pure Act, as the most perfect Being. Faith reaches God through adhesion to Christ; the terminus of faith is the Trinitarian God. The knowledge of God provided by faith embraces in fact the whole of salvation history. Indeed, faith teaches us not only about the divine essence and attributes, but about his personal mode of being and about his salvific interventions in history.

This is a knowledge more certain and profound than rational knowledge. Whereas rational knowledge grounds its certainty in the soundness of the path traversed, faith grounds its certainty in the authority of Christ. It is, therefore, absolutely certain. It is, moreover, a knowledge of God far deeper than rational knowledge. It is deeper in that it knows the personal intimacy of God; it is deeper because that knowledge engages man in his innermost reality. Faith, together with charity and hope, belongs to the realm of the theological virtues—that is, to the realm of virtues that terminate in God himself.

Saint Augustine condensed the theocentrism of the virtue of faith into a threefold formula widely used in the history of theology: *credere Deo, credere Deum, credere in Deum*.[^49] *Credere Deo* designates the motive of faith: to believe resting upon the authority of God who reveals; *credere Deum* designates the central object of faith: to believe what God has said of himself; *credere in Deum* designates the total self-surrender to God, as to Him who is our beginning and our ultimate end, which is realised in the act of faith. The act of faith implies, then, believing God, believing that God is, and giving oneself over to God. It is an act that engages the whole person and introduces him into the intimacy of God.

Indeed, through faith, whereby we believe distinctly in God as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, we are introduced into the intra-Trinitarian life to such an extent that the Trinity indwells our hearts—that is to say, there is a personal relationship with each of the divine Persons. It is, then, a profound and definitive encounter with the Triune God, which will reach its fullness in the life to come, when faith gives way to the clear vision in glory.

Thus the knowledge of faith is situated on a plane far superior to that of natural knowledge. Faith is not an extension of the natural knowledge of God, but involves a radical novelty: the novelty of the divine intervention, of the Incarnation, and of the Redemption. Men have seen and touched the Word of Life, Him who was from the beginning, and have borne witness to us of this (cf. 1 Jn 1:1–3).

And yet, despite this greatness, this depth, and this salvific efficacy, the knowledge of God provided by faith is still a very limited knowledge. Neither can faith, though it can attain to God with firm certainty, adequately express the greatness of God. The concepts that faith employs when speaking of God are likewise completely insufficient to express his greatness. God is beyond every human word and every concept. For this reason, every revelation of God always includes a certain concealment. God does not cease to be mysterious to man by the fact of revealing himself.

The reason is that the discourse of faith is also necessarily analogical. Precisely because there is no human word that can adequately express the mystery of God, even when Our Lord speaks of God he is constrained to use analogical language. It is the only language possible for speaking of the mystery. It takes noble human realities as its starting point: the merciful father, fidelity, justice, wisdom, love. These are perfections that are really in God, but in a manner that surpasses all possible language. They are present in an infinite degree. God is Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:16). Knowing how to use analogy thus becomes one of the key questions in theological enquiry. It is an especially important matter when speaking of God. We shall dwell upon it in the following chapter.

### The Importance of Natural Theology

In the contemporary era, no one has formulated with such precision and forcefulness the objections that, from a theological perspective, can be raised against the *analogia entis*—and consequently against the compatibility between the God known by the light of reason and the God of revelation—as Karl Barth. "The *analogia entis* is for Barth the invention of the Antichrist and the only serious ground for not becoming a Catholic, according to the famous phrase in the preface to the first volume of his *Church Dogmatics*. The explanation for this extreme formula is clear: Barth sees the *analogia entis* and the natural theology founded upon it in the same vein as the modernist theology of the Enlightenment and of liberalism, against which he directly contends."[^50]

By this rejection of analogy, Barth aims to prevent reason from becoming the judge of faith; but what he actually achieves is that faith is stripped of all rational support, thereby ceasing to be an *obsequium rationabile*. Barth subsequently qualified this harsh rejection of the *analogia entis* and of natural theology, showing greater understanding towards the Catholic position, which has always attached great value to natural theology.[^51] When commenting on the well-known passage of Romans 1:19–22 in his *Commentary on the Epistle to the Romans*, in its original formulation—that of the first edition (*Der Römerbrief*, 1919)—Barth does not appear completely to exclude the possibility of a true knowledge of God starting from sensible things. In the second edition of the *Römerbrief* (1922), Barth affirms that God can be known only by faith. The consequence is very concrete: the denial that a natural theology is possible.[^52]

In his writings after 1946, Barth appears to return to a more favourable position regarding the capacity of natural reason to know God, and consequently regarding the possibility of natural theology. In any case, it is important to bear in mind that "Karl Barth does not have great confidence in man's cognitive powers; he reaffirms the futility of rational proofs for the existence of God and confirms his solution to the problem of faith."[^53]

This denial of the validity and possibility of a natural theology occurs because the following theological truths have not been sufficiently taken into account: the creation of the world, which is not only the handiwork of God, but, precisely for this reason, also bears stamped within itself the trace of the goodness and wisdom of the Creator. Nor has sufficient account been taken of the fact that the human intellect is a gift of the Creator, endowed with the capacity to find truth, even though this involves effort and errors. Finally, sufficient account has not been taken of the fact that, without this natural capacity for truth existing in human reason, faith could not be a reasonable service, but only an arbitrary will to believe.

This does not mean that man's journey towards God is reduced to rational argumentation, that this argumentation becomes a rationalist exercise, or that reason turns into the judge of the act of believing. In speaking of natural theology one is not speaking of the complete itinerary of man towards God, but only of the possibility of understanding—and making understandable—to the natural intellect of man the reasons that exist for affirming the existence of God. As we have already seen, the great theological tradition—from the Holy Fathers to our own day—has always affirmed that it is possible for human reason, amidst grave difficulties and limitations, to attain something of the knowledge of God; more still, that the desire for God is inscribed in the human heart in order to propel it into this search. It is a search that engages the whole man—head and heart—and which entails, for that very reason, its own intellectual adventure.

The rejection of natural theology would, moreover, impede the normal development of faith. It seems obvious that some natural knowledge of God is indispensable for receiving revelation. Only if one begins from the premise that God exists can one receive revelation as the word of that God whom one has already accepted, even if only in shadow. Natural theology is an indispensable preamble to supernatural theology; and in turn, given man's present condition, faith protects natural theology and preserves it from errors. But to protect does not mean to supplant.

Natural theology has the noble task of forming part of the *praeambula fidei*.[^54] That natural knowledge is a necessary presupposition for receiving faith. It is only that: a presupposition that must never become a judge. In theology it will always be necessary to overcome the temptation of rationalism. But that temptation is not overcome by renouncing reason, but by opening the intellect to the consideration of the full truth. The God in whom the effort of reason terminates, precisely by virtue of his absolute transcendence, must be regarded as a mysterious being who transcends every word and all created knowledge. God is not an object that can be defined or mastered by human reason. God cannot be affirmed except as He who is beyond all objects and all beings.

It is clear, too, that natural theology is very limited. It cannot arrive at a Saviour God, because salvation depends upon the free divine initiative, which cannot be known *a priori*, nor can it be metaphysically deduced from the knowledge of created things. The history of salvation culminating in the Incarnation of the Word is a mystery of divine freedom and gratuitousness, which cannot be deduced—as is obvious—from the consideration of the being of things.

Nevertheless, the divine decision to give himself to man through revelation began with the creation of man in the image and likeness of God. God's revelation in creation is prior to supernatural revelation, but ordered to it and forming part of it. "Man is by nature and by calling a religious being. Coming from God and going to God, man lives a fully human life only if he freely lives by his bond with God."[^55] Yet divine revelation is not reduced to this natural religiosity. It is only a first step in the journey of God towards man. It is an itinerary that embraces the whole of salvation history and is concentrated in Christ, in whom the face of God is definitively revealed.
