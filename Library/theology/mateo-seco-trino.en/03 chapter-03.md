## Chapter 3: The Features of the God of the Covenant in the New Testament {#mateo-seco-trino.3}

The New Testament teaching on God is deeply rooted in the teachings of the Old. When the New Testament speaks of God, it has in mind Yahweh—that is to say, the one God, blessed for ever, who manifested Himself to Moses and spoke through the prophets (cf. Rom 1:18–25); the attributes with which He is described—above all His oneness—are the same as those we have seen ascribed to Him in the pages of the Old Testament. And yet it is no accident that it is precisely here, on the question of God, that the most fundamental differences between Jews and Christians persist. For the articulation of the concept of God that takes place in the New Testament, despite assimilating the essential features of Old Testament teaching, implies a radical novelty that goes beyond a mere development or evolution of the Old Testament concept.[^1]

This radical novelty springs from a new revelation which, in a certain sense, infinitely surpasses all previous revelations. God has revealed Himself personally in Christ, in the Son: "In many and various ways God spoke of old to our fathers by the prophets, but in these last days He has spoken to us by a Son, whom He appointed heir of all things, through whom also He made the worlds and the ages" (Heb 1:1–2). This is a new revelation of the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob, which definitively unveils His intimate life and His salvific plan for mankind.

The passage from Hebrews just cited shows the continuity between the two Testaments, whilst at the same time underscoring the specific character of the New: in the New Testament, God has not merely spoken words concerning Himself, but has sent His Word made flesh; and this Word has dwelt among men and expressed in definitive language the truth about God. This is a fundamental affirmation in the New Testament, which Our Lord formulates with utter clarity: "He who has seen me," He says to Philip, "has seen the Father; how can you say, 'Show us the Father'?" (cf. Jn 14:9).

Hence New Testament teaching on God implies at once continuity and discontinuity with that of the Old Testament. It implies continuity, because the God of whom the New Testament speaks is the very same God who had already begun to reveal Himself in diverse ways in the Old Testament. It is to the God venerated by His people that Jesus appeals, calling Him His Father (cf. Mt 5:48), His *Abba* (Mk 14:36). At the same time, this New Testament teaching implies discontinuity and radical novelty, for it differs from the corresponding assertions of the Old Testament by the fact that all of them, without exception, possess a concrete and tangible point of reference: the face of Jesus Christ (2 Cor 4:6).

Here lies what is most fundamental and novel in New Testament doctrine on God: God has definitively revealed in the Son the mystery of His being and of His will, because through Him He says to the world everything that can be said and heard concerning God (cf. Jn 15:15). For anyone who inquires after and seeks God, it can and must suffice to see and hear the word that Jesus speaks (cf. Jn 14:8) and which He Himself is.[^2]

This is a novelty that entails definitiveness and immediacy. The mystery of God is revealed in a new and definitive manner through Christ and in Christ. In giving us His Word, God has already told us everything in Him.[^3] The definitive character of Jesus' teaching on God is grounded precisely in His condition as the God-Man, whereby His words are, in the fullest sense, human words of God.[^4] For this reason, employing an expression of Saint Thomas Aquinas, it can be said that, in Christ, God teaches immediately (*in Christo Deus docet immediate*).[^5] This immediacy implies that Jesus not only teaches the truth directly, but that He is also Himself the very Truth taught: Christ is in Himself the revelation of God, and whoever sees Him sees the Father (cf. Jn 14:9; 12:45).[^6]

Jesus' teaching on God attains its culminating point precisely in the revelation of the mystery of the Trinity—a revelation which implies, alongside the affirmation that there exists only one God, the affirmation that this one God is simultaneously Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (cf. Mt 28:19). Here lies what is most specific to the God who is revealed in Christ. This revelation of the Trinitarian mystery of God, in turn, constitutes an essential part of the saving mediation of Jesus Christ, since the salvation of man is brought about precisely in his ineffable insertion into the intimate life of God. Man is made a child of God in the Son through the Holy Spirit.

### Continuity with the Old Testament

This radically new situation, which arises with the revelation of the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity and according to which the oneness of God so firmly professed by the people of Israel does not imply a oneness of persons, is nonetheless characterised by a clear harmony between the two Testaments. The God of whom Jesus speaks is the God of Abraham, of Isaac, and of Jacob (cf. Mk 12:26–27); the traits with which He describes Him are likewise substantially the same, although in Jesus they find a new dimension and perspective.

Jesus responds to the question of the scribe concerning which is the first commandment by referring precisely to Deuteronomy 6:4–5: "The first is, 'Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God, the Lord is one'" (Mk 12:29; cf. Mt 22:37)—that is, by pointing back to the fundamental text of the Old Testament confession of the oneness of God. The continuity between the two Testaments emerges with particular clarity here, in the reaffirmation of Old Testament monotheism made in the New. Consequently, the New Testament affirmation of the Trinitarian mystery cannot be understood as a "mitigation" of Old Testament monotheism, or as a "weakened monotheism", but rather as a deepening into the intimate life of the one and blessed God. Jesus of Nazareth not only confirmed the monotheism of the Old Testament, but deepened it. For Christians there exists only one true God. Thus one of the chronologically earliest texts of the New Testament summarises it: "Hence, as to the eating of food offered to idols, we know that 'an idol has no real existence,' and that 'there is no God but one'" (1 Cor 8:4).[^7] Monotheism is taught by the New Testament with as much force as in the Old; the revelation of the mystery of the Trinity must be understood in no other way than as an ineffable opening up of the infinitely rich reality of the one true God.

### God is One, Good, Wise, Just, and Powerful

It was stated a moment ago that the features with which Jesus of Nazareth describes God are substantially the same as those of the Old Testament, and that in Him they find a new dimension and a new perspective: God is good, wise, just, powerful, full of pity and mercy in a manner unsuspected by the men of the Old Testament. These divine attributes find a new expression as they are revealed in the face of Jesus Christ.

When Jesus speaks of God, He has as the background of His thought the entire Old Testament doctrine on creation and on the supreme lordship of God: "He answered, 'Have you not read that He who made them from the beginning made them male and female, and said, "For this reason a man shall leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two shall become one flesh"? So they are no longer two but one flesh. What therefore God has joined together, let not man put asunder'" (Mt 19:4–6; cf. Mk 13:19).

As in the Old Testament, so too in the New the transcendence of the Creator over the world is taught: "The God who made the world and everything in it, being Lord of heaven and earth, does not live in shrines made by man, nor is He served by human hands, as though He needed anything, since He himself gives to all men life and breath and everything" (Acts 17:24–25). The New Testament insists upon the transcendence of God with respect to the world at the same time as it proclaims faith in the mystery of the Incarnation; and it does so by affirming, in a manner far superior to that of the Old Testament, both the transcendence of God and His closeness to the world and to man. God not only continues to care for the world down to its smallest details (cf. Mt 6:25–31; Jn 5:17), but in Christ He has drawn near to men in a new way, far surpassing the manner in which He had drawn near through the prophets: Christ is the Word of God, who exists from the beginning with God and who has now pitched His tent among men (cf. Jn 1:1–14).

In contrast to the Judaism of His day, which understood the transcendence of God with respect to this world in a way that at times left His closeness to man in shadow, Jesus insists upon the closeness of God to each individual. Man must penetrate into the interior of his heart, for there, in the depths of his heart, is the God who sees in secret (cf. Mt 6:4–6). The Kingdom of God is so near that Jesus says it is "in the midst of you" (Lk 17:21).

Yet Jesus' words concerning God stand in close connection with the Old Testament not only because they refer to the same subject and describe Him with the same attributes, but also because Jesus uses the same language. Jesus also speaks of God employing the anthropomorphisms and anthropopathisms found in the Old Testament, thereby making unmistakably clear the ineffably personal being of God. God is someone who finds a point of reference in the human person. This is seen, for example, in the parables of the prodigal son (cf. Lk 15:11–32) or the wicked tenants (cf. Mt 21:33–41).

The attributes with which God is described are the same as in the Old Testament: God is one, omnipotent, king, judge, holy, and lover of creation. Jesus' preaching about God, however, is accompanied by two features that make it unmistakable: the proclamation of the imminence of the Kingdom of God and the demand for personal conversion in order to enter it (cf. Mk 1:15), and the use He makes of the expression *Abba* (Mk 14:36) to address the Father.

In the early preaching of the Church, the urgency with which Jesus spoke of the Kingdom of God became preaching about Jesus Himself, for He is the Kingdom (*Autobasileia*). For this reason, the central message of the New Testament is constituted by the witness borne to the death and resurrection of the Lord.

As Gentiles were progressively converted to Christianity, emphasis was placed upon teaching concerning the oneness of God (cf., e.g., 1 Thess 1:9–10; 1 Cor 8:6; Eph 4:4–6; 1 Tim 2:5), since conversion to Christianity entails faith in one God and the renunciation of idolatry. In the preaching addressed to the Jews, stress was laid upon the fact that Jesus had been raised by God (cf. Acts 2:24; 1 Pet 1:21) with a clear theological intent: this resurrection implies divine seal and approval upon all that Jesus had said concerning God and, specifically, upon His revelation of the Trinitarian mystery. In this regard, it must not be forgotten that the true reason for the condemnation of Jesus to death was His teaching about God and His claim to be the Son of God with a filiation superior to that of other men.[^8]

God is one. As in the Old Testament, the proclamation of Deuteronomy 6:4—"Hear, O Israel: The Lord our God is one Lord; and you shall love the Lord your God with all your heart..."—to which Jesus appeals (Mk 12:29–30), forms the backbone of New Testament teaching on God. In the New Testament writings, repeated profession of faith is made in one God (cf., e.g., 1 Cor 8:4–6; Eph 4:6; 1 Tim 2:5; Rom 3:30), in a God who is unique (cf. Rom 16:27; 1 Tim 1:17; 6:15) and from whom all things come (Rom 11:36; Heb 2:10; 1 Cor 8:6; Rev 4:11). The New Testament writers repudiate all idolatry with the same vigour as the Old Testament prophets (cf., e.g., Rom 1:18; Eph 4:17–24; 1 Pet 4:3). The affirmation of the oneness of God is a constant theme in doxologies and early Christian hymns (cf., e.g., 1 Tim 1:17; 6:15–16; Rom 16:27; Rev 7:10–12).

One does not find in the New Testament a systematisation of the divine attributes. As in the Old Testament, the enumeration of these attributes emerges in the course of prayers, formulas of faith, and narratives of divine interventions. God alone is good (Mk 10:18–19). He is holy (Jn 17:11; 1 Pet 1:15; Rev 3:7; 4:8; 15:4), eternal (Rom 16:26), wise (Rom 16:27), faithful (1 Cor 1:9; 10:12–13; 2 Thess 3:3).

God is Lord of heaven and earth (Mt 11:25). The New Testament—just like the Old—does not contemplate the omnipotence of God in the abstract, but in relation to the fulfilment of His promises and His intervention in history. All things are possible for God (cf. Mk 14:36). He is the Almighty (cf. Rev 4:8; 11:17; 15:3).

And yet, the "God of Jesus", even when Our Lord confines Himself to mentioning Him with the attributes with which the Old Testament describes Him, is portrayed in an unmistakable and new way. He is a God who is the God of the excluded and the despised. His image is an extension of what the prophets declared when presenting Him as the one who champions the poor, the orphan, and the widow, but it goes far beyond. Suffice it to recall the closeness of God to sinners: it is not merely that He forgives them if they repent, but that He goes in search of the lost sheep and places it on His shoulders, and that He has given His only-begotten Son so that the world might not perish but have eternal life (cf. Jn 3:16). Conversely, to content oneself with external observances is to mock God, for what God asks for is the heart, total trust, absolute love.[^9]

### The Novelty of Jesus' Teaching

The principal novelty of Jesus' message concerning God lies in the manner in which He calls Him Father and, consequently, in the revelation of the Trinitarian mystery. God is the Father of Our Lord Jesus Christ (cf. 2 Cor 1:3) in a special and unique way. Jesus, who speaks of the God of the Covenant, of the Creator of the world who revealed Himself to the Patriarchs, to Moses, and to the prophets, when He addresses Him calls Him Father in a way no one had ever done before. God is Jesus' *Abba* (cf. Mk 14:36; cf. Mt 26:53; Lk 22:42; 23:34; 23:46). By using the Aramaic word *Abba*, Jesus addresses God as Father in the affectionate manner in which Jewish children addressed their fathers. That Jesus chose precisely a word from childhood vocabulary to speak with God might be explained in the context of His exhortation to become like children before God (Mt 18:3) or to receive the Kingdom of God like a child (cf. Mk 10:15; Lk 18:17), but this is not sufficient. Christian faith, already in the earliest disciples, interpreted Jesus' expression *Abba* as the expression of an intimate relationship with God and an exceptional consciousness of divine sonship.[^10]

The expression *Abba* on the lips of Jesus possesses immense theological importance and definitively stamps the Christian understanding of God. In it is expressed, above all, total trust in the Father. It is highly significant that this is the form in which Jesus addresses the Father in the prayer of Gethsemane (Mk 14:36). The early Christian community made this expression its own, as attested in passages such as Romans 8:15 and Galatians 4:6. It is the very Spirit of Christ, dwelling in believers, who causes them to cry: *Abba! Father!* This intimate and familiar way of addressing God implies a new understanding of the Godhead. This expression—even without entering into the question of how frequently Jesus used it throughout His life[^11]—illuminates the numerous passages in which Jesus speaks of the fatherhood and mercy of God.

The expression *Abba*, as witness to Jesus' intimate relationship with the Father, also has an unmistakable bearing on Christology. It is evident that Jesus calls God His Father in an exclusive and untransferable way, applicable to no other: "All things have been delivered to me by my Father; and no one knows who the Son is except the Father, or who the Father is except the Son and anyone to whom the Son chooses to reveal Him" (Lk 10:22). This manner of speaking justifies the fact that, whilst all men are truly affirmed to be children of God (cf. Mt 5:48; 6:4–8; 23:9), Christ is said to be the Only-Begotten of the Father (cf. Jn 1:14–18; 3:16; 1 Jn 4:9). This filial relationship of Jesus to His Father stands on a distinct and superior level to that which other men have with God. It is very significant that Jesus never uses the expression "our Father" so as to place His sonship on the same level as ours; rather, He always distinguishes between "my Father" and "your Father" (cf. Mt 5:45; 6:1; 6:8; 7:11),[^12] and between "my God" and "your God": "I am ascending to my Father and your Father, to my God and your God" (Jn 20:17).

The filial relationship of Jesus to the Father appears with special prominence in the Gospel of Saint John. For Saint John, Jesus' filiation to the Father is so intimate and essential that upon it is grounded the affirmation of His pre-existence with God before the creation of the world (cf. Jn 1:18; 6:46; 8:58; 17:24). It has been noted that Saint John employs the name "Father" to designate God 115 times in his Gospel, using it exclusively to designate the origin of the messianic mission of Jesus: the Father is the One who sends, the Son is the One sent (cf., e.g., Jn 20:21).[^13]

The expressions used by Saint John are abundantly eloquent. Here are some examples: "The Son can do nothing of his own accord, but only what he sees the Father doing (...) For the Father loves the Son, and shows him all that he himself is doing; and greater works than these will he show him, that you may marvel. For as the Father raises the dead and gives them life, so also the Son gives life to whom he will" (Jn 5:19–21); "For as the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself" (Jn 5:26; cf. also Jn 6:57; 6:65; 8:29; 8:55; 10:15; 17:21). This unity between Father and Son is so strong that it is described as "being one thing": "I and the Father are one (...) the Father is in me and I am in the Father" (Jn 10:30, 38). In Saint John, too, Christ does not merely call God Father; He affirms that He is one with the Father, which is to say that His being as Son of God is the very being of God.[^14]

Dependent upon this new way in which Jesus calls God His Father is the force and realism with which Jesus proclaims that God is the Father of all men. Thanks to Christ we dare to call God our Father. When the disciples ask Jesus to teach them how to pray, the first thing Our Lord teaches them is to call God Father: "Our Father who art in heaven" (Mt 6:9). The daring boldness to call God our Father is the fruit of Jesus' consciousness of His own full sonship.[^15]

Hence it is said that, in His preaching about God, Jesus of Nazareth brings to fulfilment the Old Testament teaching on the divine attributes. This is seen with special clarity when treating of the goodness of God. Jesus reveals in His own Person the goodness of the Father. In Christ, the natural Son of the Father, is revealed the love of the Father towards those whom He predestined to be conformed to the image of His Son (cf. Eph 1:3–10; Rom 8:29). Jesus' own actions are a transparency of the universal love of God the Father for all men. His dealings with sinners and tax collectors to forgive and welcome them are the visible sign of God's loving embrace, opened to all men as Father (Mk 2:17).[^16]

The originality with which Jesus calls God His *Abba* is indissolubly linked to His teaching on the Trinitarian life. Yet this confession of Trinitarian faith takes place within the strictest confession of faith in the One God. Early Christianity felt a deep revulsion against apotheoses, so widespread in contemporary paganism. The divine sonship of Jesus, the Christ, is not the result of an apotheosis, and therefore does not come into conflict with monotheism.[^17] In Christian teaching there is no "deification" of the man Jesus, but an authentic "incarnation" of the *Logos*. This balance in Trinitarian monotheism is evident even in the precision with which the New Testament uses the word "God", *Theos*. The term *theos* with the definite article—*ho Theos*—is applied almost exclusively to God the Father, whilst reserving the same term without the article for Jesus. *Ho Theos* in the singular and with the article refers to God as Creator, or else to God the Father, but is not used to designate God the Son or God the Holy Spirit. Each of the three divine Persons is God—*Theos*—but only the Father, or God taken in the oneness of His nature, is called *ho Theos*.[^18]

### Jesus' Preaching as Testimony Concerning the Father

The revelation that Christ makes of the Father presents itself above all as a testimony—that is to say, as the manifestation of someone who is a witness precisely because He has seen. It is the revelation made by the Only-Begotten Son, the One who eternally subsists with the Father. It is, therefore, a wholly unique and supreme revelation.

This uniqueness is underlined in John 1:17–18, precisely by pointing to the transcendence of God: "For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. No one has ever seen God; the only-begotten God, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has made him known." In verse 17 a comparison is drawn between Moses and Jesus against the backdrop of the theophanies recounted in Exodus (cf. Exod 3:1–15; 19:16–25; 34:6–9). The contrast is evident: the Law came through Moses, truth and grace came through Jesus Christ. This contrast springs from the different relationship each possesses with God. In the first half of verse 18, Saint John specifies that the vision Moses had of God was not a direct vision; the vision of God properly so called belongs only to the Only-Begotten. God is invisible to human eyes. This is an assertion repeated elsewhere in Saint John (cf. Jn 5:37; 6:46). From this general rule the Only-Begotten of the Father is excluded, precisely because He is God and is therefore in the bosom of the Father (cf. Jn 6:46; 8:38; 3:11–13).[^19]

This is the reason for the manner in which Christ is able to speak of God: with an immediacy which Saint John does not hesitate to characterise as testimony (cf., e.g., Jn 3:11). Hence the true and unique mystagogue is Jesus Christ, who, as God and Son of God, is privy to all the divine mysteries and for this reason is capable of introducing men into the mystery of God.

The assertion of the immediacy of the revelation that the Son makes of the Father is underlined in one of the most solemn Lucan passages: Luke 10:21–22. Jesus, filled with joy before the divine mercy, exclaims: "I thank thee, Father, Lord of heaven and earth, that thou hast hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to babes (...) All things have been delivered to me by my Father; and no one knows who the Son is except the Father, or who the Father is except the Son and any one to whom the Son chooses to reveal him."

Jesus rejoices at seeing that the Kingdom of God reaches the little ones. The arrival of the Kingdom is essentially linked with the revelation made to them and which they have accepted. By the expression "these things", Jesus refers to the mystery of the Kingdom of God—that is, to everything He has come to reveal, and in particular the knowledge of who the Father is and who it is that has come to reveal Him. Verse 22 runs parallel to many passages in the Fourth Gospel. "The Son" and "the Father", with the definite article, correspond to John 1:18 and 16:15. Jesus demonstrates here a clear consciousness of His singular transcendence. The mutual knowledge of Son and Father recalls John 17:25. Knowing Jesus—knowledge of Jesus—is equated with knowledge of the Father.[^20]

The nature of the Only-Begotten determines the novelty and definitiveness of the revelation that takes place "in these last days" (Heb 1:1–3). In this text, as in those cited earlier, the definitiveness and fullness of the revelation made by God in the Son is highlighted, in contrast to the revelations made previously. This is due precisely to the singularity of the Only-Begotten, to His superiority over the prophets and the angels. The ultimate and definitive revelation is indeed made through the Son, but it is expressed with the preposition *en*, which, while it can bear an instrumental sense, "by means of" (cf. Acts 17:31; Heb 1:2), can also retain a locative sense, since God reveals Himself in all the actions and in all the words of the Son, for in Him the fullness of deity dwells bodily.[^21]

In Jesus, God becomes accessible to men with a human face. Hence the Son is immediately called the "radiance of the glory" of the Father (Heb 1:3). The divine glory is the divine nature itself which, insofar as it is manifested to men, elicits in them wonder and praise. If God is light (cf. 1 Jn 1:5), the Son is the radiance of that light, the effulgence of that light. What this expression signifies is clear: radiance proceeds from the light and is ever united to it, so that it is impossible to see the light except in its radiance. Hence the Son, radiance of the Father, is of Himself and in Himself the full manifestation of God. Thus did the Fathers of the Council of Nicaea understand it when calling the Son "God of God, Light of Light".

### The Divine Filiation of Jesus of Nazareth and the Mystery of God

As has been repeatedly stated, the essential difference between the two Testaments finds its most important foundation in the affirmation that Jesus of Nazareth is the Only-Begotten of the Father; and consequently Yahweh is Father and is also Son, since this Son—being a son in the strict sense—is of the same nature as the Father. Here lies the essential difference between the God of the Old Testament and the God of the New. What is new in the Christian conception of God does not represent something like an organic evolution of the concept of God in the Old Testament. Rather, it has arisen thanks to a radically new divine revelation, a revelation which, judged by human standards, infinitely surpasses all preceding revelations concerning God: in Jesus of Nazareth, God speaks and acts in this world in so direct a manner that from the beginning He calls Himself the Son of God.[^22]

The divine sonship of Jesus of Nazareth is the key to understanding the underlying reason upon which New Testament teaching concerning the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity rests. Indeed, it is in the revelation of Jesus' natural filiation to the Father—a filiation which, being natural, entails equality with respect to the divine nature and distinction with respect to the relation existing between Father and Son—that the revelation of the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity begins. In the order of the revelation of the mystery, equality and distinction between the Father and the Son flow precisely from this unique filiation which Jesus affirms He possesses in relation to the Father.

In the new and full meaning of the name of Father bestowed upon God, the new conception of the Godhead proper to the New Testament is made manifest. Just as the singular religious conception of Israel found its most prominent expression in the theophany of the burning bush by naming God Yahweh, so now the most prominent expression of the New Testament is that God reveals Himself fully in the Son.[^23]

Saint John presents the central mystery of Christian life in the prologue of his Gospel, declaring precisely that "in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God (...) and the Word became flesh (...) and we have beheld his glory, glory as of the only-begotten Son from the Father, full of grace and truth" (Jn 1:1–14). It is the very same thought that we encounter in the celebrated Christological hymn of Philippians: Christ Jesus, "though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself (...) Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name which is above every name (...) and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord [Kyrios], to the glory of God the Father" (Phil 2:5–11). This conviction is expressed with full firmness in the formula with which baptism has been administered from the earliest times: "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit",[^24] thereby confessing the equality of the saving power of the three divine Persons.

### The Truth and Love of God in Trinitarian Dimension

The expressions *glory*, *radiance*, *light*, and *wisdom*, so frequently applied by Sacred Scripture in all fullness to God, lead to the consideration of God as truth. God is light and in Him there is no darkness at all (1 Jn 1:5). He is absolute clarity and translucence. For this reason He must be regarded as subsistent Truth, primordial truth. All truth—all the truth and intelligibility that shines forth in creatures—comes from Him and is grounded in Him. "Since God is truth, His life is eminently luminous. The divine life is realised under the form of Trinitarian existence. Trinitarian life is, consequently, supreme luminosity and intelligibility. But our eyes cannot comprehend this life of light (...) This is what is expressed by the words telling us that God is truth. They mean that God is the clearest and most intelligible reality among all that we can know (cf. Jn 14:6)."[^25]

Saint Augustine expressed it beautifully when exclaiming: "O eternal truth, true love, and beloved eternity! Thou art my God! For thee do I sigh day and night!"[^26] And Saint Thomas writes: in God, "His being is not only conformed to His intellect, but is His very act of understanding; and His act of understanding is the measure and cause of every other being and of every other act of understanding; and He is His being and His understanding. Whence it follows that not only is truth in Him, but that He is the first and supreme truth itself."[^27]

It may be said that, for the New Testament, all the truth of God is condensed in Jesus. A central theme in Christ's revelation concerning the mystery of God consists precisely in His identifying Himself with the Truth: He not only reveals, but is in Himself—in His Person—the very truth revealed. For this reason, the concept of truth applied to Jesus—"Jesus is the Truth"—differs from any abstract conception of truth. Jesus is the saving truth for men insofar as, being the Way, He illuminates humanity's steps towards the Father.

In the New Testament the Spirit is likewise identified with truth. Jesus calls Him the "Spirit of truth" (Jn 14:17), the "Spirit of truth, who proceeds from the Father" (Jn 15:26). The mission of the Spirit likewise has to do with revelation—with leading man into all truth, which is to say, with leading man to that truth with which Christ identified Himself: "When the Spirit of truth comes," Jesus says, "he will guide you into all the truth; for he will not speak on his own authority, but whatever he hears he will speak (...) for he will take what is mine and declare it to you. All that the Father has is mine; therefore I said that he will take what is mine and declare it to you" (Jn 16:13–15).

The Old Testament, in describing the divine life, insists upon the goodness and mercy of God. By sending in the fullness of time His only Son and the Spirit of Love, God reveals His most intimate secret (cf. 1 Cor 2:7–16; Eph 3:9–12). As Saint John affirms, "God is love" (1 Jn 4:8, 16). "He Himself is an eternal communication of love: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and He has destined us to share in that exchange."[^28]

God is essentially Love, and at the same time this Love exists personally in God and is called the Holy Spirit.[^29] Here is how Saint Augustine expresses it: "The Father is to the Son, who is the truth, the true origin; and the Son is the truth (*veritas*) born of the true Father; and the Holy Spirit is the goodness (*bonitas*) flowing from the good Father and the good Son; from none of these is the divinity (*divinitas*) distinct, nor is the unity (*unitas*) to be severed."[^30]

> "God in his intimate life," writes John Paul II, "is love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8, 16), essential love, common to the three divine Persons. The Holy Spirit is personal love as the Spirit of the Father and of the Son. For this reason He 'searches the depths of God' (1 Cor 2:10), as uncreated Love-Gift. It can be said that in the Holy Spirit the intimate life of the Triune God becomes wholly gift, the exchange of reciprocal love between the divine Persons, and that through the Holy Spirit God exists as gift. The Holy Spirit is, therefore, the personal expression of this self-giving, of this being-love. He is Person-Love. He is Person-Gift (...) At the same time, the Holy Spirit, consubstantial with the Father and the Son in divinity, is love and (uncreated) gift from which derives, as from a source (*fons vivus*), every bestowal upon creatures (created gift): the donation of grace to men throughout the entire economy of salvation. As the Apostle Paul writes: 'God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us' (Rom 5:5)."[^31]

### Monotheism and Trinity

During His earthly life, Christ was a faithful observer of the Law and always showed Himself to be a devout Israelite who accepted revealed doctrine, as He explicitly stated: "Think not that I have come to abolish the law and the prophets; I have come not to abolish them but to fulfil them" (Mt 5:17). Jesus, for example, went up to the Temple as the privileged place of encounter with God. Jesus did not scandalise the Pharisees by preaching a God different from the God of the Covenant, but by affirming that this God was His Father in a supreme and unique way, by arrogating to Himself divine prerogatives such as the forgiveness of sins or lordship over the Sabbath, and by manifesting the merciful will of God towards sinners.

Here is how the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* describes Jesus' attitude:

> "Jesus scandalized the Pharisees by eating with tax collectors and sinners (cf. Lk 5:30) as familiarly as with themselves (cf. Lk 7:36; 11:37; 14:1). Against those among them 'who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and despised others' (Lk 18:9; cf. Jn 7:49; 9:34), Jesus affirmed: 'I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance' (Lk 5:32) (...) Jesus gave scandal above all when He identified His merciful conduct toward sinners with God's own attitude toward them (cf. Mt 9:13; Hos 6:6). He went so far as to hint that by sharing the table of sinners (cf. Lk 15:1–2) He was admitting them to the messianic banquet (cf. Lk 15:22–32). But it was above all by forgiving sins that Jesus placed the religious authorities of Israel before a dilemma. For, as they observed in their justifiable shock, 'Who can forgive sins but God alone?' (Mk 2:7). By forgiving sins, Jesus either is blaspheming as a man who makes himself God's equal (cf. Jn 5:18; 10:33) or is speaking the truth and His person really does make present and reveal the name of God (cf. Jn 17:6–26) (...) Only the divine identity of Jesus' person can justify so absolute a claim as 'He who is not with me is against me' (Mt 12:30); or when He says that there is in Him 'something greater than Jonah... something greater than Solomon' (Mt 12:41–42), something greater than the Temple (Mt 12:6); when He reminds them that David had called the Messiah his Lord (cf. Mt 22:43–45); when He affirms, 'Before Abraham was, I am' (Jn 8:58); and even: 'I and the Father are one' (Jn 10:30) (...) Jesus asked the religious authorities of Jerusalem to believe in Him because of the works of His Father which He did (Jn 10:36–38) (...) Such a call to conversion in the face of so surprising a fulfilment of the promises (cf. Is 53:1) helps us understand the Sanhedrin's tragic contempt in judging that Jesus deserved to die as a blasphemer (cf. Mk 3:6; Mt 26:64–66)."[^32]

The Trinitarian mystery is the mysterious way in which the One God is a personal God.[^33] So to speak, the revelation of the Trinitarian mystery cannot be regarded as a dilution of the oneness of God, but rather as a deepening into the mysterious life of this infinite and eternal Being. In turn, from the revelation made by Jesus Christ onwards, the confession of the oneness of God is inseparable from the confession of the Trinitarian mystery. God is One: there is only one God.[^34] Our Lord frequently appeals to this Old Testament doctrine. Thus, for example, He reiterates, referring back to Deuteronomy, that one must love Him with all one's heart, with all one's soul, with all one's mind, and with all one's strength (cf. Mk 12:29–30). And whilst He underscores so vigorously that there is only one God, He lets it be understood that He Himself is the Lord (cf. Mk 12:35–37). For this reason, His disciples can affirm without any hesitation that to confess that Jesus is Lord is the distinctive mark of Christian faith. This is not contrary to faith in the One God. In the same way, to believe in the Holy Spirit, who is Lord and giver of life, introduces no division whatever into the one God.[^35]

This confession of one God—the monotheistic affirmation—belongs to the very core of the Trinitarian mystery. And on the other hand, the confession of Trinitarian faith is not to be conceived as something added onto the idea of a single God, but rather as the way in which, when the fullness of time arrived, the oneness of that God is made manifest.

The rational argument showing that the Supreme Being must be one is clear: indeed, the absolute and infinite character of God excludes the existence of a second god, since this second god would limit the first.[^36] The unity of God excludes a multiplicity of gods, for it is the highest unity,[^37] as corresponds to the most simple and supremely transcendent Being—that is to say, to the Being full of knowledge and of love. Such a consideration leads to the thought that such a unity of supreme life not only does not exclude the trinity of Persons, but rather includes it. Indeed, as the Holy Fathers already perceived,[^38] without that plurality in unity, would not God be a solitary supreme being who would need the world and thus forfeit His divine being? On this basis it is understandable that, for the Fathers of the Church, the Trinitarian confession was the concrete shape of Christian monotheism.[^39] For Trinitarian monotheism is that faith which shows us how the personal God is knowledge and Love in fullness, fruitfulness, communion, and life; in what way the maximally simple Being lives as a personal being.[^40]

### The Natural Knowledge of God

The New Testament teaching on this important question proceeds along the same coordinates that have already appeared when analysing the theme in the Old Testament: God is within reach of the human intellect, for His glory is manifested in the beauty of the world; the sacred writers express astonishment at the blindness afflicting those who fail to render their adoration to God. These affirmations are developed at length in Romans 1:18–25 and in Saint Paul's speech at the Areopagus (Acts 17:22–29).

#### Romans 1:18–25

The context in which this passage is situated is the affirmation of the absolute and universal need that all men have of God's forgiveness and, consequently, the absolute and universal need of a Saviour. All, Jews and Gentiles alike, have sinned; all fall short of the glory of God, and all stand in need of salvation. The Gospel reveals the righteousness and holiness of God, by which the believer is justified. In this context, precisely when describing the pervasive atmosphere of sin in which the pagan world was immersed, Saint Paul presents a grave description of the moral maladies of the society of his day and a resolute diagnosis. These maladies are the result of having failed to recognise God through His creation:

> "For the wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness and wickedness of men who by their wickedness suppress the truth. For what can be known about God is plain to them, because God has shown it to them. Ever since the creation of the world his invisible nature, namely, his eternal power and deity, has been clearly perceived in the things that have been made. So they are without excuse; for although they knew God they did not honour him as God or give thanks to him, but they became futile in their thinking and their senseless minds were darkened. Claiming to be wise, they became fools, and exchanged the glory of the immortal God for images resembling mortal man or birds or animals or reptiles. Therefore God gave them up in the lusts of their hearts to impurity, to the dishonouring of their bodies among themselves, because they exchanged the truth about God for a lie and worshipped and served the creature rather than the Creator, who is blessed for ever! Amen" (Rom 1:18–25).

This passage from Romans powerfully evokes the argumentative *iter* of the passage from Wisdom cited earlier: the invisible God manifests Himself to man through His works; creation points to the Creator; the intellectual journey of the philosophers ought to have culminated in the Maker of the world, yet it deviated into the deification of created things; and such a deviation is by no means free from human guilt.

Pauline thought can be synthesised around these three major affirmations:

a) *The human intellect has the capacity to transcend the created world and attain to the knowledge of God.* It is indeed an intellect which, in contemplating the works of the Creator, can transcend them; furthermore, it is called to transcend them, to such an extent that when man fails to attain to the knowledge of God, it can be said that he has blocked, that he has imprisoned the truth through unrighteous conduct. In the Pauline text, therefore, the religious or pre-religious dimension of the intellect is affirmed—a dimension inherent to the human intellect as such, that is, to the human intellect of all epochs and cultures.[^41]

b) *The world is a revelation of God.* The Pauline assertions are clear and emphatic: God has manifested what is invisible in Himself—His eternal power and His deity—through visible things, so that it can be said that God reveals Himself objectively in them.

c) *Man has the capacity to know and recognise God.* It is affirmed here, just as in the Book of Wisdom, that the pagans had some knowledge of the existence of God, but did not know how to recognise Him as God—that is, they failed to hold a concept of God that transcended the mere deification of natural forces, nor did they know how to treat Him with the due adoration and thanksgiving. What is criticised is not that they were atheists, but that they failed to recognise God by transforming their knowledge of creation into authentic religious knowledge and into an authentic religious attitude of surrender to God.

This is a knowledge which, as seen from other Pauline passages (cf., e.g., 2 Cor 3:5; Phil 2:13), is a human task, yet not merely human, but prompted by the grace of God. To pass from the knowledge of God to the recognition of God implies a profound attitude on man's part—a conversion—for which the grace of God is necessary. Yet between this knowledge of God and the recognition of God there exists an authentic continuity. This is equivalent to saying that in natural revelation itself there already exists a call to personal conversion; that is to say, "God calls man not only to knowledge, but to the adoration and thanksgiving proper to His dialogical, utterly gratuitous grace. Natural knowledge thus appears, in the light of Romans and from the perspective of God Himself, as a moment or stage towards the recognition proper to faith (...) Natural knowledge is an interior moment of recognition within faith and remains as a moment of humanity and reasonableness."[^42]

#### The Speech at the Areopagus (Acts 17:22–29)

The conviction of the indissoluble bond between the natural knowledge of God and the recognition of God presides over the famous speech of Saint Paul recorded in Acts:

> "Standing in the middle of the Areopagus, Paul said: 'Men of Athens, I perceive that in every way you are very religious. For as I passed along, and observed the objects of your worship, I found also an altar with this inscription: "To an unknown god." What therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you. The God who made the world and everything in it, being Lord of heaven and earth, does not live in shrines made by man, nor is he served by human hands, as though he needed anything, since he himself gives to all men life and breath and everything. And he made from one every nation of men to dwell on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their habitation, that they should seek God, in the hope that they might feel after him and find him. Yet he is not far from each one of us, for "In him we live and move and have our being"; as even some of your poets have said, "For we are indeed his offspring." Being then God's offspring, we ought not to think that the Deity is like gold, or silver, or stone, a representation by the art and imagination of man'" (Acts 17:22–29).

This is a speech in which the *captatio benevolentiae* ("Men of Athens, I perceive that you are very religious")[^43] is grounded upon the affirmations contained in the passages cited earlier: the affirmation of divine revelation in creation; the affirmation of the possibility of natural knowledge of God; the pagan deviation regarding the true nature of God; and the call to the recognition of God starting from creatures. In the text we are commenting upon, a new nuance appears: an invitation to know God precisely in man, who is the image of God and of divine offspring.

Neither here is there any direct intention to prove the existence of God, for it is taken for granted that the listeners accept it; all that is intended is to explain the true being of God, so that the hearers may recognise as God the One who has revealed Himself in His creation, but who at the same time transcends it. The closeness of God to man—or rather, the closeness of man to God—is underscored with special force, for it is said that "in Him we live and move and have our being". This proximity of God is shown in the aid He grants to all who seek Him. It is, then, a closeness that is not purely cognitive, but active and providential, having a clear bearing upon the recognition of man's capacity to find God. We can recognise God not only because He has revealed Himself in creation, but because He grants us strength to grasp that revelation. Our being comes from God and is sustained in existence precisely because we live in Him. The Pauline discourse here employs the affirmation of God's action in the world as a reason to justify the call to recognise Him in the life of creatures.[^44]

For Sacred Scripture, not only are the existence of God and His closeness to man clear, but so too are the capacity that man possesses to recognise Him in His creatures and the responsibility for truth suppressed if he fails to recognise Him. Scriptural citations in this regard could be multiplied considerably—both those that warn of the responsibility falling upon man for his ignorance of the true God (cf., e.g., Wis 14:12–31; Ps 14:1–3; 53:1–5; Rom 3:20, etc.) and those that simply praise God because His glory is manifested in the harmony and beauty of creation (cf., e.g., Ps 104:1–33).
