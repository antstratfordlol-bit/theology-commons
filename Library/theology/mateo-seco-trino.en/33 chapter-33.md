## Chapter 33: The Indwelling of the Trinity in the Soul of the Just {#mateo-seco-trino.33}

The study of the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul crowns the study of the divine missions, for indwelling constitutes the term of the missions. The Word was sent into the world to unite all humanity to Himself; the Holy Spirit is sent into our hearts to unite us with Christ and thereby unite us with the whole Trinity. Indwelling, in effect, is nothing other than man's communion with the three divine Persons—a communion equivalent to a mutual relationship of presence.[^1] That communion is the *raison d'être* of the work of creation and redemption; it is likewise the *raison d'être* of man.[^2]

Trinitarian indwelling is a relation of presence between the three divine Persons and man in a dialogue of knowledge and love whereby God dwells in man, or stated with greater precision, man is possessed by God to the point where God causes him to dwell in Himself. The presence of indwelling is a relation that, like every relation, includes not only two terms related to one another—the divine Persons and man—but also the ground or reason on account of which those terms are related.

The indwelling of the Trinity is studied in two distinct loci: in the treatise on God, precisely in this place, where the study is framed within the perspective of the divine missions;[^3] and in supernatural anthropology, since indwelling and grace are indissolubly linked.[^4]

We are here confronted with one of the nuclear themes of theology. As has rightly been written, "Trinitarian indwelling is the principle, source, and goal of God's salvific action as an anticipation of beatitude. For this reason we may say that it belongs to the central core of theology, of the life of souls, and of the Church."[^5]

### The Fact of Indwelling

The indwelling of the Trinity in the soul is clearly and abundantly affirmed in Sacred Scripture: *If anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him* (Jn 14:23). In numerous passages the Holy Spirit is spoken of as sent or given to the disciples (cf. Jn 15:26; 16:7; Gal 4:6; Rom 5:5; 1 Cor 3:16–17). The Spirit who raised Jesus from the dead indwells Christians (cf. Rom 8:11). Charity is a gift of the Spirit, who cries out in our hearts: *Abba, Father* (cf. Rom 8:15).

The New Testament calls Christians—and more specifically, the body of Christians—temples of the Holy Spirit. In them God dwells in a manner analogous to how He dwelt in the Temple of Jerusalem (cf., e.g., 1 Cor 3:16–17; 6:19; 2 Cor 6:16). The explicit revelation of the presence of the Trinity in the soul, obviously, belongs exclusively to the New Testament, for only here is the mystery of the Most Holy Trinity revealed. Nevertheless, this revelation is preceded by a long Old Testament tradition concerning the nearness of God, especially to the people of God. This presence is nothing other than a personal relationship of knowledge and paternal love, manifested, for example, in God's converse with the patriarchs (cf. Gen 17:1–2; 18:17); at Sinai (cf. Ex 19:5–8; Lev 11:44–45); in speaking with Moses face to face, as a man speaks with his friend (cf. Ex 33–34). The presence of Yahweh accompanies the people and is manifested in a special way in the tent of meeting (cf. Ex 40:34–38) and in the Temple consecrated by Solomon (cf. 1 Kgs 8:10–13). The Messiah shall be called Emmanuel, *God with us*, because upon Him shall rest the spirit of Yahweh (cf. Is 7:14; 11:2). Walking in the presence of Yahweh is the pious Jew's supreme joy.

According to the Old Testament, God is present to all the earth. He is in the heights of heaven and in the abyss (cf. Ps 139:4–12). To this presence of immensity, clearly affirmed, is added the presence of God in the midst of His people and of the saints with whom He maintains a personal dialogue. This special presence of Yahweh is manifested, for instance, in the charismatic activity of specially chosen men to whom Yahweh sends His spirit. The Judges possess a special strength to deliver the people because they have the spirit of God (cf. Jdg 3:10; 6:34–35; 11:29); the spirit possessed by Moses grants him strength to guide the people through the wilderness (Ex 18:22–25; Num 11:17). The spirit of God can change hearts of stone, turning them into hearts of flesh (cf. Ezek 36:25–28).

The Spirit of Yahweh descends upon the Virgin at the Annunciation (cf. Lk 1:35). It is the same Spirit who anoints Jesus as Messiah (cf. Lk 4:21). The Messiah is the bearer of the spirit of Yahweh (cf. Mk 1:9–11; Mt 3:13–17; Lk 3:21–22). The Spirit descends at Pentecost upon the Apostles and transforms their hearts (cf. Acts 2:1–21).

It is within this atmosphere of divine nearness that one must read the numerous passages of the New Testament that speak of the presence of the Spirit in the hearts of Christians. Biblical affirmations concerning the presence of God converge upon Trinitarian indwelling as upon their fullest expression. They began with the affirmation of Yahweh's proximity to the world and His friendly conversation with Adam in paradise, continuing to delineate themselves thereafter in an ascending movement—ever more intimate and personal—in Yahweh's interventions in the Covenant. This presence of God in man culminates in His Son, who is the tent—the tabernacle—which God pitched to dwell among men (cf. Jn 1:14). In this ascending movement, the Spirit appears as the generating principle of the interior life: first in Christ, in whom He resides in fullness; next in the Church, to which He is sent as sanctifier; and finally in the hearts of Christians (cf. 2 Cor 13:13).[^6]

Patristic doctrine on indwelling finds its most characteristic formulations when treating of the divinisation of the Christian through the work of the Holy Spirit. St Basil offers eloquent proof of this.[^7] The teaching of the Fathers can be summarised in three fundamental propositions:

a) God properly indwells the soul of the just;  
b) The three divine Persons are present in the soul;  
c) This presence is the work of the Holy Spirit.  

Behold how St Augustine formulates it:

> "The love, therefore, which is from God and is God, is properly the Holy Spirit, through whom the charity of God is poured forth in our hearts, through which the whole Trinity indwells us. Wherefore most rightly the Holy Spirit, although He is God, is also called the Gift of God. And what is this Gift properly to be understood to be, except charity, which leads to God, and without which whatever other gift of God does not lead to God?"[^8]

Although the doctrine of indwelling belongs to the faith of the Church, the Magisterium echoed it explicitly only at a very late date. Among the ancient Creeds, only that of St Epiphanius mentions indwelling, precisely when confessing the divinity of the Holy Spirit: "I believe in the Holy Spirit (...) who spoke in the Apostles and indwells the saints."[^9]

Leo XIII was the first to treat systematically of indwelling, considering it as an effect of grace and the work of the Holy Spirit:

> "[God is present] by grace in the just soul, as in a temple, in an entirely intimate and singular manner. From this follows that bond of charity whereby the soul is most closely united to God—more than a friend can be united to his most beloved friend—and fully enjoys Him. This admirable union, which bears the name of indwelling, differs only in condition from that whereby God is united to the blessed. And although it is accomplished by the whole Trinity—'we will come to him and make our home with him' (Jn 14:23)—nevertheless it is attributed to the Holy Spirit as something peculiar."[^10]

Pius XII, in *Mystici Corporis*, attaches great importance to the presence of the Spirit in the Church—it is here that he calls Him the soul of the Church—and affirms, at the same time, that our adoptive filiation is the work of the Spirit:

> "We are well aware that many veils intervene to prevent the understanding and explanation of this mysterious doctrine—which refers to our union with the divine Redeemer, and in a special way to the indwelling of the Holy Spirit in our souls (...) But let all hold as a general and unshakable rule (...) the following: in this mystical union, they must reject every form that would cause the faithful in any way to pass beyond the order of created things and erroneously encroach upon the divine, such that not a single proper attribute of the eternal God could be attributed to the creature as its own. And furthermore, let them firmly and with all certainty maintain that in these matters everything is common to the Most Holy Trinity, insofar as everything refers to God as supreme efficient cause. (...) The divine Persons are said to dwell inasmuch as, being present in an inscrutable manner in created souls endowed with intellect, they enter into relation with them through knowledge and love."[^11]

Pius XII is referring here to classical theological principles which have already presented themselves to us on numerous occasions. The theological milieu within which he moves, as we shall see further on, is that of St Thomas: the presence of indwelling takes place through acts of knowledge and love. In a more personalist key, John Paul II describes Trinitarian indwelling as a mutual vital openness between the divine Persons and man:

> "The Triune God opens Himself to man, to the human spirit. The hidden breath of the divine Spirit causes the human spirit to open, in its turn, before the salvific and sanctifying openness of God. Through the gift of grace, which comes from the Spirit, man enters into a new life, is introduced into the supernatural reality of the divine life itself, and becomes a dwelling of the Holy Spirit, a living temple of God (cf. Rom 8:9; 1 Cor 2:10ff.). In effect, through the Holy Spirit, the Father and the Son come to man and take up their abode in him (cf. Jn 14:23). In communion of grace with the Trinity, man's vital space is dilated, elevated to a supernatural level by the divine life. Man lives in God and from God: he lives according to the Spirit and desires what is spiritual."[^12]

The theological tenor of this cited text is close to Thomas Aquinas. The gift of grace proceeds from the Spirit; by means of that gift, man is introduced into a new relationship with God. Indwelling is thus the work of the Spirit through grace.

### The Concept of Indwelling

The indwelling of the Trinity entails a new mode of God's presence to man, far more intimate than the presence of immensity. Through the presence of immensity, God is present to him inasmuch as He bestows being upon him; through indwelling, He is present as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—that is, He is present inasmuch as man is placed in a differentiated relationship with each of the Persons.

We say *new mode of presence* because, on the one hand, indwelling presupposes the presence of immensity, and on the other hand, it implies a most close relationship with God no longer merely as author of being, but as origin and end of man's divinisation. Through the presence of immensity God is present to all things by essence, presence, and power—that is, inasmuch as all things receive their being from Him, are present to His knowledge, and are subject to His power.[^13] The presence of indwelling, by contrast, pertains exclusively to the rational creature, and only insofar as the creature participates in the divine nature, that is, insofar as it is divinised.

The Trinitarian missions, as stated in the preceding chapter, entail a new mode of presence of the sent Person in the rational creature, for the mission of a divine Person presupposes that He is already present in the place to which He is sent. Indwelling is solely a new mode of presence. Yet this new mode of presence must be understood in all its force, as something real, not as a metaphor or hyperbole. It is a substantial presence, not merely dynamic. It is not merely the gifts of the Spirit that fill man, but the Holy Spirit Himself who indwells our hearts. The abundant and explicit texts of Sacred Scripture that speak of indwelling admit of no other interpretation. Hence theologians characterise indwelling as a substantial presence.

The presence of indwelling is far superior to all divine gifts and to all charisms of the Holy Spirit, for it consists in the fact that the Holy Spirit Himself is truly handed over and given to us. St Thomas expounds this as follows:

> "To be sent belongs to a divine person inasmuch as He exists in someone in a new way, and to be given belongs to Him inasmuch as He is possessed by someone. Neither of these can occur except by reason of sanctifying grace. There is a common mode whereby God is in all things by essence, presence, and power (...) Above this common mode there is another special mode that belongs to the rational creature, in which God is said to be present as the known in the knower and the beloved in the lover. And since the rational creature, by knowing and loving, attains through its operation unto God Himself, according to this special mode God is not only said to be in the rational creature, but also to dwell in it as in a temple. Therefore, no other effect than sanctifying grace can be the reason why a divine person is present in a new mode in the rational creature: and consequently only through sanctifying grace is a divine person sent and temporally proceeds (...) In the very gift of sanctifying grace the Holy Spirit is possessed and dwells in man, and on this account the Holy Spirit Himself is both given and sent."[^14]

God makes Himself present to man as Triune because His tripersonal intimacy is attained through man's direct knowledge and love. From this perspective, the importance and necessity of the explicit revelation of the Trinitarian mystery is illuminated. Only through this revelation can man explicitly know and love each of the divine Persons.

### Diverse Opinions on the Ground of Indwelling

In studying the concept of indwelling, there have already presented themselves to us—as could not be otherwise—certain reasons upon which theologians ground this new presence of God to man. A good example is that of St Thomas just cited. According to that text, indwelling is the fruit of faith and charity: the Trinity is present in the soul as the known in the knower and the beloved in the lover. Consequently, St Thomas places indwelling among the effects of created grace, since it is grace that infuses the theological virtue of charity into the soul.

We are here confronted with the question of what constitutes the ground (*fundamentum*) of divine indwelling. The question—and the answers—bear a profound analogy to what is said concerning the presence of immensity. That presence, too, is a relation between God and the world and requires a ground to support the reference between two such disparate beings.[^15] That ground linking infinite being and finite being can be none other than the divine action, insofar as it grounds the being of things. Likewise, in the new presence—indwelling—there exists a relation of indistance between the three divine Persons and man. Between the two there exist both the infinite difference and the intimate closeness that mediate between the Creator and the creature.

The problem facing the theologian when seeking the ground upon which to establish this new mode of presence lies in the fact that all God's actions *ad extra* are common to the three divine Persons; therefore, it does not seem possible to find the reason for that new presence in divine efficient action alone. It is a question difficult to answer. Hence the solutions proposed are numerous, none of them universally convincing. Here are the principal ones:

a) *The ground of this presence is found in acts of knowledge and love.* This is the explanation we have already seen put forward by St Thomas:

> "There is a special mode [of God's presence] that belongs to the rational creature, in which God is said to be present as the known in the knower and the beloved in the lover. And since the rational creature, by knowing and loving, attains through its operation unto God Himself, according to this special mode God is not only said to be in the rational creature, but also to dwell in it as in His temple."[^16]

This position has numerous advantages: by not relying on a differentiated divine efficient action upon the soul, it preserves the axiom that works *ad extra* are common to the three Persons, whilst throwing into relief the power of the theological virtues. These virtues are called *theological* because they attain God in Himself. St Thomas's expressions convey with sufficient force the radicality with which the theological character of faith, hope, and charity must be taken. St Thomas employed similar expressions when referring to the divine processions by way of intellect and will: through those eternal acts, the Father is present to Himself as the known in the knower and the beloved in the lover.[^17]

The great majority of theologians can be said to position themselves within this broad stream, seeking to complement the affirmation that God is present to the soul as known and loved in order to avoid this new mode of presence being attributed directly to the soul alone. Thus, for example, Suárez pays special attention to the friendship that arises between the soul and God through the virtue of charity—a friendship which "demands" the most perfect presence possible.[^18] According to this view, charity is, above all, an exigency that God make Himself present.

John of St Thomas looks primarily to the experience of God, describing this presence as the fact that God makes Himself present through grace as an object that can be experientially known and enjoyed. And precisely because the ground of that joyful presence is placed primarily in the acts of the soul, it is insisted that the presence of indwelling presupposes that of immensity, and that the soul performs these acts as the fruit of the elevation received through grace.[^19] A similar position is found in A. Gardeil, R. Garrigou-Lagrange, M. Cuervo, H. Bouëssé, and J.-H. Nicolas. According to them, the ground of indwelling is found in the supernatural relationship that the theological virtues establish between man and God.

b) *The ground of indwelling is found in the divine action in infusing grace into the soul.* This is the opinion of G. Vázquez,[^20] the Salmanticenses,[^21] and P. Galtier.[^22] Galtier employs the image of a seal, which must be applied for its impression to remain stamped upon things; when the seal is applied to a fluid substance, it must be held constantly upon it. Grace, according to this comparison, would be like a seal that likens man to the Trinity, requiring the presence of the three Persons as distinct, continuously sealing the soul. The image is felicitous. Yet the difficulty remains: how is it possible that efficient causality *ad extra*, which is common to the three Persons, can be the reason for such a differentiated presence?

c) *The presence of indwelling takes place by means of a quasi-formal causality*—that is, a causality resembling that of form upon the composite which it informs. This is the opinion of M. de la Taille, K. Rahner, F. Bourassa, and Ch. Baumgartner, among others.[^23] According to this view, the three Persons, in communicating divine life to the soul, do so in a distinct manner, corresponding to their personal mode of being. From this there would arise a special relation of the soul to each of the divine Persons. The problem with this opinion lies in the concept of quasi-formal causality. God cannot enter into composition with any created being. Therefore, either quasi-formal causality is close to efficient causality, in which case it encounters the same difficulties as Galtier's position; or it is close to formal causality, in which case it faces the same problems as applying formal causality to an infinitely simple and transcendent God.

The variety of opinions and the nuances introduced even within each school bear witness to the difficulty of establishing speculatively the ground upon which this most intimate relationship between man and God rests. It is doubly mysterious. Viewed from the standpoint of God, indwelling belongs to the mystery of God's self-communication of His own personal being through the missions; viewed from the standpoint of man, Trinitarian indwelling belongs to the mystery of the rational creature's elevation to the point of being introduced into the intimate life of God.

In any event, it is clear that the indwelling of the Trinity establishes a reference between the three Persons and man—a relation of presence in which the Covenant established by God with His people culminates. It is, therefore, a relationship clothed in the mysteriousness proper to all that is personal, and in the mysteriousness implied by the most intimate relationship possible between the rational creature and God. It is scarcely surprising that this relationship between man and God should transcend all comprehension and be capable of filling, without satiety or weariness, an entire eternity.

St John of the Cross expressed this beautifully in a dense text in which Trinitarian indwelling is described as participation in the Trinitarian relations:

> "Nor is it to be held as impossible that the soul should be capable of so lofty a thing—that the soul should breathe in God as God breathes in her by way of participation; for, given that God grants her the favour of uniting her in the Most Holy Trinity, wherein the soul becomes deiform and God by participation, what incredible thing is it that she too should perform her work of understanding, knowledge, and love, or, to put it better, have it performed in the Trinity together with it as the Trinity itself does, but in a communicated and participated manner, God effecting it in the soul itself? For this is to be transformed into the three Persons in power, wisdom, and love; and in this the soul is like unto God, and to the end that she might attain unto this He created her in His image and likeness."[^24]

For St John of the Cross, indwelling consists in man, grafted into Christ (cf. Jn 15:1–8), being introduced into the bosom of God to live the life of God. To say that God dwells in man is the same as saying that God causes man to dwell in Himself:

> "He said that in him who loved Him, the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit would come and make their abode in him (cf. Jn 14:23); which was to be by causing him to live and dwell in the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit in the life of God."[^25]

### Trinitarian Indwelling, Grace of Christ, and Divine Filiation

There exists an indissoluble link between sanctifying grace, the invisible mission of the Holy Spirit, the theological virtues of faith and charity, our adoptive filiation, and Trinitarian indwelling. Indwelling is one facet of the deification or sanctification of man, which is a reality at once mysterious and exceedingly rich.

The opinion of theologians is likewise not unanimous in determining the order in which these elements ought to be considered. In any case, it is essential not to forget that the diverse facets contemplated by theological reflection in the deification of man are inseparable. The deification that makes man a child of God has no sufficient explanation except through uncreated grace—that is, through God's self-communication to the soul by the gift of the Spirit, who elevates it to the point of making it capable of knowing and loving the three Persons of the Trinity. This elevation is such that man directly attains the divine Persons with his acts of knowledge and love, permitting him to have a relationship with God so robust that one may say without exaggeration that the Persons are present in the soul as the known in the knower and the beloved in the lover.

At the same time, to be made children of God in the Son, with all the radicality found in the New Testament, entails the indwelling of the Trinity in the soul. In effect, we are made sons in the Son upon being grafted into Him (cf. Jn 15:1–8) by the Holy Spirit. But being grafted into Christ entails a filial relationship to the Father, given our union with Christ and His essential and absolute filial relation to the Father.

Many theologians conceive indwelling as an effect of sanctifying grace. This is so especially for those who, like the followers of St Thomas, judge that acts of faith, hope, and charity constitute the ground of indwelling. Now, these virtues are infused into the soul together with grace. Hence these authors conceive indwelling as an effect of grace.

In contemporary theology, however, there is a progressive shift of emphasis, considering uncreated grace—God who gives Himself to man and indwells him—as the ground of the divinising effects in the creature. From this perspective, the coming of the three Persons "precedes" what is customarily termed created grace.

Indeed, if one inquires into the relationship between the Holy Spirit and grace, most theologians incline to think that grace is the effect of the self-bestowal of the Holy Spirit upon the soul. The whole patristic tradition, above all the Greek Fathers, conceives the divinisation of man as the work of the Spirit. Created grace would thus be the gift of uncreated grace. There are passages of Scripture that present charity as the fruit of the action of the Spirit already present in the soul. Here are some examples: *The fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience...* (Gal 5:22); and *hope does not disappoint us, because God's love has been poured into our hearts through the Holy Spirit who has been given to us* (Rom 5:5).

Man is constituted a new creation in Christ through grace.[^26] This new creation is nothing other than the elevation of man to the point of making him a partaker of the Trinitarian life. This participation is grounded in our filiation in Christ. Hence, in the logical order of our concepts, divine filiation is the reason for Trinitarian indwelling. Stated differently: our relation to the Persons of the Father and the Holy Spirit takes place through our filiation in Christ. Just as God reveals Himself definitively to us in Christ, so too He indwells us—causes us to dwell in Him—through the filial relationship of Christ.

> "Grace, and therefore divine filiation, is a new and deeper participation in the divinity, not only as Being, but as Life. But the divine life is the Trinitarian life; wherefore divine filiation is the mode in which, through grace, man participates supernaturally in that divine life. Man enters thus, in a mysterious and ineffable manner, into the very dynamic of the intra-Trinitarian life, without ceasing to be man, and—a luminous mystery which human eyes cannot see clearly on account of its excessive radiance—without God changing, without His ceasing on that account to be the Absolutely Transcendent."[^27]

Divine transcendence leads once again to the consideration that filiation is an operation *ad extra*. Like grace, divine filiation, considered in its origin, is a relation to the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit inasmuch as They are one only God and one single principle of our sanctification. Yet this single principle is a Trinitarian action, in which each Person is present as distinct from the others. The work of sanctification can be called Trinitarian in all the rigour of the expression.[^28]

This affirmation serves as a counterweight to those views which, following Thomas Aquinas, place the theological virtues as the reason for indwelling. In this sense, an important study notes the following:

> "Undoubtedly, St Thomas frequently speaks of the presence of the divine Persons in the just man inasmuch as he knows and loves them; but there is no lack of texts in which that intentional presence (as the known in the knower and the beloved in the lover) appears to be grounded upon a prior, foundational presence of the divine Persons."[^29]

In the mysterious event of man's deification, supernatural elevation through grace, divine filiation, and the presence of God in the soul are inseparable realities that mutually support and illuminate one another.

### Trinitarian Indwelling and the Spiritual Life

Theology is fond of considering the entire work of creation and redemption—that is, all God's action *ad extra*—as a movement that proceeds from the Trinity and makes its way towards the Trinity. The material world is directed towards the rational creature, and the rational creature is endowed with its marvellous natural qualities—its capacity for knowledge and love, its self-possession in freedom—so that it may realise them in the conscious and free participation in intra-Trinitarian love, becoming itself a gift of self.

It is no exaggeration to state that "the whole Christian life is built upon a fundamental fact: God has given Himself to us and invites us to respond to His gift."[^30] This loving exchange with the Trinity takes place already on this earth, for through baptism we are made a new creation in Christ. It is a new life which bears within itself the tendency to grow and develop until reaching its fullness in the life of heaven. The new life in Christ is not something merely future, but something already present and operative in the Christian. The Spirit dwells in the soul in grace, pouring forth His gifts upon it, above all charity, which impels us to spend our lives for love in the daily tasks of each day under the motion of the Spirit; the interior life of the Christian is gradually built upon the reality of his divine filiation in Christ.

The liturgy is the model upon which to base personal piety. Now, the object of the liturgy is the glorification of the Trinity. This was likewise the centre of the life and death of Jesus: to give glory to the Father through His obedience and self-surrender. Immersed in the activity of Christ, the liturgy possesses an essentially Trinitarian structure. The piety of every Christian must likewise possess a Trinitarian structure.

In the liturgy, baptism is administered in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit; prayers are directed to the Father, through the Son, in the Holy Spirit; the psalms conclude with the Trinitarian doxology, which, in turn, is universally present in the prayer of the Church. All celebrations of the sacraments and sacramentals explicitly manifest that salvation comes from the Trinity and culminates in union with the Trinity.

From the very first steps of the Church's life, the most essential dimension of Christian piety was marked by this Trinitarian stamp. This was "naturally demanded" by the theological nature of the Christian life, which flows from baptism, and by the liturgy, which is the paradigm of Christian piety. It is likewise the paradigm for all moral acting of the Christian.

The Christian life demands, therefore, docility to the Paraclete, who is sent into our hearts to fill them with charity; it demands anchoring one's own existence upon the rock of divine filiation, imbuing all our thoughts and deeds with the filial spirit of those who can address God calling Him *Abba*—the very prayer of Jesus—through the Spirit who has been given to us.

Trinitarian truth belongs also to the most intimate structure of all theological endeavour—the theologian directs his gaze towards the God who has revealed Himself in Christ—including its moral dimension. It may justly be said that if moral theology wishes to be authentic theology, it must reflect upon God, the world, and man from a very precise vantage point: the mutual personal self-giving existing between God and man, which reaches its zenith in mutual indwelling. Christian morality is the morality of the children of God.[^31]

St Josemaría Escrivá bears powerful witness to the centrality of the Trinitarian mystery in the journey towards God, in a passage of singular theological and ascetical relevance:

> "We had begun with vocal prayers, simple, delightful, which we learnt in our childhood and which we should never want to abandon. That prayer, which began with such childlike simplicity, now flows along a broad, gentle, and secure riverbed, for it follows the pace of friendship with Him who declared: *I am the way* (Jn 14:6). If we love Christ in this way, if with divine daring we take refuge in the opening that the lance left in His Side, the Master's promise will be fulfilled: *If anyone loves me, he will keep my word, and my Father will love him, and we will come to him and make our home with him* (Jn 14:23).
> 
> "Then the heart feels the need to distinguish and adore each of the divine Persons. In some way, it is a discovery made by the soul in the supernatural life, like that of a little child opening its eyes to existence. And it holds loving converse with the Father, and with the Son, and with the Holy Spirit; and it readily submits to the work of the life-giving Paraclete, who is bestowed upon us unmerited: the supernatural gifts and virtues!"[^32]

Union with Christ, living consciously the reality of our divine filiation, docility to the Holy Spirit, intimate converse with the Triune God who dwells in the heart of the Christian—these are essential traits of Christian spirituality lucidly highlighted, not without a special grace of God, by St Josemaría.

The liturgy calls the Holy Spirit the "sweet guest of the soul" (*dulcis hospes animae*).[^33] To Him we owe everything in our interior life, including Trinitarian indwelling. He infuses grace into us. He unites us with Christ. He works our sanctification. The Holy Spirit builds up the Church. Hence the vigour with which spiritual authors insist upon docility to the Holy Spirit. That docility is one of our primordial duties towards this sweet guest of the soul.[^34]

Authors of spiritual theology customarily conceive Trinitarian indwelling as the foundation of mystical experience. According to this, the mystical life would imply an experience—in the darkness of faith—of the Trinity indwelling our hearts. That experience is, in turn, an "anticipation" of heaven, as St John of the Cross writes when commenting upon the "touches" of God in the soul:

> "...And thus it can only be said, and with truth, that it tastes of eternal life; for although in this life it is not enjoyed in perfection as in glory, nevertheless this touch, being a touch of God, tastes of eternal life. And thus the soul here tastes of all the things of God, there being communicated to her strength, wisdom, and love, beauty, grace, and goodness, etc.; for since God is all these things, the soul tastes them in a single touch of God, and thus the soul enjoys according to her faculties and her substance. And from this good of the soul there sometimes overflows into the body the anointing of the Holy Spirit..."[^35]
