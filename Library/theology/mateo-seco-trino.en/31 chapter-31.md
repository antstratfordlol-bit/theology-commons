## Chapter 31: The Will of God {#mateo-seco-trino.31}

The life of God is the life proper to a personal being: the life of a being who possesses Himself in the acts of knowledge and love. In the previous chapter, God was contemplated in His most simple and eternal act of knowledge. We now enter upon the study of the will and the love of God with a consideration symmetrical to that made in the treatment of divine knowledge: in God, will and act are identical; God not only has love, but is a most simple and eternal act of infinite love, to such an extent that in Sacred Scripture He is defined as love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8).

From its very first pages, the Bible brings into relief the absolute sovereignty of God, His power, His love, and His mercy. The God of the Covenant is a God who loves the good and hates evil. A God who acts with sovereign power. He spoke a word and things were made (cf. Gen 1:3–31). The New Testament, which likewise refers to the creative will of God (cf., e.g., 1 Cor 12:18; 15:38; Rev 4:11), presents the divine will above all as redemptive and salvific. God desires all men to be saved (1 Tim 2:4).

The whole history of salvation rests upon this infinitely generous will of God, who before the foundation of the world chose us in the Son that we should be holy and blameless before Him (cf. Eph 1:4). It is a will that not only marks the master lines of history, but descends to the concrete actions of men. Doing the will of God is the fundamental requirement that Christ formulates for His disciples (cf. Mt 12:50), to such an extent that only he who does that will shall enter the kingdom of heaven (cf. Mt 7:21). And in the supreme hour of the Garden, Jesus prays, submitting Himself totally to the will of the Father: *Not my will, but yours, be done* (cf. Lk 22:42).

### Will and Intellect in God

The testimony of Sacred Scripture regarding the existence of will in God is overwhelming. This truth is indissolubly linked to the guiding thread of the whole of Sacred Scripture: God is the God who takes the initiative of the Covenant. The existence of will in God is also a truth readily accessible to the discourse of reason. To this end, it suffices to follow the analogy that exists between God and man, who is made in His image and likeness.

This is the path followed by St Thomas Aquinas when he argues:

> "There is will in God by the same reason that there is intellect (...) In whoever possesses intellect there is will, just as in whoever possesses senses there is animal appetite. Therefore, since there is intellect in God, there is will. And just as His understanding is His being, so too is His willing."[^1]

An eternal act of love necessarily follows upon the eternal act of divine understanding. Put another way: given the infinite divine simplicity, this act of love is nothing other than the very act of self-understanding which, knowing itself as the supreme good, loves itself. In this sense, according to our mode of speaking, the act of the will follows upon the act of the intellect, and is not confounded with it.

Clearly, when we speak of the existence of will in God, we are not referring to a volitional faculty that is brought to perfection by its act. That is the structure of the will in men. But in God there is no potentiality whatsoever. What has already been said with respect to divine knowledge must be repeated here: God is pure and most simple act. Consequently, He is a pure and most simple act of will, that is, an act of love, which is the proper act of the will. This act of willing cannot be, as in us, the activity of a faculty or power, since it is impossible for the slightest potentiality to exist in God. Nor is it a structure of acts, such that one act of love follows another. God is a most simple and subsistent act of love. It is an act of love that simultaneously embraces all that is lovable. Still more, the selfsame and unique act of love is exercised simultaneously by the three divine Persons according to their proper mode of personal being. The act of loving is identified so fully with the being of God that this act *is* the being of God.[^2]

According to the ordinary course of our rational discourse, the existence of will in God is also deduced from the consideration of other divine attributes. Such is the case, for example, with divine omni-perfection. God would not be the most perfect being if all perfections were not in Him, including the will. Nor could He be provident or merciful if He had no will; nor could He be infinitely happy if an infinite love did not exist in Him, for happiness is the other side of love. All these arguments reaffirm from the rational point of view what is clearly a truth pertaining to faith.[^3]

Although the fact that God possesses a will is readily accessible to human discourse, the firm and perennial affirmation of this profoundly personal character of God, as a being endowed with intellect and freedom, is an unmistakable feature of revealed teaching concerning God: God freely creates the world and freely establishes His covenant with man. By contrast, for Plato, for example, the goodness of God consists in a metaphysical goodness, necessarily diffusive of itself. The world, therefore, does not proceed from a benevolent and free act of God, but from a mere metaphysical necessity of the Good. Plotinus defends something similar: God does not create things freely; rather, because He is the Good, He diffuses Himself necessarily by the necessity of His nature.[^4] One senses here how the concept of creation is decisive for the whole of Christian teaching on God.

It is highly significant that all pantheists and all determinists find themselves forced to deny that God has a free will. It may even be said that here we see how freedom in history and in human action depends on the freedom with which God, in the beginning, created the world. The history of thought is eloquent on this point: to deny the freedom of God leads to denying freedom in history.

### God Is Love

In speaking of the will of God, a twofold identification has been made: the divine will has been identified with its act, and that act has been identified with love, which is the proper and primary act of the will. This follows from the absolute divine simplicity. Moreover, this is the only way to take in all its fullness the Johannine affirmation that *God is love* (cf. 1 Jn 4:8).

Here is how St Thomas Aquinas reasons:

> "The act of the will tends to good and evil as its proper objects; good is the principal and per se object, evil is the secondary and indirect object, that is, insofar as it is opposed to the good (...) In whoever, then, there is will, there must necessarily be love."[^5]

According to our manner of speaking, the first movement of the will is love. Without this movement, no other movement of the will can occur: hatred is the rejection of what opposes the beloved good, desire is longing for the beloved good, joy is rest in the beloved good, and so forth. All the volitional activity of the intelligent being rests, then, upon love as its primary act.

Divine simplicity has led us, furthermore, to identify the divine will with its primary and proper act. It also leads us to consider attentively the nexus that exists between love and the beloved object. There is nothing caused in God. God cannot even be the cause of Himself. Now, according to our mode of knowing, the object upon which love falls acts as the final cause of love. This cannot be so in God, who is full and eternal act: in God, love must be conceived as a most simple act in which lover, love, and beloved are identified to such an extent that the beloved object cannot be understood—so to speak—with such otherness with respect to the lover that it could be conceived as the final cause of His love.[^6] God is not the final cause of Himself either.

The love of God is as infinite as His goodness and His intellect. It is, therefore, a single act of love, which is universal. God embraces with His love everything that is good. God loves, then, all that exists, for everything that exists is good. There is nothing upon which some ray of the love of God does not fall.

Within the universality of this love, it is necessary to emphasize that the love of God falls primarily and necessarily upon Himself. God loves Himself with an infinite and eternal love. And He rejoices infinitely in that love. The infinite Good, known perfectly in all its infinite depth, is loved infinitely. It is a single act of love which is, at the same time, Trinitarian. Theologians, as has been seen, usually designate this love as essential love (*amor essentialis*)—which is identified with the divine essence—and, at the same time, distinguish it from Love-Person (*Amor-Persona*), who is the Holy Spirit.

Richard of Saint Victor writes:

> "Beyond all doubt, in the supreme simplicity, being and loving are the same thing. Thus, in each of the three, His person and His love are the same. That there are several persons in a single divinity means precisely that there are several who possess one and the same love, the supreme love, or rather, that they are several in having one and the same love, by virtue of their different characteristic. In God, then, this person is nothing other than this supreme love differentiated by its property; the second Person is nothing other than the supreme love distinguished by a different property; the third Person is nothing other than the supreme love distinguished by a third property."[^7]

God loves Himself with an infinite and necessary love. Many reasons make this cardinal affirmation intelligible. God is the infinite Good and knows Himself with infinite perfection. And since the primary object of the will is the Good, it follows that He loves Himself in an infinite, necessary, and eternal act of love. The Good cannot fail to love the Good infinitely.[^8]

From the intrinsic necessity with which God Himself is the primary object of His love, it follows that God cannot create the world except for His glory. There is an evident reason for this, to which allusion has already been made: God cannot have any end outside Himself. If He had one, He would not be the uncaused First Cause: His action would be caused by an end external to Himself.

On the other hand, only in God, who is the infinite Good, does the rational creature find its end, made as it is for the knowledge of the ineffable and for the love of an infinite that is intimately personal. The infinite Good cannot fail to draw limited good to Himself. The contrary is simply unthinkable. How could the infinite Good not be the centre that attracts the love of limited good, of an intelligent and free person made in His image and likeness? When it is said that God cannot have an end outside Himself, one is only noting the ontological centrality that the supreme Good occupies with respect to every other good. Both the divine nature and the good of the creature demand this centrality with metaphysical necessity.

Can this affirmation be interpreted as the existence of an absolute and transcendental egoism in God? Authors such as Kant, Hermes, and Günther have hastily interpreted it in this way. According to them, the Christian doctrine of the creation of the world for the glory of God and, more concretely, the affirmation that the ultimate end of man can be none other than the glory of God, would simply be the assertion that a transcendental egoism exists in God.

This assertion is possible only if many aspects of Christian doctrine concerning God have been misunderstood. In the first place, God's infinite transcendence over all things, His infinite goodness, which renders Him objectively worthy of infinite and supreme love. This transcendence makes it impossible, moreover, for God to enter into collision with the creature. Quite the contrary: the creature springs from Him, sinks its roots in Him, and finds its perfection precisely in its union with Him.

In considering this question, it must be kept in mind, furthermore, that divine infinite perfection entails that God needs nothing. God does not create the world out of an internal need or to obtain something from the world, but out of an ineffable and absolute gratuity. The infinitely wise and powerful One is not an indigent being, but an infinitely happy being. And precisely for this reason, when it is affirmed that God creates the world for His glory, this affirmation cannot be interpreted as though God had created the world thinking of His own enrichment or to derive some benefit from it—that is, with a "transcendental divine egoism"—but as an act of infinite gratuity and generosity. God creates the world and, in particular, man out of love, not to increase His glory, but to communicate it.[^9]

This same question can be considered from the perspective of the world. To be created for the glory of God is, so to speak, the greatest glory of the creature. That which is the image of infinite love can find its rest only in union with infinite love.[^10]

The love with which God loves Himself not only contains nothing egoistical, but this love forms part of God's holiness. Egoism consists precisely in preferring oneself to the Good. God, who is the supreme Good, in loving Himself does nothing other than love the supreme Good infinitely. It is a most pure love that renders it impossible for God to will sin. It was stated earlier that hatred is a secondary movement of the will: the movement by which the lover rejects everything that harms the good of the beloved. God's infinite holiness—which is identical with His eternal act of love—implies the rejection of sin, the hatred of evil.

Love and joy are in God formally, that is, as they are in themselves. They are in God according to their highest perfection, without the limitations and modes proper to their existence in created beings. They are in God formally because love and joy answer to the primary and proper act of the will, which is directed towards the good. By contrast, acts directed towards evil—desire, sorrow, anger, hatred—can be found in God only in a metaphorical sense, for such affections involve in themselves an element of imperfection: they presuppose either the absence of a good or the presence of an evil. And in God no evil exists. It is said that God hates evil, not because hatred exists formally in God, but because God rejects evil with the firmness proper to love for the Good.[^11] From this point of view as well, God's love for the Good, which is Himself, grounds His infinite liberality towards creatures and His rejection of evil. God will never make a pact with evil.[^12]

### God's Love for Creatures

The affirmations of Sacred Scripture concerning God's love for all creatures, and especially for man, are constant and of absolute radicality. They speak of eternal love and tenderness, of a love so great that it leads Him to give His Son to the world, so that the world might not perish, but have life through Him (cf. Jn 3:16). The fact that God loves Himself does not mean at all that God cannot will that which is outside Himself, especially man; rather, that love—and the joy it carries with it—makes possible the ineffable divine liberality towards all creatures and, in particular, towards man.

God loves man with a love of benevolence, that is, with a love of friendship (*amor amicitiae*). In fact, the creation of man has no other meaning than this: to make possible a dialogue of friendship with God. God created us precisely to communicate His fatherhood to us by making us sons in the Son through the Spirit (cf. Eph 1:5–6).

This love of God for creatures—especially His love for man—raises two serious difficulties for human thought:
a) Does loving another not mean being attracted by the beloved? Does it not mean being in dependence upon him?
b) Does loving another not amount to being in a situation where one can be wounded by the rejection of the other? Does loving another, when that other is a vulnerable being, not entail being able to be wounded by the suffering of the beloved?

Are not all these things unworthy of the most perfect and sovereign being?

The first difficulty brings into relief the need always to underline the supreme gratuity and liberality of God's love; the second leads us directly to what in contemporary theology is known as the suffering of God. Both difficulties must be resolved clearly if God's love is not to be misunderstood.

#### a) Uncaused Love

The first difficulty arises when considering that God is the uncaused first cause and that love seems always to involve an element of passivity. It is the goodness and beauty of the beloved that cause love in the lover. This is so in the love that arises in the created being: the purest love a man can have, even the most sublime act of love in glory, will always be a caused love, just as his nature is caused. The goodness of the beloved causes in us the attraction of love. It is the goodness of God, contemplated unveiled in glory, that causes supreme love and joy in us. But in God there can be nothing caused. This difficulty led the deists to deny that God could love anything other than Himself. Consequently, according to this view, God would love nothing outside Himself, not even man; He could only love Himself, since love for something extra-divine would place Him in an unacceptable dependence upon the created being.

The texts of Sacred Scripture that speak of God's love for man, and indeed of a love of friendship, are exceedingly clear and numerous. The difficulty is resolved by observing that God's love for His creatures is an eternal act of love which, like His act of understanding, involves no passivity. It is not the goodness of things that causes love in God; rather, it is God's love that causes the goodness of things. The love of God is essentially active and creative: God's love for things is not an effect produced in Him by the goodness of things, but the cause of that goodness. It is a love that creates and infuses goodness into creatures.[^13] The divine will is not, then, affected by created goods, but produces them. It is, therefore, an infinitely gratuitous love.[^14] This gratuity is displayed with particular clarity in the gratuity of vocation. Every vocation, even that of Christ, is absolutely gratuitous. The Incarnation could not be merited by Christ.[^15]

And yet, this gratuity of divine love does not mean arbitrariness, but supreme liberality. The love of God is governed by His wisdom and by His goodness.[^16] It is the love of God—with the infinite wisdom of love—that communicates itself to creatures, that gives itself to man from creation to the supreme humiliation of the Cross. That *kenosis* is, simultaneously, absolute gratuity and supreme wisdom. It is not an unreasonable act. Theology here once again employs the ancient adage that good is diffusive of itself (*bonum est diffusivum sui*), stripping this axiom of the undertones of necessity with which it was employed by Plato or Plotinus. It is entirely coherent with the infinite goodness of God that He should not only diffuse His goodness, but communicate Himself to the creature to the point of becoming man and introducing man into His intimate Trinitarian life.

#### b) God's Love as the Cause of All Beings

God is the cause of all that exists: its exemplary cause, its efficient cause, and its final cause. As has already been seen, creation does not proceed from a necessity of the divine nature, but from the free determination of His will. It is this free determination that confers upon things being and all their perfections, which is what renders them lovable. The Council of Sens (1140/1141) rejected Abelard's proposition according to which God would always act constrained by the necessity of His own nature.[^17] The Church teaches that the world is not the product of necessity, of blind fate, or of chance, but proceeds from the free will of God, who willed to make creatures share in His being, His wisdom, and His goodness.[^18]

Just as, citing St Augustine, it was stated in the previous chapter that God does not know things because they exist, but rather they exist because He knew them before they existed, so too must it now be said that God does not love things because of the perfections they possess, but rather they possess those perfections because God loved them beforehand. And just as divine knowledge is most simple and eternal, embracing all being in a single act, so the love of God is a single, most simple, and eternal act by which God embraces all that exists. From this perspective, one cannot speak of diverse degrees of love in God. The act of love, in God, is a single act, not a concatenation of acts.

When one speaks of degrees in the love God has for creatures, one is speaking not from the standpoint of the intimate act of love in God, but from the standpoint of the effects of that love in creatures:

> "God loves everything He has made; but He loves rational creatures more than others, and among rational creatures He prefers those who are members of His only-begotten Son, and above all He loves His only-begotten Son."[^19]

This is the mystery of divine election, which St Paul expresses in words as strong as these: "For those whom he foreknew he also predestined to be conformed to the image of his Son, in order that he might be the firstborn among many brothers. And those whom he predestined he also called, and those whom he called he also justified, and those whom he justified he also glorified" (Rom 8:29–30). God loves all creatures, each with a most singular love that bestows diverse goods upon it. And in this sense, He loves each one in a distinct and unique manner.

#### c) Freedom of Divine Love

The Church has steadfastly rejected any attempt to conceive creation—and consequently God's love for creatures—as something necessary in God. With respect to everything extra-divine, God remains sovereignly free. We have just cited the rejection of Abelard's thesis that God acts necessarily. This rejection was followed by that of the theses of Eckhart,[^20] Wyclif,[^21] and the Güntherians,[^22] which denied the freedom of God in creating. To these are added the affirmations of the Council of Florence[^23] and the First Vatican Council[^24] concerning divine freedom with respect to creation. These affirmations gather the constant teaching of Sacred Scripture regarding the absolute sovereignty of the God of the Covenant: He always holds the initiative in the work of creation and in that of salvation (cf. Rom 9:18; Eph 1:5; 1 Cor 2:11). God is free, therefore, both to create or not to create, and to create this world or another, or to choose one path or another for the salvation of man.

Divine freedom with respect to creation is a consequence of His rich intra-divine life: God stands in need of nothing outside Himself; He "diffuses" Himself eternally in His intimacy in the Trinitarian dialogue of knowledge and love, with infinite happiness. Creation is not, then, necessary; it is the fruit of free divine choice. Here is how St Thomas Aquinas reasons:

> "God wills His goodness necessarily, in the same way as the human will necessarily wills happiness (...); things other than God He wills insofar as they are ordered to His goodness as to an end (...). Consequently, since the goodness of God is perfect and can exist without other beings, which can add no perfection to it, it follows that, in an absolute sense, it is not necessary that He should will things other than Himself. Nevertheless, it is hypothetically necessary: on the hypothesis that He wills them, He cannot not will them, because His will cannot change."[^25]

This argument is the customary one for demonstrating divine freedom in His love for created things. God is perfect in Himself. This perfection implies that God is in need of nothing outside Himself and, therefore, is free in everything concerning the extra-divine world. Stated otherwise, the extra-divine world can impose no necessity upon God. The end of the cited text draws attention to the need to purify the concept of freedom when applying it to God: since God has freely decided to create, that decision in God is eternal and immutable, and in this sense it is necessary. That is, it is free, but it is not contingent.

Indeed, the categories of necessity and freedom can only be applied to God analogously and purified of all the imperfections with which we know them when observing them in created things. In us, by definition, freedom presupposes change, for it proceeds from a structure of acts: we know, we evaluate, we decide; in us, free action is necessarily contingent. In God, as has been repeatedly said, there is neither a structure of acts—He is pure act—nor contingency. For this reason, the creative act cannot be conceived in God as something contingent and temporal, but as something eternal. Divine freedom consists in the fact that to the act of God's willing—in itself necessary, eternal, and immutable—there corresponds in time a term—the creature—which could not exist. Hence one may say in all truth that God loves us eternally and yet that we are contingent, even though the love God has for us is not contingent. God makes His free choice in all His eternity in the single act of love that constitutes His essence.[^26]

Given the infinite divine simplicity, the act by which God freely creates creatures cannot be conceived as an action superadded to the act by which He loves Himself with necessary love. In God there is a single act of love that takes necessary complacency in the divine goodness, and that maintains its sovereign indifference (*indifferentia dominatrix*) with respect to every created good, which He calls or does not call into existence according to His free will.[^27] Even though the created being is contingent, the love with which God wills us is never contingent. For this reason, it may be said that the free act of God is nothing other than the necessary act of love of the divine goodness, insofar as it denotes a non-necessary relation to creatures. It is contingent, then, only extrinsically, by reason of the fact that what was willed is a contingent thing.[^28]

#### d) Freedom in Creating This World

The affirmation that God created the world out of His goodness has led to the question of whether, given the goodness of God, this world is the best of all possible worlds. This is the current of thought usually designated in philosophy as optimism, and which we are interested in considering here only insofar as it is necessary to complete what has been said regarding divine freedom with respect to creation. The question may be formulated thus: given that God is infinitely good, was He inwardly urged, at the time of creation, to choose the best of all possible worlds? It can also be framed in reverse: since God must do the best, this world, which is the one He created, is the best of all possible worlds. Hence this position is called optimism.

Plato is usually cited as one of the most eminent representatives of this current.[^29] His reasoning is simple: The world proceeds from the goodness of God: "He was good, and the good can never have any jealousy of anything. And being free from jealousy, he desired that all things should be as like himself as they could be."[^30] Thus the world would have all possible perfection. If it has no greater perfection, it is because that is impossible, given the unavoidable limitation of finite beings: "The world is the best possible world, for it is an image; if there were no evil in it, it would be identified with its model."[^31]

Plato does not pose with full precision the question concerning God's freedom in creating this world, among other reasons because he lacks the concept of creation. However, his notion of the Good as diffusive of itself leads him to inaugurate the view that this world is the best of all possible worlds, since the Good must necessarily have sought its best diffusion.[^32]

The Stoics also fall into this optimism for somewhat different reasons. According to the Stoics, God is not distinguished from the world. In this pantheism, in which nature is identified with God, the world appears as a manifestation of the divinity and, therefore, as a being perfect in itself and unitary. Evil, then, would be no more than an inevitable accident that befalls particular beings in view of the global development of the universe. For Plotinus too, this world is the best of all possible worlds, since it owes all its existence to the Good by way of emanation.[^33]

However, the question of whether this world is the best of all possible worlds is formulated with exactness in Christian environments in which the concept of creation *ex nihilo* is explicitly present. This has already been seen in Abelard: by the proper necessity of His nature, which is the Good, God could not have acted otherwise when creating the world. Abelard is not affirming that God is in dependence upon something outside Himself; the divine necessity of which Abelard speaks refers to His intimate being: to the requirements of His own nature. By requirement of this nature of His, God would not be free in choosing which world to create.

The great representatives of optimism in modern philosophy are Malebranche (†1715) and Leibniz (†1716). Here is Malebranche's reasoning: "The more perfect a work is, the better it expresses the perfections of its craftsman, and honours him all the more as its perfections better express the perfections he possesses. Thus God wills to make His work as perfect as possible."[^34] According to Malebranche, God would not be free in choosing the creaturely order to create, seeing Himself constrained to choose the best order for creation. This seems to be deduced from expressions such as these: God "is almighty in the sense that He does whatever He wills and that nothing can oppose Him; but He is not almighty in the sense that He could have acted by ways that were not the wisest."[^35] The problems raised by this optimism are remarkable. Here are two objections that can be raised against it: Is it certain that God could not have created a better world than this one, a world in which there was not so much suffering? If God necessarily chooses the wisest ways, is it certain that the Incarnation of the Word was gratuitous?

Leibniz draws attention anew to the fact that it is necessary to consider the world as a whole when speaking of its goodness and, above all, when "justifying" God for having permitted evil. One cannot reproach God for the existence of evil in the world, for He has chosen the best order among those that were possible. According to Leibniz, being infinitely good, God was urged to this choice by the moral necessity that springs from His own goodness. Thus, although He has permitted evil, this permission is entirely justified. Leibniz's justification, however, imperils the affirmation of the existence of freedom in God with respect to the created world. Leibniz argues that God's inability to choose a different world to create is similar to the situation of the perfect mathematician who cannot calculate except correctly, or that of the perfect sage who wills nothing other than the best. It is commonly objected against Leibniz that God's goodness requires that whatever He creates be good; it does not follow from this that He must create the best.[^36]

Leibniz's position leads inevitably to the denial of divine freedom in the creation of this world. And, above all, it leads to the denial of the gratuity of the Incarnation of the Word. Similar are the positions of Günther and Rosmini. The affirmations of the First Vatican Council concerning the fact that God created with absolute freedom are sufficiently clear and deliberate. In His action *ad extra*, God would not be truly free if, among all possible worlds, He had to create a determined world, the hypothetically best.

In an article entitled "Whether God can make things better than He makes them", St Thomas indicates a good way not to become entangled in this dialectic:

> "When it is said that God can make things better, if the word *better* is taken as a noun, the proposition is true, for He can make something else better than any of the things made; and even in the case of the same things, He can make them better in some respects (...) But if the word *better* is taken as an adverb and signifies the mode of action on the part of the agent, God cannot make things better than He made them, because He cannot make them with greater wisdom or with greater love."[^37]

From the standpoint of the things made, they can be better. To say the contrary would be to deny the limitation of the created world and the infinite divine perfection in its capacity to be imitated. From the standpoint of Him who makes them, it is impossible to make them better, for they are made with infinite wisdom and love. Optimism is rejectable only insofar as it calls into question divine freedom and the gratuity with which He fills the world with gifts. Once again we encounter the mystery of the divine will as manifested in human election, that is, in vocation, for example in the choosing of the Apostles. From the standpoint of the subject chosen, what doubt can there be that God could have created and chosen better men? From the standpoint of God who chooses, what doubt can there be that the divine choice could not have been made with greater wisdom and prudence? *O the depth of the riches and wisdom and knowledge of God! How unsearchable are his judgments and how inscrutable his ways!* (Rom 11:33).

### The Will of God and Evil

The affirmation of divine freedom in creating this world in which evil exists in such abundance raises the question of how God's goodness is compatible with the free divine permission of the existence of evil. It is a question studied at length in the treatise on creation,[^38] and which here is of interest only from the standpoint of God: namely, how God's love for the creature, especially His love for man, is compatible with the permission of evil. What does it mean to say that God loves the creature when it is affirmed at the same time that He freely permits the evil that befalls the beloved creature? How do infinite goodness and the permission of evil coexist in God?

Before beginning the study of the relationship between evil and the will of God, it is fitting to recall that we stand before one of the greatest mysteries of theology. In a certain sense, moral evil belongs essentially to salvation history, for there would be no salvation without evil from which to deliver. Moral evil has its origin in created freedom. Physical evil, however, is bound up with the very fabric of the world in its present situation. Thus, for example, in order to live, some creatures must kill others. Moral evil finds a first divine response in the expulsion from paradise and in the promise of a redeemer. Throughout the whole of Sacred Scripture, God always appears as the One who wills the good of man and yet permits his fall. God never appears as one who permits evil because He cannot prevent it, but as one who permits it for the highest designs. God's sovereignty is absolute and universally acknowledged. A fine example of this is the Book of Job. The mystery of sin finds its highest expression and its definitive response in the death and resurrection of the Son of God. The victory of Christ on the Cross entails the victory over all evil, including death, which is the last enemy of God to be destroyed (cf. 1 Cor 15:26). Scripture closes with the glorious vision of the new heavenly Jerusalem descending from heaven adorned as a bride for her husband (cf. Rev 21:1–22:5). The theological consideration of evil and suffering must never lose sight of the whole of salvation history.[^39]

With respect to the question of how a good God can permit evil, the answer has always been nuanced:

a) It belongs to faith that God can in no way will sin, neither directly nor indirectly. God can never be called the author of sin, because God can never be its cause;[^40] God never wills iniquity (cf., e.g., Ps 5:5; Rom 3:5; 9:14; 1 Jn 1:5; 3:9). This entails that God cannot cause sin either directly or indirectly: that is, neither by inclining towards sin nor by withholding the necessary aids to avoid it. In this sense, all the causality of sin resides in man; God can only be considered the permissive cause of sin. Naturally, this permission implies a reason in God, a reason wonderfully expressed in the Paschal Exsultet: *O felix culpa!*

b) God wills and is the cause of other evils—physical evils—not because He wills them directly in themselves, but in view of the good that follows from the permission of that evil. It is impossible, indeed, for God to will any evil in itself. From this perspective it is false to say that God positively wills evils to happen in order to reorder them to the good. One may say, however, that God wills physical evil in the sense that He positively wills the order of nature to be preserved, an order which includes the existence of certain evils. Following the example of St Thomas Aquinas, if one wills the lion to live, this includes indirectly willing the death of the stag. And in this sense it can be said that God wills physical evil not in itself, but indirectly, that is, in willing the good indissolubly linked to it.

c) But God can never will evil—not even physical evil—directly, as Hugh of Saint Victor thought.[^41] Good arises from evil only accidentally. For this reason one cannot say that God wills an evil in order to reorder it to the good. St Thomas writes:

> "According to some, although God does not will what is evil, He nevertheless wills that it exist or happen, because, although what is evil is not good, it is nevertheless a good that evil exist or be done; and they said this on the grounds that things which are evil in themselves are ordered to some good (...) But this mode of speaking is not correct. That good should follow from a sin is foreign to the intention of the one who sins, just as it was foreign to the intention of the tyrants that their persecutions should serve to make the patience of the martyrs shine forth; and therefore it cannot be said that such an order is implied in the formula *it is good that evil exist or be done*, because a thing is never judged by what is accidental to it, but by what belongs to it per se."[^42]

When speaking of the relationship between the will of God and the existence of evil, it is necessary to employ these distinctions elaborated throughout history. In this way one avoids treating this matter incorrectly. But these distinctions do not explain the mystery to us; they only bring us to its threshold. And, above all, while they speak to the intellect, they touch the heart but little. Sacred Scripture, using poetic anthropomorphic language, shows us God actively committed to suffering-with man. And, by way of divine actions, it shows God as the Saviour of man. Far from pure passivity and mere permission, God intervenes to deliver man. The advent of messianic times is characterised precisely by this: preaching the good news to the poor, proclaiming liberty to captives, recovering sight for the blind (cf. Lk 4:17–19; Is 61:1–3). Jesus Himself is the divine answer to the question of the existence of evil in the world; His Cross is the practical—and salvific—answer to the question of God's relationship with evil. The Almighty combats evil precisely through the wisdom and the power of the Cross. He does not merely permit evil; He fights against it. For God so loved this world that He gave His only-begotten Son, that the world should not perish, but be saved through Him (cf. Jn 3:16).

### Divisions of the Divine Will

The precision required to speak properly of the relationship of evil to the divine will leads theologians to establish the following distinctions within the divine will, formulated according to our human mode of speaking by reference to the relationship that objects bear to the will of God:

a) *Necessary will and free will*: The distinction requires no lengthy explanation. God loves Himself with necessary love and creatures with free love.[^43] Let it be remembered, however, that one can speak of "necessity" and "freedom" in God only analogously.

b) *Antecedent will and consequent will*: By antecedent will (*voluntas antecedens*) we refer to God's willing prior to the free choice of the creature; by consequent will (*voluntas consequens*), we refer to God's willing taking into account the free decision of the creature. As is obvious, the antecedent will is not always fulfilled. Thus, for example, God wills with antecedent will that all men be saved; but salvation depends also on the cooperation of their freedom.

c) *Absolute will and conditioned will*: This is a distinction already used by the Holy Fathers precisely with the intention of making clear the universal salvific will.[^44] The absolute will is always efficacious, whereas the conditioned will depends on the condition that the creature must freely supply.

d) *Simple will and ordained will*: By simple will (*voluntas simplex*) is meant the divine will insofar as it wills the end of things; by ordained will (*voluntas ordinata*) is meant the will that orders the means so that things may attain their end.[^45]

e) *Efficacious will and inefficacious will*: The absolute will is always efficacious, whereas the conditioned will is efficacious only when the creature freely cooperates.

f) *Will of good pleasure and will of the sign*: By will of good pleasure (*voluntas beneplaciti*) is understood the internal will of God, and by will of the sign (*voluntas signi*), the manifestation of that internal will. The will of the sign is subdivided according to the diverse modes in which God acts or makes His will manifest: by means of fulfilment (*impletio*), permission, precept, prohibition, and counsel,[^46] according to the terminology of St Bonaventure; or, following the nomenclature of St Thomas Aquinas, by means of prohibition, precept, counsel, operation, and permission.[^47] The first pair corresponds to the two ways in which God acts: by operating or by permitting; the other division refers to how God manifests His will to men, and is a logical division: by commanding, prohibiting, and counselling.

### God Desires All Men to Be Saved

The affirmation belongs to St Paul: *This is good, and it is pleasing in the sight of God our Savior, who desires all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth. For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus, who gave himself as a ransom for all* (1 Tim 2:3–6). St Paul affirms with absolute clarity that God wills the salvation of all. It is a sincere and profound will, which at the supreme moment of salvation history is manifested precisely in the self-giving of Christ for all men. The universality of redemption and the universal salvific will thus appear indissolubly interwoven: the universality of redemption is the expression of the sincerity with which God wills the salvation of all men.

In this lapidary statement, St Paul gathers a teaching steadfastly present throughout Sacred Scripture. It appears already in Genesis, in the first promise of the Redeemer (cf. Gen 3:15), and is made explicit again in the election of Abraham, when God promises him that in him all the families of the earth shall be blessed (Gen 12:2–3), all the nations of the earth (cf. Gen 22:18). The universal salvific will is repeatedly manifested in step with the prophecy of the Messiah (cf., e.g., Is 2:1–12; 18:7; 19:21–22), for the Messiah awaited by Israel brings a universal salvation. For this reason Israel is considered a light to all peoples: the nations shall walk in the light of Israel (cf. Is 60:1–16). The Servant of the Lord is appointed as a light to the nations, to bring salvation to the ends of the earth (cf. Is 49:6). This universality of salvation is reflected with equal clarity in the messianic psalms: *Ask of me*—says the Lord, addressing His Son—*and I will make the nations your heritage, and the ends of the earth your possession* (Ps 2:8).

This universality of His saving mission occupies the forefront of Jesus' preaching. He is hailed by Simeon as *a light for revelation to the Gentiles* (cf. Lk 2:32), and proclaimed by John as the Lamb of God who takes away the sin of the world (cf. Jn 1:29, 36). Jesus presents His self-giving as the fruit of the resolute will of the Father that the world should live (Jn 3:16–17), and on the night of the Last Supper He announces that His blood will be shed for the many for the remission of sins (cf. Mt 26:28).

The Pauline text that heads this section must be read in the light of all these passages, and also in the light of another celebrated text in which St Paul affirms that Jesus is the new Adam, drawing an eloquent parallel between the universality of sin and the universality of redemption: *Therefore, just as sin came into the world through one man, and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all sinned (...) Therefore, as one trespass led to condemnation for all men, so one act of righteousness leads to justification and life for all men. For as by the one man's disobedience the many were made sinners, so by the one man's obedience the many will be made righteous* (Rom 5:12, 18–19). Through the blood of Christ, the Father reconciles to Himself all things in Him, making peace by the blood of His cross, whether on earth or in heaven (Col 1:20).

In the light of these texts and of many more that could be adduced, it is clear that God's universal salvific will is a real and sincere will, with the sincerity of the Cross. However, this will does not impose necessity upon things; that is, it is not accomplished of necessity. Numerous texts also speak of the possibility of the perdition of some, for those who do wickedness shall not inherit the kingdom of heaven (cf., e.g., 1 Cor 6:9–10). Jesus Himself warns of the possibility of damnation with images that cannot be interpreted as mere hyperboles. Such is the case, for example, in His description of the Last Judgment (cf. Mt 25:31–46).

It is possible that, despite the universal salvific will, salvation may not in fact reach everyone. The contradiction between the seriousness of this divine will and the possibility of its non-fulfilment poses a grave theological question, for it requires accepting that the divine will is not always accomplished, and not at all because of any lack of seriousness in the divine will. We find ourselves once again before the mystery of the relationship between divine will and human freedom, now considered in its capacity to reject the divine will. How is this capacity to reject the almighty divine will possible in human freedom?

Throughout history, not a few theologians have attempted to resolve this question by denying seriousness to the universal salvific will, overawed by the divine majesty and omnipotence and, above all, struck by the gravity implied in the fact that God's will can be definitively rejected. With the same steadfast insistence, the Church has intervened to reject these positions. Already in the Nicene Creed it is clearly affirmed that the Word, "for us men and for our salvation, came down from heaven...", understanding this affirmation in a universal sense.

The Second Council of Orange formally rejected as incompatible with Christian faith the assertion that God predestines anyone to evil,[^48] and the Council of Quierzy rejected the position of Gottschalk, who reduced the salvific will solely to those who are saved and denied that Christ died for all men.[^49] These texts already delineate the constant line in theological thought, abundantly expounded by the Holy Fathers: God wills all men to be saved; Christ died for all men; those who perish do so through their free rejection of the divine will. Similar texts are found in the Council of Valence, in which the doctrine of John Scotus Eriugena is rejected, wherein, among other things, a predestination to evil was defended. Concretely, it rejected the assertion that anyone could be damned by the desire of God, that sinners are damned because they lack the necessary divine help to be good, or that some are predestined to evil.[^50]

The universal salvific will and the universality of redemption were reaffirmed anew in the condemnation of several Jansenist propositions: the proposition stating that Christ did not shed His blood for all, but only for the predestined, was rejected as blasphemous. Such a thought—it is said—is contrary to the goodness of God.[^51] The universal salvific will and the universality of redemption appear indissolubly linked: the universality of the salvation offered in Christ is proof that the universal salvific will is real and sincere.

The Church was born with an awareness of universal mission in accordance with Christ's mandate. The missionary mandate embraces all men (cf. Mt 28:19–20). So the Holy Fathers saw it, always understanding that Christ died for all,[^52] and therefore that God does not will any man to perish. If anyone perishes, it is by his own free choice, not because God destined him to perish. "The divine will is the salvation of all," writes St Gregory of Nyssa. "If some are saved and others perish, the cause lies not in the divine will, but in the free choice of those who receive the word of God."[^53]

The necessity of reconciling the universal salvific will with the possibility of the perdition of some led the Holy Fathers to distinguish—as has already been seen—between antecedent will and consequent will:[^54]

> "It is necessary to know that God, by His primary and antecedent will, wills all to be saved and to become partakers of His kingdom, for He created us, not to punish us, but because He is good and to make us share in His goodness. But He wills sinners to be punished, because He is just. The primary and antecedent will is, so to speak, good will (*eudokia*), and God Himself is its cause; the second, consequent and permissive, has its cause in our conduct."[^55]

This thought dominates subsequent theology. The salvific will is universal, and to it corresponds the provision of the necessary means of salvation to all. This being so, perdition can have no other cause than the rejection of this offer. Here is the mystery: that man can "thwart" the salvific will and render the blood shed by Christ useless for himself. But the apory posed to reason is not solved—as Gottschalk and the predestinationists wished—by denying the seriousness of the salvific will. God creates no one for suffering, but for happiness. Gottschalk's position may be described as one of those monsters produced by the sleep of reason when theology is severed from the reading of Sacred Scripture.

Theologians usually follow the Damascene in the solution he proposes to this question: God wills with antecedent will that all men be saved. The antecedent will is a serious will, which cannot be interpreted as a mere *velleitas*. And yet, despite His omnipotence, this will can be thwarted by human free choice. This does not mean that God is not almighty, but that God wills, with consequent will, that man exercise his will freely to the extent of being able to reject the divine will, should he so decide. "Whence it is seen," concludes St Thomas, "that whatever God wills simply comes to pass, although what He wills with antecedent will does not come to pass."[^56]

It is an attempt to make human language as precise as possible in expressing the core of the mystery. After St Thomas, theology wandered through the labyrinth of the *De auxiliis* controversy, which shed no greater light on the question. The affirmations of the diverse aspects of the mystery were already clear in the Magisterium of the Church and in theological tradition: on the one hand, divine omnipotence, the sincerity of the salvific will, and the universality of redemption; on the other hand, the reality of human freedom and the genuine possibility that this freedom may reject the divine offer of salvation.

### The Will and Wisdom of God

It is not possible for us to speak of God without taking as our point of reference man, made in His image and likeness, and more concretely, the structure of his intimate life: that is, the structure of his acts of intellect and love. But in speaking of divine wisdom and will, it is necessary to bear in mind the simplicity and eternity of God. God is a pure act of eternal and infinite love. At the same time, one must distinguish in this most simple act what, according to the human mode of speaking, corresponds to the intellect and what corresponds to the will. We have already spoken of the intellect and the will of God. The task now is to indicate the relationship that intellect and will bear to one another. Does the will in God occupy a preeminent place over the intellect? In the history of thought, this question is polarised around the current of voluntarism.[^57]

This current owes its origin to the question of whether the goodness or malice of what is prescribed in the commandments depends exclusively on the divine command. Do the precepts of the Decalogue owe their obligatoriness exclusively to divine decision, such that God could have commanded the contrary, or did God give those commandments because what was prescribed was good—so to speak—independently of His will? Could God have commanded hatred instead of love?

Voluntarism finds its first significant exponent in Abelard. Abelard maintains that God inflicts eternal punishment on infants who die without baptism, and he is troubled by the "justice" of this punishment. Abelard defends its "justice" by taking refuge in voluntarism: that punishment is just because whatever God wills is just. And it is just because it is in the act of willing it or commanding it that God makes it just. Just and unjust would depend, then, exclusively on the divine will. The conclusion is what Abelard sought: although we cannot understand how the penalty inflicted on unbaptised infants could be just, that penalty is just.[^58]

Duns Scotus is likewise troubled by certain divine interventions in the Old Testament that seem not to respect the natural law: for example, the polygamy tolerated among the Patriarchs, a tolerance that seems contrary to God's goodness.[^59] Scotus answers this question by referring to a deeper and more universal framework: he applies to it the general assertion he defends concerning the priority of the will over the intellect. In God too the will would have priority over the intellect. Hence the name voluntarism given to this approach. Scotus adds that, in the case of God, nothing external can impose itself upon His will, for God cannot lose His sovereignty. The divine will can only be determined by itself and, therefore, cannot be bound to a determined mode of acting with respect to the things He has created.[^60]

The nominalists, and specifically Ockham and Biel, radicalise the answer already given by Scotus. Good and evil would be merely extrinsic denominations of things, which, in their goodness, would depend solely on the divine will, which would be absolutely independent of everything.[^61] Here is a lucid formulation by Biel: "God does not will a thing because it is upright and just, but it is upright and just because God wills it."[^62]

This approach would find a strong echo in Descartes, far removed from the moral concerns in which voluntarism arose. For Descartes, the divine will would be almighty not only in the realm of the good, but also in the realm of the true. Divine omnipotence would imply, then, being above even the laws of being. Thus, for example, according to Descartes, even the three angles of a triangle equal two right angles because God so willed, but He could have willed otherwise. Consequently, nothing would be impossible for God, since the exclusive rule of the true and of the good depends solely on His will.

In direct opposition to voluntarism we find St Thomas Aquinas. According to him, will and intellect are distinguished in God with a distinction of reason (*distinctio rationis*), and in speaking of the relationship between the two, it is necessary to give priority to the intellect:

> "Since the object of the will is the apprehended good, it is not possible for God to will except what is contained within the reason of His wisdom, which is precisely what corresponds to His law of justice and what makes His will just and upright. Consequently, what God does according to His will is just and upright for the same reason that what we do according to the law is just, with the difference that we obey the law of a superior, whereas God is a law unto Himself."[^63]

We are faced with an important question. Voluntarism leads almost inevitably to a conception of God as an arbitrary being, for He is conceived as an almighty will governed by nothing, not even by anything internal to God Himself.[^64] Hence the force with which St Thomas argues:

> "The will does not have the character of a first rule, but is ruled by a rule: it is ruled by reason and intellect, not only in us, but also in God. In us, reason and will are really distinct things, and for this reason the will is not the same as the rectitude of the will; in God, intellect and will are really identified (...) To say that justice depends on the simple divine will is equivalent to saying that the divine will does not proceed according to the order of wisdom, which is blasphemous."[^65]

Rarely does St Thomas use such harsh language. It is necessary, indeed, to counter any conception of God as an immense and arbitrary power. And given human limitation—we can only speak of God in a human manner—it is necessary not to sever the divine will from His infinite wisdom. To make what is just or unjust depend upon the bare will of God is, then, an incorrect way of speaking; the just and the unjust depend upon the will of God which is governed by His wisdom: that is, it depends upon the divine nature itself.[^66]

Furthermore, the assertion that, in His action *ad extra*, God cannot depend on anything outside Himself must be properly applied. Clearly God is free to create or not. But once man is created—who is His image—it would be unjust, also on God's part, not to treat him as his dignity as the divine image requires. To say that the world is the effect of a good and just Creator demands adding that this Creator cannot mistreat His creatures. And once this is said, it is clear that one is affirming that the divine will is governed by the goodness of the being that constitutes it, that is, it is governed by love.[^67] Hence, following our human mode of speaking, it is necessary to say that the will of God is not arbitrary, but is governed by His wisdom. Yet since God is Love (cf. 1 Jn 4:8, 16), it is perhaps better to say that the will of God is governed by the wisdom of love.[^68]

### Justice and Mercy in God

Every action of God *ad extra* is accompanied by these two characteristics of the divine will: justice and mercy (cf. Tob 3:2; Ps 25:10). This is constantly affirmed in Sacred Scripture. *Shall not the Judge of all the earth do what is just?* (Gen 18:25). Still more, God is the champion of the weak and the oppressed against all injustice (cf. Ps 140:12; Ex 22:21–23). To such an extent is the concept of justice bound up with God's way of acting that in Sacred Scripture this concept always carries a religious nuance. One of the tasks proper to the Messiah is to establish justice throughout all nations (cf. Is 11:1–5); our Lord requires of His disciples that their righteousness exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees (cf. Mt 5:20). The righteousness of God is definitively manifested in the gift of justification given to us in Jesus Christ (cf. Rom 3:21–24).

The biblical concept of God's justice is of such richness that at times it is identified with the concept of divine holiness. The justice by which God is just is, in turn, the justice by which He justifies men: that is, His holiness. The justice of God designates, above all, the rectitude of divine love, which finds the way to unite justice and mercy ineffably in salvation history.[^69] It designates also the justice that God enacts at the end of time, for "the Final Judgement will reveal that God’s justice triumphs over all the injustices committed by his creatures and that his love is stronger than death."[^70]

Our language about God has man as its point of reference, including in the moral sphere. This point of reference, however, must always be transcended as the divine nature requires, being beyond all thought. The matter is especially delicate when speaking of justice. Utmost care must be taken to avoid speaking of God's justice in a sense univocal to human justice. Nor can it be thought that God's relations of justice with man fall within the realm of commutative justice, for all that man has, he has received from God.[^71] But justice does exist in the realm of fulfilling His promises and of pursuing the admirable order of creation and redemption as established by His wisdom.

Justice is found in God in an eminent manner, contained within supreme divine truth and holiness. In salvation history, this justice finds its highest manifestation on the Cross of Christ, where justice and mercy embrace in an ineffable manner. On the Cross there is manifested the "fullness of justice and of love, since justice is founded upon love, springs from it, and tends towards it (...) The divine justice revealed in the Cross of Christ is to the measure of God, because it is born of love and is accomplished in love, generating fruits of salvation."[^72] On the Cross, God's mercy is manifested, so to speak, as justice and fidelity of the Father to His fatherhood, as wisdom and holiness. On the Cross the perfections of God are marvellously displayed in unity. Therefore, in a certain sense, the face of God is definitively revealed on the Cross.

Hence every work of God is an expression not only of His justice, but also of His mercy. God can do nothing that is not coherent with His wisdom and His goodness. The work of His justice is always founded on love, which, before misery and suffering, manifests itself as mercy.[^73] Consequently, mercy is the primary root of every work of God, including His justice. It is manifested, moreover, in that God, through the superabundance of His goodness, bestows His gifts upon each one with greater generosity than required by the proportion of things.[^74] Hence, in the works of God, not only are justice and mercy always united, but mercy—so to speak—surpasses justice. Mercy is the beginning and end of all divine works, so that, as St Thomas says, mercy is the fullness of His justice.[^75]

In speaking of mercy in God it is necessary to make use of analogy as well, stripping this concept of the imperfections with which it is manifested in men and which are incompatible with infinite divine perfection. In God there are no passional movements, and in this sense it cannot be said that sadness exists in Him. Mercy does not entail any sadness in God, but it is truly found in Him in what it possesses of perfection: that is, in what it possesses of efficacious action in relieving evil. Still more, it is necessary to affirm that mercy is the attribute most proper to divine goodness, for it is proper to goodness to pour out its goods by succouring him who lacks them.[^76] Wrath, on the other hand, is not properly present in God, but only in a metaphorical sense, namely, insofar as His justice and His holiness lead Him to reject sin.[^77]

### The Suffering of God

In speaking of mercy in God, we have set forth the classical affirmations of theological tradition: in God there is mercy, but without sadness, for sadness is incompatible with infinite divine perfection. Nothing taints the infinite happiness of God. Now then: is it possible to love someone and not suffer-with, not share in his suffering? One understands the attempt of St Thomas to place the infinite transcendence of God and His infinite happiness beyond doubt. But in doing this, does he not gravely compromise the seriousness of His love for suffering man? Some contemporary theologians have voiced their unease with the classical formulation, preferring to speak directly of the existence of suffering in God, produced by the suffering of man. In this way they think they do greater justice to the seriousness of His love for man.

The teaching of Sacred Scripture regarding divine philanthropy, the mercy of God, is so constant that theologians have never doubted the seriousness of God's love for man. The very mystery of the Cross radically precluded this. But when considering the effect of human suffering on God, theologians have been primarily concerned to reaffirm His infinite perfection. Consequently, they have highlighted divine impassibility. In doing so, they never intended to present a God apathetic towards human suffering, but a God infinitely perfect. The words of St Thomas just cited are a good example of this. We also find this same priority in the Magisterium of the Church.[^78]

In speaking of the suffering of God, it is necessary to refer to three distinct, though connected, realms. The first is the suffering of the Word in the sufferings of Christ; the second is the suffering of God Himself in the death of the Son, that is, the suffering of Christ as a Trinitarian mystery; the third is the suffering of God in His relationship with the suffering of the world.

The Church has always confessed that the Word became flesh and suffered for men.[^79] This suffering—it is said—is the suffering of the Son in His humanity. The manner in which the suffering of Jesus' humanity affects the Word, who is the subject that suffers, is a mystery dependent upon the mystery of the hypostatic union. In this perspective, one can affirm with all radicality that God Himself has become a partaker of our suffering and has tasted death. But always remembering that "the Christology of the Church does not accept speaking formally of the passibility of Jesus Christ according to the divinity,"[^80] just as one cannot speak formally of mutability and passibility in God.[^81]

It is not strictly to this realm that the theologians of the suffering of God refer, especially the representatives of the Lutheran *theologia crucis*. The question they raise is different: according to them, the Cross of Christ would introduce suffering into the very bosom of the Trinity. That is, the suffering of God on the Cross would be a Trinitarian suffering. This is a question affecting divine immutability, and has already been treated at length in its proper place, for many of these authors not only introduce suffering into the bosom of the Godhead, but, following Hegel, conceive God as a gigantic process. Indeed, these authors, using the Cross as a pretext, present the Godhead as a gigantic dialectical process of which human history would be, at once, the realization and the reflection. J. Moltmann is one of its most typical exponents.[^82]

The third realm to which one may refer in speaking of the suffering of God concerns the suffering produced in God by human grief, including the suffering of Christ, the most innocent of all men, upon whom the maximum suffering fell. To be consistent with the affirmation of God's love for man, the goal is to apply to God the notion of suffering, purifying it of everything that involves materiality, passibility, and imperfection,[^83] and therefore purifying the notion of suffering of everything incompatible with infinite divine perfection.

We speak of the suffering of God insofar as that expression seems to accompany the seriousness of God's love for man. Therefore, as has been noted, to speak properly it is fitting to keep in mind the distinction between necessary love and free love in God, for this distinction is fundamental when applying the notion of suffering to God without detracting in any way from the infinite divine perfection:

> "Indeed, only this distinction allows us to clarify that these and other sentiments which the Bible attributes to God do not touch the sphere of intra-Trinitarian love and, therefore, are not constitutive of God, as necessary love is; they are the most moving and convincing expression of free love, above all, of God’s love for man."[^84]

We find ourselves, then, exclusively before the mystery of God in His relationship with the world and with man. Before the mystery of His mercy. Divine impassibility is not *ataraxia*, but superabundance of life and love:

> "Christian piety always rejected the idea of a Divinity untouched by the vicissitudes of its creature; it was even inclined to grant that, just as compassion is a most noble perfection among men, so too does there exist in God, in an eminent manner and without any imperfection, that same compassion—that is, the inclination (...) of commiseration, not a lack of power—and that this is reconcilable with His eternal happiness. The Fathers called this perfect mercy with respect to human misfortunes and sorrows a passion of love, of a love which in the Passion of Jesus Christ brought suffering to fulfilment and conquered it."[^85]

As John Paul II writes:

> "The conception of God as a necessarily most perfect being certainly excludes from God any pain deriving from limitations or wounds (...) But the Sacred Book often speaks to us of a Father who feels compassion for man, as if sharing his pain. In the final analysis, this inscrutable and ineffable fatherly pain will engender above all the wonderful economy of redemptive love in Jesus Christ, so that, through the mystery of piety, love may reveal itself in human history as stronger than sin. That the gift may prevail (...) On the lips of Jesus the Redeemer, in whose humanity the suffering of God is verified, will resound a word in which eternal love, full of mercy, is made manifest: *I have compassion* (cf. Mt 15:32; Mk 8:2)."[^86]

It is a suffering closely linked to sin, a suffering mysterious because of the perfection of God's love, just as the reality of the offence inflicted by man is mysterious, the depth of which only the Holy Spirit can reveal:

> "Must not convincing concerning sin, therefore, also mean revealing suffering? Must it not reveal the pain, inconceivable and inexpressible, which, as a consequence of sin, the Sacred Book seems to glimpse in its anthropomorphic vision within the depths of God and, in a certain way, in the very heart of the ineffable Trinity?"[^87]

Before the inexpressible mystery, Scripture uses an anthropomorphic language, which may appear crude when one attempts to rationalise it, but which is perhaps the closest to reality and which, in any event, is that used by God to express to us the mystery of His love for man. Although it may seem a paradox, anthropomorphic language is perhaps the least crude of all, for it allows analogy to be used effectively, presenting a God who is close to man and, at the same time, transcendent.
