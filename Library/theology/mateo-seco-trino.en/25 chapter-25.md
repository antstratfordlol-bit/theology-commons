## Chapter 25: The Theology of Divine Relations {#mateo-seco-trino.25}

The study of the divine processions leads to a readily perceptible paradox: those who proceed are the one and the same God as those from whom they proceed, and yet they are really distinct from one another. The Son is really distinct from the Father and from the Holy Spirit; the Holy Spirit is really distinct from the Father and from the Son. Indeed, it would be contradictory to speak of a real procession in God without also affirming that a real relation—and therefore alterity—exists between the terms of that procession. Yet at the same time, whilst being distinguished from one another by a real distinction, the three Persons of the Trinity are one sole God, since they are not distinguished in substance—each possessing the divine substance wholly and in numerical identity—but solely in their interpersonal relation.

Hence the notion of relation is central to the treatise on the Trinity. It is precisely in this notion that the key is found to resolving the antinomy between the unity of substance in God and the real distinction of Persons. This is corroborated by the study of the history of Trinitarian theology: it has been perceived with ever-greater clarity that the distinction among the Three of the Trinity can obtain only in the relation existing between them, and more concretely, in the so-called opposition of relation or relative opposition.

### The Notion of Relation and Its Application in Trinitarian Theology

All created beings are susceptible to accidents, since all are subject to change. But in God change is impossible, for He possesses all being: He can neither lose being nor acquire more being. He is infinite: He can neither increase nor diminish. In God, therefore, there is nothing accidental. And yet, not everything predicated of God is said according to substance. There are things that are said of the Persons, but not of the divine substance. We have seen this when studying the processions. It is the Father who begets, not the divine essence or substance. Paternity belongs to Him alone. How, then, are we to speak of those things that belong exclusively to each Person? Logically, they cannot be called substance, because the divine substance is single and common to the three Persons; nor can they be called accidents, because in God there is nothing accidental.

Saint Augustine formulates the question thus:

> "In God nothing is said according to accidents, because nothing can happen to Him; yet not everything that is said of Him is said according to substance. In created and mutable things, whatever is not predicated according to substance is predicated according to accidents. In them everything can be lost or diminished: dimensions and qualities. And let the same be said of relations of friendship, kinship, servitude (...) place, time, action, passion. Thus, in God nothing is affirmed according to accident, because in Him there is nothing mutable. Nevertheless, not everything that is said of Him is enunciated according to substance, for God is spoken of at times according to relation: the Father is related to the Son, and the Son is related to the Father, and this relation is not an accident (...) In conclusion, although to be Father and to be Son are diverse, they are not a distinct substance, because these names are spoken not according to substance, but according to relation; and the relative is not an accident, since it is not mutable."[^1]

There is one sole God and three distinct Persons. The distinction of Persons in God—that intimate life whereby the Son is referred to the Father and the Spirit to the Father and the Son—cannot be understood as pertaining to the divine substance. It is not the divine essence that begets, but the Father. Therefore, Father, Son, and Spirit designate in God something that is not the substance—which is single and unique—and neither is it an accident, because God is supremely simple. How are we to conceive of something existing in God that is neither substance nor accident?

#### Relations in God

Almost from the outset—the text of Saint Augustine is eloquent—theology has inclined towards designating by the name of *relation* everything in God that is not "according to substance". There are two fundamental reasons for this: first, the names and concepts by which the Trinitarian mystery has been revealed to us are names that speak of mutual relation, such as that which obtains between persons bound by the ties of paternity and filiation; the second reason lies in the characteristics of the metaphysical concept of relation, which does not essentially include in itself the notion of accident or change, but solely that of pure reference.

It belongs to the faith that relations exist in God. Indeed, the names of the three divine Persons are relative to one another. Father is said in reference to the Son; Gift is said in reference to the Giver. We know of the existence of processions and relations in God precisely through these personal names, for "in the relative names of the Persons, the Father is referred to the Son, the Son to the Father, the Holy Spirit to both; and at the same time, when speaking of these three Persons, even while considering the relations, one believes in a single nature or substance."[^2]

The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) teaches that the three Persons are identified with the divine substance and are distinguished exclusively by their relations of origin;[^3] the Council of Florence (1442) underlines this unity of the Persons by affirming that they possess "the same substance, the same essence, the same nature, the same divinity, the same immensity, the same eternity, for in God everything is one except where there is an opposition of relation."[^4] At the Council of Florence it is likewise seen that Greeks and Latins alike admit the existence of relations in God: relations alone introduce distinction in God.[^5]

There exist, then, real relations in God, since the names of the Persons designate authentic realities whereby one Person is distinguished from another and related to it:

> "Father, Son, Holy Spirit are not simply names designating modalities of the divine being, for they are really distinct from one another: He who is the Son is not the Father, and He who is the Father is not the Son, nor is the Holy Spirit He who is the Father or the Son."[^6]

Father, Son, and Spirit are really distinct from one another because the relations existing between them by reason of their origin are real relations.[^7] It is evident from the very nature of the concepts that, in revelation, Father and Son appear as relative names—that is to say, as referred to one another in a relation of opposition that requires alterity and distinction. The father is father in reference to a son, and the concept of son is correlative to that of father. This fact appears less evident in the name of the Holy Spirit. And yet, when examined closely, Holy Spirit likewise appears in revelation as a relative name: He is the Spirit who proceeds from the Father and the Son.[^8]

#### The Metaphysical Notion of Relation

In metaphysics, relation is one of the nine genera of accidents identified by Aristotle. It has already been stated that in God there can be nothing accidental. The employment of this concept, therefore—even whilst observing all the rules of analogy—is especially delicate, since it demands that the concept be purified of every connotation that assimilates it to an accident.

Precisely what is most essential to this concept, and what determines its use in Trinitarian theology, is its characteristic of *esse ad*: the father is referred to the son in his paternity, and the son to the father in his filiation, without that reference adding anything to the concepts of father and son.

> "Comparing the proper being of relation with that of the other accidents, we discover this difference: that whereas the other accidents affect the substance, modifying it intrinsically or extrinsically, relation is not taken in regard to a subject in which it inheres, but in regard to a term with which it establishes a reference (...) For this reason the Scholastics defined predicamental relation by stating that it is an accident whose proper and specific being consists in an order or reference between two distinct things: *cuius totum esse est ad aliud se habere*."[^9]

Relation signifies solely the reference of one thing to another, what in classical terminology is designated by the expression *esse ad*—a "looking towards". Relation does not pertain, therefore, to something that affects the substance as such, but to the reference existing between two terms. Hence relation is defined as the fact of being referred to another thing. The concept of relation implies, therefore, a subject that relates (*terminus a quo*), the term to which it is referred (*terminus ad quem*), the foundation or reason for the relation—which in God cannot be anything other than an immanent action—and the relation itself existing between them: the relation of paternity, of filiation, of spiration.

It is necessary to bear in mind, moreover, that when applied to God, these elements proper to relation must be reconciled with the divine simplicity, perfection, and unity. For this reason, the emphasis is placed on the fact that they are pure *esse ad*, total reference to the other. Given the infinite divine simplicity, that reference to the other is identified with each of the subjects of the relation—the Persons of the Father, the Son, and the Spirit—and is really identified with the single divine essence, possessed by the three divine relations.

God is infinitely simple. This is the reason why theology identifies the relations ever more closely with the divine Persons, even to the point of defining the Persons as subsistent relations. It has already been stated repeatedly and in diverse contexts: the Father is not a subject prior to paternity, but is Himself subsistent paternity. The Father is entirely a relation of paternity towards the Son; the Son is entirely a filial and infinite *esse ad Patrem*. And the same must be said of the Holy Spirit. In God, the *in se* implied by every relation—for it is the relation of a subject with respect to another—is identified with the *esse ad aliquid*.

The radicality with which these assertions must be understood becomes evident if one takes into account that, given the infinity of God, it is necessary to exclude from Him the concept of "having". God does not *have*; He *is*. Men have sons, but God is Father and Son; men have love, but God does not have love: He is Love. Men have relations, but God is relation.

Words fail to express even the little that can be glimpsed of the mystery in the light of revelation. One can understand why contemporary theologians are reticent before what has come to be called the "God of the philosophers". In the light of revelation, God cannot be conceived as a substance that subsequently unfolds into the three Persons, as though the Persons proceeded from the substance. The one God—He who has revealed Himself to us and whom the metaphysical itinerary discerns—is a subsistent relation of paternity eternally oriented towards the Son, who receives it eternally in His filial relation to the Father, whilst Paternity and Filiation together spirate personal Love: that is, they shine forth in the living flame of the spiritual Love that emanates from them and unites them in an eternal embrace of love.[^10]

It is necessary to highlight the profound difference between the concept of relation as applied to creatures and as applied to God. The principal difference lies in the fact that relations—which in created beings belong to the genus of accident—cannot be conceived in God as an accident, but as a subsistent *esse ad*. According to our mode of speaking, creatures are sustained in their substance, whereas relations—being accidents—presuppose the substance as already constituted and modify it. They are, therefore, something added to the substance. Hence philosophers include relation among the accidents. In God, by contrast, relations are not only not accidental, but are precisely that which constitutes the Persons, who are the one existing God. This already marks the essential difference when applying the concept of person to man and to God. Human beings are individual substances who realize themselves in the unfolding of their acts of knowledge and love, that is, in their relations of openness and exchange with others. A human being can close in upon himself without thereby ceasing to be a person. In God, conversely, the Persons are constituted precisely by their relation, by their openness to the other Persons: the *esse ad*, proper to relation, is the personal being of each of the Persons.[^11] As the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* states, "The real distinction of the Persons from one another resides solely in the relations which refer them to each other."[^12]

#### Existence and Number of Relations in God

Faith teaches us that there exist only two immanent processions in God: one by way of intellect and the other by way of love. These processions, which are real, are the real foundation of the relations existing within the bosom of the Godhead. The relations are, so to speak, the other side of the coin of the processions. There can be no real procession in God that does not generate a relation, and conversely, no immanent relations can exist in God without having a foundation proceeding from divine life itself.

Each of these two processions generates two relations mutually opposed to one another: that which proceeds from the term proceeding back to the principle from which it proceeds, and that which proceeds from that principle towards the term proceeding from it. Hence it is necessary to affirm the existence of four relations in God: paternity, filiation, active spiration, and passive spiration.

To the procession of the Word corresponds the relation of paternity–filiation, and to the procession of Love correspond active spiration and passive spiration. Of these four relations, only three pairs are opposed among themselves—or rather, there are two pairs of mutual opposition: paternity–filiation and active spiration–passive spiration. But active spiration is not opposed to the relations of paternity and filiation; rather, it can coincide with them. For this reason, although there are four real relations in God, only three establish a real distinction between terms—that is to say, only three of them constitute Persons. Active spiration not only is not opposed to the Persons of the Father and of the Son, but is identified with them, inasmuch as Father and Son spirate Love precisely as Father and Son. The Spirit springs forth, as a bond (*nexus*), from the love between Father and Son. Hence He is opposed exclusively to active spiration. The Spirit is opposed to the Father and the Son, not as Father and Son, but in so far as they form a single spirating principle—that is, in so far as they are active spiration. Yet that active spiration does not constitute a fourth Person, for it is not opposed to the relation of paternity–filiation.

And since there are no more than two processions in God, there can only be four relations in Him.

### Divine Relations as Subsistent Relations

Having determined the existence and number of relations in God, it is fitting to dwell upon certain questions concerning the concept of relation so as to employ it with precision in Trinitarian theology. Above all, this involves making explicit what is meant by *subsistent relation*, and how these relations are distinguished among themselves and with respect to the divine essence. These questions arise following the affirmation that God is infinitely simple and that, nevertheless, real relations exist in Him. It becomes necessary to weigh properly the doctrine of relation in order to show that it is by no means impossible for the distinction proper to relations to introduce no composition whatsoever into God.

We are considering the very core of the Trinitarian mystery: how three Persons, who are one and the same single God, are really and numerically identified with the essence or substance of that God, and at the same time are really distinct from one another. Theology can do no other than strive to present coherently—without unravelling the mystery—what is believed, and to prevent that presentation, through lack of conceptual precision, from degenerating into mutually contradictory assertions.

We believe in a mystery; we do not assert an absurdity. If it is said that there is but one sole God, it is necessarily being said that there exists only one divine substance; if it is affirmed that the Father, the Son, and the Spirit are three Persons, it is being affirmed that they are distinguished from one another by a real distinction. That distinction can be situated neither in the divine substance—which is single and unique—nor in an accidental modification of that substance, since in God there can be no accidents. In order to express this distinction, it is necessary to rely on a reality that is neither substance nor accident. That reality is relation, precisely because relation consists in being pure reference to something. Yet that reference must be real, since it establishes a real distinction among the Persons. Hence the importance of the concept of subsistent relation.

#### The Concept of Subsistent Relation

The real distinction of the Persons among themselves is nothing other than the distinction existing among the relations that constitute them. The reality of these relations depends upon the reality of their foundation, that is to say, of the processions. The Father is distinct from the Son because He begets Him, and because, consequently, His relation of paternity with respect to the Son is real. This is what is signified by the expression *subsistent relation*.

In this context, by the term *to subsist* nothing other is meant than to exist in the extra-mental universe with a reality beyond that which our mind confers upon it. The existence of real relations in God implies, therefore, that they are something more than mere concepts. Now, whatever exists really either exists in itself or exists in another as an accidental being. In God there can be no accident whatever. It is necessary, therefore, that whatever exists in Him exists in itself, and never in another, as an accident. If real relations obtain in God, they must therefore obtain as something existing *in se*. To this existing *in se* of the divine relations we give the name *subsistence*. Hence the expression *subsistent relations*.

For its explanation, it suffices to recall that from the beginning of this chapter a distinction has been drawn within relation between its *esse in*—that is, the quality of its reality—and its reference to the other, that is, its *esse ad*. Essentially, the concept of relation expresses only reference, *esse ad*. The concept of relation, in and of itself, says nothing more. Hence, when speaking of a real relation, it is necessary to add something signifying its reality: one must mention not only its *esse ad*, but also its *esse in*; not only relation as such, but the ontological quality of the related terms and the foundation of that relation.

In applying the concept of relation to God, we find ourselves once again confronted with the urgent need to employ analogy and to purify the concept of relation from all imperfection. Specifically, it is necessary to strip relation of its character as accident, thereby transcending our present experience. All the real relations known to us owe their reality to the substance to which they adhere and which they modify—that is, they owe their reality to the fact that their *esse ad* inhers as an accident in a being that is substance. In God, however, paternity, filiation, and spiration cannot be regarded as modifications of the divine substance, but as pure *esse ad*. Yet it is a real and eternal *esse ad*.

Hence theologians qualify the divine relations as subsistent relations. By this they intend to convey that these are not relations of reason (*relationes rationis*), but real relations; yet these relations are neither an accident in God, nor are they the divine substance or divine essence, which is one and unique. They are, therefore, relations whose *esse ad* is at the same time their *esse in seipsis*—that is, an *esse ad* that rests in itself. They are relations that subsist in themselves, in which *esse ad* and *esse in* are identified.

Naturally, this is a concept that the theologian elaborates exclusively in order to speak with some degree of propriety of the Trinitarian mystery. Yet he does so reasonably. Revelation speaks to us of three divine Persons related to one another. In the light of revelation, it is understood that these relations cannot be *in alio*, but *in seipsis*. Consequently, it is necessary to conceive them as relations that subsist. Without revelation, man would never have discovered the existence of relations subsisting in themselves—relations whose being consists entirely in their pure referentiality. Such relations can obtain only in God, who is supremely simple. But once the existence of three Persons in God has been revealed, and once they are described as related to one another, it is entirely reasonable to consider these relations as subsistent, as befits divine perfection.

The concept of subsistent relation is certainly paradoxical. Inasmuch as it is *esse ad*, the relation is referred exclusively to its correlative term; inasmuch as it is *esse in*, the relation constitutes itself by the very fact of being a reference to the other. The paradox lies in the fact that we find ourselves before a Relative that is absolute, or an absolute-relative—an absolute referred to its term to such a degree that its whole being (which is nonetheless real) consists in this reference.[^13]

All this has already been stated, albeit from another perspective, when speaking of the divine Persons in previous chapters. There it was observed that the entire being of the Father consists in His paternal reference to the Son, but that this reference is of such an ontological quality that it subsists in itself. The Son is subsistent filiation to the Father. What is now better expressed by the term *subsistent relation* is the fact that we are not dealing with Persons who, once constituted, relate to one another—for in that case the relation would supervene upon them once already constituted and, therefore, in an accidental manner, even if it modified them most intimately; rather, we are dealing with relations that are subsistent precisely in and through their *esse ad*.

By the expression *subsistent relation*, therefore, one indicates the unity between absolute and relative, and the radicality with which the relational aspect in God must be taken. Father, Son, and Spirit are solely and exclusively that: Father, Son, and Spirit. Their being, so to speak, is exhausted in their relation. Or, put conversely, that relation is of such power and totality that it is in itself a person. The Father is constituted by His paternity, the Son by His relation of filiation, and the Spirit by His procession as Love. There is present in all three, in an infinite degree, the perfection of love and of intimate embrace: the whole being of each consists in His own reference to the other.

#### Relations and the Divine Essence

When it is asked whence the divine relations derive their character as subsistent, the answer is that this subsistence comes to them from the fact that they are identified with the divine essence; they are subsistent relations because they are divine relations—because these relations are identified with the essence of God. That is what is affirmed when confessing that the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God. The relations are distinguished, however, from the divine essence by a distinction of reason (*distinctio rationis*), since essence and relation are distinct notions: substance is being considered in itself; relation is being considered exclusively in its reference to another. Relation and substance are two conceptually distinct notions which, in God, are one single reality. For this reason there exists between the relations and the divine essence a distinction of reason.

Even though a relation is subsistent, it remains a relation and does not turn into substance by the fact of being subsistent. In this case, relation is "a mode of being" of the substance. The expression *subsistent relation* means nothing other than this: it designates a relation that, paradoxically, subsists. Its subsistence is its relativity. The Father is not a person who begets after being constituted, but is a person constituted by His paternity; the Son is nothing other than subsistent filiation to the Father.[^14] The relations are identified with the divine substance to the point of being the unique being of God, without implying any addition—anything supervening—to that divine being.

The relations are distinguished from the divine essence only conceptually (*ratione*), but they are really distinguished from one another: paternity is really distinct from filiation, and active spiration from passive spiration. Hence the four relations in God, which are precisely the terms of the two processions: generation and spiration in their respective active and passive forms.[^15]

History shows the consequences that follow if one denies either the real identity of the relations with the divine essence, or their distinction of reason. If it were said that the relations are not identified with the divine essence, a true composition would be introduced into God. It suffices to recall the controversies surrounding Gilbert of Poitiers (*Gilbertus Porretanus*) or Joachim of Fiore. The struggle of Saint Basil and Saint Gregory of Nyssa against Eunomius regarding *agennetos* (unbegottenness) made clear that the existence of mutual references in God—Father and Son—requires accepting that differences exist in God, without the unity of His essence being thereby compromised. The Eunomians, by completely identifying the Father with the divine essence, argued that the Father cannot give that same substance to the Son, because He would then also have to give Him His own unbegottenness (*agenetes*). The Sabellians, on their part, precisely in order to underline the equality of nature among the three Persons, denied the authentic reality that distinguishes them, conceiving them exclusively as extrinsic denominations of the Godhead. Hence the necessity of affirming, at one and the same time, real identity and a distinction of reason between the relations and the divine essence.

#### The Distinction of Relations Among Themselves

Despite their identification with the divine essence, the relations are distinguished from one another by a real distinction. This real distinction is founded upon the fact that the relations, according to our mode of speaking, are the consequence of the divine processions, which lead to a real distinction between the two opposed terms of the procession.

The confession of three distinct Persons in God demands the consequent affirmation of a real distinction between the opposed terms of the relations. In order to uphold Trinitarian faith, it is not sufficient to admit the existence of real relations in God; it is also necessary to admit that these relations are really distinguished from one another by reason of their mutual opposition. The Father begets the Son, and cannot originate from Himself; the Son proceeds from the Father, not from Himself, nor can He beget Himself. Son and Father are opposed and, therefore, distinct and mutually inconfusible. The same holds true for the Spirit.

Hence these theological axioms common in Trinitarian theology:

"Relation multiplies the Trinity,"[^16] for relation alone introduces differences in the Trinity. The Three of the Trinity are subsistent relations who differ solely by the opposition of their relation.

"In the divine, all things are one, except where the opposition of relation requires otherwise."[^17] This axiom is applied with precision, for example, by the *Quicumque* Creed, which highlights the distinctions among Father, Son, and Spirit, while specifying that although Father, Son, and Spirit are each almighty, there are not three almighties in God, but one almighty.[^18]

"In God, the absolute is not divided by relation,"[^19] for relation is not an accidental modification of substance, but three references of the one and identical substance. The relation of opposition introduces difference between the Persons, but introduces no division into God.

In God, "the relative is not accidental, because God is immutable."[^20]

#### The Relation of Opposition in God

And thus we arrive at what has rightly been termed the "fundamental Trinitarian law":[^21] namely, that the only real distinction that can obtain in God is that which arises from the opposition of relation, in conformity with that statement already formulated by Saint Gregory of Nazianzus and subsequently followed by theologians and the Magisterium: "Between the three divine Persons there is full identity, except for the relations of origin."[^22]

There can be no real distinction in what belongs to the essence, which is single, unique, and supremely simple. Nor can there exist a real distinction between the divine essence, the processions, and the relations. Consequently, neither does this real distinction exist between the processions and the relations that are not opposed to one another—specifically, between paternity–filiation and active spiration. Relations not opposed to each other are distinguished only by a distinction of reason. This is what occurs with active spiration in respect of paternity and filiation. Since active spiration expresses no opposition to the relation of paternity and filiation, it is identical with both, implicitly including them. Conversely, to beget and to be begotten are really distinct from one another, just as to spirate and to be spirated are really distinct. Indeed, it would be contradictory for that which is referred to a term to be identified with the very term to which it is referred.

Distinction and opposition between the related terms belong to the very essence of relation; this opposition must be real if the relation is real. It is repugnant to common sense that a thing could be both itself and its opposite. Such is the case, for example, with the concept of paternity: it is impossible for the Father to be the Son, for He would then be simultaneously Himself and the opposite of Himself. To attribute real relations to God and yet deny a real distinction between the opposed terms would be contradictory.

> "It belongs to the essence of relation to be a certain reference of one thing to another, which causes one to be opposed to the other by relative opposition. Therefore, since there is real relation in God, it is necessary that there also be real opposition. But relative opposition includes distinction within its concept. It is indispensable, then, that in God there be real distinction: not indeed according to absolute reality, which is the divine essence wherein maximal simplicity and unity are found, but according to relative reality."[^23]

Between Father, Son, and Spirit there is, therefore, authentic alterity, since they are really distinguished by the opposition of their relation, which is a real relation. They are distinguished and, at the same time, united. Indeed, relative opposition is not in itself incompatible with the consubstantiality of the relative terms, since relative concepts say nothing of the essence of those concepts, but only of the reference whereby they are mutually related. If, then, one can conceive of an alterity consisting solely in relative opposition, to the exclusion of all other distinction, this alterity can be realised in the very heart of consubstantiality itself.[^24] The Father, by the very fact of imparting to the Son His entire substance in begetting Him, makes Him one with Himself—one single God, for it is His very substance—and yet distinct from Himself, since the Son possesses that substance as received.

When speaking of identity and alterity in God by reason of relations, it is fitting to recall that a relation between distinct persons always implies that those persons have something in common. Without that community, relation would be impossible. Neither alterity nor multiplicity can exist without reference to something that is in some manner within the same order. Among created beings, no matter how much likeness exists, this relation of more or less perfect likeness can never attain to identity. In God, conversely, identity of essence and distinction of relatives are united to such a degree that the three relatives—Father, Son, and Spirit—are, at the same time, one and the same single God: three Persons who are in all things the same, except in the fact of mutual reference, since the opposition of relation takes place not with respect to substance, but solely and exclusively with respect to the opposed term of that relation.[^25]

In this light, the Trinity is revealed to us as "the most perfect communion among those who are distinct; and, as such, it is a light that illuminates the human screen of our interpersonal relationships and allows us to understand to what real communion of knowledge and love we, the children of God, are called. Thus the Trinity, in its dimension of communion, is good evangelical news for human coexistence. Indeed: we who are distinct are called to full communion. This communion is with the Father and with the Son in one and the same Spirit."[^26] Our Lord, on the supreme night of the Last Supper, appealed to Trinitarian unity as the framework and source of union among men: *That they may all be one; even as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be in us* (Jn 17:21).
