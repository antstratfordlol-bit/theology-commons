## Chapter 29: The Theological Concept of Personal Life in God {#mateo-seco-trino.29}

In His interventions in salvation history, God always reveals Himself as a living being in infinite fullness. God is the Creator and the source of being and of life; of all the life that exists outside Himself (cf., e.g., Gen 1:27; 14:22; Is 65:17; Col 1:15–20; Eph 4:24). Human reason attains the very same affirmation when it contemplates God in His status as pure and most simple act: God is indefectible life.

> "Life is in God in the most proper sense. To understand this, let it be observed that, if life is attributed to beings that act by themselves (...) the more perfectly this belongs to a being, the more perfect will be the life within it (...) Wherefore, if there were a being whose nature was its very understanding, and which received the nature it possesses from no one else, that being would possess the supreme degree of life. Now this being is God; and therefore in God life attains its supreme degree."[^1]

God lives in the highest degree, with a life superior to all created forms of life. Still more, God does not *have* life; rather, He *is* Life (cf. Jn 14:6). This statement is self-evident, yet it is fitting to return to it now, as we near the end of our consideration of the mystery of God. God is not a god of the dead, but the living God and the fountain of life. Everything that has been said regarding the divine attributes or the intra-Trinitarian life must be contemplated within this horizon of supreme and ineffable vitality. God is Life, possessed in an infinite and eternal act of knowing and loving, which overflows—freely—in creation and in uninterrupted dialogue with the rational creature. God is not a universal and abstract concept, but Life: a most concrete and personal act of being.

God lives with the most perfect life of the spirit. Sacred Scripture teaches this as it recounts the divine interventions in history. Our Lord underscores this when speaking of His Father. It is revealed with ineffable depth in the disclosure of the Trinitarian mystery. That mystery is nothing other than the divine life vibrating in an infinite and most simple act of Love. The *communio personarum* is the manner in which He who is subsistent Love—and therefore Truth and Life—lives.

### The Living God and His Trinitarian Dimension

Life is not an abstract concept, but the most concrete reality we can conceive. Life is what is most intimate to the living being, to such an extent that one may say that, for living things, their being is their living (*vivere viventibus est esse*).[^2] In the divine life, then, God's being is manifested in all the radicality of His unity and His trinity.

For this reason, strictly speaking, what is living is not the divine essence or nature, but rather the three divine Persons, who are identical with the divine essence. Following the formulation of the *Quicumque* Creed, it can be said that the three Persons are, at the same time, a single living Being, living the *communio personarum* in a single act of intellect and will (love).[^3] Here it is especially necessary to bear in mind all that has been said regarding the *circuminsessio* (perichoresis). The Persons indwell one another by virtue of the oneness of their divine life. Yet we must remember, paraphrasing the classical philosophical axiom, that life is act, and actions belong to persons: *actiones sunt suppositorum*.

God exists in three Persons. This means that the divine life is realised in a Trinitarian form, that is, in a most simple unity lived by the three Persons. Nothing else is meant when it is affirmed that the divine essence consists in a most simple act of knowledge and of love. It is an act lived Trinitarianly. The same holds true when speaking of God's action *ad extra*: it is not the divine essence that acts, but the three Persons, who are distinguished solely by their opposition of relation.

The New Testament describes the divine life by referring at times to the personal distinction that exists in God. The Father has communicated His life to the Son (cf. Jn 5:26; 1 Jn 5:11–20), so that the Son is likewise eternal life (1 Jn 1:2). He is the Word of life, the life that was with the Father and was made manifest in these last times (1 Jn 1:1–2). The Son is the Life (cf. Jn 14:6) to such an extent that He gives life to whomsoever He wills (cf. Jn 5:21), through His word, which is spirit and life (cf. Jn 5:24; 6:63, 68), and through His body, which is the bread of life (cf. Jn 6:51, 58). The same must be said of the Holy Spirit. He is Lord and giver of life. The Holy Spirit is power and might. It is His power that causes us to be reborn to new life (Jn 3:5).[^4]

We are speaking, therefore, of three living Persons who, without losing their personal identity, live with one and the same divine life. This life is an immense and absolute existential fullness. For this reason, the One and Triune God holds absolute and unconditioned dominion over life and being. He does not possess being and life; He *is* being and life.

### The Essence of the Triune God as Mystery of Love

In considering God in His unity, following the usual order of questions in theological manuals, we paused to examine *Ipsum esse subsistens* as the formal constitutive of the divine essence. It is not that the divine essence is complex or composed of diverse parts; rather, the limitation of our human intellect requires us to "break down" and "order" the most simple divine perfection into diverse perfections according to the concepts we form from created realities. In doing so, the divine act of existing appeared to us as that primary perfection which, according to the laws of human logic, can serve as the foundation upon which to order and ground the other divine perfections. In using *Ipsum esse subsistens* as the support of all divine perfections, St Thomas relies on a "metaphysical" reading of the theophany of Exodus 3:14: God is He who is and who will be.[^5] He is an infinite and most simple act of existing.

Richard of Saint Victor drew attention to the radicality of the Johannine affirmation: *God is love* (1 Jn 4:16). In our day, numerous theologians are inclined to regard the divine essence as an act of love, infinite and subsisting in three Persons. This is not, of course, a denial of classical theology, which regards the divine act of being as the metaphysical constitutive of all divine perfection; rather, the same reality is now considered from another perspective, closer to the way in which God has revealed His intimate life. In Revelation, especially in the New Testament, God reveals Himself primarily as a mystery of love.

God is simultaneously being and love. He is subsistent Being. According to our way of conceiving things, being comes first. Everything that is, exists insofar as it has being. Thus does the biblical God appear: as the Creator of all things, as the One who holds dominion over life, as the One whose name is *He who is*. And yet, Sacred Scripture itself, especially the New Testament, presents God by emphasising above all that He is love. All His actions *ad extra* are marked by love: He creates us out of love, and above all He redeems us out of love and destines us for love.

It is entirely unwarranted to posit an opposition between these two aspects of divine perfection—being and love—an opposition that is nowhere to be found in biblical thought. God is the One who always is, indefectible life, and at the same time an infinite act of love. The passages of Exodus 3:14 and 1 John 4:16 point towards the same reality—the vital fullness of God—and must be read together.

Within this fundamental affirmation, contemporary theology underlines that the Christian God is, above all, the God who reveals Himself to us in the face of Christ crucified: and that face is the face of love and of suffering. It is clear, then, that presenting God as Love is the most adequate way to express the mysterious reality revealed to us on the Cross. The category of love is central to the treatise on God, both when considering Him in His unity and in His Trinity. The category of love is what allows us to enter with greatest certainty into the intimacy of the divine life.

It is evident that only God can be infinitely gratuitous love, because only He is superabundant and indefectible life. For this reason, according to the order in which we habitually arrange our concepts, it may be said that God could not be an infinite act of love were He not an infinite act of being. God could not be infinitely beatifying were He not infinitely happy. Yet at the same time, following universal theological affirmations concerning divine simplicity, it is impossible for God to be an infinite act of being without being indefectible Love. Here too the ancient philosophical axiom applies: *ens, verum et bonum convertuntur* (being, truth, and goodness are convertible). In God, being, truth, and love are identified in an infinite degree.

All these aspects are explicitly affirmed in Sacred Scripture. The Bible narrates the divine interventions and, through them, leads towards knowledge of the Being who acts. Although, strictly speaking, it does not engage in philosophical reflection on being, it does speak of a Being who exists always, who possesses power, who knows, who is everywhere present; a Being who precedes the existence of the world, since He creates it. While we do not find in the Bible a formal philosophical treatise on divine being, we do find an elemental metaphysics of the being of God and the being of the world. For biblical man, it is unthinkable that God should not exist. And yet, the divine attribute that Scripture highlights with the greatest force is precisely the Love of God. The beautiful language of Jesus possesses a radicality that admits of no doubt: God is love and mercy. God is the One who takes upon Himself the suffering of man. The self-emptying (*kenosis*) of God (cf. Phil 2:5–11) is in full coherence with what is most intimate to divine being, that is, with His essential love.

The observation made by C. Porro is entirely valid:

> "We are thoroughly convinced that the rediscovery of the category of love to speak of God is the most significant contribution of recent theology, and that the metaphysical reading of Exodus 3:14 must be considerably re-dimensioned. On the other hand, we do not at all agree with those who seek to cast aside discourse on God as being—and fundamentally also that of the *analogia entis*—not least because the underlying motivations frequently visible behind this option come dangerously close to accepting the unknowability of being suggested by Heidegger."[^6]

These are complementary perspectives and, viewed from the standpoint of Revelation, inseparable. Love is the mode in which Being exists. Salvation history springs from the infinite and sovereign freedom of God. Yet this sovereign freedom is exercised by God in complete harmony with His intimate nature, which is love and gratuitous self-giving. The believer must take in all its radicality the affirmation that the Cross is the ultimate revelation of what it means to be God. As John Paul II writes:

> "The cross on Calvary, through which Jesus Christ—a Man, the Son of the Virgin Mary, thought to be the son of Joseph of Nazareth—'leaves' this world, is also a fresh manifestation of the eternal fatherhood of God, who in Him draws near again to humanity, to each human being, giving him the thrice holy 'Spirit of truth'. The cross placed over Calvary, where Christ conducts His final dialogue with the Father, emerges from the very core of that love wherewith man, created in the image and likeness of God, was endowed according to the eternal divine design. God, as Christ has revealed Him, does not merely remain in close connection with the world as Creator and ultimate source of existence. He is also Father: with the human person whom He called into existence in the visible world, He is united by a bond even deeper than that of Creator. It is love, which not only creates what is good, but makes man share in the very life of God: Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. For he who loves desires to give himself (...) The paschal mystery is Christ at the summit of the revelation of the inscrutable mystery of God. It is precisely then that the words spoken in the Upper Room are fulfilled to the uttermost: *He who has seen me has seen the Father* (Jn 14:9)."[^7]

A Christian cannot regard this statement of our Lord as hyperbole. The face of Jesus is the face of God.

### The Fullness of Intra-Trinitarian Life and Its Salvific Dimension

The history of Jesus necessarily leads back to the Trinitarian mystery, for that history is both the fruit and the manifestation of the mystery. He is the Son sent by God into the world so that whoever believes in Him should have eternal life (cf. Jn 3:16). Both creation and redemption proceed from the free will of God who is Love-Trinity. Stated otherwise, the subsistent and eternal Trinity constitutes the transcendent foundation of salvation history.[^8] There is one only God, who is always and in all things a Triune God: the closest possible unity in the living and acting of the three Persons who, without being confounded with one another, indwell one another.

Divine life has its origin in the Father, the unoriginate principle (*principium sine principio*). Through eternal generation, the Father communicates His very own indivisible substance to the Son: He gives Himself. From the Father and the Son, the Holy Spirit proceeds as love and bond of unity. This vital, infinite, and eternal current overflows gratuitously in the creation of the world and of man. It is the three Persons—acting together and inseparably—who create the world.

In the Father, the Son and the Spirit have their origin, and—as was seen when treating of the divine missions—the mission of the Son and the mission of the Spirit likewise have their source in Him. The Incarnation answers, first and foremost, to an initiative of the Father, to the love the Father bears towards the world. The same is true of the mission of the Holy Spirit (cf. Jn 14:16, 26). The expressions of Jesus regarding His mission and His obedience are constant and must be taken in their full force: the Son does not come to do His own will, but the will of Him who sent Him (cf. Jn 4:34; 6:38). The Son is the One who redeems us, yet the source of redemption is found in the Father, inasmuch as He is the origin of the Son and the origin of the Son's mission.

Salvation is essentially Trinitarian. It takes its origin in the very essence of the Trinity—the processions—and finds its fulfilment in man's participation in the Trinitarian life. The sending of the Spirit into the heart of man and our adoptive filiation in the Son have no other meaning. Salvation history is, therefore, the history of the most intimate communication God can bestow upon man: the personal self-gift of the Father in the Son through the Holy Spirit. By means of this gift, man is united to the Trinity in a personal relation with each of the divine Persons. Nothing portrays this *exitus-reditus* more vividly than the essentially Trinitarian structure of Christian liturgy across all ages.

Liturgy, like Scripture, unveils the being of God—the *theologia*—through the divine works: through creation, through salvation history, and through the life of the Church; that is, through the *oikonomia*.

> "God communicates Himself in His works just as He is in Himself, namely, Trinitarianly. He reveals Himself through and in His Son, and the Son reveals Himself in the Spirit. Hence the liturgy, which unfolds before our eyes the divine salvific plan, is, like Christian faith itself, essentially Trinitarian. It displays to the believer the Trinity of persons in God: every gift proceeds from the Father, through the Son, in the communication of the Holy Spirit. This scheme provides the foundation for all the anaphoras and for all the great doxologies."[^9]

The liturgy, like the Christian life, is essentially Trinitarian in structure. This is so not only because the origin and end of creation and redemption is the Trinity, but because man's union with God is realised precisely in his being introduced into the Trinitarian mystery. The joint mission of the Son and the Spirit has as its purpose our union with the Most Holy Trinity: the Spirit—Gift, Love, Bond of Union—unites us to the Son as the branch to the vine (cf. Jn 15:1–8), thereby enabling us to participate in an ineffable manner in the life of the Trinity.

The Trinity is, therefore, man's true homeland. From its bosom the rational creature has sprung, and towards its bosom it journeys, having been created in the image and likeness of God precisely for an eternal dialogue of love. Among the genuine achievements of contemporary theology is the resolute option for what has been described as a return to "the Trinitarian homeland", that is, speaking of God "starting from His coming to us, according to that analogy of advent which is the relationship established between God and man through the gift of creation and the grace of redemption."[^10]

The mystery of the Trinity lies at the very heart of the New Testament, so much so that it has been called the substance of the New Testament.[^11] As St Caesarius of Arles writes, the faith of all Christians is grounded in the Trinity (*fides omnium christianorum in Trinitate consistit*).[^12] This is the teaching reiterated by the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*:

> "The mystery of the Most Holy Trinity is the central mystery of Christian faith and life. It is the mystery of God in Himself. It is therefore the source of all the other mysteries of faith, the light that enlightens them. It is the most fundamental and essential teaching in the 'hierarchy of the truths of faith'."[^13]

### Creation, the Work of the Most Holy Trinity

The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* places its teaching on creation under this very heading. It is a genuine lesson in theological excellence.[^14] It is sometimes said that creation is the work of God as one. This is true, inasmuch as all works *ad extra* are common to the three divine Persons. However, following the common usage of human language, it is important to remember that it is not the divine essence that acts, but the Persons. For this reason, it is better to say that creation is a Trinitarian work, since in it the three Persons act jointly and inseparably.

The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* begins its treatment of the doctrine of creation by reading together the first chapter of Genesis, the first chapter of St John's Gospel, and that of the Epistle to the Colossians: *In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth* (Gen 1:1); *In the beginning was the Word... and the Word was God... All things were made through Him, and without Him was not anything made* (Jn 1:1–3); *In Him all things were created, in heaven and on earth... all things were created through Him and for Him. He is before all things, and in Him all things hold together* (Col 1:16–17). It comments:

> "Three things are affirmed in these first words of Scripture: the eternal God gave a beginning to all that exists outside Himself: He alone is Creator (...) the totality of what exists (...) depends on the One who gives it being."[^15]

> "The New Testament reveals that God created everything by the eternal Word, His beloved Son (...) The Church's faith likewise confesses the creative action of the Holy Spirit, the 'giver of life', the 'Creator Spirit' (*Veni, Creator Spiritus*), the 'source of every good' (Byzantine liturgy, Troparion of Pentecost Vespers)."[^16]

> "The creative action of the Son and the Spirit, inseparably one with that of the Father, is in an elusive way hinted at in the Old Testament (cf. Ps 33:6; 104:30; Gen 1:2–3) and revealed in the New Covenant. It is clearly affirmed in the Church's rule of faith: 'There exists but one God... He is the Father, God, the Creator, the Author, the Giver of order. He made all things by Himself, that is, by His Word and by His Wisdom',[^17] 'by the Son and the Spirit who, so to speak, are His parts [hands]',[^18] 'Creation is the common work of the Holy Trinity'."[^19]

The *Catechism* refers here to the rich and venerable Trinitarian theology of St Irenaeus. The Son and the Spirit are presented by Irenaeus as the "two hands" with which the Father accomplishes the work of creation, and above all the human *plasis*, the fashioning of man. The *Catechism* offers a "differentiated" vision of the three Persons in the joint work of creation. Scriptural texts invite a reading in which, within unity, that which is proper to each Person is distinguished. Christian tradition, too, from the very outset, has read the work of creation in a Trinitarian perspective, considering the distinct intervention of the Word and of the Spirit.[^20]

The *Catechism* insists that this action is inseparably one. This provides the necessary counterpoint to safeguard the balance between the affirmation of God's unity and His Trinity. The *Quicumque* Creed offers an excellent example of this balance and of the proper use of language. Paraphrasing its style, one may affirm that the creative action belongs to the Three: the Father is Creator, the Son is Creator, the Holy Spirit is Creator, yet there are not three Creators, but one Creator.

In God there is unity of nature and therefore unity of operation.[^21] Nevertheless, each Person accomplishes the common work of creation and salvation according to His personal property. It could not be otherwise, since the Person who acts is a Person by virtue of being a subsistent relation opposed to the other two. The Second Council of Constantinople (AD 553) articulates divine action *ad extra* in parallel with Trinitarian doxology: "One is God and Father from whom are all things, one is the Lord Jesus Christ through whom are all things, and one is the Holy Spirit in whom are all things."[^22]

Consequently, Father, Son, and Holy Spirit do not constitute three principles of the creature, but a single principle. Yet at the same time, because it is the Persons who act, this action—without ceasing to be common—belongs to the three Persons distinctly. To the Father is attributed initiative; to the Son, mediation; and to the Holy Spirit, the bringing of the work to fullness and completion. In this common action, one also observes a certain parallelism between the action of the Trinity in creation and in the work of salvation: the Father accomplishes the work of salvation through the mediation of the Son in the Holy Spirit. The *Catechism* thus highlights the relationship between the creative act and the work of salvation. Both correspond to a single design of the One and Triune God. The One and Triune God, who possesses in Himself the fullness of life and therefore has no need of creatures, creates in order to communicate His goodness—and to communicate Himself—to the rational creature.[^23]

There is, then, a clear connection between the eternal processions and the procession of creatures, with the difference inherent in being a procession *ad extra*. Ultimately, however, the whole of creation is the fruit and unfolding of Trinitarian love. Contemporary theologians vigorously underline the importance of a Trinitarian consideration of the creative act for presenting its characteristics coherently, especially as regards its gratuity and freedom.

The dialogue of knowledge and love belongs to the metaphysical structure of spirit. We have seen this particularly within the sphere of influence of Richard of Saint Victor. From this vantage point, the conception of a monopersonal and solitary God—which is the inevitable perspective of philosophical theism—is difficult to reconcile with the sovereign gratuity of the creative act. Indeed, in one way or another, philosophical theism tends to view creation as the necessary self-unfolding of a God attempting to escape His loneliness in search of someone with whom to be friends. The Trinitarian perspective is of great help in properly framing the relationship between God and the world: God does not need the world for His personal fulfilment, for He already possesses love and friendship within Himself. The world, and specifically man, is an absolutely gratuitous divine gift; it is the gift of a God who wills to give Himself to the rational creature, making it a participant in His life of love. Even within the freedom inherent in all works *ad extra*, creation, as the work of God, bears within itself the imprint of the divine nature, which is Love.

In this perspective, the world appears embraced by intra-Trinitarian love. The world is not God, which is why it is called a work *ad extra*. Yet for this very reason, the world—though not God—is not outside the Trinity; rather, it is embraced by Its power and loving presence. God, in His intimate life, is love and interpersonal communion: for this reason He can be love for us—free, gratuitous, and infinitely generous love. The world is, so to speak, a prolongation of God's intimate love overflowing into creation and into time.[^24]

Furthermore, the mystery of creation is characterised by its Christological dimension. The world was created through the Word (cf. Jn 1:1–3). All things were created through Him and for Him, and all things hold together in Him (cf. Col 1:15–20). Creation is Christologically determined. Christ is the *raison d'être* of all things, and especially of man. God chose us before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless in His sight (Eph 1:4–5).

The Christocentrism of the Franciscan school, particularly regarding the primary motive of the Incarnation, possesses profound theological warrants and receives renewed impetus from the Trinitarian consideration of creation and its relationship to man's ultimate end. As O'Donnell recalls, Karl Barth expressed the mutual relationship between creation and redemption in a felicitous formula: creation is the extrinsic ground of the Covenant; the Covenant is the intrinsic ground of creation. "All this is equivalent to saying that God in His eternity is the God of the Covenant, the God who willed to enter into communion with men and women. The creation of man is not an afterthought in the divine plans, but was conceived in the Son from all eternity."[^25]

### Omnipotence and Providence of the Triune God

The omnipotence of God is the only divine attribute explicitly named in the Creed. In previous chapters, divine omnipotence has been studied from various perspectives.[^26] Now, at the close of the treatise, we return to omnipotence to contemplate it in its Trinitarian dimension and as it accompanies God's care over the world. In Sacred Scripture, omnipotence, creation, and providence appear in close conjunction. God's power is manifested in creation and in His care for all things, even the smallest. Power and providence characterise God's acting and reveal His Trinitarian intimacy. God's omnipotence is the omnipotence of Love. It is not a blind, arbitrary power, but the wisdom of an infinitely powerful love attentive to its creation.

God's power extends to all things, yet it manifests itself in a disconcerting way in the apparent powerlessness of God. The *kenosis* of the Son also manifests divine omnipotence. As St Paul writes, the Cross is the power of God and the wisdom of God (cf. 1 Cor 1:24–25). The Father deploys the greatness of His power in the Resurrection of Christ (cf. Eph 1:19–22).[^27] The Father's omnipotence is made manifest in the power of the Son and in the power of the Spirit. The Son is not only the creative Word (cf. Jn 1:1–3), but also the Christ who acts with the power of God (cf. Lk 11:20). Christ's charismatic activity is the manifestation of His divine power over the elements of nature and over the evil afflicting man, namely sin. One need only recall Christ's miracles. In turn, the Spirit reveals His power in the life of Christ and in His activity throughout history. The Spirit is the power of God acting within man to divinise him. Divine omnipotence is revealed as Trinitarianly structured both in the *kenosis* and in the exaltation.[^28]

The same holds true of Providence. Indeed, "the doctrine of Providence coherently unites the mystery of God as the origin of all things and the mystery of the consummation of all creation through the operation of the same Creator, who thus appears as Lord of the beginnings and the ultimate ends of Creation and of man. The same divine power and love that gave origin to things must solicitously guide them towards their consummation."[^29]

That love, as we have seen, is essentially Trinitarian. Hence divine Providence, like the creative act itself, is nothing other than the effective manifestation of Trinitarian love. The work of redemption stands at the very centre of that Providence which permits evil and sin, and heals it by means of the Redeemer. In the light of the redemptive mystery, Providence is displayed with meridian clarity in its Trinitarian structure: it springs from the love of the Father and unfolds in history through the joint mission of the Son and the Spirit.

The paschal mystery emerges from God's loving design to create man endowed with freedom and not to abandon him to the power of evil when his freedom fails. In the Christian universe, divine providence possesses an essentially Trinitarian and Christological connotation, since it finds its culmination in the mystery of the Incarnate Word. Through the mystery of the Cross, the Father reconciles the world to Himself in the Son of His love in the power of the Holy Spirit (cf. 2 Cor 5:18–19), restoring to creation the meaning it received in the creative act.

> "Redeemer of the world! In Him has been revealed in a new and more wonderful way the fundamental truth concerning creation to which the Book of Genesis bears witness when it repeats several times: 'God saw that it was good' (cf. Gen 1). Good has its source in Wisdom and Love. In Jesus Christ the visible world, created by God for man (cf. Gen 1:26–30)—the world which when sin entered was 'subjected to futility' (cf. Rom 8:20)—recovers again its original link with the divine source of Wisdom and Love. Indeed, 'God so loved the world that He gave His only-begotten Son' (Jn 3:16)."[^30]

Divine Providence is recapitulated in the economy of grace, that is, in the self-communication of the Triune God to the rational creature. For this reason, Providence itself, contemplated from the vantage point of Christian revelation, reveals itself as essentially Trinitarian.
