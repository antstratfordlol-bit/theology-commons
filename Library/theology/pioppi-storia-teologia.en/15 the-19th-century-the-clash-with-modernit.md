## The 19th Century: The Clash with Modernity {#pioppi-storia-teologia.15}
1. This is the century of total dominance of Europe, superior to the rest of the world in political, economic, cultural and technological terms. Age of Imperialism: 1871-1914, in which a positivist optimism dominates, thinking that we can now do without religion.

2. Epochal change occurred with the Enlightenment, the French Revolution and the Industrial Revolution

- Cultural change: with the Enlightenment, estrangement and distancing from Christian revelation
- Social change: advent of the bourgeoisie
- Economic change: industrialization
- Political change: liberalism and representative systems taking over after the Restoration

3. Philosophical and cultural currents of the nineteenth century:

- Romanticism: in the first half of the nineteenth century, the prevailing cultural current of Romanticism opposed to the Enlightenment of the eighteenth century: revaluation of passions and feelings against rationalism; of history against science; of nation and people against cosmopolitanism; of the Middle Ages against the Classical Age. This current is generally favorable to Christianity, even if in some cases it falls into fideism and traditionalism.
- idealism (Johann Gottlieb Fichte, Friedrich Schelling, Georg Wilhelm Hegel, Hegelian right), from which liberal Protestantism (Friedrich Schleiermacher, Adolf von Harnack) is largely derived: theology engulfed by philosophy.
- Positivism: new form of scientistic rationalism, in reaction to romanticism (which dominated the first half of the 1800s). Founder of this philosophy is Auguste Comte. Naive faith in science and progress; great optimism in the progress of humanity, which will be brought by "civilized" nations to all peoples (link with colonialism).
- Marxism: religion as opium of the people. Derivation from Hegelian left: Ludwig Feuerbach.
- ideologies as secularized religions: nationalism, socialism, scientism, liberalism.

**4. Fideism and traditionalism:**

- **Joseph de Maistre** (1753-1821) and **Louis de Bonald** (1754-1840), traditionalists: without denying the importance of reason, they emphasize the fact that human reason lives and develops in history: therefore Christian tradition and revelation are more relevant than reason in practice; in this they are influenced by Romanticism, which also revalues history, change, vitality against the abstract and static rationalism of Enlightenment culture.
- **Félicité de Lamennais** (1782-1854): a Breton priest, he shows the limits of reason, to which he opposes common sense, tradition, the universal consensus of peoples, and the authority of God. Lamennais moved from traditionalism to liberal Catholicism through ultramontanism.

**Liberal Catholicism** (Félicité de **Lamennais**, the Dominican Henri Lacordaire, Count Charles de Montalembert, the great political scientist Alexis de Tocqueville) and ultramontane Catholics (the journalist Louis Veuillot). The newspapers *L'Avenir* (liberal Catholics) and *L'Univers* (ultramontanism). Lamennais's intellectual journey from ultramontanism to liberalism: since he wants only the pope to govern the Church, he advocates the separation of church and state in order to eliminate political interference in ecclesiastical government. Practical difficulty in realizing this project in the first half of the 19th century. In addition, Lamennais, a very fiery character, moves in an exaggerated and clumsy manner, with too vehement tones. He would not accept the ***Mirari vos*** (1832) with which Gregory XVI condemned liberalism, and published a book, *Paroles d'un croyant*, in which he continued to openly support his ideas; in 1834 he was again condemned with the *Singulari nos*, then abandoned the priesthood and the Church, dying outside of it. Lacordaire and Montalembert, on the other hand, maintained a low profile line, waiting for the times to mature.

- **Louis Bautain** (1796-1867): accepts Kantian ideas of the *Critique of Pure Reason*, but man can know the realities superior to his intelligence through the help of God who offers through faith and tradition a deeper knowledge. Reprimanded by the ecclesiastical authorities, he submitted.
- **Augustin Bonnetty** (1798-1879): some propositions from his publications were reproved by the Congregation of the Index, and Pius IX in 1855 ratified his condemnation.
- **Gérard Casimir Ubaghs** (1800-1874): professor at Louvain, where he was the most prominent figure in a traditionalist school that was very active there.

**5. Semirationalism:**

- **Georg Hermes** (1775-1831): professor of theology in Bonn and Münster; excellent teacher and pious priest of spiritual life. He tries to structure a theological system from Kantian philosophy; like the traditionalists, he accepts the positions of Kant's *Critique of Pure Reason*, but, unlike them, he concludes that an adult and mature faith requires questioning the faith itself, in order to reconstruct it on a rational basis through an intellectual process. Posthumous condemnation: on September 26, 1835, four years after his death, Gregory XVI, with the brief *Cum acerbissimas*, reproved Hermes' affirmations on the necessity of a positive doubt as a requirement to reach a mature and adult faith.
- **Anton Günther** (1783-1871): Bohemian priest, lived in Vienna from 1824 until almost his death, he was not a professor, but a free researcher and writer; he tried to structure a theological system starting from Hegelian philosophy, while trying to correct its pantheism: condemned several of his theories by Pius IX in the brief *Eximiam tuam* of June 15, 1857, he submitted himself.
- **Jacob Frohschammer** (1821-1893): professor in Munich, makes an attempt similar to Günther's: several of his ideas were also reproved with the brief *Gravissimum inter* of December 11, 1862, but he did not accept the Roman correction.

**6. Tübingen School.**

1. General Considerations. The city of Tübingen had been endowed with a university since the fifteenth century; in the period of the Reformation, the duchy of Württemberg became Protestant and, with it, Tübingen and its theological faculty. During the Napoleonic Wars, a simplification of Germany's political geography took place, which also involved the elevation of Württemberg to a kingdom and the incorporation of a number of territories from other political entities in Swabia, whose population was predominantly Catholic. King Frederick II (duke and then king from 1797 to 1816), in order to meet the needs of his new subjects, founded in 1812, in Ellwangen, a Catholic theological faculty, which was transferred to Tübingen in 1817 by William I (king from 1816 to 1864) and added to the Protestant faculty. The Catholic faculty was born with only five chairs: Old Testament, New Testament, Church History and Canon Law, Dogmatic Theology, Moral Theology and Pastoral Theology. The transfer from Ellwangen, a Catholic town, to the Protestant city of Tübingen meant the possibility - or rather the necessity - to open up to comparison and dialogue with both the Protestant world and the Enlightenment and Romantic cultures.

We speak of the school of Tübingen to refer to the group of theologians who taught in the Catholic faculty of theology (in a broader sense also to those who studied there) during the first three quarters of the nineteenth century. There has been much debate as to whether or not this group of scholars can be described as a "school"; in fact, it was not a group of disciples around a master, nor was it a group of intellectuals united by some common basic ideas; what there was certainly was a common environment and spiritual and cultural attitudes shared by this group of professors. In 1819 a journal was founded that became an expression of this school: the "Theologische Quartalschrift".

Founder of the school is considered Johann Sebastian Drey (1777-1853); the best known theologian is Johann Adam Möhler (1796-1838). Other notable figures are Peter Alois Gratz (1769-1849), Johann Georg Herbst (1787-1836), Johann Baptist Hirscher (1788-1865), for the "first generation"; Franz Anton Staudenmaier (1800-1856), Johannes Evangelist von Kuhn (1806-1887), Karl Joseph Hefele (1809-1893), for the "second generation". In addition, among the scholars of ecclesiastical sciences trained at the Catholic Faculty of Theology in Tübingen, there are important names such as Pius Bonifatius Gams (1816-1892), Ferdinand Probst (1816-1899), Franz Xaver Funk (1840-1907), Paul von Schanz (1841-1905).

Characteristic of the faculty's group of professors was the attempt to create a theology that was in harmony with the culture of the time and, in this context, the importance given to history as a useful tool for theology itself. In Germany at the beginning of the nineteenth century, idealistic philosophy and romantic cultural sensibility were in great vogue, while historical studies were taking their first steps, arousing great enthusiasm and interest. In this faculty the historical-genetic method applied to the study of revelation developed: with this method, starting from the positive study of Sacred Scripture and the Fathers of the Church, an attempt was made to grasp the fundamental ideas on which to base a theological synthesis, which, however, took into account the historical development of doctrine and dogma. There is therefore an important attempt to overcome a conception of Christianity as a merely abstract, timeless and immobile reality, introducing instead into theological thought, through the idea of a vital and living organism, the notions of process and historical development. Also starting from the changed cultural horizon, in the study of revelation the theologians of the school of Tübingen were looking for an alternative way to the model of antideist apologetics, the result of the predominance of Enlightenment philosophy. Interesting is also the reflection of the Tübingen professors on the tradition that, on the basis of the theological thought of St. Vincent de Lerins (†450 ca.) - especially in *Commonitorium* - and Denys Petau (1583-1652), is presented as a living tradition, as an organic transmission.

The first director of the school was J.S. Drey; in his works, *Kurze Einleitung in das Studium der Theologie mit Rücksicht auf den wissenschaftlichen Standpunkt und das katholische System* [Introduction to the Study of Theology] and *Apologetik als wissenschaftliche Nachweisung der Göttlichkeit des Christentums in seiner Erscheinung* [Apologetics] he tried to make use of some ideas from the philosophies of Georg W.F. Hegel (1770-1831), Friedrich W.J. von Schelling (1775-1854) - also natives of Württemberg - and Friedrich D.E. Schleiermacher (1768-1834), reworked from a Catholic perspective. Drey argues that Christianity is not only an idea, a doctrine, but also a history: therefore, he tries to overcome a theology that is anchored only to a metaphysical-deductive system; on the other hand, this history, as sacred history, has its own characteristics, since it is guided by a divine plan which, although eternal, is expressed in temporality. From this perspective, the notion of revelation, together with that of tradition in the Church, plays a central role in Drey's theological work. But he distances himself from the scholastic conception of revelation, which he considers reductive: for him, revelation is not the communication to men of a series of truths by God, but rather the historical self-manifestation of God himself who communicates himself to man. Another basic idea, around which the speculation of the founder of the school revolves, is that of the Kingdom of God: it is a living organism that grows, develops and evolves in history, with a development that occurs in continuity, as a development of the starting point. In the exposition of this idea of kingdom, one can see the influence of German idealism on the theology of Drey, who tries with this idea to advocate an instance of system, a claim to reduce to completeness the entire order of knowledge: this aspect will arouse reservations in some of his followers, especially in Möhler and Staudenmaier, who in their works will reduce the scope. J.E. von Kuhn, also a dogmatic theologian, was Drey's successor at the head of the faculty: his most important studies and reflections concern the notions of tradition and dogmatic development. J.B. Hirscher dealt mainly with moral theology, making an attempt to renew this branch of the sacred sciences: he reflects on the close link between dogmatics and morals, with the latter to be a consequence and application of the former; Hirscher also tries to abandon the scholastic method, and takes as the starting point of his speculation the idea of the kingdom of God as the moral order of the world. His most important work is *Die christliche Moral als Lehre von der Verwiklichung des göttlichen Reiches in der Menschheit* [1835: Christian Morals as a Doctrine of the Realization of the Kingdom of God in Humanity].

J.A. Möhler. As far as ecclesiology is concerned, the most important figure of the Tübingen School is Johann Adam Möhler, who is also its greatest representative: born in Igersheim in 1796, having attended schools in Mergentheim and Ellwangen, he enrolled in 1816 in the theological faculty of Ellwangen; he was ordained a priest in 1819 and in 1822 became a free lecturer in church history and canon law in Tübingen, before becoming a full professor of church history there in 1828; in 1823 he made a study trip to the Protestant faculties: Würzburg, Bamberg, Jena, Leipzig, Halle, and Göttingen, where, among other things, he listened to Schleiermacher's lectures; in 1835 he moved to Munich to teach New Testament exegesis, where he died in 1838. The teaching of ecclesiastical history and the influence of the ideas of his teacher Drey led him to profound reflections on the Church, which found expression in his two great works: *Die Einheit in der Kirche, oder das Prinzip des Katholizismus, dargestellt im Geist der Kirchenväter der drei ersten Jahrhunderte* [Unity in the Church, i.e., the principle of Catholicism in the spirit of the Church Fathers of the first three centuries], of 1825; In them the author, through a return to patristic theology, seeks to open up new paths with respect to the ecclesiological approaches prevalent in his time, characterized, since the time of the controversial theology that arose after the Reformation, by purely juridical and societal models. *Die Einheit in der Kirche* was written, from the perspective of the teaching of history, to prove the organic continuity existing between Christ and the Church. Möhler wants to contribute to a rediscovery of the aspect of the "mystery" of the Church, which for him is first and foremost divine life present in history, an organism that develops over time under the guidance of the Holy Spirit, the principal inspirer of faith in the faithful and of communion in the Church; this is the work and creation of the Holy Spirit, and the same Spirit is the principal actor of the mystical unity of the Church, which is made visible in magisterial, liturgical and hierarchical unity. The Church is defined by Möhler as the external structure of a living power of love; the body of the spirit of believers that is formed in a dynamic that moves from interiority to exteriority. On the basis of these concepts, *Die Einheit in der Kirche is* divided into two parts, each of which is subdivided into four others; the first section deals with spiritual unity (*Einheit des Geistes der Kirche*) and the chapters into which it is divided bear the following titles: mystical unity (*Die mystische Einheit*), unity of thought (*Die verständige Einheit*), multitude without unity (*Die Vielheit ohne Einheit*), unity in the multitude (*Die Einheit in der Vielheit*); the second section deals with external, visible unity ("bodily": *Einheit des Körpers der Kirche*), through the path of unity in the bishop (*Die Einheit im Bischof*), in the metropolitan (*Die Einheit im Metropoliten*), in the episcopal college (*Die Einheit des gesamten Episkopates*), in the primacy of the pope (*Die Einheit im Primat*).

In *Symbolik* Möhler, taking into account some of the criticisms made by other theologians of his *Die Einheit in der Kirche*, completed the picture by emphasizing the Christological aspects of ecclesiology, harmonizing them with penumatological principles: in this work the Catholic and Protestant dogmatic doctrines are exposed and compared in an orderly and rigorous way; the ecclesiological questions are found mostly in chapter five of the first part (*Gegensätze in der Lehre von der Kirche*): the Church is understood here as the continuation or extension of the Incarnate Word, the permanent incarnation of the Son of God, a concept, this, which offers the key to well interpreting the relationships between what is visible and invisible in it, between what is human and supernatural.

The ecclesiology of the school of Tübingen, and especially of Möhler, had positive influences on the work of Matthias Joseph Scheeben (1835-1888), author of the famous work *Die Mysterien des Christentums* (1865), especially with regard to the importance given to the use of exegetical and patristic research in the elaboration of dogmatic theology; Moreover, the reflections of the Tübingen professors were at the basis of the theological movement that led to Pius XII's encyclical *Mystici Corporis* of June 29, 1943.

More generally, a relevant contribution of Möhler to theology is undoubtedly the importance given to the relationship between faith and history, expressed by him through the metaphor whereby they are in a reciprocal relationship similar to that of the soul with the body.

**7. Roman School**

1. General Considerations. This term refers to a group of nineteenth-century Jesuit theologians who were professors at the Roman College. The most important are the Piedmontese Giovanni Perrone (1794-1876), considered the founder of the school; the German Joseph Kleutgen (1811-1883), from Dortmund; Carlo Passaglia, from Tuscany (1812-1887); the South Tyrolean Johann Baptist Franzelin (1816-1886); Clemens Schrader (1820-1875), from Hannover, the true initiator of the school together with Passaglia, but who later moved to teach in Vienna and then in France. Their activity spanned roughly between 1830 and 1879, the year from which, with *Aeterni Patris,* neo-Thomism imposed itself on the Catholic theological world. During the pontificate of Leo XIII, the legacy of the school was in some way taken over by a group of Church historians, such as Theodor Granderath (1839-1902), Friedrich Heinrich Denifle (1844-1905) and Franz Ehrle (1845-1934). In a broader sense, if alumni are also considered, the names of Henry Edward Manning (1808-1892), Heinrich Denzinger (1819-1883) could be added to the list of school members, Franz Hettinger (1819-1890), Joseph Hergenröther (1824-1890), Hugo von Hurter (1832-1914), Matthias Joseph Scheeben (1835-1888), Konstantin Gutberlet (1837-1928), Carlo Maria Jovene (1840-1887?), Ottokár Prohászka (1858-1927).

In 1814 Pius VII had restored the Society of Jesus, which had been suppressed in 1773 by Clement XIV, under pressure and threats from the governments of the Bourbon monarchies, at the time proponents of an exasperated royalist policy; in 1824, ten years after the reconstitution of the order, Leo XII returned the Roman College to the Jesuits; in this environment arose the Roman School, which became with time one of the main instruments of the Holy See for the preparation of magisterial texts of the period of Pius IX: in fact, the professors of the school collaborated in the preparation of the *Ineffabilis Deus* (1854), the *Syllabus* (1864) and the outlines of the First Vatican Council (1869-1870). A common characteristic of the scholars of the school was the attention given to history and positive theology, in the wake of the great French Jesuit theologian Denys Petau (1583-1652), the Oratorian Louis de Thomassin d'Eynac (1619-1695) and Johann Adam Möhler (1796-1838), the greatest exponent of the school of Tübingen: it is therefore a question of a theology in some respects different from the scholastic-Thomist one, which in the Society of Jesus was represented at the time by Luigi Taparelli D'Azeglio (1793-1862). It should be kept in mind, however, that the term "school" is used here in a broad sense, since it is not possible to find a theological method common to all the professors, with the possible exception of Passaglia and Schrader.

2. The main theologians. Perrone is considered to be the founder of the school. In 1824 he began teaching dogmatic theology at the Roman College; in 1848, because of the establishment of the Roman Republic, he had to go to Wales, but three years later he returned to Rome and held the positions of Rector and Prefect of Studies at the Roman College until his death. He was among the theologians appointed by the Roman Curia to work on the definition of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception and the preparation of the First Vatican Council. Although very learned, his work sometimes lacks originality; Perrone does, however, have the merit of having started the school. He is in many ways indebted to Möhler of *Symbolik*, with its strong Christological emphasis: the Piedmontese Jesuit emphasizes the continuity between Christ and the Church, which is understood as a perpetual manifestation of the Incarnate Word; it is a moral person endowed with a missionary task and witness; the Church itself is also presented as the rule of faith, as *Ecclesia docens*, and the pope is its infallible head (it should be remembered that Perrone had participated in the work of the First Vatican Council).

Passaglia is a leading figure, from an intellectual point of view, of the Roman school: he also taught dogmatics at the Roman College (starting in 1845), and had to leave Rome between 1848 and 1850 (he lived in England and Belgium); he worked on the preparation of the proclamation of the dogma of the Immaculate Conception. Because of disagreements with his superiors, he left the Society in 1859; a few years later, because of his favorable attitude to the Italian Risorgimento, he ran into ecclesiastical censure; he then became a member of the Italian Parliament and a university professor in Turin; he reconciled with the Church shortly before his death. He was in many ways indebted, in his theological work, to Johann Sebastian Drey (1777-1853), founder of the school of Tübingen, especially in the field of the use of ancient sources of patristics. He wrote a *De Ecclesia Christi commentariorum libri quinque*, which remained unfinished (he wrote only three books: 1. *Ecclesiae hypotyposis*; 2. *De Ecclesiae existentia*; 3. *De Ecclesiae caussis*): it deals among other things with the images of the Church (kingdom, city, house, body, temple, bride, etc.), as well as six causes of it (*efficiens*, *instrumentalis*, *exemplaris*, *materialis*, *formalis*, *finalis*). For Passaglia the Church is the mystical body of Christ: in it man is reconciled and led back to the Father; it is the continuation of the historical presence of Christ himself, and it is vivified by the Holy Spirit; the divine-human reality of the Church is expressed by Passaglia through the two images of the body of Christ and the bride of Christ. Since Christ died for all men, all remotely constitute the Church, but only those who are chosen through baptism to be instruments of his presence in the world are currently part of it; all the baptized participate in the royal, priestly and prophetic mission of Christ; the ministerial priesthood, exercised in the *tria munera*, is the instrument with which Christ communicates himself in the Church; the Petrine primacy, which continues in the Roman pontificate, is a means of maintaining unity in the Church; moreover, the Church, insofar as divine, is infallible, and the pope is personally so (despite this conviction, Passaglia considered the definition of the dogma inappropriate). The Tuscan theologian, like the other exponents of the school, emphasizes the relationship between the Church and Christ, who is its head, bridegroom and food, but also attempts to explore pneumatological aspects; the Church, moreover, is described by him as an instrument of the Trinity in the world, as a people constituted for the salvation of humanity and creation (even if the term "people" applied to the Church is very rarely found in Passaglia, who prefers the word *coetus* instead).

Schrader - who, like Perrone, was one of the experts on the First Vatican Council - provides an ecclesiological treatment similar to that of Passaglia, but he emphasizes much more strongly the centrality of the role of the pontiff in the government of the Church.

Franzelin belongs to the "second generation" of the school: he began teaching at the Roman College in 1850, first in Oriental languages, then in dogmatic theology (since 1857); he worked as an expert in the preparation of the schema *de doctrina catholica contra multiplices errores ex rationalismo derivatos* at Vatican I: in the conciliar debate this project raised the criticism of many fathers and was questioned from its very foundations; the following works led to the drafting of the dogmatic constitution *Dei Filius.* In 1876 he was created cardinal by Pius IX. From the theological point of view, Franzelin is in some ways quite eclectic, as he follows different systems, depending on the topics covered. For the South Tyrolean theologian, the incarnation, the summit of revelation, is prolonged in the Church which is, in history, a *real magnum commentarium* of the Word of God.

In general, it can be said that the ecclesiology of the theologians of the Roman school represents a significant effort to overcome a merely hierarchical-institutional vision, with ample emphasis given to the idea of the Church as the body of Christ and to the action of the Holy Spirit who prolongs the presence of Christ in the Mystical Body.

In this sense, Matthias Joseph Scheeben, author of the famous works *Die Mysterien des Christentums* (1865) and *Handbuch der katholischen Dogmatik* (1873-1887, unfinished) is also indebted to the Roman school; although not belonging to this school, he in fact studied at the Roman College between 1852 and 1859, and was certainly influenced by the experiences of this period in his theological thought. In his ecclesiology Scheeben emphasizes with great force that the Church is to be placed together with the great supernatural mysteries of the Trinity and the Incarnation, and its reality is to be framed within God's assumption of bodily realities in the economy of salvation, in the link that the Incarnation establishes between the visible and the invisible, the spiritual and the material. To this end, Scheeben develops a theory of the sacramental character as *sacramentum et res* that makes man capable of participating in Christ's actions. The German theologian does not neglect pneumatology in his ecclesiological theory: in fact, in order to play this role of continuity with the incarnation, the Church must be vivified by the Holy Spirit. Finally, Scheeben's ecclesiology also includes reflections on the Church as immaculate bride and fruitful mother, as well as on the primacy and important role of the Roman pontificate.

8. **Vatican Council I** (1869-1870): defines dogmas of faith: 1) the possibility of natural knowledge of God by man (dogmatic constitution *Dei Filius*), thus reaffirming the condemnation of Enlightenment and positivist rationalism and traditionalist fideism; and the infallibility of the pope (dogmatic constitution *Pastor aeternus*), thus giving the coup de grace to the various types of herastianism: Josephism, Febronianism, Gallicanism, royalism, jurisdictionalism. The council is interrupted by the invasion of the Papal States by the Italian army. After the council the schism of the Vetero-Catholics occurs, inspired by the German professor of church history Ignaz Döllinger: no bishop adheres to this schism. The Vetero-Catholics ask the schismatic Jansenists of Utrecht to ordain some of their priests as bishops.

9. Antonio **Rosmini** (1797-1855). Besides being a theologian, he is the founder of the Institute of Charity (Rosminiani). Born in Trentino, he studied theology in Padua. In 1848 he was charged by Carlo Alberto, King of Sardinia, with a diplomatic mission to Pius IX, which failed, and two of his books (including *The Five Wounds of the Church*) were placed on the Index. Pius IX, however, esteems and defends him. In the theological field, he looks for a connection between his contemporary philosophy (of which he shows the limits, especially of Kant) and the Augustinian and Thomist tradition. He looks for a philosophical way that gives reason of the existence of God and of the relationship with Him of the man, that can be a valid answer to the empiricist materialism and to the idealist pantheism; in such search he considers the scholasticism an outdated instrument. He therefore begins his journey from gnoseology: he believes that all our knowledge starts from the idea of being, which does not come from our experience, neither external nor internal, but is innate, placed in us by God. When Rosmini speaks of being, he is not thinking of God (real being), but of an ideal being, that is, abstract and indeterminate. Rosmini accepts Kant's gnoseology, but modifies it in a fundamental point: the a priori element of knowledge is not subjective (as in Kant), but objective, even if it does not come from experience (as the empiricists claimed): it is not subjective because it does not come from our intellect, because our real being is finite and therefore could not think of an infinite ideal being. Therefore the a priori element comes from a divine illumination. From this idea Rosmini tries to build a philosophical and theological system. For his anti-scholastic tendency, after his death, some of his propositions were condemned in the decree of the Holy Office *Post obitum*, in 1887. In 2001 he was rehabilitated, then beatified.

10. In this century there was also the great work of collection of sources carried out by Jacques-Paul **Migne** (1800-1875), who with a truly immense work published the *Patrologia Latina* and the *Patrologia Graeca*: these two collections are still unsurpassed in terms of completeness.

11. Matthias J. **Scheeben** (1835-1888). *The Mysteries of Christianity*. He studied at the Gregorian and had as teachers the main exponents of the Roman School, and then came into contact with the School of Tübingen: hence a great interest in the study of the Church Fathers. At the same time he always maintained a great respect and admiration for Thomas Aquinas and his work. He returned to Germany and was a professor at the Cologne Seminary for the rest of his life. Scheeben places at the center of his reflection the relationship between nature and grace, showing how the two are connected, but the second is clearly superior, distinct and transcendent of the first. His work had the merit of reproposing the category of "mystery" in theology. Moreover, it is an energetic attempt to give unity to theological knowledge: both by studying the intimate connection between the different mysteries and dogmas, and by trying to overcome - also on a theoretical level - the hiatus between mystical and speculative theology. The treatise *The Mysteries of Christianity* is divided into the following major chapters:

1. "Christianity as a mystery in general";
2. "the mystery of the Holy Trinity."
3. "the mystery of God in the original creation."
4. "the mystery of sin in general and original sin in particular."
5. "the mystery of the Man-God and His economy."
6. "the mystery of the Eucharist."
7. "the mystery of the Church and her sacraments."
8. "the mystery of Christian justification."
9. "the mystery of glorification and ultimate ends."
10. "the mystery of predestination";
11. "the science of the mysteries of Christianity or theology."

12. St. John Henry **Newman** (1801-1890): Anglican pastor, vicar of Oxford University parish; was a member of the Oxford Movement, a group of young Anglican clerics committed to reviving Anglicanism, which was in a state of spiritual decline. Converted to Catholicism in 1845, he was ordained a priest in 1847. He entered the Oratory of St. Philip Neri, and implanted that institution in England. Created cardinal by Leo XIII in 1879. In his conversion were very important historical studies on the Fathers of the Church, which convinced him that Catholicism was the form that brought back to the primitive Christian community, and not Anglicanism or Protestantism. His important work is *An Essay on the Development of Christian Doctrine*, in which he presents with great depth and acuity the tension between the perpetuity of dogma and its historical development. For Newman, tradition does not mean immobility, but harmonious development, which explains with increasing clarity and richness what is already in the content of the faith since the beginning of the Church. Canonized in 2019.

13. The ecclesiology of the Church as ***societas perfecta***.

14. **Neo-Thomism**: Leo XIII launches the neo-Thomistic project with the encyclical *Aeterni Patris* of 1879; with it, within the ecclesiology of the *societas perfecta*, he wishes to endow Catholicism with its own philosophical system, that of Thomas Aquinas. Creation of the Leonine Commission for the critical edition of the works of St. Thomas. *Post obitum* decree (1887) in which some propositions attributed to Antonio Rosmini are condemned (condemnation revoked in 2000). Neo-Thomism relaunched Catholic philosophy and theology, helped to strengthen the unity of the Church, especially in the clash with totalitarianism, consolidated the structure of *societas perfecta*; on the other hand, it has several limitations: isolation and abstraction from contemporary culture, excessive weight given to metaphysics and little attention to historical development. In addition to the resumption of Aquinas, this current led to a general resumption of scholasticism: some Jesuits actualized the theology of Francisco Suárez, the Franciscans that of Bonaventure of Bagnoregio and John Duns Scotus.

15. The work of Heinrich **Denzinger** (1819-1883): professor at Würzburg. In 1854 he published an ordered collection of the most important magisterial decisions: the *Enchiridion symbolorum, defintionum et declarationum de rebus fidei et morum, which was a* great success and has come down to our times, through the updates of Adolf Schönmetzer (1963) and Peter Hünermann (1991).

16. **The Social Doctrine of the Church**

17. **French Spiritualism**

It develops from the last works of François-Pierre **Maine de Biran** (1766-1824). The historian, archaeologist and philosopher Félix **Ravaisson-Mollien** (1813-1900) advocates spiritualism as an opposition to positivism. He again places freedom at the center over positivist determinism, and presents Christian spiritualism as an aristocratic and heroic philosophy, as opposed to the positivist philosophy that would instead be a plebeian philosophy.
