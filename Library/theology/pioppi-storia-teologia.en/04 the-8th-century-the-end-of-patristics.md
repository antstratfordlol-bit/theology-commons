## The 8th Century: The End of Patristics {#pioppi-storia-teologia.4}
**1. General Background**

Thanks to the successful evangelization carried out by the Benedictines in the 7th century, **England** became the **cultural and spiritual center of** Europe: Saint **Bede** the Venerable, the last father of the Western Church (†735); **Alcuin** of York (†804). **Heptarchy** system (seven kingdoms): three Saxon kingdoms (Essex, Wessex, Sussex), three Angles (Mercia, East Anglia, Northumberland), one Jute (Kent). From Christian England, **Saxon Benedictine missionaries** left for the continent: missions in Friesland and Saxony: Saint Willibrord (Friesland); **Saint Boniface** (who died a martyr in 755 along with 52 companions), apostle of Germany: foundation of the monastery of Fulda, heart of Catholic Germany. Examples of female missionary work: Lioba of Tauberbischofsheim (710-780), Thecla of Ochsenfurt (d. 790 ca.), and Saint Walpurga of Heidenheim (710-779). Reorganization of the Frankish Church by St. Boniface, before passing into Saxony. This is mostly a peaceful evangelization.

750: conversion of carantans (Slovenian)

Bulgarian invasion, pagans, in Eastern Europe and in the Balkans; new destructions, that are added to those operated by the Avars, people of Mongolian lineage that had its base in the Hungarian plain.

The **Arabs** were arrested in three battles: Constantinople (718), Poitiers (732), and Talas (751).

- ⇒ **continuous and systematic pillage of the** Christian **Mediterranean coasts** by Saracen pirates: abandonment of the coasts by Christians: thousands of Christians are taken as slaves to North Africa
- ⇒ the Arabs conquer the most culturally developed territories of the Mediterranean world (Antioch, Alexandria, Carthage, Visigothic Spain [St. Isidore of Seville is the most cultured man in the West in the 7th century]).

The temporal power of the popes, already born with Gregory the Great, is legally consolidated with the Donation of Sutri in 728 by the Lombard king Liutprand, and then with the pontificate of Adrian I.

The Iconoclastic Controversy

The Eastern Roman emperor **Leo III the Isauric** (717-741; victor over the Arabs in 718), in **726** promulgated a religious edict establishing the removal and destruction of sacred images (**iconoclasm**, from *eíkôn* = image and *klázein* = to break). There were two reasons for this: to conform Christianity to the theories of Paulicians, Jews and Muslims (who were also present in the empire), and to impose imperial power on the monks, who had a great religious influence on the population and were very devoted to sacred images. But there was another basic approach underlying the ideas of Leo III and his successors, as the great medievalist Claudio Leonardi well explains: "the iconoclastic position had its ultimate justification in the ideology according to which the signs of Christian power in history are in the hands of political power: it is the Christian emperor who realizes the historical visibility of Christ, not the Church, the saints and their images".

The iconoclastic policy, rejected by the West, was continued by the successors Constantine V (741-775) and Leo IV (775-780); iconoclasm was fought by the last father of the Eastern Church, Saint John Damascene, who was protected from imperial reprisals, since Damascus was now Arab territory. The crisis was closed by Empress **Irene**, who ascended to the throne in 780, and in **787** convened the **Second Council of Nicaea**, in which iconoclasm was rejected: the lawfulness of sacred images derives from the incarnation.

But **for 60 years, East and West had again been** religiously **separated**: a further wound to unity, also caused by the meddling of political power. So we can see how the final separation of 1054 was the coup de grace after a series of wounds inflicted on the unity between East and West. Rome, in this long history, always stands on a position of rejection of the encroachment of imperial caesaropapism; the same cannot be said of Constantinople.

The iconoclastic question has a tail in the West, since Charlemagne, irritated at not being made a party to the decisions of Nicaea, convenes a Council in Frankfurt in 794, which, partly due to deficient translations of the Greek text (especially of the term *timetiké proskynesis* applied by Nicaea to the images), rejects the Second Nicene, and would cause a separation with the East and a contrast with Rome, if it were not for the prudent and low-profile attitude of the Holy See, which opts not to react conspicuously and waits for the issue to blow over on its own.

From the Kingdom of the Franks to the Holy Roman Empire

In the Frankish Kingdom, the monarchs of the Merovingian dynasty (descendants of Clovis), no longer had any real power, while those who really ruled were the butlers (*maior domus*), so they were creating dynasties of butlers: Charles Martel, winner of the Arabs at Poitiers, ruled from 714 to 741. His son **Pippin the Short** decided to assume the crown directly; he therefore asked Pope Zachariah (741-752) if it was more just that the one who really governed should be king; the pope needed the stability of the Frankish kingdom, which had become the only Catholic state of a certain power (the Eastern Roman Empire was then in the hands of the iconoclastic emperors); therefore Zachariah gave his ideological support to the coup d'état of Pippin, who deposed the last Merovingian king and became king of the Franks in 751. In 754 and 756 the Franks carried out military campaigns against the Lombards who were besieging Rome. In 768 Pippin died and was succeeded by his son Charlemagne**.** This with a series of successful military campaigns, managed to reunite in the Frankish kingdom almost the entire Latin Christian world:

- 773-774: conquest of Italy
- 786-799: Conquest of Brittany
- 788: Conquest of Bavaria
- 789-812: campaigns against the Slavs (Wilzi, Sorbian, Czechs), who were made tributaries; the Frankish kingdom reached the Oder border.
- 791-796: destruction of the Kingdom of the Avars, a nomadic people of Mongolian descent who had their bases in Hungary.
- 795: Expedition to Spain and establishment of the *Marca Hispanica* (Catalonia).
- 772-804: expeditions for the conquest of Saxony**:** the conflict assumed at times religious connotations: destruction of *Irmensul*, the sacred oak of the Saxons by the Franks, and of many monasteries by the Saxons. In 785 the leader of the Saxon resistance, Vitichindo, submitted and received the baptism, having as godfather Charlemagne; the Saxon nobility was quickly integrated into the Kingdom of the Franks, so that the descendants of Vitichindo 150 years later would become emperors (Henry I of Saxony, elected in 919, by the Saxon and Frankish nobility). Peasant revolts continued until 804. In 802 Charlemagne promulgated the *Lex Saxonum,* which put the Saxons on an equal footing with the other inhabitants of the empire.

Charlemagne achieved the union of the whole Christian Latin-Germanic world(except the British Isles and Asturias), which corresponded to the whole of Western Christendom. This historical phenomenon culminated with the reconstitution of the Western Roman Empire, which took place in St. Peter's Basilica on Christmas night of 800, when Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne emperor; the Holy Roman Empirewas born, destined to last more than a thousand years, until 1806.

- Political, religious and cultural unity
- buildings in Aachen capital of the Empire
- Foundation of the Palatine School, which brings together the most famous men of culture of the time, from all over Europe: Alcuin of York, Paul Deacon (†799, Lombard), Peter of Pisa (†799, great grammarian), Paulinus of Aquileia (†802, patriarch of Aquileia), Theodolphus of Orleans (†821, Visigoth, excellent Latin poet, bishop of Orleans), Einhard of Selingenstadt (Frank, biographer of Charlemagne [*Vita Karoli*], layman, widowed, was lay abbot of several abbeys).
- emendation of the Bible
- Carolingian script: end of the national writing particularism, which had prevailed in the 7th-8th centuries; the most important scripts are: Merovingian, insular, Visigothic and Benevento.

**2. John Damascene (ca. 660-750)**

He is the last father of the Eastern Church. He was born in Damascus into a wealthy and noble Christian family. His father was a kind of minister (more precisely, he was the collector of the taxes paid by the Christian subjects to the administration of the caliph); John Damascene also held this position for some time, until he experienced a strong conversion that pushed him to monastic life, in the Palestinian monastery of San Saba. In 725 he was also ordained a priest.

His major works are:

1. *Apologetic speeches* against *those who reject the sacred images*, against the iconoclasts
2. The *Source of Knowledge* (*Peghé gnóseos*), systematic exposition of the Christian faith: this work "is not an original creation, but a compendium and an arrangement, marked by scholasticism, of the theories of the Greek fathers on the truths of faith" (M. Grabmann). "Those who are familiar with the history of the doctrinal development of the ancient Church learn nothing new in it, but must greet its work as a foothold or a kind of observatory from which one can survey the territory traversed and have, finally, an overall impression of what has been achieved by the ancient Greek Church, of what constitutes its heritage" (Kattenbusch).

It is divided into three parts:

1. *Kephálaia philosophiká*: philosophical introduction, especially dialectical and metaphysical, with strong use of Aristotelianism (but also Porphyry and Ammonius). There he states at the beginning that the profane sciences are handmaidens and theology Mrs.
2. History of Heresies: masterfully and clearly traces the heresies and the councils that condemned them
3. *De fide orthodoxa* (*Ékdosis akribés tés orthodóxou písteos*): was divided into four parts by the medieval Latins
4. Treatise on God
5. Treatise on creation, world and providence, theological anthropology
6. Incarnation of the Word, Christology
7. Soteriology, Baptism and Eucharist, worship of saints, relics and images (against iconoclasm), origin of evil, eschatology

In it, of particular interest are the proofs of the existence of God, starting from the becoming of things, which supposes something stable, of the conservation and unity of the universe, of the order of the world. At the same time, in the wake of Pseudo-Dionysius, also brilliantly exposes the negative theology, emphasizing the incomprehensibility of God.

On the Trinity, there is an excellent treatment of the processions, even if, obviously, the *Filioque is* excluded. The ontological priority of the Father is affirmed, without detriment to the absolute identity of being and dignity of the other Persons with respect to the Father.

On anthropology, interesting is the theory of man as the image and likeness of God: image in nature, likeness in grace; this interpretation will have great fortune in the Middle Ages.

The *De fide orthodoxa*, translated into Latin after 1146 by Burgundio Pisano by order of Pope Eugene IV, will have a great influence in scholasticism (e.g.: it is quoted more than 300 times by Bonaventure of Bagnoregio), both for its work of systematization, and for the substantial use of Greek philosophy, especially Aristotelian. Above all, he was important for the transmission to Western scholasticism of the patristic doctrines on the Trinity and the Incarnation. For this reason St. John Damascene is sometimes called "the St. Thomas of the East," and is considered the first scholastic.

III. The *Homilies*, of a more oriental and mystical style. Worthy of mention is the Homily on the Transfiguration, in which he shows how man is called to integral deification through participation in the transfigured Christ, which is realized above all in the liturgical experience. He also wrote numerous Mariological homilies.

A scholar of music, Damascene is also prominent in Eastern Rite liturgical singing.

In 1890, Leo XIII proclaimed him a Doctor of the Church.

**3. Bede the Venerable (672-735)**

He is the last father of the Western Church, called the *doctor admirabilis*. A monk and presbyter, he lived in the twin monasteries of Wearmouth and Jarrow. He is a man of encyclopedic culture, the most learned man of his time, and is also one of the masters of medieval culture (Dante Alighieri places him in the *Divine Comedy*, in Canto X of Paradise, along with Isidore of Seville). His most important works are:

- Biblical commentaries: especially *In Genesim*; *Psalms*; *Synoptics and Acts*
- *Collectio in Apostolum*: a collection of places of Augustine on the letters of St. Paul
- *De schematibus et tropis*, on the biblical interpretation
- *De tabernaculo et vasis eius*, in which he clearly fixes the four senses of Scripture:

1. Historical-Literary
2. Allegorical
3. Moral
4. Anagogical

This is a quadripartition that will have great fortune in the Middle Ages and after (remember the medieval couplet already in use in the 12th cent:

"*Littera gesta docet*

*quod credas allegoria*

*moralis quid agas*

*quo tendas anagogia*")

- *Aliquot quaestionum liber*: in which he shows himself as a precursor of the scholastic method
- *Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum*, from Julius Caesar to 731
- Life of Saint Cuthbert
- *Historia sanctorum abbatum monasterii in Wiremutha et Gyrvum* (Wearmouth and Jarrow)
- *De rerum natura*, a sort of encyclopedia of historical, geographical and scientific knowledge
- *De orthographia*
- *De metrica arte*
- *De temporum ratione*

In dealing with the Church, he distinguishes - following Gregory the Great - between the broader concept of the *Ecclesia universalis*, which includes all the righteous beginning with Abel and even the angels, and the more limited concept of the *Ecclesia electorum*, formed by the Catholic saints, that is, the Christians worthy of that title.

Furthermore, he broadly presents the Church as the New Eve, born from the side of Christ the New Adam; and continuing the Christ-Adamo parallelism, he affirms that the Church is - like Eve the bride of Adam - the bride of Christ; if she is the bride of Christ, she is *a dear one* with Christ, and therefore is the Body of Christ. From the concept of bride, he moves on to that of mother: the Church is the mother of believers, who calls and welcomes her misguided children (sinners) with penance, and generates other children, through the preaching of the faith and the expansion of Christianity.

Concerning the pope, he is very cautious about the Roman primacy, and thinks that Peter's privileges were granted to all the apostles: "quod enim Petro dictum est, omnibus utique dictum est; hoc namque erant caeteri apostoli quod fuit Petrus."

**4. Alcuin of York (730ca.-804)**

Alchvin of Northumberland, director of the cathedral school of York (founded by Egbert, a disciple of Bede the Venerable), was called to Aachen by Charlemagne in 781 to work in the Palatine School (which he then directed) where he organized studies according to Cassiodorus' division of the *trivium* and *quadrivium.* In 796 he became abbot of St. Martin of Tours, which had a very organized *scriptorium*, and established a very flourishing school in that monastery. He is the great inspirer of religious studies and restorer of clerical culture in the Carolingian Revival.

Major works:

- *De fide sanctae at individuae Trinitatis libri tres*: deals with the foundations of the Christian faith in close dependence on the works of Augustine and Boethius on the Trinity:
- Book I: Trinity
- Book II: Creation
- Book III: Incarnation and Eschatology
- *Carolingian books*, on the question of images
- *De animae ratione ad Eulaliam*, work of psychology
- Various treatises on liturgy, which were the basis for the Carolingian liturgical reform.

He intervened in the question of images (Council of Frankfurt in 794) and in that of Spanish adoptionism.

In the *Letters,* Alcuin also develops the whole ideology of the Christian sovereign: he is invested with an important part of the prerogatives of the clergy: he is the *rex praedicator, rector, doctor et pastor*, with the task of leading the flock of the faithful towards eternal salvation, fighting against the Antichrist.

In the Palatine School there is also the Lombard **Paolo Diacono**, author of the *Historia Langobardorum*. He also writes theological works; among other things, in the ecclesiological field, he tends to reduce the primacy of the pope: in fact, he believes that the priestly power, granted to Peter in a particular way, is however common to all the apostles and their successors.

**5. Ambrose Autperto**

Abbot Campano, in a *Commentary on the Apocalypse*, makes an interesting development of the parallelism Mary-Church: Mary is the type of the Church, which is the exemplar, based on the common figure of New Eve; Autpertus, however, goes further, moving from comparison to mediation: as he argues that Mary for that title is also important mediator in the Church.

**6. Spanish Adoptionism**

The question arises from the preaching in Arab Spain of Migezio, who maintains the strange theory that all three persons of the Trinity are incarnate: the Father in David, the Son in Jesus Christ and the Holy Spirit in Saint Paul. Bishop Elipando of Toledo reacts against him, but in refuting him he also commits a theological error, claiming that Christ as a man is the adopted son of the Father, and not a natural son; he claims that the Word made himself adopted, stripping himself of his divinity at the moment of the incarnation, and filling himself with grace; this he would have done in order to save us: in fact, man would be saved by making himself similar to God in his human nature to the extent that he is adopted through grace; Christ would therefore work this in himself. The question inflames the ecclesial environment of the peninsula, with St **Beatus of Liébana**, in the Christian area, who refutes Elipandus, in the work *De Christo et eius corpore, quod est Ecclesia, et de diabolo et eius corpore, quod est Antichristus*. In this controversy emerges - for his adoptionist tendencies - also Felix of Urgel, a bishop of the *Marca Hispanica*, then part of the Carolingian domain. Felix was called several times to exonerate or explain himself, in 792 in Regensburg, in 794 in Frankfurt, in Aachen in 799: on these occasions his theories were refuted by Alcuin. Felix of Urgel seems to have abjured his errors and was sent into exile in Lyon, where he died in 818.
