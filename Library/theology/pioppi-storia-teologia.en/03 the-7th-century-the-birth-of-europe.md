## The 7th Century: The Birth of Europe {#pioppi-storia-teologia.3}
1. General Background

The birth of **Islam**. Starting from 622, in Arabia, the religious activity of the “prophet” Muhammad acquired more and more political connotations; he became a political and military leader and from Medina he imposed his rule on the whole of Arabia.

632: death of Muhammad, who had reunited the tribes of the Arabian Peninsula under a single religious and political authority; immediately afterwards the Arab expansion began, favored by the fact that the two neighboring empires, the Eastern Roman Empire and the Sasanian Persian Empire, had exhausted their energies in continuous wars between them. Conquest of Syria (635), Palestine (638) following the Battle of Yarmuk (636), Persia (636-642), Egypt (642), Afghanistan (670-680), Carthage (698), Samarkand (712). In 711 the Arabs landed in Spain and reached the banks of the Indus. Their expansion was arrested in the first half of the 8th century, in three major battles: they were in fact stopped in Constantinople by the Byzantines in 718, Poitiers by the Franks in 732, Talas by the Chinese in 751.

The **Arab invasion broke the unity of the Mediterranean world**; this sea, which until then had been an element of union for the peoples who lived on its shores (*Mare Nostrum*, it was called at the time of the Roman Empire), became the place and the way of diffusion of Arab piracy (Saracens); they quickly came to dominate the sea, while European seafaring declined completely. In the Mediterranean, until then the center of a single civilization, **three separate worlds** were created **that communicated very little between them: Europe, Byzantium, Islam**.

The Mediterranean coasts of Christian Europe are continually plundered by the Arabs; total decadence of trade and cities; the process of barbarization of Europe progresses with great rapidity.

**The axis of the Christian world shifts northward**: from the Mediterranean to continental Europe. **Europe was born**, a world forged by **three elements: the Catholic religion in its Latin version, Roman culture, and the young Germanic and Slavic peoples**.

In this nascent Europe the process of barbarization of society continued: decadence of culture, political structure, economy, trade; this situation was caused by the young but savage peoples who invaded the Empire; the Church succeeded with a patient and slow work of Christianization, to civilize this new world that was being born.

In the 7th century, Ireland was an exception to the situation of regression of civilization, due to its marginal geographic position: great development of cultural and religious life on the island. Many Irish missionary monks left for Scotland and the continent, promoting a great work of evangelization: Saint Columba (or Columcille) in Scotland; Saint Columbanus in France and Italy; Saint Gallus in Switzerland. Ireland is called the Island of the Saints. Great production of codes in insular writing. The Irish maintain the purity of Latin, being of Celtic language and therefore not subject to the process of vulgarization of the idiom, as happened in Italy, Gaul, Iberian Peninsula. They also spread the separation of words in Latin writing.

The Church reacts to this dramatic situation by trying to **convert the invading peoples**:

- 587: conversion of Visigoths from Arianism to Catholicism
- 596: Beginning of the mission of St. Augustine of Canterbury in England, which will quickly lead to the conversion of Angles, Saxons and Jutes, until then pagans.
- Under Emperor Heraclius (610-641), beginning of the conversion of the Serbs
- 670-680: conversion of the Croats
- 698: Conversion of the Lombards from Arianism to Catholicism

Often it was a matter of **mass conversions,** which would have required a long work of generations to eradicate non-Christian uses and customs; however, the Church was the great civilizer of this era, in the midst of difficulties of all kinds (just think that illiteracy was widespread, even among kings: e.g., Charlemagne learned to read at an advanced age and was never able to write). **Great success of the conversion of England, not only from the religious point of view, but also from the cultural one;** the island will become in the 8th century the center of European culture (Saint Bede the Venerable, Alcuin of York).

It is interesting to note that these missionary activities are generally entirely peaceful, not accompanied by military conquest, indeed they are directed toward peoples militarily stronger than Catholics.

2. The Church

- In Italy the threat of Lombard invasion looms over the papacy.

**Birth of the temporal power of the popes**: de facto, not de jure, the origin of the Papal States must be placed in the pontificate of **Gregory I the Great** (590-604). The cause was not a political project of power on the part of the pontiffs, but the need of the population to have a point of reference. In fact, the situation in Italy was truly dramatic: the peninsula was divided into two parts, one invaded by the Lombard barbarians, who brought destruction and oppression everywhere, the other held by the Italics and governed by Byzantium. But the power of the Roman emperors of the East was more and more theoretical: they were very busy in the wars in Asia, first against the Persians, then against the Arabs, in a strategically more important area than Italy, so the Italian lands remained in the empire had to provide for themselves: and in fact a series of political entities were born that, although they recognized the Byzantine supremacy, were in fact independent: the maritime republics of Venice, Gaeta, Naples, Amalfi, born on the power vacuum created by the imperial disengagement. In Rome, faced with this power vacuum, the population turned to the only authority that in the chaos of the time had remained steadfast: the papacy; and so Gregory I, who as a layman had been a high imperial official, had to shoulder the burden of running the government, administration and defense of the central part of Italy.

- In the Kingdom of the Franks we find an almost total barbarization, which is witnessed by Gregory of Tours with his *Historia Francorum*.
- In Ireland and Visigothic Spain the Church is living in these quiet times.
- In the East we find the monoenergist and monothelite controversies.

3. Maximus the Confessor (580-662)

According to Greek tradition, he was born in Constantinople into a wealthy family; from 610 to 613 he was secretary to the emperor. After that he experiences a conversion that leads him to undertake the monastic life. In the Syriac tradition he was born in Palestine. The two versions converge into one from 626, when he moved to the Province of Africa. In 645, in Carthage, he distinguished himself in the fight against monothelism; he participated in the Lateran Council of 649, which condemned monothelism. For this reason, the emperor sends him into exile on the Black Sea. After six years of exile, he is tried and his tongue is cut off and his right hand is severed. Exiled in Lazika, in the Caucasus, he dies there not long after.

Monothelitism arose from another strong state intervention in the religious sphere, along the lines of the *Henótikon* and the Three Chapters. Emperor **Heraclius** (610-641, famous for the war against the Persians and the recovery of the relic of the Holy Cross, linked to the liturgical feast of September 14) promulgated the ***Ekthésis* (638)**, a law that decreed the affirmation of monoenergism (in Christ there is only one way of acting of the will) and **monothelism** (in Christ there is only one will); at the origin of this act there is the usual imperial attempt to resolve the disagreement between Catholics and Monophysites on a position of compromise, to strengthen imperial unity and to curb the centrifugal tendencies of Syrians and Egyptians, Monophysites.

Constant II (641-668) also operated a totally caesaropapist policy in a monothelite function, persecuting the Catholics (as can be seen in the same personal history of Maximus the Confessor) and going so far as to capture Pope Martin I, who was taken to Constantinople and tried there, without regard for his precarious state of health, amid humiliations and mistreatment of all kinds, which quickly led to his death (649).

The problem of monothelism was to a great extent political, as can be appreciated from the way it ended: once the Byzantine emperors had completely lost the monophysite regions of Syria and Egypt, conquered by the Arabs, they lost all interest in monothelism, which was then condemned at the **Third Council of Constantinople (680-681)**. It is however the second episode of separation between the Latin and Greek Churches, a second step towards the schism of 1054. Also in the Arab conquest of Egypt we note the negative influence of politics on the Catholic faith; in fact this conquest was favored by the help of the Coptic Church, lent out of impatience and rancor against the Greek domination of the Eastern Roman Empire.

About 90 works of Massimo's have reached us, among which we remember

- the *Quaestiones ad Talassum*,
- i *Capita gnostica*,
- i *D [500] capita theologica*
- the *Liber asceticus*,
- the *Ambigua*;

Christology is found primarily in:

- *Discussion with Pirro*,
- *Theological pamphlets*,
- *Letters*.

Maximus presents a cosmology that sees a divided world, and it is up to man to bring about the union of the whole cosmos with God; since he was unable to do so because of his fall, the incarnation occurred. His theology, based on the Aeropagite and Neoplatonism, develops according to the *exitus-reditus* model.

The doctrine on God is inspired not a little by Pseudo-Dionysius: God is so immanent in the world as to become almost "transparent" to the eyes of human intelligence; but at the same time God is completely hidden in the darkness of his absolute otherness. His essence is absolutely inaccessible to creatures. Maximus the Confessor is important for the transmission of the Areopagite to the Latin Middle Ages, through his *Scolia,* which were translated into Latin.

Anthropology: man, central element of the cosmos: the meaning and value of his existence is presented with the triad "being, well-being, eternity"; starting from being, man with his good moral life can achieve eternal life: the first and the third are a gift of God; but the second, which leads to the third, also depends on human freedom.

From here we move on to Christology: for the Confessor, the incarnate Logos is the one who brings all modes of being and all essences back to unity.

Maximus also rejects monothelism with the following argument:

- in Christ there are two wills: divine and human
- the human will has two aspects: natural will and rational will
- Christ has only the natural one, that is, the one naturally oriented to the good
- The rational one requires deliberation and has as its starting point ignorance, which in Christ cannot be.

4. John Climacus (d. 650ca.)

A monk in Raithu on the Sinai Peninsula, he became abbot of the monastery.

His name is linked to his main work, the *Scala paradisi* (in Greek *Klimax tou paradeisou*), a work of asceticism and mysticism for monks that had a great diffusion in the Byzantine world and then also in the West. This work is divided into 30 chapters, 23 on the vices to flee, seven on the virtues. Points of arrival of the spiritual work of the monk must be *hesychía* (physical and spiritual = quietness of feelings and mind), and *apátheia* (absence of passions and purity of soul). Moreover Climacus has in this work beautiful passages on prayer.

5. Isidore of Seville (570-636)

Bishop of Seville, he presided over the important Council IV of Toledo in 633.

*He* is the encyclopedia of the Middle Ages.

Works:

- *Ethymologiae*: 20 books: 1. Grammar; 2. Rhetoric and Dialectic; 3. Quadrivium; 4. Medicine; 5. Laws and History; 6. Liturgy; 7. God, angels and the faithful; 8. Church and heretical sects; 9. People; 10. Words; 11. Men and monsters; 12. Animals; 13. Cosmology; 14. Physical geography; 15. Human geography; 16. Metallurgy; 17. Agriculture; 18. War and games; 19. Ships, houses, clothing; 20. Household objects
- Philosophical works:
- *De natura rerum*
- *De ordine creaturarum*
- Prosopographical works:
- *De ortu et obitu patrum* and
- *Names of the Law and the Gospel*: biographies of biblical characters
- *De viris inlustribus*: biographies of 33 illustrious Christians (of whom 12 were Spanish)
- Historical Works:
- *History of Goths, Vandals and Swabians*
- *Chronica* (from Adam to 615)
- Controversial works:
- *De haeresibus liber*
- *De fide catholica adversus Judaeos* (theological controversy: the Catholic faith has supplanted Judaism)
- *Sententiarum libri tres*: an organic collection of sentences of the fathers; for Isidore, a sentence should contain a synthetic expression of wisdom and an incentive to behave well. Very used sources are Augustine and Gregory the Great. The work was also called *De summo bono*, for its incipit "Summum bonum Deus est".
- Book I: Dogmatic Foundations:
- God and creation
- Evil and angels
- Church and Heresies
- Bible and Symbol
- Prayer
- Sacraments and New Testaments
- Book II: Natural Ascetics: Virtues and Vices
- Book III: Christian Ascetics:
- Chastisements of God
- Temptations
- Prayer
- Active and contemplative life
- Monasticism and Priesthood

In his works we find the definition of the Catholic Church: *ecclesia* = *convocatio*, "propter quod omnes ad se vocat"; *ecclesia* is different from *synagogue*: "congregari et pecora solent, convocari autem magis est utentium ratione, sicut sunt homines". *Catholica* = *universalis*, "apó tou kath'holou, id est secundum totum". Isidore strongly emphasizes the value of universality in his ecclesiology.

In the *Sententiae* and *Ethymologiae*, Isidore also presents the ideal of the Christian prince: "reges a recte agendo vocati sunt, ideoque recte faciendo regis nomen tenetur, peccando amittitur".

Finally, he closely connects the Church and the Eucharist: it is this sacrament that makes the Church, because it is priestly in all its parts.

6. Irish Ducts

Cummean († 662)

- *Epistula de Controversia Pascuali*: on the question of the date of Easter, which opposed the Irish Church to the Roman one
- *Commentarium in Evangelium secundum Marcum*
- *De figuris apostolorum*: biographies of Christ, of the apostles, of some characters of the Old Testament
- *Poenitentiale Cummeani*: this is one of the earliest examples of typical penitentials. These works "originated in Irish monasteries, as frequent confession, even of minor sins, became widespread in those environments. In earlier times, those of solemn penance, the sacrament was administered less frequently and involved a liturgical solemnity that gradually disappeared. In the monasteries of the islands, confession and penance were used as an instrument of personal perfection for those who wanted to follow Christ through that particular vocation; this system was also applied to the laity with the necessary adaptations" (A. Chacón). The Cummean Penitential was widely used throughout Western Europe. Used by confessors to assign appropriate penances for sins committed by individuals. It proceeds in a systematic order:
- Pride
- Gula
- Fornicatio
- Avaritia
- Ira
- Acedia
- Tristitia
- Vain glory
- Venial sins
- Novice deficiencies
- Deficiencies in the celebration of the Mass

But this penitential is not merely a fee schedule: Cummean also gives indications for assessing the subjective conditions of the penitent as well, as can be seen from this passage of his: "this must be observed in every penance: the length of time spent in a state of sin, what teachings the penitent has received, what passionate elements have moved the sinner, what is the extent of his [inner] strength, what is the intensity of his desire for forgiveness, and by what outward force he has been transported to sin."

Adamnanus, abbot of Iona: 624-704

- *De locis sanctis*: journey to the Holy Land
- *Vita Columbae* [Irish saint].
- *Scholia in Virgil*
