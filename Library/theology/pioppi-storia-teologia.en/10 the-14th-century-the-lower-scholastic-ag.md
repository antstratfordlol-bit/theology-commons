## The 14th Century: The Lower Scholastic Age {#pioppi-storia-teologia.10}
**1. General Ideas**

It is the century in which the transition between the Middle Ages and the Modern Age begins: it is characterized by the decadence of the ideas and institutions typical of the apogee of medieval civilization (11th-13th centuries). It is a brilliant civilization, but in the midst of a process of cultural and political change, among other things marked by the prodromes of the movement of secularization of society.

**The idea of empire falls:** the emperor Charles IV, of the dynasty of Luxembourg, renounces Italy, and with the Golden Bull of 1356 reforms the system of election of theemperor. The pope is excluded, while seven great electors are established: the Margrave of Brandenburg, the Count Palatine of the Rhine, the King of Bohemia, the Duke of Saxony, the Archbishops of Trier, Cologne and Mainz. The Holy Roman Empire (800-1806) is transformed into a de facto German state, losing its universalist aspiration.

At the same time national states begin to develop, especially France, during the reign of Philip IV the Fair (1285-1314): destruction of the Templar Order; struggle with Boniface VIII (⇒birth of ecclesiology with theologians Aegidius Romanus, James of Viterbo and Juan de Torquemada: for the first time the Church finds itself in struggle with something other than itself, the national state, and for this reason begins to reflect on itself). In the Hundred Years' War (1339-1453) against the English the spirit of the nation is forged; in this conflict there is the episode of Saint Joan of Arc. England also becomes structured as a nation.

Italy, on the other hand, while still being the dominant nation from an economic and cultural point of view, and in part also political, is organized into powerful regional states: Savoy, Genoa, Milan, Venice, Ferrara, Florence, Siena, Naples.

**Decadence of the papacy: the papacy** suffers a great crisis; it is defeated in the fight with Philip the Fair, king of France, and this contrast ends with the election of a pope from southern France, and with the temporary residence of the pope in the French *Midi*, then in Avignon, a city that, with its surroundings (the Venassian countryside) becomes papal territory (it will be until 1792). We quote some thoughts of the historian Diego Quaglioni on these events: "In the clash with the monarchy of France, creature of the Church, linked to it by a thousand spiritual and temporal bonds, the medieval papacy consumed its most serious crisis: the controversy born of the contrast between the new political forces of the century and the theocratic tradition, which from the bitter jurisdictional contrast between Boniface VIII and Philip IV began, accelerated the dissolution of an entire world of political-religious values, enclosing the germs of an age of schism and conflict among the European states and within the Church itself. The universal power of the Roman papacy was reduced; Boniface's plan to reaffirm the *papal plenitudo potestatis was* shattered against a new, revolutionary force, the sovereign state, which could no longer be denied and subdued, but rather would become the protagonist of history to come.

"Avignon Captivity": 1305-1377. The popes are all French; the curia and the college of cardinals become almost completely Frenchified; this is not well seen in other countries, especially in England, engaged in the Hundred Years' War against France. In 1377, thanks to the work of St. Catherine of Siena and St. Bridget of Sweden, the pope returns to Rome, but the following year begins the Great Western Schism by the French cardinals: we have two popes and Europe is divided into two obediences, the Roman and Avignon. This is a singular schism, in which the consciousness of the unity of the Catholic Church remains firm, but it is not possible to understand who is the legitimate pope: therefore we find saints of Avignon obedience (St. Vincent Ferrer) and others of Roman obedience (St. Catherine of Siena). The two popes make the pontifical taxation increase considerably, since it is necessary to maintain two curias and not one. All these historical vicissitudes led to a loss of prestige of the papacy in the Christian world, which will be one of the causes that will favor the development of the Protestant Reformation. On the other hand, the crisis showed the compactness of the Catholic world, which managed not to break up, but to overcome the difficult situation. Beginning with the Council of Vienne (1312), the need for reform of the Church *in capite et in membris* began to be discussed.

**Decadence of scholasticism:** in 1277 some propositions attributed to Thomas Aquinas are condemned by the bishop of Paris, Stephen Tempier. This condemnation contributes to set Aquinas aside and to the prevalence of the Franciscan school, which emphasized more love and therefore the will; in the great theologians such as St. Bonaventure or John Duns Scotus this tendency does not involve a serious problem, but in the 14th century this theology degenerated towards voluntarism and nominalism, especially with William of Ockham. There is also a great return of logic in theology, which made the scholastic works very obscure, difficult to understand, and distant, at times, from Holy Scripture. The criticism of the humanists and of Luther to the scholastic theology is actually directed towards the late scholasticism, which they studied, and not towards that of the thirteenth century. In any case, the harmonious relationship between faith and reason established in the apogee of the theology of the thirteenth century breaks down.

Here are some words of Pope Benedict XVI on this point (*Regensburg Speech*, 2006): "To be honest, one must note at this point that, in the late Middle Ages [...] in contrast with the so-called Augustinian and Thomist intellectualism, a voluntaristic approach began with Duns Scotus, which in the end led to the affirmation that we would know of God only the *voluntas ordinata*. Beyond this there would exist the freedom of God, by virtue of which He could have created and done the opposite of everything He actually did. Here there are positions that [...] could lead to the image of a God-Arbitrium, who is not even linked to truth and good. God's transcendence and diversity are accentuated in such an exaggerated way that even our reason, our sense of truth and goodness are no longer a true mirror of God, whose abysmal possibilities remain for us eternally unreachable and hidden behind his actual decisions. In contrast to this, the faith of the Church has always held to the conviction that between God and us, between his eternal Creator Spirit and our created reason, there exists a true analogy, in which certainly the dissimilarities are infinitely greater than the similarities, but not to the point of abolishing the analogy."

**Decadence of medieval cosmocentrism:** the attitude of medieval man is of substantial cosmocentrism, that is to say to feel a small part of the world and not its center; this also led to a clear perception of its limits and its condition of creature, but could also lead to a kind of fatalism. After the Great Plague of 1348-1350, which halved the population of Europe, there was a sense of recovery, a joy of living of the survivors, which contributed to the birth of humanism, an attitude that tended instead to a substantial anthropocentrism. Examples of this new sensibility are the two great Tuscan literati Francesco Petrarca and Giovanni Boccaccio.

**The idea of crusade declines:** in 1291 Acre falls, the last Christian fortress in Palestine. Resumes the Islamic advance with the Ottoman Turks: with them Islam will maintain the initiative on the Christian world until 1683 (battle of Kahlemberg, near Vienna). It also resumes the Muslim piracy in the Mediterranean. So we can appreciate the role played by the Crusades: a role of containment of Islamic attacks against Europe: during the centuries of the Crusades, Europeans manage to bring the war away from "home" and to limit the Saracen and Barbarian piracy.

**Decadence of chivalry:** medieval chivalry reached its peak from a social point of view, but from a military point of view, infantry began to become more and more important, thanks to the Italian companies of fortune (which were at the forefront of the art of warfare in this century), the Swiss infantry and the English archers.

**2. General characteristics of the theology of the fourteenth century**

1. Abandonment of metaphysics, both Aristotelian and Platonic in favor:
2. of nominalism: especially in France and England (Ockham)
3. of mysticism: especially in Germany and the Netherlands (Eckhart)
4. Birth of ecclesiological treatises, especially in Italy
5. Dissolution of universalism:
6. politician
7. cultural: philosophical and theological synthesis replaced by examination of individual problems
8. Logic prevails over metaphysics
9. Breaking the harmony between faith and reason

**Ecclesiology**

The first ecclesiological treatises arose as a result of the clash between the Church and the nascent modern state; it was a different struggle from that between papacy and empire (which can be considered an intra-ecclesial struggle); now the Church, for the first time since Charlemagne, faced an entity that stood as other than her, and claimed areas of total power, untethered from the ecclesiastical one. Until then Church and society, Church and state were confused. Now that a new power, the nascent nation-state, is opposed in otherness to the Church, the need arises to reflect on the Church itself, its powers, its nature. The first major clash of this kind occurred at the turn of the 13th and 14th centuries between Philip IV the Fair King of France and the Roman pontiff Boniface VIII.

In 1294 Pope Celestine V, a very pious and devout person but unable to bear the political weight of the papacy, resigned, realizing his inadequacy for the task. After a day-long conclave, Benedetto Caetani, from a noble Roman family, is elected, taking the name Boniface VIII. He is a very learned man, versed particularly in canon law (it was he who promulgated the famous canonical collection of *Liber Sextus*, in 1298), with a strong-willed character, but also very surly and unspiritual. In many aspects of ecclesiastical government, he was nevertheless a moderate pope with great practical sense. Unfortunately, this was not enough for him in his clash with the king of France.

In his pontificate, France began its path toward being structured into an absolute, centralized monarchy, which would culminate with Louis XIV: the king gradually sought to eliminate the power of the Church, then that of the nobility, in order to assert his own. It must be considered that medieval monarchs did not enjoy absolute power: they had strong limits in the Church, the nobility, city guilds, etc.

**First clash with France.** Philip IV the Fair, King of France, and Edward I, King of England, at war with each other, impose taxes on the clergy to finance the conflict. Boniface VIII responded with the bull *Clericis laicos* (Feb. 24, 1296), in which he threatened with excommunication clerics who paid taxes to kings without the consent of the Holy See and princes who demanded taxes from the clergy. The tone of the bull is polemical: "antiquity teaches that the laity have been hostile to ecclesiastics, and the experiences of the present time amply demonstrate this. We see the laity, not content with their own dominion, attempting to meddle with what is not permitted to them and to disregard the fact that they are interdicted all power over ecclesiastics, persons and Church property. On prelates and regular and secular ecclesiastics they impose heavy burdens [...] they endeavor in various ways to reduce ecclesiastics to servitude and to subject them to their dominion."

In retaliation, Philip IV, on August 17, 1296, blocked the flow of money from France to foreign countries (thus also to Rome), thus paralyzing the collection of ecclesiastical taxes throughout the kingdom. The pope responded with the bull *Ineffabilis amoris* of September 20, 1296, in which he reaffirmed his power as supreme judge of kings (recalling Philip's disputes with the emperor over Burgundy and with the king of England over Gascony) and admonished him not to restrict the freedom of the Church: attacks against this freedom are a sin for the pope to judge; on the other hand, the pope admitted that the clergy should help the king in case of danger, but with the pope's authorization. In short, a letter both of rebuke but also of openness to negotiations.

*Romana mater Ecclesia* Feb. 7, 1297: the pope backtracked from the *Clericis laicos,* and pointed out that the bull did not refer to taxes that the clergy had to pay for any feudal rights, and also allowed the French clergy to make "voluntary contributions" to the king, even "upon courteous and friendly invitation"; moreover, Boniface VIII in 1297 canonized Louis IX king of France, grandfather of Philip IV, and took many other distensive steps toward the king of France. Peace seems to have returned.

In **1300** Boniface VIII calls the **first Jubilee** or **Holy Year** in history. Large masses of pilgrims from all over Europe come to Rome (seems to have been about 200,000): great rise in papal prestige. Large masses of pilgrims from all over Europe come to Rome (seems to have been about 200,000): great rise in papal prestige.

**Second clash with France**: in 1301, for political reasons Philip IV has Bernard de Saisset, bishop of Pamiers, arrested, violating the privilege of the ecclesiastical forum. Boniface VIII condemns this act with the bull *Ausculta fili*. The king operates a cunning and dishonest communication strategy: he has the papal bull destroyed and replaced with a false one (*Deum time*) that is much harsher and completely offensive to the king and the independence of the kingdom, which is circulated throughout France; then, in **1302**, he convenes the **States General** (a meeting of representatives of the nobility, clergy and people): all of France shows di- solidarity with the king. Boniface VIII responds with the ***Unam sanctam*** (1302), which affirms the superiority of the pope over kings (the pontiff can judge kings, but he cannot be judged by anyone). In 1303 Philip went on the attack: he propagated a series of serious charges against the pope: heresy, immorality, etc. In September 1303, two expeditions, one of French and Italian knights, aided by the Roman noble families opposed to the Caetani, led by Guillaume de Nogaret (collaborator of the king), the other by Sciarra Colonna (rebellious Roman nobleman), reach the pope at Anagni (near Rome), attack him (**"slap" of Anagni**) and take him prisoner, to submit him to the judgment of a council; Boniface resists the pressure, and after three days he is freed by the people of Anagni, who rise up against the knights. Boniface thus regained his freedom, but died shortly afterward (October).

It should also be mentioned that at this time, in canon law, the doctrine on tria munera was being formulated, which was to have an important influence on the emerging ecclesiology.

The first separate treatises on ecclesiology are those of **ENRICO DA CREMONA** and **ANGELUS NIGRI,** both dated 1301 and entitled *De potestate papae*.

**3. Egidio Romano:** (1243ca-1316).

*Doctor fundamentalissimus* or *Doctor fundatissimus*. He belongs to the Augustinian Order, of which he was superior general. Studies in Paris following the courses given by Thomas Aquinas: being a disciple, he does not become a doctor because of the condemnation of 1277. Bishop of Bourges (France), opposes the ecclesiastical policy of King Philip IV the Fair. He is one of the editors of the bull *Unam sanctam*. Author of many theological works (among which stands out the *Commentary on the Sentences*).

He is one of the first ecclesiologists, with the treatise *De ecclesiastica potestate sive de summi pontificis potestate*, in which he sustains the hierocratic thesis of the absolute primacy of spiritual power over temporal power (it must be said, however, that this affirmation is made in his work from a perspective that is more religious than political). For him, in fact, earthly power must submit to ecclesiastical power in such a way as to constitute itself, with all its organisms and instruments, in respect and obedience to spiritual power. The pope, who is master of the spiritual order in the mystical ecclesial body, is also master of material goods, because these are established in function of the spiritual goods that they must serve and help; the pontiff possesses the *plenitudo potestatis*, which Egidio Romano conceives as a power directly derived from God and a reflection of the same divine omnipotence. Every now and then, however, appear in his treatise, expressions of a more spiritual nature, due to the influence of Aquinas his teacher, for example: "Ecclesia est Corpus Christi et Christus caput eius".

**4. Giacomo Capocci from Viterbo: †1307**

Blessed. Augustinian. Studied in Paris. Archbishop of Benevento, then of Naples. Disciple of Egidio Romano.

1302: *De regimine Christiano*: in this work, which is the first treatise on ecclesiology, he does not limit himself, like his predecessors and his master Aegidius himself, to defining the Church, but describes and illustrates it with breadth and more theological depth:

- distinctive characters
- organization in the form of a kingdom
- the spiritual mission
- the temporal mission
- the pope's powers

He begins with a dogmatic study of the Church: it possesses a dual jurisdiction, priestly and royal. All prelates exercise it, but in reality the powers belong to Christ and, down here, to his vicar, the pope. The state is a natural association, but it cannot fulfill its mission without faith and grace, so it depends on spiritual authority. The pope is therefore *regum terrae sacerdos et princeps.*

At the basis of the thought of James of Viterbo, there is a hierocratic vision of earthly power: the Church reigns and is the most perfect form of *respublica*, it is the only *societas perfecta* and the pope is the supreme monarch and ideal of the Church, and also has a certain power, even earthly, over Christian monarchs: he can judge and correct them if they do not conform their actions to the higher purpose of spiritual society. He has no power over non-Christian kingdoms.

Ecclesiology as a separate treatise arose as a result of the clash between the Holy See and the French Monarchy; unlike the struggle between the Papacy and the Empire, which can be considered an intra-ecclesial struggle, now, for the first time since Charlemagne, the Church finds a power that opposes it as another. Until then, Church and society, Church and state were confused. Now that a new power, the nascent national state, opposes the Church as an other, the need arises to reflect on the Church itself, its powers, its nature.

**5. John of Paris:** (Jean Quidort).

It proposes a middle way between that of the king's legists and the supporters of papal power.

In 1303 he writes *De potestate regia et papali*, in which he rejects the theocratic theses based on Scripture and the donation of Constantine. Instead, he asserts that the two powers are independent of each other, both in their origin and in their actions. Political power derives by natural right directly from God, and the state is something natural, first and essential to man, prior to Christianity, and furthermore pursues a moral and not only a material end (these are all concepts taken from Aristotelianism of the time). The Church originates in the theandric structure of the incarnation, in the dual nature of Christ, who is head of the Church.

The pope has legitimate power, but he is not the owner of all ecclesiastical property; dogmatic authority is diffused through the members of the Church, and the council is superior to the pontiff in matters of faith: in fact, the pope cannot define the faith without the intervention of the general council. The king must ensure the temporal security of his subjects; he can incur spiritual sanctions, but the pope cannot depose him; at most he can excommunicate all those who remain faithful to him, but then it will be up to the people to depose him (thus Quidort follows the theory that power resides in the people, who delegate its use to the king). If instead the pope scandalized the Church and did not want to correct himself, the king could try to depose him directly or through the cardinals, or through the people, who could force him to renounce the papacy.

The pope, according to Quidort, is a privileged member of the Church and is "quasi caput Ecclesiae propter unitatem Ecclesiae conservandam."

**6. Peter de Marsh**

He writes a *De potestate papae*, also trying to limit excessive curialist claims:

- rejects the theory that the apostles received their power from St. Peter, just as the bishops do not receive theirs from the pope; it is only up to the latter to limit the scope of that power;
- Regarding the power of the pope:
- he possesses spiritual power in the Church
- possesses the temporal one in its states
- possesses a jurisdiction over sovereigns only indirectly, by reason of the spiritual one, and only for what pertains to it.

**7. Marsilius of Padua (1275-1343)**

He writes the *Defensor pacis* (1324), a work in which he rejects the primacy of the pontiff, and affirms a subordination of the Church to the state. He is a forerunner of secularism, and his work is pervaded by a strong antipathy and aversion to the papacy and the entire hierarchical structure of the Church. He denies any power to the Church, as the only power must be the civil power; the clergy is reduced, in his vision, to specialized technical personnel for the celebration of rites and the study of theology, but without posing as a separate class and antagonistic to the state power, but as any other profession, always within the rules dictated by the civil power. For the important and collective decisions of Christendom, Marsilius foresees a general council, convened by the emperor. After the publication of this work, he had to flee from Paris, and repaired to the court of the emperor Ludovico il Bavaro, who was fighting against the Avignonese papacy.

**Nominalism**

**8. William of Ockham:** 1280-1349ca

*Venerabilis inceptor*. Franciscan of the spiritual current, follower of the emperor Ludovico the Bavarian in his struggle against the papacy. Condemned by the pope in 1328, he takes refuge at the imperial court.

Initiator and creator of the *modern way*. In his work prevails the logic against the metaphysics, the nominalism against the realism, the will against the intellect.

He breaks the harmony between faith and reason achieved by the scholasticism of the 13th century: after him, fideism and rationalism will be more frequent. From him, moreover, begins the separation between philosophers and theologians (until now the characters of the history of philosophy coincide with those of the history of theology: from now on no more).

Ockham's "razor": principle of economy (*entia non sunt multiplicanda sine necessitate*): eliminates scholastic distinctions such as:

- essence / existence
- act / power
- nature / substance
- natural knowledge of God / revealed knowledge of God (reason arrives only at the first principle, not God)

Nominalism: only what exists is real, and only what is individually distinct exists; only what is physically separable is individually distinct. It thus eliminates causality, becoming, and space.

Causality is denied in the sacramentaria: occasionalist theory. Ockham has a very weak discourse on the Eucharist; among other things, he interprets in a subjective and spiritual sense the terms *memoria* and *repraesentatio, and* not in a realist sense, paving the way for future Protestant theories; it is not for nothing that Luther was trained as a Catholic in the school of nominalist theology.

Moral theology: Ockham denies natural inclination to good and happiness: morality has a theological, not philosophical foundation. For him freedom = will = capacity for self-determination.

Will superior to the intellect and "free" from the intellect.

Primacy of the divine will: intellect led back to will; in God knowing=wanting.

Bad acts are such because God forbids them; instead, they are not forbidden because they are bad.

In ecclesiology he denies the total necessity of the Church for faith, positing the hypothesis of the survival of a single faithful. Moreover, for him, faithful to his nominalism, the Church is only the sum of many individual Christians. He recognizes the primacy of the pope, but limited to the purpose of doing what is necessary to prevent the Christian faith and the common good from being endangered; however, he denies the *plenitudo potestatis* and the infallibility of the pope.

Political thought: Ockham was among the supporters of the independence of the empire vis-à-vis the Church. He argued that the pope is not the source of imperial power: instead, it resides in the people, through the great electors. Political authority comes from God but through the people. He therefore denies the need for papal confirmation of imperial election. The pope has no supreme power in the civil field, except in the case of a power vacuum due to the failure of the civil authority itself: but these are exceptional cases, not the norm. The emperor, as well as other Christian princes, can intervene to chastise a heretical or scandalous pope or cleric.

**Rhenish-Flemish mystical theology**

**9. John Eckhart:** 1260-1327

Dominican. He wrote the *Opus tripartitum*. It proposes a knowledge of God based on mysticism and contemplation; it treats a lot of the divine names; it is inspired to the Pseudo-Dionysius and to the neoplatonism. In 1329, after his death, some expressions taken from his preaching in German, containing inaccuracies of pantheistic flavor, are condemned.

**10. John Taulero:** 1300-1361

Dominican. He works in Strasbourg. For prudence he did not write, but his preaching was transcribed by the faithful.

**11. Enrico Susone:** 1296-1366

Swiss Dominican. He works in Constance. He wrote mostly in German.

**12. John Ruysbroeck:** 1293-1381

Secular priest. Founds a hermitic order. Writes in Flemish. His main work is the *Ornament of the Spiritual Wedding*, in which he presents three stages of union of the soul with God: active, affective, contemplative. Through him, Rhenish Dominican mysticism penetrates into Flanders.

**13. Gerard Groote:** 1340-1384

Considered the founder of the *modern devotion* that will develop greatly in the '400 in the Netherlands. The influence of Ruysbroeck is very strong in him.

**Other currents**

**14. The precursors of humanism.**

In Italy, especially in Tuscany, a cultural and even theological movement of rejection of scholasticism, Averroism and in part Aristotelianism developed in favor of a return to ancient sources, including the classics, Augustinism and Platonism. This movement will become much stronger in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries. In the '300 the major exponents of this thought are the poet **Francesco Petrarca** (1304-1374), the hermit **Luigi Marsili** (†1394), the Florentine humanist **Coluccio Salutati** (1331-1406), Florentine notary: in Salutati's writings are interesting some attitudes in which the search for Christian salvation in the world, with its trials and battles, in service to family, friends, and the city, is revalued, although at the same time he shows great appreciation for monastic life (*De saeculo et religione*). Salutati is also important for philology and biblical studies for the foundation of the first regular chair of Greek in Florence, to which he called the Byzantine scholar Manuel Chrysolora (1350-1415).

**15. Thomism.**

The Dominicans try to defend Thomistic theology corporately. Sometimes with excessive ardor, as in the case of **Juan de Monzón** (1340-1415?), of Avignon obedience, who in the name of St. Thomas accused the supporters of the Immaculate Conception of heresy: this sparked a controversy and the order squared off behind him; the University of Paris therefore expelled the Dominicans; Juan de Monzón's appeal to the Avignon pope ended in his condemnation; he then abandoned Avignon obedience and switched to Roman obedience.

**Dante Alighieri** (1265-1321): it is also worth mentioning the poetic genius of the Florentine, who transposed into a high poetic production in Italian (the Divine Comedy) the theology of St. Thomas Aquinas. Moreover in the *De monarchia, he* sustains the importance of the universal monarchy of the Empire, derived from God, fighting instead the theses that gave ample power to the papacy in the political field, above all towards the Empire.

**16. The Heresy of John Wicliff**

Wicliff (1320-1384) was a priest from a noble English family, highly regarded in his time for his bold preaching. In it:

- He rails **against the temporal power and the wealth of** the Church, which in his opinion are irreconcilable with the doctrine of Christ: he advocates the absorption of ecclesiastical goods by the state.
- assimilates the **fiscalism** of the papal curia to **simony**
- is totally **opposed to monasticism**

In 1378 he received an admonition from the Archbishop of Canterbury, but it all ended there. So Wicliff persists in advocating his ideas, which are increasingly radicalized. It is interesting to note the singular **consonance** between them and those that **Luther** will have about 150 later:

- The true **Church** is not the hierarchical **Church,** but the **invisible community** of the predestined.
- excommunications and **ecclesiastical censures have no value**
- **every believer is a priest**
- **the papacy is the antichrist**
- rejection of Tradition, in the name of **Scripture alone**
- rejection of transubstantiation, replaced by the theory of **remanence** or consubstantiation
- auricular **confession** would be a **belated institution**
- **Rejection of: ecclesiastical celibacy, indulgences, worship of saints and relics, sacred images, pilgrimages, prayers for the dead**.
- Finally, he asserts that a national Church, reformed and governed according to Scripture, must replace the hierarchical Church, dominated by the Roman Curia.

Wicliff begins to have followers who spread his ideas preaching throughout England, called **lollards** by opponents (from *lollium* = weeds). This radical preaching causes a social upheaval, the **peasants' revolt of 1381**; suppressed the revolt, the authorities begin to worry, for social-political reasons, the ideas of Wicliff: in the **Synod of London** in 1382 are condemned as heretical 24 of his propositions, and he must abandon teaching at Oxford. However **Wicliff remains undisturbed** and dies quietly in 1384. But his ideas will continue to spread. Considered dangerous by the ruling classes, they will lead the social elements in power to take repressive measures: in 1400 the English Parliament votes the law *De haeretico comburendo*, which introduces in the kingdom the Inquisition, until then practically unknown in England. Many lollards end up at the stake and the movement is gradually eradicated.
