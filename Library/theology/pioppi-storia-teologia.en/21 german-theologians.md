## German theologians {#pioppi-storia-teologia.21}
Romano GUARDINI (1895-1968): Italian, but moved to Germany at the age of four. In 1923 he taught in Berlin in the chair of Katholische Weltanschauung. In 1939 the National Socialist government suppressed the chair, and Guardini resumed teaching after the war, in Tübingen and Munich. Close to the liturgical movement. He is a non-analytical thinker, but led to broad visions of synthesis (also influenced in this by the teaching subject of his Berlin chair). In him we note a good synthesis between theology and spirituality, and he uses St. Bonaventure and Pascal as important sources of his thought. He is a supporter of a Christology full of faith, which - far from the secularized interpretations of liberal Protestantism - becomes the basis of a straight and healthy anthropology; it is therefore for a theology that also incorporates the spiritual aspect, that it is a Christological meditation; these ideas are present above all in his most important work, Der Herr, published in 1937. Thus Roger Aubert describes Guardini: «... who combines the Latin clarity of form with the Germanic completeness of thought, he was not a systematic theologian but an intuitive thinker , very sensitive to the aspirations of young people and also to the complexity of the “concrete-living”. He has elaborated a very rich work, which is located on the boundaries between theology, religious philosophy, spirituality, psychology and literature, concerned with taking into due consideration the position of man in front of God from a non-religious point of view. static but existential and even dramatic, tirelessly reminding us that "God is not only an almighty He but the living You" ».

Odo CASEL (1886-1948), Benedictine of the Maria Laach Abbey. After completing his studies in Rome at the Anselmianum, he was the main diffuser in Germany of the Liturgical Movement which arose in France at the Abbey of Solesmes. He was a friend of Guardini, with whom he had a fruitful collaboration. Casel and the Liturgical Movement want to place the liturgy as an important source for all theology; in the liturgy the revealed mysteries are presented in a lively way to the Christian people, in a more so

concrete and complete, and closer to the biblical text, than the abstract definitions of the theological schools and conciliar documents. The liturgy, according to the supporters of this movement, can nourish and renew theology widely, starting from the sacramentary, making them overcome the canonistic and pastoral level that at the time they had mainly assumed.

Karl RAHNER (1904-1984), a German Jesuit, taught in Innsbruck, Munich and Münster. He was perished at the Second Vatican Council. He creates a real theological system, in imitation of Thomas Aquinas, but instead of using Aristotelian philosophy, his working tool is Heidegger's existentialism, and basically the thought of Hegel and that of Kant. His theology is based on what he calls the "anthropological turning point" (anthropologische Wende): since man is a self-conscious spiritual being, nothing can be communicated to him as simply coming from outside of him; and therefore also the scientific study of Christian revelation - theology - must start not from God who reveals himself, but from man to whom God is revealed, in a vision that gives more space to the action of the knowing subject, man. He therefore begins by finding in the human intelligence a dynamism, an existential a priori, which implies an openness to the infinite, to the mystery: however, all this is present in an athematic way. He is a scholar who is very attentive to the dialogue of the theologian with the desacralized world in which he lives and works.

The Bavarian Johann Baptist METZ (1928-2019), professor in Munich and Münster, is the diffuser of political theology: he believes that in the face of the challenges of modernity, Catholic thought, after a first phase of clear opposition in a counter-revolutionary and traditionalist sense, it closed in a "privatization of the faith", a kind of individualism that does not allow the creation of ideas of political and social change. He advocates a reaction to this situation, which leads to the Gospel bringing about real and beneficial political and social changes. Metz deals with these themes in his work Zur Theologie der Welt (1968): he does so always bearing in mind the risk of falling into a traditionalist or confessional attitude, which he wants to avoid. In fact, he is well aware that no human concretization (political, social, national) can ever be considered a complete realization of the Gospel message. He calls his work "critical-negative political theology", precisely to emphasize that the task of theology is the criticism of the insufficiencies, in the light of the Gospel, of the various existing systems, and not the positive proposal of a project, which does not it is up to theology.
