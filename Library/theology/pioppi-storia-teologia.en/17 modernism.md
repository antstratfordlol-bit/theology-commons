## Modernism {#pioppi-storia-teologia.17}
The century opens with a great theological crisis in the Church: the modernist crisis. This theological environment represents a Catholic version of liberal Protestantism. The great problem of modernism consists in favoring a dangerous immanentism in the Catholic faith, maintaining the traditional formulations, but reinterpreting them with a different sense, precisely subjectivist and imma-nentist. It is an unsuccessful attempt to find an agreement between Catholicism and liberal culture. The repression of the hierarchy held back the movement, but did not solve the problem that had caused it, namely the dichotomy between Catholic and modern thought.

1890-1894: Alfred Loisy (1857-1940), teaches Biblical Exegesis at the Institut Catholique in Paris

1893: encyclical Providentissimus Deus, on the inerrancy and inspiration of the Scriptures

1894: Loisy, having fallen into suspicion, resigns from teaching exegesis and goes on to teach ancient languages

1902: Loisy publishes L'Évangile et l'Église, in which he disputes the institution of the Church by Christ (Christ preached the kingdom and the Church came out), while arguing, against Adolph von Harnack (who placed a total otherness between Christ prophet of the parousia and the Church), a substantial continuity between Christ and the Church; for Loisy, even if Christ believed the end of the world was imminent and did not want to found the Church, in spite of everything the Church flows naturally from the preaching of Jesus, once the time that separates his disciples from the parousia has lengthened. The problem is that basically he does not grant Christ a real profound knowledge of his mission, which leads to seriously questioning his divinity.

1903: five works by Loisy are listed on the Index

1905: Antonio Fogazzaro publishes the novel Il Santo, pervaded by modernist ideas.

1905: the mathematician Edouard Le Roy publishes the article What is a dogma? In La Quinzaine, interpreting dogmas as expressions of religious subjectivity.

1906: Jesuit George Tyrrel († 1909), accused of modernism, is expelled from the Society. In his books by him Lex credendi and Through Scylla and Carybdis advocated a symbolic interpretation. He will die outside the church.

1907: Ernesto Buonaiuti († 1946) publishes Letters of a modernist priest, reducing Christianity to social reformism and eschatologism. He will die outside the church.

1907: July 3: the Lamentabili decree of the Holy Office condemns 65 modernist propositions; on 8 September Pius X publishes the encyclical Pascendi, in which he exposes and refutes modernist ideas

1908: Loisy refuses to submit and is excommunicated; in the same year he published Les Évangiles synoptiques: he affirms that "God is the author of Sacred Scripture as he is the architect of the Basilica of St. Peter" and disputes the historicity of the passion and resurrection of Christ. He will die outside the church.

1909: Romolo Murri († 1944), exponent of the Christian democratic movement, is accused of modernism and excommunicated.

1909: an organization headed by Msgr. Umberto Benigni, the Sodalitium Pianum, carries out an intense research and reporting activity of people suspicious of modernist ideas

1910: motu proprio Sacrorum Antistitum, which imposes the anti-modernist oath

1913: Albert Houtin, modernist († 1926) publishes the Histoire du modernisme catholique

1921: the ecclesiastical authority dissolves the Sodalitium Pianum.

1925: Friedrich von Hügel, a layman, who had tried to create a network of contacts between the various characters of modernism, dies.

1926: major excommunication for Buonaiuti.

The Belgian historian Roger Aubert wrote: "to objectively appreciate the intransigence and severity of Pius X [...] it is necessary to place the events in their frame and remember that the situations were much more confused then than today . In the presence of the disturbing processes of religious liberalism, materialist socialism and proud scientism, which tended to promote the advent of a new world in which man would be free of God, it seemed absolutely necessary for Pius X to put Catholics, dazzled and impressed by the safety of their opponents, on guard against the temptation to seek a compromise too soon before having sufficiently extolled the claims of the modern spirit to obtain with certainty what they contained that was acceptable and fruitful ".

It can be said that Pius X was able to face a delicate situation with great certainty of judgment, and that Loisy's condemnation was justified and advantageous for the Church. Instead, the maneuvers of the integrists created problems, which had the effect of creating a climate of suspicion throughout the Church and curbing theological research.

On the other hand, it is undeniable that the theological tendency of the most extreme modernists was in the line of an immanentization of the faith, in the footsteps of liberal Protestantism: all the emphasis was placed on the imp

an act that the Gospel message has on every man, denying the historicity of the facts narrated therein or underestimating their importance.

Finally, it must be said that not infrequently men of secular culture had a hostile attitude towards Catholic modernism: in Italy one can recall the aversion to this movement on the part of the idealist philosophers Giovanni Gentile and Benedetto Croce; the latter wrote "I believe that modernism is in a few cases a condition of painful and very respectable perplexity of some souls of noble temper, in which faith and thought struggle. In other more numerous cases, it is an amateurism between philosophical and religious (yet, not true philosophy or true religion), exercised by time wasters, who chat about these topics out of fashion and celebrate their rites in those temples that are cafes. And, in the remaining cases, it is an unscrupulous attempt by disbelieving and dishonest pretenders and brothers to stay inside the church and enjoy its economic advantages or turn its power to their own ends. Of these three elements of modernism, only the first deserves sympathy, and none of the three has speculative importance ».
