## The 11th Century: The Controversy Between Dialecticians and Antidialecticians {#pioppi-storia-teologia.7}
**1. General Background**

Starting from the year 1000, the Western Christian world experiences a recovery and growth, from all points of view: it begins a process of development that will continue uninterruptedly until the nineteenth century, when Europe rises to the undisputed role of dominant continent. After 1000 begins to occur in Europe:

- Economic growth: technical innovations in agriculture. Rebirth of the cities and commerce. The Italian maritime republics regain control of the Mediterranean, tearing it away from the Muslim states; this action culminates, at the end of the century, with the First Crusade.
- cultural growth: for example,
- *ars dictandi*: rebirth of latin grammar
- music: Guido d'Arezzo: pentagram
- theology: saint Anselm
- great dispute between dialecticians and anti-dialecticians.
- Political development: Europe manages to develop its own defensive force against invasions; indeed, it begins to expand: progress of the *Reconquista* in Spain, beginning of the Crusades (First Crusade: 1096-1099). Creation of strong and stable kingdoms: Holy Roman Empire, France, Poland, Hungary and the Norman states of England and Sicily. Development of the experience of Italian city-states (communes), with notable aspects of democratic, corporate, or representative government.

The Struggle for Investments

With the early death of Otto III, the dynasty of Saxony was extinguished, and the empire passed first to St. Henry II (1002-1024), who died childless, then to Conrad II (1024-1039), who inaugurated the dynasty of Franconia; he was succeeded by Henry III (1039-1056), who with the Synod of Sutri (1046) received from the Roman clergy the delegation of the election of the pope: thus we have a series of popes who are chosen directly by the emperor (they are all German popes); this measure was due to the need to remove the papal election from the intrusions of the Roman noble families. In fact, Henry III chose some very good people for the papacy.

One of them, St. Leo IX (1049-1054), transformed the College of Cardinals: belonging to it had until then been an almost honorary title; Leo IX transformed this institution into what it is now, the Senate of the Church, elevating to this office the most spiritually and culturally prominent clerics of the time, such as Umberto da Silva Candida, Ildebrando di Soana (later Pope Gregory VII), Federico di Lorena (later Pope Stephen IX), and St. Pier Damiani.

Later, Pope Nicholas II, in 1059, would promulgate a law on the election of the pope, freeing it from imperial choice: the pontiff would be elected by the cardinals; to maintain with legal fiction the tradition of election by the Roman clergy, each cardinal was given the title of a Roman church or a suburbicarian diocese.

He then began an activity of reform of the Church aimed at restoring discipline and eradicate simony and nicolaitism; to achieve this, the laity had to be ousted from ecclesiastical appointments (investitures), since this was the cause of the decadence of the customs of the clergy. This reform is called Gregorian Reform, from the name of the most important pope among those who supported it: Gregory VII (1073-1085). He was supported in his reform work by the Cluniac Order, but had to clash with Emperor Henry IV (1056-1106), who aimed to organize an imperial Church. Gregory VII's central idea was that the Church, the bride of Christ, should be free and not a servant.

In this conflict between the papacy and the empire, called the Investiture Struggle, and fought at all levels (religious, political, military, spiritual, propaganda, etc.), the popes were able to take advantage of the support of the most powerful person of the time, Countess Matilda of Canossa, who owned vast fiefdoms in Italy and Germany, a woman very devoted and conquered to the papal cause. Other allies of the papacy were the Pataria, a popular movement, sometimes violent, opposed to corrupt clerics, and the Norman Kingdom of Sicily, which came to control all of southern Italy, driving out the Arabs and Byzantines and eliminating the last Lombard duchies. The struggle ended in the XIII century.

The Eastern Schism

In the eleventh century, the religious unity between East and West was definitively lost, already put to the test repeatedly, as we have seen, by the intrusions of the Byzantine imperial power in the Patriarchate of Constantinople. The various diatribes and the estrangement of the two worlds caused by the Arab conquest of the Mediterranean had led to a deep and widespread antipathy between Greeks and Latins: the former considered the latter barbarians, the latter considered the Byzantines intriguing, cowardly and disloyal. It came to the final break.

Already the patriarch of Constantinople Sergius II (999-1019) had the name of the pope removed from the liturgical prayers of the Byzantine Church. Finally, Patriarch Michael Cerulario (1043-1058) began a strong anti-Latin policy: he defined the Latins as heretics, especially for the fact of using unleavened bread in the Eucharist (hence the derogatory term "unleavened"); in 1053 he closed all Latin churches and monasteries in the Eastern Empire, and caused episodes of profanation against the Eucharist according to the Latin rite, considered invalid by some Greeks of the time. Pope Leo IX sent Cardinal Umberto da Silva Candida as a legate to Constantinople to try to negotiate with Cerulario: but the cardinal was received in the most rude way possible (for example he was not allowed to celebrate Mass), so he, seeing the impossibility of negotiating, excommunicated Michael Cerulario, who in turn excommunicated the Latins, without listening to the advice to moderation of Patriarch Peter of Antioch. The schism, in addition to the peoples of the empire, dragged with it the Bulgarians, the Serbs, the Russians and the Romanians.

The schism favored a growth of papal power in the West.

Missions

Evangelization of Iceland (1000), Sweden (1008), Viking Greenland.

**2. The *Dictatus Papae***

In this century, thanks to Gregory VII and the exponents of the Gregorian Reform, a theology of the papacy was developing that placed the figure of the Roman pontiff at the center and summit of ecclesiastical power, as well as the supreme judge of all of Christendom, even in the sociopolitical sphere. These ideas are expressed in this famous document of 1075.

**I.** "Quod Romana Ecclesia a solo Domino sit fundata".

That the Roman Church was founded by God and God alone.

**II.** "Quod solus Romanus pontifex iure dicatur universalis."

That the Roman pontiff is the only one who can rightly be called universal.

**III.** "Quod ille solus possit deponere episcopos vel reconciliare."

That he alone can depose or reinstate bishops.

**IV.** "Quod legatus eius omnibus episcopis praesit in concilio etiam inferioris gradus et adversus eos sententiam depositionis possit dare"

That in any council his legate, even if lesser in rank, has authority superior to that of the bishops, and can pass sentence of deposition against them.

**V.** "Quod absentes papa possit deponere".

That the pope may depose the absentees.

**VI.** "Quod cum excommunicatis ab illo inter caetera nec in eadem domo debemus manere."

That, among other things, one may not dwell under the same roof with those whom he has excommunicated. **VII.** "Quod illi soli licet pro temporis necessitate novas leges condere, novas plebes congregare, de canonica abbatiam facere et e contra, divitem episcopatum dividere et inopes unire."

He alone has the legitimacy, according to the needs of the moment, to make new laws, to assemble new congregations, to found abbeys or canonries; and, on the other hand, to divide the rich dioceses and unite the poor ones.

**VIII.** "Quod solus possit uti imperialibus insigniis."

That he alone may use the imperial insignia.

**IX.** "Quod solius papae pedes omnes principes deosculentur".

That only to the pope should all princes kiss his feet.

**X.** "Quod illius solius nomen in ecclesiis recitetur."

Let only his name be spoken in the churches.

**XI.** "Quod hoc unicum est nomen in mundo."

Let his name be the same throughout the world.

**XII.** "Quod illi liceat imperatores deponere."

That he is permitted to depose emperors.

**XIII.** "Quod illi liceat de sede ad sedem necessitate cogente episcopos transmutare"

That he is permitted to transfer bishops as needed.

**XIV.** "Quod de omni ecclesia quocunque voluerit clericum valeat ordinare."

That he has the power to ordain a priest of any church, in any territory.

**XV.** "Quod ab illo ordinatus alii ecclesiae praeesse potest, sed non militare; et quod ab aliquo episcopo non debet superiorem gradum accipere"

That he whom he has ordained may direct another Church, but may not make war on it; moreover, he may not receive a higher rank from any other bishop.

**XVI.** "Quod nulla synodus absque praecepto eius debet generalis vocari".

Let no synod be called "general" without its order.

**XVII.** "Quod nullum capitulum nullusque liber canonicus habeatur absque illius auctoritate."

That a text can only be declared canonical under its own authority.

**XVIII.** "Quod sententia illius a ullo debeat retractari et ipse omnium solus retractare possit"

That a judgment of his cannot be reformed by anyone; on the contrary, he can reform any judgment issued by others.

**XIX.** "Quod a nemine ipse iudicare debeat".

That he may not be judged by any.

**XX.** "Quo nullus audeat condemnare Apostolicam Sedem appellantem."

May no one condemn those who appealed to the Holy See.

**XXI.** "Quod maiores causae cuiuscumque Ecclesiae ad eam referri debeant".

That all *maiores causae*, of any church, should be brought before him.

**XXII.** "Quod Romana Ecclesia numquam erravit nec in perpetuum Scriptura testante errabit."

That the Roman Church has never erred; nor, according to the testimony of the Scriptures, will it ever err for eternity.

**XXIII.** "Quod Romanus pontifex, si canonice fuerit ordinatus, meritis beati Petri indubitanter efficitur sanctus testante sancto Ennodio Papiensi episcopo ei multis sanctis patribus faventibus, sicut in decretis beati Symmachi papae continetur."

That the Roman pontiff elected canonically, is without doubt, by the merits of St. Peter sanctified, according to what is said by St. Ennodius bishop of Pavia, and confirmed by many holy fathers favorable to him, as we read in the decrees of St. Symmachus pope.

**XXIV.** "Quod illius praecepto et licentia subiectis liceat accusare"

That, at his command and with his consent, vassals have title to bring charges.

**XXV.** "Quod absque synodali conventu possit episcopus deponere et reconciliare."

That he may depose or reinstate bishops without convening a synod.

**XXVI.** "Quod catholicus non habeatur, qui non concordat Romanae ecclesiae"

That he who is not in communion with the Roman Church is not to be considered Catholic.

**XXVII.** "Quod a fidelitate iniquorum subiectos potest absolvere."

May he free his subjects from the obligation of obedience to princes who have imposed their power by force.

In applying these ideas, Alexander II operates a centralizing thrust in the Church through:

- exemption of monasteries from episcopal power
- codification of the right of dispensation
- liturgical unification (strong limitation of the Greek rite in Italy and of the Visigothic one)
- delivery of the pallium made in Rome
- development of pontifical legacies

Moreover, Urban II, by promoting the crusades, rises to the role of the most important reference point of Christianity.

The Gregorian Reform, if it sets the Church free, eliminating the confusion of the Carolingian world, also damages its unity. The *Ecclesia* becomes something different from the *Imperium* and *Societas*, but this happens at the cost of a clericalization of the Church, in which Church = Clergy.

**THE CONTROVERSY BETWEEN DIALECTICIANS AND ANTI-DIALECTICIANS**

Great dispute between dialectics and anti-dialectics: the first advocates of the use of dialectics (= philosophy) in theology; the second (conservative) advocates of a theology more linked to sacred scripture and mystical contemplation, and against the use of dialectics, which they consider a form of rationalist pride.

***A) Antidialectics***

**3. Saint Pier Damiani (1007-1072)**

Monk of the Hermitic Congregation of St. Columba, prior of the Monastery of Fonte Avellana. Bishop of Ostia. In 1057 he was created cardinal by Leo IX, within the process of transformation of the College of Cardinals into the Senate of the Church. A very important figure of the Gregorian Reform, he carried out various embassies in France, Ravenna and Florence. He dedicated great energies to the animation of hermitic life.

From the theological point of view, he is an anti-dialectician, who views with some suspicion the use of philosophy in sacred doctrine, preferring to move within the furrow of monastic theology. He accentuates to the maximum, against the "pride" of the science, the ideal of the *simplicitas* in the faith, exhorting to the renunciation of the intelligence to try to probe with the philosophers the secrets of the cosmos and the man, and to the radical submission to the only true *auctoritas*: the sacred scripture and the tradition of the fathers. His theology therefore remains a *lectio* of the *auctoritates* and a preservation of the heritage of the previous tradition. For him, the spiritual life leads to a kind of participation in divine wisdom, which is completely superior to all human knowledge; indeed, the acquisition of human knowledge often becomes an obstacle to this superior spiritual wisdom.

His fundamental concern is the reform of monastic life, against the abuses that have penetrated it: for this reason he strongly emphasizes the theme of *contempt* for the world, *contemptus saeculi*.

Major works:

- *Liber Gomorrhianus*: against clerogamy
- *De divina omnipotentia*: emphasizes in it the concept of *philosophia* as *ancilla theologiae*
- *Contra errorem Graecorum de processione Spiritus Sancti*
- *Vita beati Romualdi*: Saint Romuald, founder of the Hermitage of Camaldoli, was also an animator of organized hermitism in the eleventh century.
- *De fide catholica*: deals with the Trinity and the Incarnation
- *Antilogus contra Judaeos*

In the field of soteriology Pier Damiani is a supporter of the theory of the rights of the devil over man. In the ecclesiological field, even though he was an advocate of the Gregorian Reform, he was more moderate. He still has a Carolingian vision, he underlines the importance of the emperor in the Church; opposing himself to Umberto di Silva Candida, who considered invalid the simoniac ordinations, Pier Damiani considers them valid; he also sees in the College of Cardinals a senate that shares the power with the pope (similarly to the Senate of the Roman Empire).

**4. Otlone of St. Emmeran (1010-1070)**

He is also strongly anti-dialectical. He writes the *Liber de tentationibus suis et scriptis*; the *Dialogus de tribus quaestionibus*; the *Liber metricus de doctrina spirituali*.

Dialectics is for him a temptation; it is a misunderstood application to science; it is not without value, but it cannot give us true knowledge, which is attained through meditation on Holy Scripture.

**5. Manegold of Lautenbach (1030-1103)**

Antidialectic: He writes the *Liber ad Geberardum* and the *Opusculum contra Wolfelmum Coloniensem*.

Monk, a supporter of the Gregorian Reform, was for this reason also imprisoned for some time.

The *Liber ad Geberhardum*: written around 1085, deals with the theme of the relationship between the pope and the emperor: there he argues an interesting theory that anticipates contractualism. The power of the king/emperor derives for him from a kind of social contract: the people raise the king above themselves to be protected against tyranny and mistreatment; if the king becomes a tyrant, the people can rebel, but must first have the consent of the pope, the head of Christendom. If the pope judges against the king, he can release his subjects from their oath of allegiance to the emperor, but not because of any inherent power, but when he realizes that the contract is no longer being observed by the king.

The *Opusculum contra Wolfelmum* deals with the relationship between faith and reason. Manegold leads in it a harsh criticism against philosophers; he argues that in order to understand truths superior to the senses and intelligence, human thought inevitably errs if it is not guided by piety. The action of the Holy Spirit is a *sine qua non* for right knowledge. The soul can rise from its deficiency and misery only by accepting the truth preserved and proposed by the Church through the inspiration of the Holy Spirit.

***B) Extreme Dialectics***

**6. Berengar of Tours (d. 1088)**

He was born in Tours. He studied at Chartres where he was a student of Fulbert of Chartres; from 1030 he taught at Tours, where he had Saint Bruno (founder of the Carthusians) as a student, and where in 1040 he became chancellor and head of the school.

Extreme exponent of the dialectical environment: for him, dialectical reason is the supreme norm and guide in the perception of truth in the whole field of knowledge. Thus he wrote: "no one will dispute, unless he is stupidly blind, that in the search for truth reason is indisputably the best guide. It is proper to a great soul to always have recourse to dialectics; to have recourse to it is to have recourse to reason, and he who does not do this, renounces that which honors him most. For what is the image of God in him is his reason" (*De sacra coena adversus Lanfrancum*).

His best known work is the *De sacra coena*, on the Eucharist, in which inserting himself in the dynamic-symbolic current (Agostino⇒Ratramno⇒Berengario), arrives however to grant only a spiritual presence of Christ in the sacrament. Other works on the same subject, written in controversy with his contradictors (Adelmanno of Liege and Lanfranco of Bec) are: *Purgatoria epistula contra Adelmannum*; *Rescriptum contra Lanfrancum*. It should be noted that Adelmann (who wrote to Berengar a heartfelt *De veritate corporis et sanguinis Domini,* begging him in memory of their common master Fulbert of Chartres to remain in Catholic doctrine, maintaining peace in the Church) continues the Eucharistic tradition of the Church of Liège, already begun with Raterio of Verona, and culminating in the thirteenth century with the institution of the feast of Corpus Christi.

For Berengar, the Eucharist is first and foremost a sacrament, and this is, according to the teachings of Saint Augustine, a sacred sign, a visible figure of invisible grace. Through the words of consecration, the bread and wine are determined, remaining what they were, to signify the Body and Blood of Jesus Christ. The Eucharist, therefore, is a sign; it cannot be identified with the true Body of Christ, as Pascasius Radbert argued, because the sacrament, as such, only possesses the capacity to refer to something else, to the *res sacramenti*, but without identifying with it. In Berengar's thought, *sacramentum* and *res* are two different and separate realities: the first is perceivable with the senses; the second, indicated by the sign, is invisible: it can be known only by the eyes of the believing heart, and belongs to the domain of the spirit.

He further maintains that nothing can be converted into another existing substance without an external transformation also taking place; that the Body of Christ cannot be in different places; that it cannot be contained in the space of the species. Therefore, in the Eucharist there is only a symbol of the Body and Blood of Christ.

Ultimately, for Berengar, the presence of the Body of Christ in the Eucharist is not true and substantial; instead, it is a presence *in figure*, *in similitude*, *intellectualiter, spiritualiter*, *in virtute*.

His doctrine is condemned by the Lateran Council of 1059, and he has to retract with a formula written in a very hard form, with expressions really at the antipodes of his thought: "Ego Berengarius [...] ore et corde profiteor de sacramento dominicae mensae [...]: scilicet panem et vinum, quae in altari ponuntur, post consecrationem non solum sacramentum, sed etiam verum corpus et sanguinem Domini nostri Jesu Christi esse, et sensualiter, non solum sacramento, sed in veritate, manibus sacerdotum tractari et frangi et fidelium dentibus atteri".

Berengar resumes his studies. He states that in the Eucharist Christ is present *not per absumptionem, sed per assumptionem*. The change of bread does not imply a disappearance: *panis sacratus in altari, salva sua substantia, est corpus Christi, id est non amittens quod erat sed assumens quod non erat*. The idea expressed by the term *assumptio is of the* utmost importance and can certainly be understood in conformity with dogma. But all the difficulty comes from the fact that Berengar does not see the link between the real presence, which the term *assumptio* wants to signify, and transubstantiation, which he rejects. In fact, transubstantiation expresses a truly spiritual presence which is not accessible to the senses and the imagination, but only to the intellect and faith; and thus allows us to eliminate an excessively materialistic interpretation against which Berengar struggles and to establish what is the true form of the Eucharistic presence, founded on total conversion.

Having again supported his theories, however, they are again reproved at the Lateran Council of 1079, where he is enjoined to make a profession of faith drawn up in a manner more respectful of his sensibilities. "Ego Berengarius corde credo et ore confiteor panem et vinum quae ponuntur in altari per mysterium sacrae orationis et verba nostri redemptoris substantialiter converti in veram et propriam ac vivificatricem carnem et sanguinem domini nostri et post consecrationem esse verum Christi corpus, quod natum est de virgine et quod pro salute mundi oblatum in cruce pependit, et quod sedet ad dexteram Patris, et verum sanguinem Christi quod de latere eius effusus est, non tantum per signum et virtutem sacramenti, sed in proprietate naturae et veritate substantiae. Sicut in hoc brevi continetur et ego legi et vos intellegistis, sic credo, nec contra hanc fidem ulterius docebo. Sic me Deus adiuvet et haec sacra Evangelia". This synod also welcomes the term *transubstantiatio*.

Berengar's heresy remained a pure school doctrine (it was not a heresy with a popular following); it had the positive consequence that it pushed many theologians to deepen the Augustinian theology of the sign, which in fact was insufficient in sacramental theology; these studies led in the following century to find a satisfactory definition of the sacrament of the new law, which then led to the identification of the sacramental septenary.

The controversy provoked by Berengar of Tours was undoubtedly very fruitful; Grabmann writes about it as follows: "the presence of Berengar had as a positive effect the unfolding of a new activity in the theological field, which sought to re-establish, against an excessive use of dialectics, the patristic point of view and to balance the relationship between *auctoritas* and *ratio*. The result of this controversy was a great advance in the exposition of Eucharistic dogma. The Blessed Sacrament became the object of theological treatises to a much greater extent than in the ninth century. These monographic treatises led to a clarification of the Eucharistic dogma from all sides, led to an examination of its connections with other points of Christian doctrine, and thus awakened a taste for a systematic treatment of rather large parts of theology. [...] The controversy [...] also produced a progress and an enrichment of theological terminology": in fact the terms *substantia*, *accidens*, and *transubstantiatio* began to be used in the Eucharistic sphere.

In opposition to Berengar, the Eucharist, until then often called by the name of *Corpus mysticum, began to be* called *Corpus verum*, a title that until then was reserved for the Church; the latter then began to be called *Corpus mysticum.* There was therefore a reversal of terminology.

**7. Roscellino (†1120)**

He was born in Compiègne. Brilliant teacher, he had relationships with Ivo of Chartres, Anselmo of Aosta and Lanfranco. Cited before the Council of Soissons (1092), rejected the doctrine that was attributed to him and therefore was not condemned. He went to live in England and then returned to France, in Besançon and Loches.

Little of his work has come down to us directly, but it is possible to reconstruct his thought fairly well.

He, too, conducts the use of dialectic in an unbalanced manner. Of his thought we have no direct source. In Loches he was master of Peter Abelard, who later defeated him severely in a public dispute.

He is the father of medieval nominalism: for him the universal does not exist *in re*, nor *in mind*, but only in language; it is therefore a mere *flatus vocis*. The general idea is only a name: the true reality is only that of individuals.

Applying the nominalist theory to the trinitarian doctrine, he denies by reason the oneness of substance, and speaks of *substantia trina et triplex*, although he then affirms that by revelation we must believe in the dogma of unity and trinity in God: "it could truly be said that there are three gods, if usage permitted."

***(c) Moderate dialectics (from which the first scholastic is derived).***

**8. Fulbert of Chartres (d. 1028)**

Disciple of Gerbert of Aurillac. He founded the School of Chartres. He gives an important role to dialectics and profane science, but at the same time warns against exaggerating its use, and reaffirms the centrality of the study of Sacred Scripture and the Fathers of the Church, within the framework, however, of a harmony between the study of revelation and that of the philosophical disciplines.

**9.** The best work in the polemic against Berengar is probably, already in the following century, that of the canonist and theologian **Alghero of Liège** (1070-1132): he wrote the *De sacramento corporis et sanguinis Dominici*, and the *De misericordia et iustitia*. He too, like Adalmanno, belongs to the eucharistic tradition of Liege. His work had a considerable influence on the *Decree* of Gratian, among other things on the question of the method of concordances of discordant canons.

**10. Bernold of Constance (†1100): he is a** canonist rather than a theologian, but he is also important for theology because, in his canonical works, he anticipates the Abelardian method of concordance and discussion of conflicting canons and rulings of the Church Fathers.

Two other important canonists of the eleventh century are **Burcard of Worms**, who wrote the *Liber decretorum* between 1012 and 1022, and **Anselm of Lucca**.

**Irnerio** (1060ca-1130), a master of law and founder of the University of Bologna, wrote a book of *Sentences*, which mixes legal and theological issues; it is very interesting because we can see in it, in the theological parts, an incipient attempt to systematize the doctrinal matter.

**11. Lanfranc of Bec (1005-1089)**

Born in Pavia. In 1042 he entered the Benedictine monastery of Bec, in Normandy, of which he became abbot in 1059. In 1070 he was appointed archbishop of Canterbury.

His major works include:

- *In omnes Pauli epistulas commentarii*
- *Liber de Corpore et Sanguine Domini adversus Berengarium Turonensem*

In the controversy on dialectics he is in a moderate and middle position: in fact he supports the right use of it against an excessive contempt and an exaggerated exaltation. For him the dialectic, the rhetoric and all the liberal arts, constitute a valid help to build the theological knowledge; but the philosophical rationality must always be ordered to the comprehension of the truth of faith, recognizing that the will of God is the omnipotent and eternal cause of the being of all the things and respecting the supreme freedom of the unfathomable reasons that govern the work of God. For Lanfranco, therefore, the theologian cannot pretend to use the dialectic to insert even the divine within a rationalized cosmic system; however, if he uses the dialectic at the service of the truth of faith, and without ever opposing it, a correct *scientia fidei* can be achieved.

Lanfranc is also Berengar's great opponent on the eucharistic question: he recalled in this polemic that the conversion of bread and wine into the body and blood of Christ had been taught by the fathers through the centuries, and it was necessary to remain faithful to that teaching. In this exposition Lanfranc speaks of a conversion of earthly substances into the essence of the body of the Lord, and of the permanence of the appearances of the bread and wine; thus he clearly introduces the distinction between substance and species, which is fundamental to solving the problem. With him begins the ontological explanation of the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist, presence according to his essence or substance, therefore in the truth of his being, but without his manifesting himself in his natural phenomenal form. Less developed is instead his theology of the Mass: for Lanfranco the Mass not only represents the death of Christ, but makes present, under the visible species of bread and wine, the flesh and blood of Christ, the reality of the sacrifice (immolation *in sacrament*) that the Church offers to the Father. The doctrine on sacrifice is not directly addressed; however, a certain distinction is already observed between the preterite sacrifice of the cross, which is remembered, and the Eucharistic sacrifice, which the Church offers in the liturgy.

Lanfranco di Bec, in addition to his written work, is very important for his didactic activity at the head of the School of Bec: among his students are Anselm of Aosta, Pope Alexander II, the great canonist Ivo of Chartres.

**12. Ivo of Chartres (1040ca-1116)**

Disciple of Lanfranc at Bec, and co-disciple of Anselm of Aosta, from 1090 bishop of Chartres: he was the greatest canonist of the century, endowed also with a serious theological formation. His canonical work has influences on theology as to the systematization of the matter: taking the scripture as a model, he divides the canonical matter to be treated in faith and moral ("quaedam sic esse posita, ut tantum scirentur et crederentur [...]; quaedam vero sic esse iussa, ut observarentur"). This division will be resumed in the first *summae* of the beginning of the XII century.

He too, like Bertoldo of Constance, is important for the establishment of the method of concordance of discordant canons, which will then pass from law to theology.

In addition to this, Ivo of Chartres has transmitted to the theologians of the twelfth century a large amount of patristic quotations, divided by topics.

Finally, his *Decree* has theological parts:

- Book I contains a treatise on dogmatics including the doctrine of the one and triune God, Christology and soteriology, eschatology and predestination, and baptism. In it one finds many quotations from councils, from the papal magisterium, from some of the fathers (such as Augustine and Fulgentius of Ruspe), and from medieval theologians such as Raban Maurus.
- Book II has a long section on the Eucharist.
- Book V deals extensively with the primacy of the Roman Church: it is, however, moderate in its assertions of papal supremacy
- Book XVII has as its subject the three theological virtues.

**13. Anselm of Aosta (1033/34-1109)** [*Doctor magnificus*].

He was born near Aosta into a noble family; he began his studies at the Abbey of Fruttuaria; at the age of 27, after the death of his mother and because of misunderstandings with his father, he left home and went to France. In 1060 he entered the Benedictine monastery of Bec, where he became prior (1063), then abbot (1078). In 1093 he succeeded Lanfranc of Bec as archbishop of Canterbury. In his English period, because of clashes with King William II the Red about the rights of the Church, he was exiled twice: 1097-1100 and 1103-1106 (strong supporter of the Gregorian Reform and the freedom of the Church, he is however the bearer of a more theological and less juridical ecclesiology). During his periods of exile he lived in Italy and France.

He is the greatest theologian of monastic theology, of which he is considered the last exponent and the link with scholastic theology.

He argues for the usefulness of the intellect deepening the truths of faith: *fides quaerens intellectum*. With the intellect, one can offer rational demonstrations of the datum of faith, *rationes necessariae* that explain its intrinsic rationality, presupposing faith: and in theological works it is important for Anselm not only to rely on *auctoritates*, but also to search with his own intelligence for such *rationes*. It should be noted that for Anselm, the truths of faith are rational not in the sense that reason can find them with its own strength, but that reasoning correctly, it does not find them contrary, but rather requires them for the correct explanation of reality.

Starting from the truths set forth in Scripture, Anselm embarks on a true intellectual quest in order to arrive at the intelligence of faith; he always begins with faith, but uses reason to discover the senses that are still obscure. For this reason, at times Anselm's works do not have the form of a complete exposition of a series of truths, but rather that of a true and proper intellectual search, made up of questions and attempts at answers.

In a certain sense we can say that Anselm does not seek to understand in order to believe: faith is the basis; he believes in order to understand, and then he applies reason to understand even better.

In him a clear step from the *sacred page* to theology takes place; he also has very clear the distinction between theology (systematization of revelation) and philosophy (rational explanation of the universal order).

Moreover, his doctrine is the expression of a spiritual experience of the truth, which makes it possible to know and understand it; but this does not mean that his theology is merely mystical or sapiential; once the spiritual experience is established, it is then a matter of preparing a true intellectual work.

- In the two works Monologion (1076) and Proslogion (1078) he deals with the existence and nature of God and the divine attributes, the relationship between God and creatures, the supreme good and freedom. In the first he demonstrates the existence of God with four *a posteriori* proofs, similar to the five ways of St. Thomas Aquinas:
- There must be a good for which all good things are good;
- there must exist the greatest being that can exist, from whom the whole order of things receives greatness;
- there must be a reality from which all existing things derive their existence;
- entities are ordered according to a scale of perfection, for which there must be a supreme and fully perfect nature, and that makes it possible for the things that exist to be part of it.

Thus, with an Augustinian and Platonic attitude, Anselm accepts the principle of causality and merges it with that of participation. It is interesting that all this discourse is carried out on a philosophical level, without using Revelation. The *Monologion* is a summary of a series of lectures given to the monks of the Bec, when he was its prior.

Moreover, in the *Monologion*, Anselm "makes a meaningful adaptation and formulation of Augustinian Trinitarian theory, grounded in psychological terrain," thus contributing to its dissemination and adoption by First and Second Scholasticism" (M. Grabmann).

In the second work (which has as its subtitle *Fides quaerens intellectum*) he uses the famous so-called ontological argument (so called by Kant), in which he starts from the very idea of God, within which the necessity of his existence must be placed. In summary, Anselm's reasoning in the *Proslogion* unfolds as follows: even those who deny the existence of God admit that God is the entity of which it is not possible to think of a greater entity (*id quo maius cogitari nequit*). But consequently one must also admit his existence, because otherwise one could think of an entity that, in addition to the attributes recognized as proper to God, also possessed that of existence, and therefore was greater than him.

- De grammatico (1085)
- De veritate (1085)
- De casu diaboli (1085)
- De libertate arbitrii (1085): takes up the theme, already debated in the ninth century, of predestination; freedom for Anselm is not the possibility of choosing between good and evil, because even God, the angels and the blessed in heaven are free. He defines freedom as *potestas servandi iustitiam*, or as *potestas servandi rectitudinem voluntatis propter ipsam rectitudinem*. So he sees the perfection of freedom above all as freedom from sin (sin = less freedom); freedom cannot do evil and cannot be understood as *potentia peccandi et non peccandi* since it is ordered to the good. Instead, this possibility is proper to free will, the importance of which, however, is underestimated by Anselm.
- Epistola de incarnatione Verbi (1094): a polemical work against Roscellino, who, as a nominalist in philosophy, taught tritheism in theology, arguing that, otherwise, it should be admitted that the Father and the Holy Spirit became incarnate together with the Son. Anselm responds that Trinitarian dogma implies a distinction between persons and nature, a distinction that cannot be understood unless we overcome nominalism. The persons/nature distinction allows us to understand that persons are opposed to each other because of their relationships: for example, the one who generates is different from the one who is generated; but by virtue of their nature, persons remain one. Therefore, it can be said that it is the Son who became incarnate, and not the other two persons, without impairing the unitary character of the divine nature.
- Cur Deus homo (1098): treatise on soteriology. Theory of vicarious substitution; sin robs God of his honor and can be repaired only by means of a satisfaction of that nature that man is incapable of providing on his own: Christ, on the other hand, being man and God, can offer such reparation, which he does by his passion and death. This theory, even if it remains a little too juridical, on the other hand refutes and overcomes definitively the early medieval theory of the rights that the devil would have acquired over man. The *Cur Deus homo* is perhaps the best work of Saint Anselm.
- Liber de conceptu virginali et originali peccato (1100): deals with the theme of original sin in great depth and breadth: compared to Scotus Eriugena, Anselm is much closer to St. Augustine and less influenced by Greek patristics. He also deals with this theme in *Cur Deus homo*. His thought starts from the consideration of the misery of the condition of fallen man: well, this condition cannot come directly from God, but must originate in some abuse of man, precisely in original sin. Saint Anselm's greatest merit, however, lies in a further clarification of the essence of original sin, which he distinguishes from concupiscence: Saint Augustine, and above all his disciples, tended in fact to identify the two concepts. For the Archbishop of Canterbury, however, original sin is not concupiscence, but the deprivation of the justice that every man must possess, that is, of the primitive justice in human nature; furthermore, Saint Anselm asserts that this lack of justice resides in the will of man, and not in the movements of concupiscence in themselves. Even with regard to the transmission of original sin, Anselm differs from the *doctor gratiae*: the cause is not in vicious concupiscence, but in the will and solidarity of nature of every man with Adam. St. Anselm embraces, even if more moderately, the ultra realist theory of Scotus, and then argues that all human nature was as concentrated in the progenitors, and every human being was present in Adam *seminaliter*; it is not that we have all personally sinned - he points out - but "de illo futuri eramus et tunc facta est illa necessitas ut cum essemus peccaremus". Concupiscence has therefore only a function of *conditio sine qua non*, for its contribution in producing a person, but it is not the cause of original sin. As far as the consequences are concerned, Saint Anselm does not propose anything substantially different from the theologians who preceded him: it must be remembered, however, that he decidedly sustains the permanence of free will in man, even if he recalls that man alone cannot recover his lost justice. With regard to the fate of children who die without baptism, he thinks, like St. Augustine, that they are punished with hell, but he opens to further developments because of his insistence that original sin in children is less serious than actual mortal sins. In addition, Anselm's texts seem to accept the Immaculate Conception (following a constant English theological tradition), while others reject it, preferring to think that Our Lady was completely purified when she accepted to become the mother of Jesus.
- Meditatio redemptionis humanae (1100)
- De processione Spiritus Sancti (1107): written at the request of Pope Paschal II, after Anselm had participated in the Synod of Bari in 1101, at the time of his first exile. In controversy with the Orthodox on the *Filioque*, he affirms that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, and shows the scriptural foundations of this statement: this doctrine is therefore not at all heretical, as the Greeks claim.
- Epistola de sacrificio azymi et fermentati (1107): controversy with the Orthodox (Eucharistic matter)
- De concordia praescientiae et praedestinationis et gratiae Dei cum libero arbitrio (1108): deals with the theological problem human freedom // divine foreknowledge; human freedom // divine grace. On the subject of foreknowledge, inspired by Boethius, he argues that it is a mistake to consider it as prior in time. Anselm posits two wills in man, one toward his own happiness, the other toward righteousness; to will with righteousness requires grace, which man can receive only from God. In addition to this prevenient grace, without which there can be no justice, there is subsequent grace. For man does not lose his freedom through grace: his will must give its consent to good or evil, but in this choice it also has the help of grace to direct it toward righteousness. Therefore, in order to do good, both elements, grace and freedom, are required: "gratia et liberum arbitrium non discordant sed conveniunt ad justificandum et salvandum hominem".

Anselm's Method:

*fides (*credo)→reason of faith (its coherence)→*species*→→: final vision of the mysteries of faith.

Strangely enough, in the twelfth century St. Anselm will be little remembered by theology, to be recovered later, in the thirteenth, especially by Franciscan theology. However, he left a great inheritance both methodological and content, and many successful formulations: just to give an example, his is the famous Trinitarian formula "in Deo omnia sunt unum ubi non obviat relationis oppositio".

Anselm is, however, a first class thinker: his theological speculations are very deep and accurate, which is even more surprising if you think of the limited philosophical tools at his disposal. As Hasse wrote: "Anselm stands halfway between Thomas and Augustine, and only with these two is he to be compared, for his place is among the greatest thinkers. But Augustine and Thomas are personalities who complete a process, Anselm is a personality who gives a beginning. Augustine in him rejuvenates himself to establish the foundation on which Thomas Aquinas will build."

**14. Odon of Cambrai (d. 1113)**

Exponent of the current of the dialectics. Disciple of Anselm of Aosta.

Born in Orleans; directed the schools of Tournai; in 1092 he founded the hermit community of St. Martin, which he then aggregated to the Cluniac order. In 1105 he became bishop of Cambrai.

For Oddone, dialectics is the introduction to theology.

He wrote a *De peccato originali*, in which he follows in the footsteps of his master; among other things, he is the first, after Saint Augustine, to write a treatise entirely and solely devoted to the theme in question: the *De peccato originali*, to be precise. With regard to the definition of original sin, he closely follows that of Saint Anselm of *privatio iustitiae debitae*, being therefore a deficiency rather than a substance; he also devotes much space to the question of transmission, comparing the creationist thesis with the translationist one, and showing the reasons in favor of the former.

To explain original sin he argues that the soul in its God-made creation is new in person, but not in nature.
