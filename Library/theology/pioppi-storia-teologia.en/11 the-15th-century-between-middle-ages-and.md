## The 15th Century: Between Middle Ages and Modern Age {#pioppi-storia-teologia.11}
**1. General Features**

Italian Humanism, which spread to the rest of Europe (cultural apogee of Italy): anthropocentrism and cult of classicism. This cultural movement, although innovative, should not be considered as a completely radical change, especially with regard to the Catholic faith. Although it had a very different way of presenting itself and acting from the medieval one, the humanist movement was always an expression of a deeply Christian world. In fact, it is often guided by the popes themselves. Lorenzo Valla, after his investigation on the donation of Constantine (*De falso credita et ementita Constantini donatione*), is called to work at the papal court. Development of nepotism. The problem of the reform of the Church *in capite et in membris becomes* more and more pressing, without, however, finding an effective solution.

Important dates:

- 1414-1418: Council of Constance, which puts an end to the Great Western Schism; development of conciliarist ecclesiology
- 1438: Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges: birth of Gallicanism
- 1431-1445: Council of Basel-Florence and ephemeral union with the Greeks; brief Basel Schism (1439-1449): conciliarists elect Pope Felix V, the last antipope.
- 1453: fall of Constantinople; flight to Italy of numerous Greek scholars. Ends the War of the Hundred Years between England and France
- 1492: arrival of Christopher Columbus in America (throughout the century there are voyages of exploration of the Portuguese, and then also of the Spanish, on the African coasts).
- 1498: Vasco da Gama reaches Calicut, India.

There are four important areas in theology:

- The resumption of Thomistic studies
- The prolongation of the dominance of nominalism
- Economic and Social Moral Studies
- Ecclesiology

**THE TOMISM**

Resumption of Thomism; at the end of the century, in Germany, the *Summa* began to be commented on in the faculties of theology, until in the 1500s, with the School of Salamanca, it definitively replaced Peter Lombard's *Sententiae.*

**2. John Capreolo:** 1380-1444

French Dominican. *Thomistarum princeps*

He writes the *Defensiones theologiae divi Thomae Aquinatis*, based on the following outline:

following Peter Lombard's commentaries on the *Sentences* (written by almost every theologian of the time, as commentary on the *Sentences* was one of the core courses of theology faculties):

1. presentation of Tommaso's thesis
2. presentation of opposing theses (scotists, occamists)
3. solution (in favor of Tommaso)

**3. Dionysius of Leeuwen:** 1402-1471

Flemish Carthusian monk. *Doctor ecstaticus*. He studies to Cologne, center of the German Thomism. It enters the Chartreuse of Roermond and in 1433 it becomes regent. In 1451-1452 accompanies the papal legate Cardinal Niccolò Cusano in the visit to the provinces of the Meuse and the Rhine. He founds the Charterhouse of Hertzogenbusch.

He wrote more than 200 works, including:

- Philosophical: comments to Boethius;
- Theological: commentaries on Lombard's *Sentences;*
- dogmatic-ascetic: in these it depends a lot on Areopagita
- comments to the pseudo-Dionysius,
- *De contemplatione*,
- *De donis Spiritus Sancti*,
- *De fonte lucis ac semitis vitae*:
- *Summa fidei orthodoxae*: synthesis of the thought of Thomas Aquinas, by Dionysius himself defined "medulla operum sancti Thomae".

**4. Peter of Bergamo:** †1482

Italian Dominican; professor in the Dominican Studio in Bologna. Elaborates a general synopsis of the works of Thomas Aquinas: *Tabula aurea* (1475), divided into two parts:

- *Index universalis in omnia opera D. Thomae de Aquino*: an index of subjects that would make it easier to search by themes within the writings of the *doctor communis*.
- *Concordantia locorum doctoris Angelici quae sibi invicem adversari videntur*: this part is more doctrinal and aims to give consistency to the work of Aquinas, seeking harmony between points of his thought at first glance discordant.

**5. Dominic of Flanders: †1479**

Flemish Dominican who taught in Italy, in Pisa, Florence and Bologna. He is the greatest exponent of Italian Thomism of the '400. He wrote the *Summa divinae philosophiae*. Like Peter of Bergamo, he always defends the maximum coherence and concord in the discordant passages of Aquinas. What instead he doesn't do with Albertus Magnus, of whom he presents with great finesse and intelligence the different solutions and positions, not always in agreement, within his huge work.

**6. Peter Schwarz:** †1482, in Latin also called Petrus Niger

Wrote the *Clypeus thomistarum*

**7. Peter Crockaert:** †1514

Flemish Dominican

He taught in Paris, where he was master of Francisco de Vitoria (founder of the School of Salamanca), and where he began to substitute Aquinas' *Summa* for Lombard's *Sententiae.*

**8. Vincenzo Bandello:** 1435-1506.

Dominican and rigorous Thomist. Inquisitor in Bologna and vicar general of the Lombardy Province of his order. He became strongly involved in the dispute of the Immaculate Conception. In 1476 Pope Sixtus IV, Franciscan, approved the office of the Immaculate Conception composed by the Franciscan Leonardo di Nogarola: this triggered a controversy in Italy between the Franciscan and Dominican theologians, the latter very much against the office, for a corporate defense of Thomas Aquinas. Bandello was the one who was most involved in the dispute, with the *Tractatus de singulari puritate et praerogativa conceptionis salvatoris nostri Iesu Christi* and the *Liber ducentorum et sexaginta sanctorum, virginem Mariam in originali peccato fore conceptam dicentium, dicta continens*. The dispute ended with the intervention of Sixtus IV who in 1483 condemned those who considered it sinful to celebrate this office.

**9. SCOTISM**

The most important of these was the school of the University of Padua, whose greatest exponent was the Franciscan **Francesco della Rovere** (1414-1484), who later became Pope Sixtus IV. To remember also the Bosnian Juraj Dragisic (1450-1520), he too Franciscan, better known by the Italianized name of **Giorgio Benigno Salviati**, formed in Padua and rather cosmopolitan character.

**10. Recovery of the PLATONISM,** to work above all

- of Cardinal **Giovanni Basilio Bessarione** (1403-1472): Greek, advocate of the union of Florence: he wrote a *De Spiritus Sancti processione*, and an *In calumniatorem Platonis*, in which he compares Plato and Aristotle and points out what of their thought can be used by Christian theology and defends Plato from the criticism of George of Trebizond.
- and **Marsilio Ficino** (1433-1499): with great foresight he realizes that a separation between faith and philosophy is taking place; he wants therefore to reform *sapientia* and *religio* together in order to lead them back to harmony, within a unique sapiential experience. Tries therefore with Platonism to formulate a theodicy that is acceptable to philosophers, combining a *pious philosophia* with a *docto religio*; in this construction also tries to present in a accorde way Plato and Aristotle. Prominent among his works is the *Theologia platonica*.

Close to them we find **Giovanni Pico, Count of Mirandola** (1463-1494): a noble layman who died at a young age, endowed with a superior intelligence and an uncommon ability to learn and memorize. True example of humanist, in the *Conclusiones nongentae* he presented a philosophical-theological synthesis of ancient and modern knowledge, Jewish, Arab, pagan and Christian: there is therefore a synthesis of Jewish thinkers with also cabalistic elements, of Persian scholars, of Hermes Trismegistus, of Greek philosophy, with particular attention to Neoplatonism, of Arab philosophers, and in the Christian field of Pseudo-Dionysius, Albertus Magnus, Thomas Aquinas, Duns Scotus and other scholastics. The *Conclusiones* were however condemned for containing heretical doctrines together with the orthodox ones, without however serious consequences on the person of Pico della Mirandola. His work is characterized by a great anthropological optimism, which, however, goes hand in hand with a theocentric humanism. He also wrote a *De hominis dignitate*, which represents the highest document of the humanistic speculation on man: he, being created in the image and likeness of God, is a proportional medium between God and the cosmos.

**NOMINALISM:** the theological predominance of this current continues in the '400.

**11. Jean Gerson:** 1363-1429.

He is called the *Doctor christianissimus*.

French. Born in Reims into a peasant family. Became chancellor of the University of Paris in 1395. Moderate Occamist.

He re-evaluates the theology of St. Bonaventure against the rationalizing tendency of Aristotelian scholasticism; and he presents *modern devotio* against heterodox mystical tendencies.

Works: *Theologia mystica*; *De consolatione theologiae*; *De unitate Ecclesiae*.

For his opposition to the rising Gallicanism, in defense of papal power, he must leave France and goes into exile in Austria. In ecclesiology he is moderately conciliarist: he admits the power of the council on the pope only of judicial nature, when it comes to judge his legitimacy to less. In any case it does not recognize the popes the impossibility of falling into error.

In *De consolatione theologiae he* is inspired by Boethius' *De consolatione philosophiae; the* work is written in exile; theology is presented as consolation for the wounds of the divisions of Christendom, caused at times by the autonomy of philosophy. Theology is superior to philosophy, reaching a deeper, intuitive, contemplative knowledge of truth, which it then, however, must express using philosophical tools.

**12. Gabriel Biel:** 1410ca-1495

German Augustinian. Nominalist and moderate occamist. Studies in Heidelberg, Erfurt and Cologne.

Close to the circles of the Brothers of Christian Life and *modern devotion*. Vicar general and cathedral preacher in the Diocese of Mainz, then professor at Tübingen. He writes the *Epitome pariter et collectorium circa IV libros Sententiarum*.

In the Mariological field she is an immaculist.

In sacramental theology it follows a median path between Thomistic causalism and Occamian occasionalism: the sacraments are the *sine qua non cause* of the sacramental effect, but not by their own nature, but by voluntary divine institution.

On grace: Biel strongly emphasizes that man's salvation depends more on divine grace than on human will; man cannot, by his own strength and works, merit *de condigno* salvation: it is God who, by reason of the covenant he has made with man, saves him *de congruo*. is therefore very important for its influence on the thought of Martin Luther (and also on that of Philip Melanchthon), he too a German Augustinian, not much later. In general, in the Germanic Augustinian world of the fifteenth century, the idea prevails that there is no causal link between the *habitus gratiae* in man and justification by God, but only a coincidence in the design of God, which is always totally different, different and free.

In ecclesiology, he is an assertor of the fullness of the pope's spiritual jurisdiction.

In moral theology he develops extensively the themes concerning the virtue of justice, especially those concerning the practice of trade and commerce.

**Johannes Geiler of Kaisersberg**, a preacher at the cathedral of Strasbourg, was close to Biel in his sensibilities and attitudes: he was a moderate Occamist, but maintained a prudent link with tradition. Able to make even the most complex theological concepts understandable in his preaching, he was strongly interested in the reform of the Church. He wrote extensively on justification, emphasizing God's omnipotence and mercy, but at the same time inviting the faithful to act as if salvation depended on their own efforts, especially through prayer, which predisposes to grace. Although critical of the theological value of the decretals, he nevertheless had admiration for the great scholastics of the golden age, such as William of Auxerre, Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventure of Bagnoregio, and John Duns Scotus. He also showed great respect, among his contemporaries, for Gabriel Biel.

**13. ALBERTISM**

In contrast to nominalism, there is also another current of the via antiqua that is related to Albertus Magnus rather than to Thomas Aquinas: it was present mainly in Cologne and its main exponent was the Flemish **Eimeric of Campo**. From Cologne albertism spread to Heidelberg and Krakow.

**MORAL THEOLOGY**

**14. Sant'Antonino di Firenze:** Antonino Pierozzi: 1389-1459,

Dominican, bishop of Florence from 1446 to 1459.

As a bishop in Florence, despite his friendship with Cosimo de' Medici, lord of the city, he did not hesitate to condemn the actions of the government detrimental to the freedom of citizens.

Deals a lot with issues of social morality (Florence great center of European finance in the '400).

He writes a *Chronicon*, from Adam to contemporary times: a historical work much influenced by theological conceptions; it includes vast biographical parts intended to be a moral example.

*Summa theologica (or Summa moralis)*:

- 1st pars:
- soul
- nature
- faculty
- sin
- law
- 2nd pars:
- capital vices: it dwells much on avarice, dealing with economic, mercantile, social, financial issues; it also studies wealth, factors of production, value, price, currency, property, labor contracts
- lie
- grades
- infidelity
- 3a pars: morals of the different states of man:
- married
- widowers
- soldiers
- different professions
- 4a pars:
- virtues
- gifts of the Holy Spirit

In addition to Antoninus of Florence, Saint **Bernardine of Siena** (1380-1444) dealt with these issues. Bernardino degli Albizzeschi, a Franciscan, was a great preacher, but also versed in theology. He was famous for spreading devotion to the Name of Jesus. In his preaching on moral issues shows well aware of the functioning of the economic system of the time: in fact, while condemning usury, recognizes as valid the concepts of "loss of earnings" and "emerging damage" that make it possible and lawful for the lender a certain honest gain.

**ECCLESIOLOGY:** great debate between conciliarists and anticonciliarists

**15. Theodoric of Niem:** †1495

Secretary of John XXIII, pope of the Pisan obedience. He is conciliarist: in fact, he argues that the council can depose the pope.

**16. Pierre d'Ailly:** †1420

Chancellor of the University of Paris; cardinal. Occamist.

He denies that the pope is more important than the council.

**17. Nicholas Cusanus:** (Nikolaus Krebs) 1405-1464

Bishop and Cardinal.

Theory of "learned ignorance": negative theology. *De docta ignorantia*, written in 1440, on his return from a trip to Constantinople. In 1449 he published the *Apologia della dotta ignoranza*, to defend himself from accusations of pantheism that had been addressed to him.

He develops a theology centered on Platonism and Augustinism, with a certain criticism of scholastic thought and its methods, and in a special way of Aristotelianism (in any case influences of Albertus Magnus and Duns Scotus persist in him). For example, Cusanus criticizes the Aristotelian notion of entity to define the divine substance; the concept of truth based on the principle of non-contradiction and third exculso; the idea that the process of motion cannot not have a beginning; the centrality of learning from the senses through abstraction.

For Cusano the theological discourse should rather flow through figures, enigmas, symbols, signs, similarities. This is inevitable, since man, part of a world that is contradictory and fragmented, must speak in theology of a God who is absolute unity, coincidence of opposites, inseparable identity, equality, proportion. This distance between man and God is recomposed by Christ, man and God; Cusanus' theology is therefore based on a Christological anthropocentrism: it is man who, through the revelation in Christ, is able to understand and transmit something about the inaccessible divinity. Precisely for this reason the theological discourse cannot be only the scholastic one, so rich in rational demonstrations.

In *De concordantia catholica* (1433) he advocates a moderate conciliarism, but after the schism of the Council of Basel, at which he was present, he will become anticonciliarist. He will then carry out important missions of ecclesiastical policy in Germany in favor of the Holy See.

Also in *De concordantia catholica he* discusses the idea of the possibility of union of all philosophies, theologies and religions in the Christian religion; he comes to recognize that the other religions were attempts to approach the divinity by human rationality, and that therefore each of them has something good, has a partial truth; if they were to rejoin the only true theology, the one that arises from the coincidence of human research with divine revelation (Christianity), then a concordance could be reached.

Similar themes are taken up in *De pace fidei*, a dialogue in which Italian, Greek, Indian, Islamic, Jewish, Bohemian Hussite and English Lollard intervene, to reach a final doctrinal reconciliation in the diversity of forms and rites, cultures and traditions.

He also writes, at the request of Pope Pius II, a refutation of Islam, the *Cribratio Alcorani*.

In 1436-1437 he devoted himself to astronomical studies, to study the reform of the calendar, and wrote *De reparatione kalendarii*.

**18. Juan de Torquemada:** 1388-1468

Spanish Dominican; cardinal. He was born in Valladolid and died in Rome. Studied in Paris. He was papal theologian at the Council of Basel-Ferrara-Florence-Rome.

He is the greatest ecclesiologist of the century. Anti-onciliarist, strenuous defender, in the Councils of Constance and Basel, of the superiority of papal power over the Council Fathers; he published several books of controversy on this subject:

- *Contra decreta concilii Costantiniensis*
- *Tractatus contra avisamentum quoddam Basileensium quod non licet appellare a conciliis ad papam*
- *Tractatus notabilis de potestate papae et concilii generalis auctoritate*
- *Flores sententiarum Divi Thomae de potestate papae.*

But his theology goes beyond dispute and presents a complete dogmatics on the Church.

*Summa de Ecclesia*: four books. Work dedicated to Pope Nicholas V

- 1. The Church: definition: "universitas fidelium quae unius veri Dei cultus, unius fidei confessione convenit".

He also talks a lot about the Church as the Body of Christ.

Application of the theory of the four causes to the Church:

- efficient: Christ and the Sacraments
- material: faithful
- finale: glory
- formal: unity with Christ through faith
- 2. The Roman primacy: Torquemada is also clearly anti-Conciliar here. He grants the pope supreme power in the Church; but, following Aquinas, he is an advocate of the distinction between temporal and spiritual power; therefore, the pope does not have direct temporal power over the whole world, but only indirect power, when required by his spiritual function as pastor. However, the pontiff in his spiritual power is the shepherd of the Lord's entire flock, the head of the mystical body of Christ, the prince of the universal Church, and is endowed with *infallibilitas*: the judgment of the Apostolic See cannot err in matters of faith and in those necessary for salvation. He defended the Roman primacy at the Council of Florence, in discussions with the Greeks.
- 3. The Councils: against conciliarism
- 4. Schisms and heresies

**HUMANISTIC THEOLOGY**

Humanistic culture also penetrated into theology, where it was distinguished by a certain criticism of scholasticism and a greater attention to philology and to scriptural and patristic sources. To be mentioned in this current:

**19. Ambrogio Traversari** (1386-1439), a Camaldolese monk from Florence, who works extensively on the re-proposal of the Fathers of the Greek Church, also in view and occasion of the dialogue with the Orthodox that preceded the Council of Ferrara-Florence.

**Other important theologians of the century**

**20. A** very interesting personage, in the theological panorama of the century, is **Paul of Santa Maria** (also called Paul of Burgos): he was born into a Jewish family in 1350 and was given the name of Selomó ha Leví: in 1380 he became Grand Rabbi of Burgos; in 1390 he converted to Catholicism. From 1391 to 1395 he studied theology in Paris. In 1403 he became bishop of Cartagena and in 1415 of Burgos. He was also grand chancellor of King John II of Castile. He wrote a commentary on Nicholas of Lira, the *Quaestiones XII de nomine divino tetragrammaton*, and a polemical work against Judaism.

**21.** Also worth mentioning is the English Carmelite theologian Thomas **Waldensis** (Thomas Netter, born Saffron Walden, 1377/80-1430). Provincial of the English Carmelites, he participated in the Councils of Pisa (1409) and Constance. He wrote important works against the heresy of Wicliff, emphasizing the risks of *sola scriptura* and the importance of tradition: the best known is the *Doctrinale antiquitatum fidei catholicae Ecclesiae*. In it he bases his refutation of Wicliff, not on scholastic theology and philosophy, but on biblical and patristic data, especially on Augustine; in fact, the Lollards considered Wicliff a new Augustine; Netter also skillfully uses Ambrose, Hilary, Basil, Jerome and Cassian. For all that, the work of Waldensis became an important patristic repertoire, later on, for the controversialist theologians, *primarily* Robert Bellarmine. Thomas Netter also devoted himself in practice to the repression of the lollards.

**22.** Another theologian who opposed the theories of the Lollards was the Welshman **Reginald Pecock** (1395-1460): bishop and member of the King's Privy Council, in the last years of his life he fell into disgrace, was accused of heresy and rebellion and died confined to Thorney Abbey. Unlike Netter, Pecock wrote his works in English: The *Donet*, *The Folewer to the Donet*, *The Reule of Chrysten Religioun*, *Repressor of Over Much Blaming of the Clergy*, *Book of Faith*; in spite of this, he appears in substance much more tied to the scholastic method to refute the errors of the Lollards. In his work there is a great optimism in the capabilities of reason and logic, and because of this he was convinced that heresy should be eradicated by reasoning rather than force. He believes that when reason speaks with a certain voice one must always follow it; when it doubts, one must turn to the guidance of the Church.
