## The 12th Century: Early Scholasticism {#pioppi-storia-teologia.8}
In this century we witness the great flowering of medieval civilization, a historical phenomenon that is called by medievalists the Renaissance of the twelfth century: after the difficult centuries of the Dark Ages, Christian Europe is the protagonist of a remarkable economic, political and cultural development.

Just think, by way of example for the economic and political sphere, of the flourishing of cities and trade, the prodigious development of the Italian maritime republics, the Crusades, the *Reconquista* in the Iberian Peninsula, the consolidation of the Norman kingdoms of England and Sicily, the great imperial project of the Hohenstaufen. Great development of the chivalrous ideal in the noble society.

As far as culture isconcerned - which returned from the monasteries to the cities - it is enough to remember that in the 12th century there was a rebirth of Roman and canonical law studies (Concordantia discordantium canonum, also called Decree, by Gratian), the Latin translation of Greek philosophical works. In the twelfth century, there was a rebirth of studies of Roman and canon law (*Concordantia discordantium canonum*, also called *Decree*, by Gratian), the translation into Latin of Greek philosophical works, the spread of *ars dictaminis*, through which Latin was purified of all its early medieval deformations, the maturation of the poem of chivalry and Provençal poetry, and the structuring of the *Frühscholastik.*

With the latter, the incipient scholasticism, wefind ourselves in front of the structuring of theology *through* the application of philosophy to the *sacred page*, in order to arrive at an orderly and reasoned exposition of the truths of faith: we are therefore witnessing the birth and the very first steps of Western scientific thought, the attempt to build a system of knowledge by causes; in this regard, the phrase of Honorius Augustodunensis is exemplary: "the exile of man is in ignorance, and his homeland is wisdom".

The weakness of this theology lies in the paucity of philosophical works then known, which are limited to Aristotelian *Logic Vetus*, Plato's *Menon* and *Phaedo*, along with the grammar of Donatus and Priscianus. This is the grammatical or terministic theology, which, based on the organization of the subject by Peter Lombard (*Sententiae in quattuor libros distinctae*) and on the method of Peter Abelard, seeks progress in theological knowledge through the logical and grammatical analysis of the sentences of the fathers or of the great theologians, in order to harmonize their differences and to deepen their meaning. In its theological work, it combines respect for tradition with decidedly innovative aspects (*tamquam nani super humeros gigantum*, said Bernard of Chartres): the reasonings are full of logical-grammatical exercises aimed at analyzing the value, content, and form of a given sentence. The importance of this theology in the history of thought lies therefore very much in the methodological aspects, as well as in the actual contents.

However, they began to translate the works of the Greeks, often through Arabic translations, thanks also to the opening of Europe to other worlds caused by the Crusades: already in the '80s of the century there was the translation of Aristotle's *Logic Nova*, and gradually also Metaphysics.

Thus, we have three aspects of twelfth-century theologythat prepare for the mighty development of the great scholasticism of the thirteenth century.

1. Structuring theological matter in an orderly fashion: the culmination of this work is Peter Lombard's *Sentences*.
2. The creation of an effective methodology ofknowledge by causes, through the application of grammar and logic to the *sacred page*: the most important character for this is Peter Abelard.
3. The translation of the works of the Greek philosophers, particularly the metaphysical works of Plato and Aristotle.

We can therefore say that, for the great medieval civilization, the IX-X centuries were childhood, the XI adolescence, the XII youth, the XIII maturity.

The home of theology in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries is France, and in particular Paris.

Birth and development of universities, promoted and encouraged by ecclesiastical authority: Paris (Sorbonne) for theology; Bologna for law; Salerno and Montpellier for medicine; Oxford for other sciences. Thus, we can clearly see how the Church promotes cultural development.

A positive consequence of the Eastern Schism was the fact that the authority to convene ecumenical councils passed from the Byzantine emperor to the pope: thus began the series of councils of the second millennium, all held in the West and convened and directed by ecclesiastical authority. In the twelfth century were celebrated:

- the First Lateran Council (1123) which sanctioned the end of the Investiture Struggle.
- the Second Lateran Council (1139): establishes the Truce of God
- Lateran Council III (1179): promulgates a new law for the election of the pope.

The Investiture Struggleceased in 1122, with the Concordat of Worms, ratified the following year by the First Lateran Council. A compromise was reached between the papacy and the emperor Henry V: for the nomination of bishops, the power of the pope in Italy and that of the emperor in Germany prevailed, but the emperor could not perform acts that implied canonical power (such as the delivery of the ring and the crosier), which remained the prerogative of the pope in Germany.

The struggle between the papacy and the empireflared up again with Emperor Frederick I Barbarossa: he wanted to impose real rule over Italy. The most important popes in this controversy were the English Adrian IV (1154-1159) and the Italian Alexander III (1159-1181). The struggle ended with the military defeat suffered by Frederick I in the battle of Legnano (1176) by the army of the Lombard League, followed by the peace of Venice (1177) and the Lateran Council III (1179).

New religious orders:

- Carthusians (1084): Saint Bruno
- Cistercians (1098), founded by Robert of Molesmes; St. Bernard: in 1150 they already had about 300 monasteries scattered throughout Europe
- Premonstratensians, who combined religious life with the pastoral care of souls (an anticipation of an aspect of the mendicant orders): founded by Saint Norbert in 1120;
- Trinitarians (1198), founded by St. John of Matha, who dedicated themselves to the ransom of Christian slaves in North Africa.

The **Crusades**

These were military expeditions, sponsored by the popes, whose participation was linked to a spiritual benefit (indulgence). To understand the phenomenon well, it should be framed within the Islamic aggression against Christian territories in an almost uninterrupted way since the seventh century: therefore, have the characteristic of wars in defense of Christianity, for centuries threatened by Islam, which, once recovered the domain of the Mediterranean thanks to the Italian maritime republics (Venice, Genoa, Pisa, Amalfi), brings the war in enemy territory. The proximate cause of the crusades are the harassment to which Christian pilgrims were subjected in the Holy Land by the Turks, who took over from the Arabs in the domain of the area in the eleventh century. So the Crusades were born with the idea of defending the pilgrims and then to regain the Holy Sepulchre.

The First Crusade, promoted by Urban II and led by Godfrey of Bouillon, composed mainly of French, Flemish and Italians, started in 1096 and ended in 1099 with the conquest of Jerusalem and the establishment of the Frankish States of the Holy Land: Kingdom of Jerusalem, County of Tripoli, Principality of Antioch, County of Edessa, Kingdom of Lesser Armenia, Kingdom of Cyprus.

In 1144 the Muslims conquered the County of Edessa: the Second Crusade wasorganized in response to this event, in 1147: it was promoted by Pope Eugene III, preached by St. Bernard of Clairvaux, and led by King Louis VII of France and the Roman Emperor Conrad III. Edessa was not reconquered, but the Islamic advance was stopped.

In 1187, the Christian army was destroyed at Hattin, near the Lake of Tiberias, by the armies of Saladin, who was thus able to retake Jerusalem. The Third Crusade(1187-1189) was then organized, with the participation of Philip II Augustus, King of France, Richard the Lionheart, King of England, and Frederick Barbarossa, Emperor of Rome: the expedition did not reconquer the Holy City, but saved the Frankish states from total *debellatio.*

The crusades had many positive aspects: They reduced Muslim raids in Europe; they favored internal peace in Europe (before a crusade could start, peace had to reign throughout the Christian world); they diverted outward a number of energies (landless nobles) that would otherwise have imploded within; they favored the mental and cultural openness of Europeans who came into contact with other rich civilizations; It is not correct to say that they created a climate of intolerance; in the Frankish states there was often a peaceful coexistence of different religions; wars were often fought with a chivalrous spirit on both sides (interesting is the Christian literary representation of Saladin as a great Muslim knight, noble and brave).

The crusades favored the rise of military orders: orders of warrior monks, who combined religious life with the defense of Christianity in the Holy Land: the most famous were the Hospitallers of St. John (later called the Knights of Malta), the Teutonic Knights and the Templars (who received their rule from St. Bernard).

**The theological environment of the 12th century**

Throughout the early Middle Ages theology was often reduced to the repetition and analysis of the texts of the *auctoritates*, and did not produce truly original thinkers, except in the moment immediately following the Carolingian Revival. The only exceptions to this panorama are in fact John Scotus Eriugena, who in the 9th century succeeded in composing an original and at the same time profound work, and perhaps the three main authors of the famous Eucharistic controversy of the 9th century.

It is in the eleventh century that we see a powerful rebirth of theological studies, with Lanfranc of Bec, Anselm of Aosta, the heresy of Berengar, the controversy between dialecticians and anti-dialecticians. The fact is that, in this century and then in the following one, next to the usual *auctoritates*, i.e. the sacred scripture and the fathers, they began to quote and comment on texts of the *magistrates*; the *lectio* was gradually replaced by the *quaestio*, as Yves Congar explains: "c'est l'instrument logique aristotélicien qui a formé les esprits à l'emploi de plus en plus systématique et développé de la *quaestio*. Il s'agit alors, non de répondre simplement à quelque difficulté, mais d'un procédé méthodique d'élaboration de la connaissance : mis en présence de propositions opposées, l'esprit doit trouver une raison en faveur de l'un des termes de l'alternative, ou quelque distinction permettant d'attribuer à chacun sa part de vérité". The *sacred page* was replaced by the *sacred doctrine*; the first *summae* and the first books of *sententiae* began to appear: "depuis des siècles, la base de l'enseignement est constituée par le commentaire de texte [...]. La lecture du texte (*lectio*) peut aboutir à des gloses rédigées, qu'il faut se garder de considérer comme des œuvres d'un genre et d'un intérêt mineurs. Ces écoles sont loin de présenter un visage unique. Force est même de reconnaître qu'au point de vue des méthodes Chartres est devancée par d'autres villes. À Laon, Anselme a mis au point la technique de la question. De son côté, Abélard a posé dans le *Sic et Non* les bases d'une réflexion critique et contradictoire. Hugues de Saint-Victor a défini les règles de l'exégèse biblique en privilégiant le sens littéral. [...] Toutes ces innovations rendront possible la composition, au siècle suivant, de manuels d'enseignement évolués, en particulier les sommes" (M. Lemoine).

From the simple copying of explanatory glosses of patristic texts, they passed to a deeper understanding of the problems, until they reached works of greater doctrinal density; moreover, they produced works that present a more ordered and reasoned structuring of the treated arguments, such as the *Sententiarum libri IV* by Pietro Lombardo, who divided the arguments to be treated making use of the schemes *res-signa*, *uti-frui*, exposed by Augustine of Hippo in *De doctrina christiana.*

In short, also from the theological point of view, the XI century, and especially the following one, represent a period of great effervescence and expansion: this development, particularly in the XII century, took place with the birth and growth of a series of schools, generally born in the ancient schools of the cathedrals of the cities, and in opposition to the monastic ones, around a series of *magistri*, a new figure that was developing at the same pace with the rebirth of the urban municipalities.

**Anselm of Laon**

One of the first schools was that of Laon, founded by Anselm of Laon (†1117), who as a young man had studied at Bec, under the influence of Anselm of Aosta: the books of *sententiae* of this school are the first to present an attempt to systematize and order the subject matter, moving away from the early medieval genre of the exegetical treatise. In these works, still based on collections of sentences of the fathers, and whose systematization of the theological matter is still rather rudimentary (they follow the articles of the Creed or the order of Sacred Scripture), one can already glimpse, however, a first form of theological dispute, which will later lead to the structuring of the *quaestio.* The importance of Anselm lies above all in having founded a true and proper school; he gathered around him a series of disciples, to whom he systematically expounded his thought: Among them were his brother Radulfo (†1136); Alberic of Reims (†1141, who was to direct the School of Reims from 1121 to 1136, before becoming archbishop of Bourges); **Honorius Augustodunensis** (a profound connoisseur of Scotus Eriugena's work and author of the *Elucidarium sive dialogus de summa totius christianae theologiae*, divided into three parts: God, man and redemption; evil, original sin and penance; eschatology); Hugh of Rouen (†1164); it should also be kept in mind that Abelard, Gilbert Porreta and William of Champeaux were also his disciples for some time. It must be admitted, however, that in Anselm of Laon, while the work of systematization is important, the use of reason in relation to *auctoritas* is still rather timid. Fruit of his work and his school are: the *Glossa ordinaria* (initiated by them, and not by Walafridus Strabo), the *Sententiae divinae paginae* and the *Sententiae Anselmi*.

At the beginning of the century, the School of Laon attracted many students from France, England, Germany and Italy. Its characteristics were a certain conservatism, a cautious use of *ratio* and dialectics with respect to the *auctoritates* (Church Fathers, ecclesiastical authorities), but at the same time a good preparation in the philosophical field. The result was a rather balanced theology.

**Peter Abelard**

The school of Peter Abelard (†1142) was marked by a powerful use of dialectics and reason, by a greater space given to ancient philosophy and by a certain reorganization of the *auctoritates of the* fathers, whose defects of imprecision, vagueness, and lack of terminological univocity are highlighted, not so much to deny their authority as to try to clarify their statements.

The importance of Abelard - who relies on Augustine of Hippo - and of his school lies therefore in the attempt to give to the *intelligence of faith* a real scientific methodological guise, which he partly borrows from the methodology of canon law of the period immediately preceding him (rules of conciliation): what it wants to add to the faith and to the scriptural datum is not so much oriented, as in the School of Saint Victor, to contemplation, as to the construction of a system that already has the requirements of science; the dialectic has two tasks: one critical, of defense, of demolition of the arguments of the enemies of the faith, the other constructive, aiming at a deeper understanding of dogma.

His innovative theological method is expounded especially in his works *Introductio ad theologiam* and *Sic et non*:

1. affirmation of *auctoritas*;
2. arguments that contradict it;
3. problem solution.

In *Sic et does not* present the methodology: when you find conflicting patristic *auctoritates* you have to:

1. check if these passages are authentic, i.e. they are not apocryphal, or the result of errors by amanuensis
2. check if the same author has not then denied this statement later, as in the case of the *Retractationes* of Saint Augustine
3. in the event of a discrepancy between ecclesiastical canons on the same subject, it is necessary to check: their purpose; the level of obligatory nature; the historical aspect, since over time some canons may cease to be in force
4. for discrepancies between patristic texts it will be necessary to check if different Fathers, using the same terms, do not intend to mean different concepts
5. if there is still a contradiction after all these checks, then one must compare the two *auctoritates*, studying them carefully and expressing a reasoned preference.

Obviously, these techniques apply to non-biblical Christian literature, and cannot be applied to sacred scripture.

The purpose of all this discourse is not to undermine the authority of the Fathers of the Church, but rather to offer a didactic and research method to encourage the solution of difficult cases and the advancement of theological knowledge.

A limitation of this methodology is not considering that the patristic passages should be studied in their context, keeping in mind the whole work, and not just isolated sentences; a criticism of the use of isolated sentences is missing; but perhaps such a criticism would have been impossible in the situation of the culture, philosophy and theology of the twelfth century.

Although Abelard and his disciples present some limits due to an excessive rationalism and to the lack of a complete theory of knowledge, we find ourselves in front of the premises of the theological thought of the following century, with its adaptation to the sacred doctrine of the Aristotelian concept of science. In any case, his enthusiasm for reason is almost never extreme, as he himself stated: "nolo sic esse philosophus ut recalcitrem Paulo. Non sic esse Aristoteles, ut secludat a Christo".

It is practically with Abelard that *quaestio is* added to *lectio* in teaching: *lectio* was the explained and commented reading of the sacred text (and then of the Lombard's *Sentences*); *quaestio* was instead a more creative exercise of weighing, evaluating different interpretations or consequences, trying to reconcile them or choosing one as right and explaining why.

To this school belong, among others, Master Roland, Master Omnebene, Robert Pulleyn, and Robert of Melun. In their general works they usually follow the subdivision *fides*, *charitas*, *sacramentum*, to organize the subject matter (according to the idea of Abelard, who in his *Introductio ad theologiam* stated: "tria sunt, ut arbitror, in quibus humanae salutis summa consistit, fides videlicet, charitas et sacramentum").

To Abelard and his disciples we probably also owe the use of the term "theology" in its current meaning. "Ce n'est que petit à petit que le mot Theologia [...] arriva sous l'influence d'Abélard à désigner dans les écoles une science distincte de l'exégèse" (J. De Ghellinck); "the term 'theology', to designate a synthesis of Christian doctrine, is first used by Abelard, who places it in the text of his first work 'Theologia christiana'" (F. van Steeberghen).

From the philosophical point of view, Abelard's critique of nominalism with respect to the question of universals is also important; at the same time, he rejects an extreme realism: for him, the universal has a real or extramental dimension, which he calls "state": but he does not succeed well in clarifying its definition; with his middle position between hyperrealism and nominalism, he nevertheless opens the door to the solution that will be expressed in the 13th century.

For morality, it must be said that Abelard grants great space and importance to intention, but he does this not in the current subjectivist manner, but as a sign of the search for a true interiority.

For Christology, Abelard advocated an ambiguous theory: he believes that Christ has a true and perfect human body, and a true and perfect human soul: but they are not directly united to each other, otherwise this would lead to a human person, thus to Nestorianism. Therefore the human body and soul of Christ are hypostatically assumed by the divine person in a separate manner. Elsewhere, however, he held that *hic persona quae Christus est* was not any of the three divine persons, and such a statement would smack very much of Nestorianism. In the *Introductio ad theologiam he* then states that "Deus igitur nec caro nec homo proprie dicendum est".

Finally, in his critique of Roscelin's trinitarian theory, Abelard assumed accents that might recall Sabellian modalism: in fact, he blurred the real difference between the three divine persons. He also questioned the equality of the nature of the Holy Spirit with that of the Father and the Son. He then affirmed that the Holy Spirit is the soul of the world, but at the same time he did not reach pantheistic conclusions from this theory. Finally, he thought that Greco-Roman writers, such as Plato, Seneca or Virgil, had some notion of the Trinitarian mystery. Bernard of Clairvaux had these doctrines condemned by the Synod of Soissons (1121).

With regard to the *communicatio idiomatum*, he believed that absolute names, such as "power" or "wisdom" could be preached about individual divine persons. The inflexible Bernard of Clairvaux also had these doctrines condemned by the Synod of Sens (1140).

Abelard was one of the most acute and original theologians and philosophers of the twelfth century; his merits are balanced by a certain self-satisfaction expressed by his abilities, by the merciless criticism against other scholars of the time less gifted than him, by the preference for dialectical subtlety over a solid patristic culture. Moreover, he failed to make a clear methodological demarcation between philosophy and theology.

He was also one of the most significant Latin poets of the Middle Ages for his hymns, and he is the only scholastic who wrote an autobiography, the *Historia calamitatum*. Grabmann defines him as follows: "the quantity and variety of his literary works speak of a superior talent who worked with ease and cultivated the most diverse fields of human knowledge even with relatively modest tools." On the other hand, it must also be said that he was more of a skilled dialectician than a profound theologian: in particular, he lacked a deep, assimilated, spiritual knowledge of Augustine and the Fathers of the Church.

**The Victorians**

In Paris, on the other hand, the School of St. Victor developed, founded by **William of Champeaux** (†1121), characterized by a biblical and patristic point of departure and by a mystical coloring; in particular, they are greatly influenced by Augustine, whom they try to place at the basis of their thought and method, albeit with the necessary adaptations. Without denying the value of dialectical and philosophical tools, the scholars of this school tend to relegate them to a secondary role; oriented towards contemplation, they criticize the fact that dialectics or the liberal arts are considered an end in themselves. More inclined to the sacred *page* than to *sacred doctrine, they* subordinate the role of the arts to that of theology, and are especially interested in morals, pastoral care and the exegesis of the sacred books; of notable importance are their studies on the ways of explaining and interpreting Sacred Scripture, through the three steps of *littera*, *sensus* and *sententia*.

The greatest work of this school is perhaps the *De sacramentis christianae fidei* composed by **Hugh of St. Victor** (of noble German family, 1096ca-1141) between 1132 and 1137, which is one of the first complete systems of dogmatics made in this historical period; the systematic works of the Victorians mostly follow the scheme based on the division between *creatio* and *restauratio*, that is *opus conditionis* and *opus restaurationis*: the first part deals with the mysteries before the coming of Christ (treatise on God, creation, original sin ....), the second those after it (Christology, sacraments ...). This work had a great influence on the scholasticism that followed it. It can be said to be the first wide-ranging *summa*, with an orderly, complete and profound exposition of dogmas, a very successful meeting point between patristics and scholasticism; it is a systematic exposition of the content of revelation, the result of a consistent personal reworking.

In Christology Hugh refutes the Abelardian theory, reaffirming against it the direct union of soul and body in the human nature of Christ.

According to Hugh, theological studies are closely linked to spiritual growth: the soul, dominated by the passions and attracted by sensible forms, forgets its nature: and therefore only through doctrine can it be restored to itself. This doctrinal wisdom is achieved through reason and revelation, forms of knowledge which Hugh succeeds in presenting with great harmony of mutual relationships. He shows a truly great passion for every kind of knowledge and wisdom.

Such great appreciation of Hugh of St. Victor for human knowledge appears in his work *Eruditio didascalica* (or *Didascalicon*), an introduction to the study of the liberal arts, in which we find a classification of human knowledge:

- Intelligence
- Theoretical
- Theology
- Mathematics
- Physics
- Practice
- Economy
- Policy
- Science
- Art of weaving
- Art of Navigation
- Agriculture
- Hunting
- Medicine
- Theater
- Logic
- Grammar
- Art of Discourse
- Demonstration Theory
- Rhetoric
- Dialectics.

In addition, within the framework of the theory of knowledge, Hugh of St. Victor develops the reflection on the knowledge of faith: for him, it exceeds reason, but it absolutely cannot be against reason.

Hugh of St. Victor is also very important for the sacramentary doctrine: he divides the sacraments into major and minor and, even if he does not reach the expression of the septenary, he offers a definition of sacrament that will be very close to the definitive one: he writes in fact in *De sacramentis christianae fidei*, that "sacramentum est corporale vel materiale elementum foris sensibiliter propositum ex similitudine repraesentans et ex institutione significans et ex sanctificatione continens aliquam invisibilem et spiritualem gratiam". Hugh, finally, is a theologian who combines in himself a great intellectual preparation with a deep spiritual life and a conduct of high moral level. A. Harnack has considered him the most influential theologian of the twelfth century.

A work that denotes a profound influence of Hugh of St. Victor is the *Summa Sententiarum* of **Otto of Lucca**: however, it has a different structure, in seven books:

1. Trinity and Incarnation
2. Creation in general and angelology
3. Exameron, creation of man and original sin
4. Moral (Decalogue)
5. Baptism
6. Confirmation, Penance, Eucharist, Extreme Unction
7. Wedding

In it we find for the first time the ternary *sacramentum tantum*, *res et sacramentum*, *res tantum* in the treatment of the Eucharist

In addition to the founder and Hugh, this school counts among its major representatives such figures as Goffredo of San Vittore (†1194), and the Scotsman **Richard of San Vittore** (†1173). Of the latter, a disciple of Hugh, is very famous the work *Libri sex de Trinitate*, in which work "a laissé une théologie trinitaire supposant la thèse augustinienne de l'identité de l'âme et de ses facultés et fondée sur une analyse de l'amour" (Y. Congar). He attempts to explain the non-irrationality of the Trinity, with the argument that God must also have in Himself the perfection of love, and this leads to postulate a community of persons so that it can be in God Himself all love, without necessarily having to direct it out of itself. Richard also seeks a harmony between mysticism and rationality in theological knowledge: "ex dilectione itaque manifestatio et ex manifestatione contemplatio et ex contemplatione cognitio" (*De Trinitate*, Prologus).

In the field of spiritual theology, Richard of Saint Victor left us three very interesting works: two mystical works - *Benjamin Major* and *Benjamin Minor* - and a commentary on the Song of Songs.

Important for scientific methodology is his *Liber excerptionum*, in many ways dependent on Hugh's *Didascalicon.*

Richard is the author of the twelfth century closest to Anselm of Aosta in terms of method, style, content and attitudes. In the line of this great theologian and in consonance with the environment of the Victorians, Richard affirms in equal measure the importance of philosophical speculation for a good theology, but also that of the moral purity and sanctity of life of the theologian.

Towards the end of the century, the School of San Vittore developed a current that was very hostile to the application of dialectics to revelation, the principal exponent of which was **Gualtiero di San Vittore**, famous for his book *Contra quattuor labyrinthos Franciae*, in which he vehemently attacked Abelard, Gilberto Porreta, Peter Lombard and Peter of Poitiers. In addition to them, he also expresses his disapproval of Aristotle and John Damascene. It is therefore a resumption of the anti-dialectical motives of the eleventh century: a warning, albeit exaggerated, to reduce theology to a mere rational explanation, neglecting the aspect of mystery.

In the same line of criticism of dialectics, albeit more moderately, are the German brothers Arno († 1175) and Gerhoh († 1169) of Reichersberg.

These voices were added to that of Bernard of Clairvaux, who, during the first half of the century, had repeatedly warned against the excesses of the dialectical method, at times excessively harsh.

About the middle of the century, with the arrival in the West of Aristotle's *Logic Nova*, the development of scholastic theology enjoyed a further decisive impetus. The *Organon*, now known in its entirety, entered the sphere of developing logico-linguistic interests: it proved to be an excellent tool for clarifying the problems that arose from the equivocal use of words. The resulting techniques were used on a large scale to determine the meaning of the *sacred page*. At the same time there was a considerable development of the liberal arts, especially grammar, which also had a considerable influence on the study of the *sacred page*. Thus the didactic method was also changing: people began to pose a series of speculative problems that simple reading did not present, and they began to apply to theology the techniques of other disciplines, as can be seen clearly in Peter Abelard's famous *Sic et non.* All this often led the theologians of this era to subtle disquisitions of grammatical analysis, which acquired in their works a significant theological significance.

**Peter Lombard**

Fundamental is the work of Pietro Lombardo (†1160) and his school. Lombardo was born in Novara around 1095: he studied in Lucca, Bologna, Reims, Paris: here he attended the School of San Vittore, then, around 1140, he settled as an independent teacher in the Cathedral School of Paris. He died bishop of this city, but had a very short episcopate (1159-1160).

His main work is the *Sententiae in quattuor libros distinctae*: it is the best attempt to systematize the theological matter of the time, so that it was the main textbook of theological studies until the XVI century.

Karl Werner - Austrian theologian and historian of theology of the nineteenth century - in one of his works of 1858 (*Der hl. Thomas von Aquino*), explained the success of the *Magister sententiarum as* follows: "it is easy to trace the reasons why Lombard's work gained such authority. There was a widespread need to be able to see, in a systematic context, the whole of ecclesiastical theology in the form it had taken up to that time. Pietro Lombardo offered this possibility, guaranteeing the maximum possible objectivity, with competence but without prolixity, taking into account the highest patristic authorities and having Augustine as his base, excluding any superfluous digression. A sort of grandiose serenity runs through the whole work; remarkable are the coherence and immediacy of the conception and of the exposition, which offers not so much research as a mature judgement on the most reliable previous studies; it, with a meditated choice, highlights precisely what is most instructive and useful and - moreover - that depth of feeling related to mysticism which is proper to the Augustinian conception and which could not fail to make an impression on any age; all this made the *Sentences* a classic of its kind".

Many later theologians wrote commentaries on his Sentences: e.g., Bonaventure of Bagnoregio, Thomas Aquinas, John Duns Scotus, William of Ockham, and Gabriel Biel. In this work Lombard, moreover, makes a balanced synthesis between the feeling, attitude and method of the Victorians, and those of the Abelardian school. The Sentences are divided according to the Augustinian scheme of *uti-frui*:

- Book I: *res quibus fruendum est*: treatise on God (together *de Deo uno et trino*)
- Book II: *res quibus utendum est*: angelic and material creation, grace, original sin, present sin
- Book III: *res quibus fruuntur et utuntur*: Christology and soteriology
- Book IV: *signa*: sacraments

"Les Sentences sont entrées dans le régime scolaire qui est comme l'épine dorsale de la Scolastique; elles ont été commentées comme un classique du vivant de leur auteur (v. 1157), et, à partir de 1215 (à Paris), par tous les futurs maîtres. Leur succès est essentiellement celui d'un manuel scolaire, avec ce que le genre comporte de via media [...]. Pour la première fois, on avait un instrument d'enseignement livrant les éléments de chaque question selon une distribution systématique imparfaite, mais commode" (Y. Congar).

"Vers 1150, à Paris, devenue le centre intellectuel de la chrétienté occidentale, après trois ou quatre siècles d'un pénible enfantement théologique, une oeuvre vient au jour, dont la célébrité dépasse assurément la valeur, mais dans laquelle il est permis de voir le point d'arrivée de toutes les tentatives précédentes : en même temps, c'est de cette oeuvre que prennent leur point de départ tous les travaux ultérieurs. Nous voulons parler des Quatuor Libri Sententiarum de Pierre Lombard [...] que le XIIIe siècle place déjà à côté de la Bible [...] dans l'enseignement de la théologie" (J. de Ghellinck).

Lombard's sources are mainly Augustine (a thousand citations) and, to a much lesser extent (between 15 and 100 citations): Ambrose, Hilary of Poitiers, Gregory the Great, Jerome, Isidore of Seville, Bede the Venerable, John Damascene, John Chrysostom. They generally do not come from a full reading of the patristic texts, but rather from chains, and especially from the *Ordinary Gloss* and the *Decree* of Gratian.

Lombard is important for the definition of sacrament, in which he inserts the idea of causality, taking a step forward with respect to Hugh of St. Victor: the sacrament of the new law is therefore a sensitive sign instituted by Christ that *confers* grace (and not only *contains it*, as Hugh had stated). It also definitively resolves the question of whether or not marriage was a sacrament, in a positive sense.

In the Trinitarian sphere it should be remembered that the Novarese identifies the Holy Spirit with charity: "charitas Spiritus Sanctus est, qui operatur in singulis prout vult".

In addition to the *Sentences*, his work of *Commentaries on the Pauline Epistles* was very famous.

Other exponents of this school are Gandolfo of Bologna (†1186; Gandolfo's *Sententiae*) and Peter Manducator (†1178), Master Martin, and later Prepositino of Cremona (†1210) and Stephen Langton (†1228). This school stands in a balanced middle ground between the excessive rationalism of the proponents of the dialectic and the extreme conservatives who rejected its use in theology *altogether*, representing a good combination of patristic and biblical authorities with the speculative rationalism advocated by the new generation of scholastic theologians; it is also characterized by a considerable influence of Augustine of Hippo, who is generally the most cited *auctoritas.*

The most prominent works of this school were, in addition to the very famous *Quattuor Libri Sententiarum* (1155-1157) of the founder, are:

- the *Libri V Sententiarum* of **Peter of Poitiers** (†1205);
  - Book 1: deals separately with God (existence, omnipotence, will, science and foreknowledge, predestination and reprobation), and with the Trinity: thus, he seems to be the first author to introduce the separation between the treatise *de Deo uno* and that *de Deo trino*
  - Book 2: creation and fall of angels; *hexaëmeron*; creation of man and woman; original sin; sin in general; free will
  - 3rd book: virtues; the theological virtues; *virginitas* and *perseverantia*; grace, justification, merit; repentance and confession; types of fear in relation to repentance (innovation from previous *summae*: virtues are placed before the incarnation of the Word; later ones, even Thomas Aquinas, will usually se- follow this new pattern)
  - 4th book: christology (with an innovative chapter on Christ *caput Ecclesiae*)
  - 5th book: sacramental theology (it is the first or one of the first to use the expression *opus operatum*); eschatology
- the *Sententiae* of **Gandolfo of Bologna**: they are indeed very dependent on those of Novara: indeed they function almost the role of epitome, even if there is a greater use of dialectics;
- of the end of the century are the *Summae* of
- Pietro Capuano (*Summa "Vetustissima veterum"*),
- **Prepositino of Cremona** (*Summa "Qui producit ventos"*)
- and Stefano Langton.

In part dependent on the school of Peter Lombard, but on the other hand authors characterized by a strong independence of thought with respect to their master, are Otto of Ourscamp (†1171) and the English Robert **of Melun** (Robertus Meludonensis, †1167), the latter actually a disciple of Abelard and Hugh of St. Victor, but indebted to the theologian from Novara for what refers to the systematization of his *Sententiae*; they have a general division on biblical basis: sacramenta Veteris Testamenti (from creation to incarnation) and sacramenta Novi Testamenti (incarnation, sacraments and eschatology, but this part is unfinished and deals only with the incarnation). In his *Quaestiones de divina pagina* he shows an ability to use the new methodology, combined however with a great closeness to the Fathers of the Church and a deep knowledge of them. He often enters in controversy with Abelard and agrees instead often with Hugh of Saint Victor. They are very interesting in its philosophical elaborations of free will and the immortality of the soul. He intervened in defense of Abelard, polemicizing against Bernardo of Chiaravalle. Robert of Melun taught in Paris and then moved his school to Melun: he had as a disciple John of Salesbury; his academic career was interrupted by his appointment as bishop of Hereford, in 1163.

A disciple of Otto of Ourscamp is **Simon of Tournai** (1130-1201ca.), with whom an original form of Aristotelian rationalism begins to take shape, and who gives us the first example of the literary genre of the *Disputationes*; he was in any case strongly influenced by the Porretan current of thought, and is ascribed by some scholars to this school. Simon of Tournai is also a profound connoisseur of the work of John Scotus Eriugena, sympathizes with Porreta and Abelard, and is an enthusiastic admirer of Aristotle.

The fundamental contribution of Peter Lombard and his disciples is found above all in the balanced harmony between faith and reason, between *auctoritas* and dialectical instruments, which allowed to save, in the period of the great controversies on the use of dialectics, what was good in the Abelardian doctrine, purified from its exaggerations and asperities; in this regard, the opinion of Martin Grabmann is interesting: "the diversity of judgments on the theological-literary qualities of the *Magister* can be explained by remembering that in him are found united the scientific methods of the Victorian School and the method of Abelard and his school. Indeed, the *Libri sententiarum* show a double face: on the one hand, the attitude of the Victorians, inspired by deep faith, guided by ethical-mystical feelings, firmly anchored to authority, cautious and reserved towards dialectics; on the other hand, the characteristic tendency of the Abelardian school to reserve a wide space to dialectics, to enjoy contrast and criticism, and also in the orderly arrangement of theological matter: the *Sententiae* of the Novara theologian were the most popular textbook throughout Europe until the sixteenth century, when they began to be replaced by the *Summa of* St. Thomas Aquinas, and to become a master in theology was required to have commented - usually for the space of two years - as a bachelor, the work of the master of Novara. This teaching function was called *baccalaureus Sententiarum*: "by the time of Thomas d'Aquino, the function of the bachelor was fairly well established in the curriculum of the university. Normally the bachelor had to lecture on the Bible *cursorie,* that is, he was a *biblicus* for one year unless the equivalent had been exercised in some recognized studium or priory. Then he became a lecturer on the Sentences for at least one, more often two years, and in some cases four years" (J.A. Weisheipl). It seems that the first teacher who publicly commented on Lombard's Sententiae was Alexander of Hales (+1245). Some authors, among them Sofia Vanni Rovighi, believe that this work is the culminating point of the process, in which the work of the various schools of the twelfth century converges.

**The School of Chartres**

Also in France, cradle of the theological renaissance of this century, was born and operated the School of Chartres, characterized by a strong realism, the effort to reconcile Platonism and Aristotelianism, a great interest in the sciences and a bold application of the dialectical method, which earned to a statement of its exponent **Gilbert Porreta** (†1154), bishop, concerning the trinitarian dogma (regarding a difference he posed between God and the divinity), the condemnation of the Council of Reims in 1148: "de primo tantum capitulo Romanus pontifex diffinivit, ne aliqua ratio in theologia inter naturam et personam divideret, neve Deus divina essentia diceretur ex sensu ablativi tantum, sed etiam nominativi". It should be noted that the Council of Reims does not censure the person of Gilbert, but only the proposition in question, without caring to establish or not the authorship of the bishop of Poitiers.

The council examined four propositions of Gilbert Porreta:

- There is a real difference between God and the divine essence, and between the divine essence and its attributes
- The same real distinction is found between essence and persons
- The three Persons are eternal
- The properties present in God are eternal and are not God

He bases his thinking on the distinction between *Deus* and *divinitas*, a distinction which he believes does not invalidate divine simplicity.

He also develops a theory of participation on a Platonic basis: in the work *In libro Quomodo substantiae bonae sint* he tries to answer the question of Boethius: "quomodo substantiae bonae sint in eo quod sint, cum non sint substantialia bona"; for Gilbert Porreta, substance is formed through a first participation in being; once thus constituted, it participates in forms that remain distinct from it; thus *esse* is placed on a plane of equality with respect to the other forms that enter into the constitution of the real: such as corporeality, size, whiteness, etc.

Regarding universals, Porreta takes a position in the realist complex, although expressed not without a certain uncertainty and confusion.

Porreta remains, however, the greatest metaphysician of his century: it is therefore an author who exercised a very important influence, which in his creative effort poses a whole series of problems that will be resolved only in the next century, thanks to the construction of a solid and consistent ontology.

Besides Porreta, prominent figures of the Chartres School are also:

- Bernard of **Chartres (**†1124/1130): founder of the school; this is how John of Salisbury (*Metalogicus*, I, 24) describes the didactic activity of Bernard of Chartres, one of the initiators of the school: "in reading the passages taken as models, Bernard pointed out what was simple and in accordance with the rule: the grammatical figures, the rhetorical tones, the sophistical subterfuges, the relationships between the passage studied and the other sciences, and he emphasized everything. He was careful, however, not to teach everything about everything, but considered the intellectual capacity of his hearers and imparted to them, in due time, the amount of knowledge they could receive. On every occasion he inculcated deeply into their minds the two qualities which make style brilliant: the propriety of terms which takes place when to the noun the adjective and the verb are equally accorded; the figure which occurs when words are taken, for a just reason, in a sense different from their natural meaning. Knowing that exercise strengthens the memory and sharpens the intelligence, he incites some by exhortations, and others by rewards to induce them to imitate what they heard.... And since it is not good for religion to be kept away from school and life, he proposed a subject suitable for edifying faith and morals, and capable, like a lecture, of inciting all those present to good.
- Radulfo Ardente,
- **Nicholas of Amiens**, at the end of the century wrote the *Ars catholicae fidei*, a systematic exposition of the truths of faith, presented in the form of a rational system, with clear apologetic intentions.
- **Theodoric of Chartres** (†1150): he tries to deduce from Plato's *Timaeus* the general principles of an exemplarist philosophy to be applied to the study of Christian doctrine. He writes *De sex dierum operibus* and *De Trinitate, librum hunc*.
- **Clarembaldo of Arras**: disciple of Hugh of Saint Victor and Theodoric of Chartres; friend of san Bernardo. He writes a Comment to the *De Trinitate* of Boethius; he asserts with decision the existence of the universals: for him humanity literally exists, and the individuals derive from the diversity of the accidents. Ideal realities really exist in God, who is therefore present in all things through his essence; to avoid pantheism he distinguishes between the forms present in bodies and the purest forms that are in God.
- **Bernard Silvestre**: (also known as Bernard of Tours). He writes the *De mundi universitate libri duo*, in which he tries to connect the classical cosmogony with the story of *Genesis* (especially the part of the *opus ornatum*, not infrequently resorting to Platonic categories. The work is divided into:
- *Megacosmus*: creation of the universe
- *Microcosmus*: creation of man
- **William of Conches** (1080-1146): among his works the *De philosophia mundi* and the *Dragmaticon philosophiae*; he tries to base his theological speculation on the *Timaeus*: in particular he insists on the universality of divine causality and on the identity between the Platonic world soul and the Holy Spirit. He believes that the world is perpetual, a concept different for him from "eternal", which is applicable only to God. He was a teacher of John of Salisbury.
- **John of Salisbury** (d. 1180), Englishman; disciple of Abelard, he later moved to the School of Chartres. Back in England, he was a friend of King Henry II and Archbishop Thomas Beckett. Exiled by the king, he went to France where he became bishop of Chartres. He believes that the true philosopher must be modest, moderate and realist. For this reason he abandons the Platonism typical of the School of Chartres to embrace Aristotelianism. He also confronts skepticism, supporting a realism that has as its basis the foundations of certainty: the senses, reason and Revelation. His most important works are the *Metalogicus* and the *Polycraticus*.

On the question of universals he argues - wanting to follow Aristotle, but according to his interpretation of the Stagirite - a moderate realism, criticizing both the nominalists and those who believe that universals should be considered *res*. The genera do not have a current existence except in the judgment that forms them by virtue of the process of abstraction, but they are *substantialia*, have a certain reality in things.

In the *Polycraticus* John of Salisbury expounds his political theory, of submission of the temporal order to the spiritual one; he is in perfect line with political Augustinism. The authority of princes derives from the Church, which has the power of the two swords (spiritual and temporal) but delegates the temporal to monarchs. If the prince becomes a tyrant, the pope can depose him; he even goes so far as to justify tyrannicide.

- **Alain of Lille** (d. 1203), an atypical Cistercian who wrote his *Summa* shortly after mid-century. He also wrote an apologetic work against the Albigenses, Waldensians, and Jews: *De fide catholica contra haereticos sui temporis praesertim Albigenses*. And a work of theological methodology: *Regulae theologicae*.

**Grammatical theology or terminology**

This is the name given to a group of disciples of Peter Lombard, among whom **Peter Capuano** (†1214) was also active as a theologian: the starting point of their reflections is undoubtedly the material collected so neatly and abundantly by the *Magister Sententiarum*, but the method of work is probably inspired by that of **Peter of Poitiers**, "the most faithful disciple of Lombard", according to the definition of Grabmann; in fact, in Pittaviense - and therefore in Capuano and in the other exponents of the group - the *auctoritates of the* fathers lose the central role they have in Novarese, to be reduced to being the starting point of arguments or objections, or sometimes to corroborate solutions, and come mostly to play the function of support to an autonomous and preponderant logical-dialectical reasoning (while remaining always within the limits of orthodoxy and respect for the "giants", according to the famous phrase of Bernard of Chartres).

"In Peter of Poitiers, dialectics has a prominent place among the means intended to carry out this fundamental program of Scholasticism, something that is not the case in Anselm of Aosta. It follows that certain factors, certain means that provided Anselm's works with speculative depth, mystical interiority and force of attraction, richness of content, are lacking. In fact, an excessive use of dialectics makes the mind turn towards particular, isolated questions and problems [...]. Even when dealing with tradition, even when using the Sentences of the Fathers and previous theologians, the influence of Dialectics will make one stick more to words than to ideas and fail to enter into the spirit of the patristic works" (M. Grabmann).

And this feature appears indeed in Capuano even more pronounced than in Peter of Poitiers. This interest in logic and dialectics is expressed in all these authors with a very careful attention to the terms, to their grammatical and logical value within the proposition, in a continuous test of the proposed assertion with logical tests, transformations in negative of the sentence (to see if it continues to be correct), distinctions, tests *per compositionem et divisionem*, etc.. All these logical-grammatical exercises, which are so tedious and often obscure to modern readers, have, however, the value of being a proof of the need, the attempts, the desire of the authors of the twelfth century, to develop a method of argumentation. All these logical-grammatical exercises, which are so boring and often obscure to modern readers, have however the value of being a proof of the necessity, of the attempts, of the desire of the authors of the twelfth century to develop a scientific method, to base their knowledge on a sure and irrefutable basis, already tempered and forged in a rigid examination of correctness: therefore, even if sometimes their reasoning appear to the eyes of the contemporary man with a tint of abstruseness, the purpose behind such arguments is not at all in discordance with the way of proceeding of modern science.

To all this contributes also the use of the abelardian method of *Sic et non in the* form that it assumed - or, better said, in the overcoming of this method produced - in the second half of the century: this evolution presented the positive aspects of a greater fine-tuning of logical tools, of a greater rigor and of the now unfailing presence of the concordance operated by the author on the basis of his personal opinion, but at the same time it came to suffer from the disadvantages derived from the prevalence of the logical-dialectical exercise operated on the discordances, compared to the content-based search for the true meaning of the scriptural and patristic texts that were being analyzed: to put it in modern terms, pure rationality prevailed over history and the contextualization of *auctoritas.*

These *Summae* - among which, in addition to the one written by Pietro Capuano, should also be counted that of **Prepositino di Cremona** -, however, take for granted the knowledge of the *Sentences of* Lombardo, and, on the basis of them, try to open new ways: for this reason they do not have the tone of an exposition of the whole theology (as the Novarese in his famous "four books" did), but of collections of theological questions and debates gathered by themes: they represent therefore a faithful portrait of the theological teaching of the time.

**Monastic theology in the XII century**

In addition to the development of the so-called "incipient scholasticism," a branch of monastic theology continued to flourish throughout the century which gave more importance to the knowledge attained through contemplation and love than to that obtained through the application of reason. In the twelfth century, in fact, we find some very clear names of this theological genre: besides Bernard of Clairvaux, Rupert of Deutz (†1135), Peter the Venerable (†1156), William of Saint-Thierry (†1148), Aelred of Rievaulx (†1167), Isaac of the Star (†1178 ca.), and the Benedictine Peter of Celle, Bishop of Chartres (†1187).

Of **Bernard of Clairvaux** (†1153), it is very interesting to recall his theory on freedom: he believes that there are three types: freedom of nature, by which we can choose to do one thing or another, even evil or good; freedom of grace, by which God helps us to free ourselves from sin and from our bad tendencies; freedom of beneplacitus, proper to the blessed, which can have in a temporary way even some great contemplatives in earthly life.

Bernard, called by Jean Mabillon "ultimus inter patres, primis certe non impar", assumed in many ways a negative attitude toward philosophy, both Platonic and Aristotelian; he took a very firm and ardent stand against the theories of Abelard and Gilbert Porreta, those that were erroneous and those that he considered such. It should be pointed out, however, that his criticisms are mainly present in polemical works and in others of preaching to monks; but there are also passages of the Cistercian abbot in which a positive evaluation of science is noted. It must be said, however, that for him true science always remains "a supernatural wisdom flowing from the knowledge of God and of oneself, powerfully inflamed by divine love" (M. Grabmann).

"The main reason why a place should be reserved for Bernard in the history of scholastic method does not consist so much in these content influences as in the fact that the 'Doctor mellifluus,' by his personal action and with the weight of his whole spiritual orientation, put a brake on the invasion of hyperdialectics in the theological field, countered the danger of a rationalistic disintegration of the Christian mysteries, and, with his works so sincere and profound, fought against a formalistic impoverishment of scientific activity" (M. Grabmann).

Bernard is, however, a gigantic character of the 12th century: he is the most influential religious personality of the century, the greatest preacher of the Middle Ages, and the founder of medieval Christocentric mysticism; he is also very important for Mariology and the spread of Marian devotion.

Of the abbot **Rupert of Deutz** (1080-1129) it is worth mentioning his effort to reconcile *auctoritas*, which for him is the fundamental guide in theological discourse, with *ratio,* of which he has a moderately positive concept, and which he sometimes views with suspicion, for example when he opposes the *Schola Christi* to the *scholae magistrorum*. A proponent of a theological biblicism, he is a mystic with a lively sense of theological systematics. He tends to bring back the biblical contents in a great unitary picture given by the Trinity (*De Trinitate et operibus eius*; *De glorificatione Trinitatis et processione Spiritus Sancti*). He also wrote works on the relationship between divine will and moral evil (*De voluntate Dei*; *De omnipotentia divina*).

He is a strong proponent of the superiority of monastic life: "omnes apostoli vere fuerunt monachi".

**William of Saint-Thierry** is very close to the mysticism of Saint Bernard, of whom he was a great friend. He also wrote mystical-dogmatic works, including the *Speculum fidei* and the *Aenigma fidei*. He also wrote polemical works against Abelard and Gilbert Porreta.

Rupert and William, both from Liège, also wrote extensively on the Eucharist, contributing to the Eucharistic tradition of the city of Liège, which in the following century would lead to the institution of the liturgical feast of Corpus Christi.

**Other characters of the theology of the XII century**

An interesting figure is also **Adelard of Bath**: English, a teacher in Paris and Laon, he then traveled throughout Europe and North Africa, acquiring much knowledge of the Islamic world. He is a Platonist: he believes that the soul is locked in the body as in chains, from which, however, can be freed through the study of the arts.

We must also mention a very special personage who had a certain influence in the following centuries: **Joachim of Fiore** (1130-1202). Born in Calabria, he became a Cistercian and, after a pilgrimage to the Holy Land, founded a hermitic monastery at San Giovanni in Fiore. His theology is pervaded by a desire for spiritual reform of the Church. He is important for his theology of history, in which he distinguishes three ages:

1. that of slavery or servile obedience (Old Testament, God the Father), characterized by the work of married men
2. that of filial obedience (Christ, Church, sacraments), characterized by the work of clerics
3. that of freedom (Holy Spirit, still to come, with a prophetic prediction made in 1260), characterized by the work of the monks. In this third age the Church will know true spiritual fullness and freedom.

Therefore, his theology of history is in contrast to the Augustinian theology (Christocentric), and is intended to be presented as Trinitarian. This doctrine will be the basis of many apocalyptic doctrines of the following centuries. In the Lateran Council IV some of his theories on the Trinity were condemned.

Spiritual exegetical method: while the exegesis of the Old Testament is based on *littera*, and that of the New on *allegory*, Joachim advocates the exegesis of the "eternal gospel," based on concordance.

Outside the environment of the schools we also find **HERIAN HUGO** (1115-1182), who lived for a long time in Constantinople. A profound connoisseur of Greek patristics and Byzantine theology, he wrote several works linking the Latin and Greek traditions, including a book on the *Filioque*.

Finally, because of their special importance for the transmission of Arabic philosophy (Avicenna *[Liber de causis*], Avicebron [*Fons vitae*], Al-Gazali, Al-Farabi, Averroè), we recall the Spanish Jew converted to Catholicism **Domenico Gundisalvi** (*De anima*; *De divisione philosophiae*), then, already in the next century, Michele Scoto, working in Toledo and Naples.

For exegesis, the greatest exponent of this century is **PIETRO CANTORE** (d. 1197): he is also representative of a less dialectical and speculative, and more practical-positive theology. He is author of the moral work Verbum *abbreviatum*, an analysis of the theological and cardinal virtues and opposed vices; and of the *Summa de Sacramentis*, where we find a profound Eucharistic theology. In the first of these two works, Cantore also sets out a methodological treatment of biblical studies: in them, *lectio* constitutes the foundation of the building, *disputatio* represents the walls, and *praedicatio* is its roof. He also wrote a book of *Distinctiones*, also called Summa Abel, which is an alphabetical dictionary of theological terms; in fact, it has as its subtitle: *Expositiones vocabulorum biblie secundum alphabetum*.

For Mariology, worthy of mention is the English theologian **EADMERUS OF CANTERBURY** (1060ca.-1126ca.), a Benedictine, friend and biographer of Anselm of Aosta, who wrote in defense of Mary's immaculate conception in his work *De conceptione Sanctae Mariae*. He stands in continuity with an English tradition of several centuries of defense of this Mariological theory, which would be proclaimed dogma in the 19th century. Similar works, again in England) are also the *Liber de celebranda conceptione beatae Mariae contra beatum Bernardum* by the monk **NICOLA DI SANT'ALBANO** (d. 1162) and the *Sermo de conceptione sanctae Mariae* by the Benedictine abbot **OSBERTO DI CLARE** (d. 1158).

A very interesting figure is the premonstratensian and bishop **ANSELMO OF HAVELBERG** (1099-1158). A legate in Constantinople of Emperor Lothair II, he left in the work *Dialogorum adversus Graecos libri tres*, a memoir of his theological disputes with the Orthodox. He also carried out diplomatic missions for the German king Conrad III. He was papal legate in the crusade against the Vendi, and an active proponent of Germanic colonization of territories across the Elbe River. In the service of Emperor Frederick I Barbarossa, he was killed in the siege of Milan. He is highly original in theology for the space he grants to salvation history, which for him is both sacred and ecclesiastical; this in contrast to the logicism and phys-sism to which scholasticism tended instead. "Anselme de Havelberg a exprimé avec vigueur la mobil-ité et les variations du royaume de Dieu dans l'Église elle-même. Tout à l'opposé d'Abélard, prônant une unité abstraite de la foi dnas les propositions logiquement identiques, Anselme ob-serve les faits, à l'intérieur desquels est ensuite définie l'unité de la foi è travers la diversité suc-cessive des temps, dans une "Église" qui va d'Abel au dernier des bienheureux" (M.-D. Chenu).

**Concluding remarks**

It should be kept in mind that the subdivision by schools in the XII century is in many ways approximate: for various authors it is difficult to establish clearly which teacher they followed with preference: to offer some examples:

- Prepositino of Cremona, disciple of Pietro Lombardo, results notably influenced also from the Porretan ideas, through Simone of Tournai, that also is disciple of Oddone of Ourscamp;
- or Stefano Langton, who, if for the dogmatic works is clearly Lombardian, nevertheless in the exegetical field is rather to be ascribed to the school of San Vittore.
- Pietro Cantore (author of the moral work *Verbum abbreviatum*, analysis of the theological and cardinal virtues and of the opposite vices; and of the *Summa de Sacramentis*, where we find a profound Eucharistic theology) is considered by Grabmann as the founder of a school of his own, of biblical-moral orientation,
- to which also Peter *Manducator is* ascribed. This man, also called Peter Comestor, taught in the Lombard chair from 1164 to 1169: he wrote a *De Sacramentis*, in which we find among other things one of the first very precise expositions of the theory of transubstantiation.

All this variety of schools and intellectual effervescence led to numerous disputes and controversies between the exponents of the various currents of theological thought, and, in the first half of the century, between their founders and Bernard, a strenuous defender of orthodoxy: it was in fact the abbot of Clairvaux who first had the doctrines attributed to Abelard condemned by the Synod of Sens (1141), then that on the Trinitarian dogma, by the Synod of Reims (1148).

It was in this environment of such intense intellectual life, of the search for new paths and original solutions, of the effort to systematize knowledge, of debates and vast literary production, that the foundations were laid for the apogee of scholasticism, which took place in the thirteenth century.

Merit of the theology of the twelfth century is undoubtedly the application of philosophy to the revealed data to make it more intelligible; limitation of this theology is the scarcity of philosophical knowledge of the time, which were reduced roughly to the logic, dialectics: in the next century, increased philosophical knowledge in the metaphysical field, and developing the methodology developed by the *magistrates* of the incipient scholasticism, theologians will be able to pose much deeper and crucial issues.

*"The* twelfth century and the first decades of the thirteenth are the epoch in which the foundations of the doctrine and method of the Second Scholasticism are laid; they are times of evolution and development, of research and testing. In this epoch the systematic aspect of the Second Scholasticism is largely prepared. The Sentences and the Summae of the twelfth century have built - and made available to the second Scholasticism, albeit in broad strokes - the plan of the theological doctrinal edifice" M. Grabmann.

Another aspect, in some ways surprising, but explainable with the almost absolute attention of the theologians of the twelfth century to create a methodology and a systematization of theology, is the almost total fall into oblivion of Anselm of Aosta: the holy archbishop of Canterbury will have to wait until the next century to enter the Olympus of theology.

In any case, "whatever school they come from, the collections of sentences and theological sums, which abound at the end of the twelfth century, are compilations of materials organized according to a more or less rational theological plan, but we are not able to find in them true theological systems and, even less, true philosophical syntheses made available to Christian doctrine. Theological speculation continues to have an eclectic and fragmentary character" (F. van Steenbergher).

**The ecclesiology of the XII century**

In the 12th century we have the first three ecumenical councils at the Lateran, the first ones convened by the pope.

The consequences of the Gregorian Reform are consummated. The Carolingian vision comes to an end; the Church is now seen as a hierarchy; the central element goes from being the monk to being the cleric.

For the anti-Berengarian polemic we move from the Church as *corpus verum* to *corpus mysticum*.

The ecclesiology of monastic theology

Bernard of Clairvaux: very important from the ecclesial point of view for his main role in the solution of the Schism of Anacletus, in favor of Pope Innocent II. He presents the Church as a bride (in his *Commentary on the Song of Songs*).

It supports the two-sword theory:

- in spiritual field: direct power
- in the temporal field: indirect power, through kings

The pope has the power of coercion in these areas.

So he resumes the doctrine of the two swords (temporal and spiritual power), submitting to the pope also the temporal one: the pontiff does not have to use it directly, but he can command to use it, having the supremacy over the empire.

The figure of the pope rises to great social and political importance with the Crusades: Bernard is the great preacher of the Second Crusade.

Bernard, in reference to Eugene III, Cisrtercense, calls the pope *Vicarius Christi*. In *De consideratione*, addressed precisely to this pope, he criticizes the extension and excessive use of papal powers, without denying their legitimacy; from this point of view he is an anti-curialist, though respectful of the Holy See.

Honorius Augustodunensis argues for the superiority of spiritual power over temporal power.

Gerhoh of Reichersberg (d. 1169) takes up the themes of the Gregorian Reformation; supports the *infallibilitas* of the *Ecclesia Romana*, but not of the pope; criticizes the expansion of the action of the Roman Curia.

Isaac of the Star: more speculative than practical-legal approach. Emphasizes the fact that the Church is the bride of Christ; also borrows from St. Augustine the idea of the Church as *Christus totus*.

Peter of Celle: the Church imitates the heavenly court with its hierarchy of orders and angelic choirs. For him, the Church consists primarily of clerics.

Rupert of Deutz offers a presentation of the Church that is nourished by all biblical types.

Gioacchino da Fiore, with his theory of the three ages, seen as a figure: in the *Ecclesia spiritualis* is found the future of the present Church. His ecclesiological thinking greatly conditioned the current of Franciscan spirituals.

Herrada of Landsberg (d. 1195), abbess of the Monastery of Hohenburg in Alsace, offers an orderly presentation of types and figures of the Church in her work *Hortus deliciarum.*

Hildegard of Bingen: In her work *Scivias*, she presents the Church as woman, city and bride devoted to fruitfulness. It is a contemplative and spiritual Church. She announces a prophetic age - in which there will be more room for lay people and women - similar to the age of the Holy Spirit of Joachim of Fiore.

Peter Cantor and Peter Manducator: spread the use of the term *Ecclesia militans*, although the first to use it seems to have been John of Salesbury; previously the term *Ecclesia peregrinans* was used.

The ecclesiology of scholastic theology

Hugh of St. Victor: follows very much St. Augustine. He deals with ecclesiology in *De sacramentis christianae fidei*, book II, part II: the unity of the Church which is the Body of Christ.

Faith and baptism make the faithful and the Church as *universitas fidelium*. She is called the Body of Christ because of the Spirit of Christ, *propter Spiritum Christi*. "Quando christianus efficeris, membrum Christi efficeris, membrum Corporis Christi, participans Spiritum Christi" (*De sacr. Christ. fidei*).

Spiritual power is responsible for establishing earthly power and for judging it if it is not good and does not work good. For him, human society is Christianity and Christianity is the Church: for this reason every power and every force derive from a single principle, and the duality of powers (temporal and spiritual) is only apparent; from the unity of creation derives also the unity of power in the ecclesiastical hierarchy, superior to the temporal one.

Peter Lombard: treats of ecclesiology in *Sent.* , liber III, distinctio 13: trattato *de Christo capite*; this is a novelty. It is also a sign of the clericalization of the Church brought about by the Gregorian Reform. There is also a connection with the Eucharist, in which:

- res contenta et significata ⇒caro Christi
- res significata et non contenta ⇒unitas Ecclesiae

Another novelty is the notion of "sacramental character," used especially in the theological treatment of the sacrament of order (for the presbyterate, not the episcopate), which is based on the Eucharist, and thus leads to a hierarchization and clericalization of ecclesiology.

Peter of Poitiers: the treatment of the Church is linked to that of *gratia capitis*. Grace comes to the Church as the fruit of Christ's grace, as a created grace, possessed in fullness by the Head and poured out from Him on the whole body. It is an analytical theology on the efficiency of the humanity of Christ and the sacraments. It is an ecclesiology that is not very pneumatological: in fact, in general, in the 12th century, the idea that the Church is born from the side of Christ on the Cross prevails; on the contrary, it is very rare that its birth is placed with Pentecost.

In Gratian's *Decree* (*concordantia discordantium canonum*, 1140), the Roman Primacy is reaffirmed, in the line of Nicholas I and Gregory VII. But the pope is called *vicarius Petri*, and not *vicarius Christi*. Furthermore, princes are subject to the jurisdiction of the Church with regard to the spiritual aspects of their lives and actions.

Pope Alexander iii was a supporter, but a moderate one, of the power of the pontiff with respect to monarchs: the power of the king did not consist only in service to the Church: it had its own sphere of action. At the same time, Alexander favored a certain centralization of power in favor of the Roman See in the ecclesiastical structure: he assigned the canonization of saints to the pope; in the area of doctrinal magisterium, he transferred many matters of faith from the provincial councils to the Holy See.

John of Salesbury, who had been secretary to Thomas Beckett, is a fiery hierocrat: in the *Polycraticus* he argues that the prince is the minister of the priesthood, and that he exercises that part of sacred duties which is reputed unworthy of priestly hands.

In general, throughout the ecclesiology of the twelfth century, the theological thesis of the possibility of the heretical pope remains present.

In addition, popular lay heresies against the ecclesiastical hierarchy spread, in part because Gregory VII, in his fight against the simoniac and Nicolasite clergy, had blown on the embers of the pataria, a popular movement in northern Italy. In the twelfth century we have quite a few of these heresies: Peter of Bruys, the Cathars, the Waldensians, Arnaldo of Brescia, Hugh Speroni.
