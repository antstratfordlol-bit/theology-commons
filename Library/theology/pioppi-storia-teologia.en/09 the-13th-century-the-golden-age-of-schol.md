## The 13th Century: The Golden Age of Scholasticism {#pioppi-storia-teologia.9}
This is the century of the full maturity of medieval civilization. In fact, it reached a high level of civilization, very open. The cultural and religious unity of Europe is still very strong. The Crusades and the Mongol invasions put Europe in contact with other great civilizations (Byzantium, Islam, China, India). The process of *Reconquista* in the Iberian Peninsula advances (1212: Battle of Las Navas de Tolosa). France is on its way to becoming the great European power, taking the place of the Holy Roman Empire, exhausted by its centuries-long struggle against the papacy: of great importance for this is the long reign of St Louis IX (1226-1270).

The thirteenth century was the time of the great scholasticism: in the previous century the scholastic method had been developed, that is, the application of philosophy to the study of doctrine in order to transform it into knowledge for causes with Peter Abelard and other theologians; an organized and complete structuring of the theological knowledge had also been experimented (Peter Lombard and *Summae*); moreover, many translations of the works of Plato, Aristotle and other Hellenic philosophers, and of the Greek and Oriental Fathers had been carried out. All this comes to maturity in the thirteenth century: it is now possible to harmonize the Greek metaphysical knowledge (recently translated) with the methodology developed in the previous century.

The great theologians of the century were almost all members of the mendicant orders: Alexander of Hales (d. 1245), Bonaventure of Bagnoregio (1221-1274) and John Duns Scotus (1263-1308), Franciscans; Albertus Magnus (1193-1280) and Thomas Aquinas (1224-1274), Dominicans. The most successful synthesis of the great scholasticism is the *Summa Theologiae* of Thomas Aquinas.

Great development of chivalric and troubadour **poetry** in vernacular languages: Italian, Provençal, French. Dante Alighieri (1265-1321) with the *Divine Comedy* makes an attempt to express in poetry the theology of Thomas Aquinas. Splendor of the European courts, with **great sovereigns,** such as the emperor and king of Sicily Frederick II of Hohenstaufen, St. Louis IX of France, Henry III of England.

In religious life we find the great novelty of the **mendicant orders** (Franciscans, Dominicans, Carmelites); they present important innovations with respect to the Benedictine or monastic scheme in general:

1. These are centralized orders, unlike the Benedictines in which in practice each monastery was autonomous; there is therefore no vow of *stabilitas* and the friars can be moved from place to place according to the needs of the order.
2. They set up their monasteries in the cities, and not in the countryside: this fact is important because in the 13th century urban life had fully blossomed again, and the mendicants contributed effectively to keeping the Christian spirit alive in the cities, just as the Benedictine monasteries had been islands of peace and spirituality in the feudal countryside of the early Middle Ages.
3. They have a strong pastoral commitment, assuming a more flexible structure than the Benedictine one, which was more centered on monastic life itself.
4. The fact of living poverty in a radical way is also a relevant aspect: in fact in the Christian society of that time there was a growing intolerance towards the luxury and wealth of the high clergy, which often tended to take on heterodox characteristics; the mendicants, and above all the Franciscans, were able by their example to show that even in the Church the spirit of evangelical poverty was still very present and thus to keep large masses of people in Catholicism. In this regard, it must be considered that even in the Middle Ages anticlericalism developed, but its characteristics were very different from today's: in fact, it was not a denial of the Church, but rather a form of complaint, sometimes through satire and irony, about the reprehensible conduct of some ecclesiastics; therefore, it was a Christian anticlericalism, different from that which arose from the 18th century onwards, which instead aimed at criticizing the whole of Christianity.

In the life of piety, great development of **Eucharistic devotion**, specifically of the Eucharist as presence: *Corpus Christi*.

Armed evangelization of the Baltic countries by the Teutonic Order.

Three **ecumenical councils were** held in the thirteenth century:

1. Lateran Council IV: 1215: established the norm of the Easter precept and annual confession.
2. I Council of Lyon: 1245: the five plagues of the Church of the time are discussed; they are:
3. the faults of the clergy
4. the loss of Jerusalem, reconquered by the Muslims
5. the danger of the Eastern Latin Empire to be taken over by the Greeks
6. the danger of the Mongols
7. the struggle that opposed the pope to the emperor, thus lacerating Christendom
8. II Council of Lyon: 1274 (participation of Bonaventure of Bagnoregio)

Union with the Greeks, which lasted for a short time (until 1282); it was wanted above all by Emperor Michael Paleologus: it failed because of the hostility of the Greek clergy. *Professio fidei* of Michael Paleologus

The **crusades** in the thirteenth century: they fall into the hands of the Italian maritime republics, which derive great economic and commercial profits from them.

- 1204: 4th Crusade: sack of Constantinople and foundation of the Latin Empire (1204-1261)
- 1229: 5th Crusade, by Emperor Frederick II: Jerusalem returns to Christianity
- 1244: new fall of Jerusalem in Islamic hands
- 1248-1249: 6th Crusade, of Saint Louis IX, in Egypt
- 1261: Fall of the Latin Eastern Empire: Constantinople reconquered by the Byzantines.
- 1270: 7th Crusade, of Saint Louis IX (who dies of the plague), in Tunisia.
- 1291: fall of Acre, the last Christian fortress in Palestine.

**Genghis Khan** (1167-1227), at the head of groupings of nomadic Mongol and Turkish tribes, founds an immense empire stretching from Korea to Eastern Europe, Indochina, and the Middle East. 1236: the Mongols annihilate the Bulgarians of Kama; 1240: they invade Wallachia and Poland; 1241, Battle of Liegnitz: the Mongols destroy a German-Polish army; 1241, Battle of Sajo: the Mongols destroy the Hungarian army: Europe is saved for the death of the Great Khan Ogodai (1241), son of Genghis Khan. Batu Khan, who commands the western horde, withdraws from Europe to control the imperial succession. This great empire allows direct contacts between Europe and East Asia, hitherto interrupted by the Muslims. Together with Venetian merchants (Marco Polo), many Franciscan missionaries travel the Silk Road (Giovanni di Pian del Carmine, Odorico da Pordenone): foundation of Latin-rite dioceses in China and India; the Chinese dioceses will disappear after 1368, when a xenophobic Chinese revolt will drive out the Mongols and will put on the throne the Ming dynasty, which will persecute the Christians, seen as a foreign element (Mongol).

In Italy, **definitive defeat of the Ghibelline party** (imperial), with the battle of Benevento (1266), in which King Manfred of Sicily, son of Frederick II of Hohenstaufen, is defeated by the Guelphs. With Innocent III and his immediate successors we have the height of the political power of the pope and the hierarchy, with the development of excessive clericalism. The papacy seems to have won the age-old struggle against the empire, but in reality these two universal institutions have worn out and their decline will open the way for national monarchies. The policy of the papacy, both in Europe and in Italy, will consist from now on in avoiding the formation of a kingdom too powerful compared to the others: in Europe to avoid the return of a Catholic monarch who wants to arrogate to himself the direction of the Church; in Italy to defend the Papal State.

1209-1229: Crusade against the Albigensians or **Cathars**, dualist heretics derived from Manichean⇐Paulician⇐Bogomiles. Dualist heresy; corporeality is negative; groups of pure ones who live in fasting and continence (food and marriage are considered negative), while most would be saved by a kind of sacrament to be received at the end of life, the *consolamentum* (it seems, however, that over time immoral practices spread). They become numerous in the south of France; often aggressive and violent, they take possession of the ecclesiastical goods: many nobles become cathars to take possession of these goods; after the murder of the papal legate Peter of Castelnau by the Albigensians, a crusade against them is announced. A very hard war begins, which is seen by many as an opportunity to operate a feudal redistribution.

In 1231, with the constitution *Excommunicamus*, Pope Gregory IX created the medieval Inquisition. Until then, the repression of heresy had been the task of the local bishops, and it must be said that throughout the early Middle Ages and until the thirteenth century, the inquisitorial activity of the bishops is very small. Gregory IX introduced a series of local institutions that had to trace their power back to the pope. Moreover, in areas where there were more widespread heresies he delegated their repression to members of the mendicant orders (especially Dominicans) and to secular clergymen who moved from place to place. This he did in part to protect the people of southern France: in fact, the victorious nobles of the crusade were taking advantage of the situation to bring heresy trials against their personal enemies, with much injustice. The medieval Inquisition operates until the end of the thirteenth century, then this power is usurped by the monarchs. In general, the processes are fair and fairly benign. There are cases, such as that of the inquisitor of northern France Robert Le Bougre, who, in 1241 is suspended from his post and sentenced to perpetual imprisonment by the Roman authorities for abuse (excessive brutality) in the exercise of his task. Of the approximately 1,000 trials that have come down to us of Bernard Gui, famous for his rigor as an inquisitor and who operated between 1308 and 1322 in the south of France, the death sentences are 42. However, the medieval Inquisition was not a unitary institution coordinated by Rome, as was the modern one, which arose with the Council of Trent: it lacked a clear bureaucratic structure, unambiguous rules of procedure and a unitary procedural order.

In the theological sphere there is a great **dispute about the use of Aristotle**: the Synod of Paris in 1210 prohibits the *lectio of* the Aristotelian books of natural philosophy and of the Arab commentators to the same (but not the personal reading); in the Statutes of the University of Paris in 1215 the books of metaphysics and natural philosophy of Aristotle are prohibited, as well as the work of Averroè.

Great development of Aristotelian philosophy in the Faculty of Arts, which also influences the Faculty of Theology.

1228: letter of Gregory IX to the professors of theology of Paris, in which the pope warns them against an immoderate use of philosophy, which instead must remain the handmaiden of theology. In 1229 there is a clash between the university and the forces of order that causes a strike of the university that lasts about two years. Gregory IX, concerned about the situation, writes a long letter on April 13, 1231 (*Parens scientiarum Parisius*), in which, among other things, he takes up the theme of Aristotelian works forbidden in 1210 and 1215, but with a certain openness: he establishes that before being used in the university, the works of the Stagirite must be examined and purged of any suspicion of error. Ten days later, on April 23, Gregory IX creates a commission for the examination of these books, headed by William of Auxerre: but he dies on November 3, 1231, and the work of the commission stops.

In this century, there is still no part of theology specifically dedicated to ecclesiology; nevertheless, concepts and themes are developed and studied that will then be taken up in the treatises *de Ecclesia*, which will see the light of day in the following century: the Church, in the 13th century, is seen above all as the Body of Christ, while the figures of "bride" and "mother", much used in patristic and monastic theologies, are overshadowed. Moreover, it is an ecclesiology that is largely Christological, with little presence of the action of the Holy Spirit: the Church is born from the open side of Jesus, and not at Pentecost. To look for ecclesiological themes one must, in the thirteenth-century sums, go to the treatise *de Christo capite*: Christ as head of the body transmits grace to the whole Church as God, not as man. Moreover, for the theory of vicarious satisfaction affirmed with Anselm of Aosta, the humanity of Christ is considered only as the moral cause of grace, a position that is quite different from that of the Fathers of the Church, especially the Greek ones.

Still in the theological field, and also philosophical and scientific in general, it is important, in the XIII century, the continuation of the work of **translation** of the Greek philosophical works, directly or through Arabic: the most important centers of translation are Toledo and Naples.

Finally, in the university world, in the middle of the century, a **dispute** took place that transcended the academic sphere and threatened a strong limitation of **the mendicant orders**; these, in Paris, were increasingly at odds with the seculars for the conquest of university chairs. A great opponent of the mendicant orders was William of Saint-Amour, who succeeded in convincing Pope Innocent IV to revoke, with the bull *Etsi animarum* (November 21, 1254), the privileges of the Dominicans and Franciscans: from now on they would no longer be able to receive the faithful in their churches on Sundays and feast days, and they would always have to ask the permission of the parish priest to hear confessions and preach in the parishes. In this operation he was helped by the fact that a Franciscan master, Gerard of Borgo San Donnino, had written a *Liber introductorius*, in which he took up the theories of Joachim of Fiore, granting a special importance to the Franciscan order in the establishment of the age of the Spirit, and coming to affirm that the writings of the Calabrian abbot were like a third gospel. But in December, a few days after the issuance of the bull, Innocent IV died, and he was succeeded by Alexander IV, who on December 22 revoked the bull, thus saving the mendicants, but the general of the Franciscans, John of Parma, had to resign for not having acted with a firm hand against Gerard. In 1255 William of Saint-Amour returned to the attack with the book *Tractatus brevis de periculis novissimorum temporum ex Scripturis sumptis*; he was answered by Thomas Aquinas with the *Contra impugnantes Dei cultum et religionem*, and Bonaventure of Bagnoregio (from 1257 general of the Franciscans) with the *Quaestiones disputatae de perfectione evangelica*. In the end William was condemned by the Holy See, but the Dominican general Umberto di Romans was able to conduct a moderate and pacifying policy, even if the tension continued for a while. At the same time Bonaventure punished Gerard of Borgo San Donnino with singular severity, withdrawing from him the faculties to preach and to confess and locking him up in prison (where he died); moreover he repressed in the order every form of Gioacchinoism, and instituted a trial against Giovanni da Parma, who was confined almost until his death in the hermitage of Greccio.

**The great theological development of this century is fostered by three orders of events:**

1. Political events: the advance of the Reconquista in Spain, the crusades with the creation of the Frankish states of the Holy Land and the constitution of the Latin Empire of Constantinople with its satellite entities, favor exchanges with Greek, Arab and generally oriental cultures.
2. Social events: the development of communes and trade guilds favors the organization of universities.
3. Religious events: the birth of mendicant orders provides human material with great dedication and motivation.

**The great scholastic**

**1. ROBERT OF COURÇON: †1218**

English by birth. Disciple of Peter Cantore. Cardinal legate of Innocent III in Paris, undertook reorganization of the university curriculum, and proclaimed a ban on aristo-telic metaphysics and physics in 1215. He died on crusade, in Egypt, in 1218. Theologically, he is important for the stabilization of the division of theology into the two parts de fide and de moribus, dogmatic and moral. This division had been expressed earlier by Ivo of Chartres, but it was with Robert of Courçon that it began to spread.

**2. William of Auxerre: †1231**

Secular Priest.

Aristotelian. He uses the philosophy of the Stagirite, but in a discreet and moderate way. He was part of the commission in charge of the examination of the texts of Aristotle, according to the norms of Gregory IX, but death caught him before he could reach any important result.

*3*: 4 books:

1. God
2. Creation
3. Christology and Morality
4. Sacramentary and Eschatology

Theology as *cognitio certa per causas*

Absolute otherness of theology with respect to any other science

**3. Alexander of Hales: †1245**

*Doctor irrefragabilis*

Master of Saint Bonaventure. Secular Master, in 1236 he entered the Franciscan Order.

*Glossa in quatuor libros Sententiarum Petri Lombardi*: Alexander is the first Parisian teacher of whom we have information that uses the Lombard as a textbook, thus beginning a tradition that will last until the sixteenth century.

*Summa halensis*: written by Alexander of Hales and his Franciscan disciple John de la Rochelle; continued after their deaths by St. Bonaventure, then by William of Melitona. Divided into 4 books:

1. God
2. Creation
3. Incarnate Word; morals
4. Sacraments

*Quaestiones disputatae*

Theory of illumination: the intellect learns first principles and universals not by abstraction, but by divine illumination.

Universal hylemorphism (inspired by Ibn Gabirol): angels and souls composed of matter and form

Exemplarism: God is the exemplary cause of everything; everything is the image of God.

Very important for the development of Eucharistic theology, but he holds that only rational beings can have access to the real presence, and only humans who can truly understand what is hidden beneath sacramental symbols can truly communicate: therefore neither animals nor unbelievers, even if they consumed a consecrated host, would receive the sacrament.

Furthermore, in the ecclesiological sphere, he makes a distinction: the members of the Church do not coincide with the members of the Body of Christ.

**4. Philip the Chancellor: †1236**

Chancellor of the University of Paris

He writes the *Summa de bono*. In this work there is a conspicuous use of philosophy: Philip merges Aristotelianism, Neoplatonism and Arabic philosophy, and uses together many Christian sources such as Augustine, Pseudo-Dionysius, Boethius, Anselm of Aosta, Bernard of Clairvaux. He is certainly an innovative character and does not belong to the group of conservatives.

**5. William of Auvergne: †1249**

Secular priest, master from 1228, then bishop of Paris.

A great thinker, he combines the Latin and Augustinian traditions with Greek (Aristotle), Jewish (Maimonides) and Arabic (Averroes, Avicebron, Avicenna) philosophers, while criticizing aspects of their thought.

He criticizes the excessive use of philosophy (particularly Aristotelianism, especially in the thought of Al-Farabi, Al-Ghazali, and Avicenna), which in his opinion would often lead to statements contrary to faith, such as universal hylomorphism, the eternity of the world, and creation through intermediaries (in this regard he reproduces the theory of the separate agent intellect).

Étienne Gilson has defined William's thought as avicennizing Augustinism.

His best work is the *Magisterium divinale*. He writes a *Summa de virtutibus et vitiis*.

**6. Roberto Grossatesta:** 1168-1253

English. A teacher as early as 1185 and 1190, he taught at Oxford from 1200 to 1209. In 1209 he probably went to study in Paris. In 1214 he returned to Oxford, where a few years later he became chancellor of the university. From 1235 bishop of Lincoln.

Close to the Franciscan school: he is the mentor of the first generation of Franciscan theologians from Oxford (joined in 1224), as well as the animator of philosophical, theological and especially scientific studies in Oxford in the first half of the 13th century.

In theology he is an Augustinian. He is opposed to the use of the *Sentences of* Peter Lombard and Aristotle in his teaching, and instead advocates commentary on sacred Scripture; the use of Novarese could, according to Grossatesta, cause a departure from the sacred text.

In philosophy, however, is a proponent of an eclectic Aristotelianism: it has as its basis the Stagirite, but accepts deep neo-Platonic influences: both Augustinian, Arab (Avicenna) and Jewish (Avicebron).

He translated from Greek into Latin the *Nicomachean Ethics*, the letters of Ignatius of Antioch, the scolii of Maximus the Confessor, and other important works.

He wrote a commentary on the Epistle to the Galatians, one on the Psalms, a *Moralitates super Evangelia*, and a *Hexaëmeron*.

**7. Roger Bacon:** 1214-1293

Science studies; importance of experience; empiricism

*Opus maius*, Opus *minus*, *Opus tertium*

In Paris, in the Faculty of Arts, he commented on Aristotle in his lectures as early as the 1940s, despite the fact that the prohibition to expound the thought of the Stagirite was still in force.

**8. Bonaventure of Bagnoregio:** 1217-1274

*Doctor devotus*, or *doctor seraphicus*

Born Giovanni Fidanza, in Bagnoregio (Viterbo), he studies in Paris from 1236 to 1248. Disciple of Alexander of Hales. He teaches in Paris in the Franciscan school from 1248 to 1257.

General of the Franciscans in 1257. Shortly thereafter, on the initiative of Alexander IV, he is appointed *magister* of the university, but he will have no time for academic work, being caught up in the affairs of governing the order. Cardinal in 1273. He dies in Lyon in 1274, during the works of the Council of Lugdunense II.

Works:

- *Commentarii in quattruor libros Sententiarum*: fruit of his teaching work
- *Breviloquium*: summary of *Commentarii*
- Comments on the Holy Scriptures: Ecclesiastes, Wisdom, Gospels of John and Luke.
- *Quaestiones disputatae*
- *De reductione artium ad theologiam*
- *Collationes de decem praeceptis*
- *Collationes de donis Spiritus Sancti*
- *Collationes in Haxaëmeron*
- *Itinerarium mentis in Deum*: six steps of the mystical ascent

Philosophy without theology inevitably falls into errors: e.g.:

- Eternity of the world
- Determinism
- Unique intellect

Theology has a practical, ethical, rather than speculative purpose; it seeks above all the *bonum.*

For the existence of God, in addition to a *posteriori* demonstrations, he also presents *simultaneous* demonstrations, along the lines of the Anselmian argument.

It creates the term *circumincessio* in Trinitarian theology: dynamic unity of the Three Persons, each co-present in the others.

On the incarnation motif, he tends to reconcile the two views (Irenaeus and Augustine) that held: one that the Word would have become incarnate even if there had been no original sin, the other that linked the incarnation to sin.

Christocentric theology: Christ as *verbum* (love of writing), *exemplum* (everything is the image of God), *centrum.*

Sacramentary: the efficacy of the sacraments comes from God, who sees the sign and gives the grace, but not from a cause intrinsic to the sacrament.

Mariology: denies the Immaculate Conception.

Ecclesiology: the ultimate foundation of the visibility and sacramentality of the Church lies in its conformity with Christ the Head (Comm. IV Sent.). A theorist of papal monarchy in the Church, he is, however, very discreet and prudent as we move from the ecclesial to the political realm.

In general, he realizes the danger posed by extreme, rationalistic Aristotelianism, and seeks to oppose it.

Proclaimed Doctor of the Church in 1588, by Sixtus V.

**9. Albertus Magnus:** 1205-1280.

*Doctor universalis*

Dominican. Bishop. Teaches in Paris from 1240 to 1248. Teacher of St. Thomas, who in 1245 begins to take his courses in Paris.

Aristotelian, declared that his task was to "make Aristotle intelligible to the Latins."

Great scientific interest: geography, astronomy, medicine, zoology and botany.

Main works: *Summa de creaturis*; Commentary on the Sentences.

He criticizes the Eucharistic theory of Alexander of Hales for which animals and infidels do not have the capacity to make contact with the real presence.

Ecclesiology: deals extensively with the communion of saints, emphasizing the biunivocal relationship between the mystical body and the Eucharist; it is because of the Eucharist that we can call the Church the body of Christ. Albert conceives and describes incorporation into Christ by the Eucharist with great realism. Like Alexander of Hales he argues that there is a difference between members of the Church and members of the mystical body. Finally he deals with the indefectibility of the See of Peter.

**10. Thomas Aquinas:** 1224-1274.

*Doctor angelicus*; *doctor communis*. Dominican

Life: 1230 Montecassino; Naples, Paris, Cologne; Paris (teaching since 1252); Rome (theologian of the papal court); Paris 1269; Rome-Naples 1272; between 1258 and 1273 he composes his monumental theological work; 1273 mystical episode (*tota palea*). 1323: canonization. In 1567, Dominican Pope Pius V proclaims him a Doctor of the Church.

He is also the author of liturgical hymns: *Pange lingua*, *Lauda Sion*, *Adoro te devote*.

Importance to his work of William of Moerbecke, Greekist, translator of Aristotle

Groups of works:

- Comments on Holy Scripture
- Comments on Aristotle
- Systematic works
- *Commentum in quattuor libros Sententiarum magistri Petri Lombardi*
- *Summa contra Gentiles*
- *Quaestiones disputatae*
- *Quaestiones quodlibetales*
- *Summa Theologiae*: divided into three parts, as St. Thomas explains at the beginning of the quaestio II of part I: "ad huius doctrinae expositionem intendentes, primo tractabimus de Deo; secundo, de motu rationalis creaturae in Deum; tertio, de Christo, qui, secundum quod homo, via est nobis tendendi in Deum".
- *First*: God, creation

The five ways; the distinction between essence and actus essendi; the treatise on the Trinity, a synthesis of the doctrines of Augustine of Hippo and Richard of St. Victor.

- *Secunda*
- *Prima secundae*: fundamental morality: the moral life is connected with the pursuit of happiness, which culminates in the blessed life in Heaven. So morality follows an intrinsic law in human nature. Difference between acquired and infused virtues, and thus valorization of good acts performed by infidels or persons who are not in a state of grace.
- I. The end to which man tends
- II. The acts to reach the end
- 1° - in general
- 1. Human acts in themselves
- 1) those proper to man
- a) their psychology
- b) their morality
- 2) Common ones with animals
- a) passions in general
- b) individual passions
- 2. The principles of human acts
- 1) Intrinsic
- a) powers
- b) the clothes: good (virtues and gifts) and bad (vices: sins)
- 2) Extrinsic
- (a) the law
- b) grace
- *Secunda secundae*: special morals (division by cardinal and theological virtues); charisms (*gratiae gratis datae*); states of life; religious life
- *Tertia*: Christology, sacramentaria (seen as a continuation of Christology)
- Brochures

Harmonious synthesis of faith and reason.

Use of Aristotelian science: *demonstratio*, syllogism

In the area of ecclesiology, the *Summa*, as was usual at the time, does not have a specific section on the Church. It is discussed in the treatise on Christ the Head. Thomas recovers the thought of the Fathers, placing the humanity of Christ as the instrumental cause of the Church and not only moral. He emphasizes the sacramental nature of the Church, which, like the sacraments, has two aspects, the visible and the invisible. He also connects ecclesiology with sacramentary: the hierarchical constitution of the Church derives from the sacrament of order; the *res tantum* of the Eucharist is the unity of the Mystical body, a living unity, which is the grace of charity.

Condemnation in 1270, by Stephen Tempier, Bishop of Paris, of 13 philosophical-theological propositions of Avicennian and Averroist flavor: these are errors already denounced by St. Bonaventure in 1267 and 1268. Second condemnation, in 1277, by Stephen Tempier, at the request of Pope John XXI, of 219 propositions; condemnation of the Dominican Robert Kildwarby, Bishop of Canterbury, also in 1277, which reproduces 30 propositions, some of which reflect the thought of Aquinas. This second condemnation also made those of Paris, less explicitly directed at Thomas, acquire an anti-Thomist tinge. In 1278 William de la Mare published the *Correctorium Fratris Thomae*, which in 1284 was approved and recommended by the general chapter of the Franciscan Order meeting in Strasbourg.

**11. Pietro di Giovanni Olivi:** 1248-1298

A strict Franciscan in the area of poverty, he was, however, an advocate of the pope's personal inerrancy.

**12. John Duns Scotus:** 1266-1308

*Doctor subtilis*

Franciscan. He operates in Oxford.

*Ordinatio*, first called *Opus oxoniense*

Rejects the theory of illumination in favor of that of abstraction

He greatly emphasizes the supreme freedom of God and his will: voluntarism; among the divine attributes he grants great importance to omnipotence.

Christology: argues for the predestination of the Word at the incarnation, even if man had not sinned.

Anthropology: superiority of the will and love over the intellect. Grace consists in charity, which has its seat in the will. The beatific vision is an act of love, and therefore of the will.

Find a solution to explain the Immaculate Conception.

It opens a great hiatus between theodicy and theology, greatly limiting the Thomasian analogy of entity.

**13. Ramon Llull:** 1232-1316

*Doctor illuminatus*

Native of Palma de Mallorca.

Apologetic works for the conversion of Muslims and Jews, based on the conviction of the rationality of faith. For this also studies and learns the Arabic language.

Main works: Ars *magna* (*Ars compendiosa inveniendi veritatem seu ars magna et maior*); *Art breu*.
