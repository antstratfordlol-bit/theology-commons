## The 10th Century: Monastic Theology in Times of Crisis {#pioppi-storia-teologia.6}
**1. General Background**

The general situation of barbarismin society continues: the assaults of Vikings, Hungarians and Saracensravage Europe.

A characteristic of this century is that ecclesiastical power ends up being controlled by the laity. The fact that it is the laity who choose the candidates for priestly positions, encourages abuses of simony (selling and buying ecclesiastical positions) and nicolaitism (priests living with concubines).

At the parish level: the phenomenon of the "private churches": in the countryside and in the more remote areas, the ecclesiastical authority did not have the means to establish pastoral centers; the feudal lords then took care of this, building churches and endowing them with a patrimony: but they then wanted to control the appointment of the parish priest, whom they chose directly, sometimes from among their own serfs.

At the episcopal level, the problem arises because of the *renovatio imperii* promoted by the Dynasty of Saxony. It ascended to the German throne in 919 and to the imperial throne in 962. The emperors were Henry I (919-936), Otto I (936-973), Otto II (973-983), Otto III (983-1002) and Henry II (1002-1024). They gradually succeeded in restoring order to the feudal anarchy that had affected the Holy Roman Empire and in stabilizing the Hungarians (955). To overcome the problems that arose from the Chapter of Quierzy of Charles the Bald (877), which made hereditary the major fiefs and thus favored the creation of local dynasties, they began to attribute the fiefs to the bishops: these, not having children, could not create dynasties and at their death the feud returned to the emperor, who chose another feudal lord (bishop). It is the system of bishop-counts: birth of large ecclesiastical principalities in the Empire, such as Cologne, Trier, Mainz, Liege, Salzburg, Trent (which will last until the Napoleonic invasion of Germany). The problem lies in the fact that, under this system, the emperors then want to control the appointments of bishops, to increase their political power in the Empire.

At the papal level, we find ourselves in what has been baptized the Iron Age ofthe papacy: the choice of the pope, important from a political point of view (the pope is the guide of the Papal States and the one who confirms the imperial election with the coronation) falls into the hands of the noble families of Rome and central Italy: above all, the Theophilatti family is famous, whose main exponents are two women: Marozia and Teodora. The period opens with the macabre process staged by Stephen VI against the exhumed remains of Pope Formosus, his predecessor (*Synodus ad cadaver*).

From 896 to 999, 27 popes succeeded each other on the papal throne, many elected as a result of family pressure; many died violent deaths in an attempt to regain autonomy. Some led immoral lives. In general, however, a very exaggerated version, due mainly to Protestant historiography, of this period has developed.

A great pope is John X(914-928): he succeeded in freeing central Italy from the scourge of the Saracens, leading himself an army against their base and destroying it (battle of Garigliano, 915): he gave in this way a great relief to the populations of Latium, Abruzzi and Campania, that for years had been oppressed by the continuous looting and violence of the Arabs. Unfortunately John X was imprisoned by Marozia and then killed in captivity.

In 910 the foundation of the Benedictine monastery of Cluny takes place: it will be very important for the reform of customs and ecclesiastical discipline in the following century. Liturgical splendor and perfect organization of the monastery, with a rule not too hard, based rather on obedience than on great sacrifices. The monastery has the second largest church in the Christian world (after St. Peter's Basilica) which was a splendid artistic treasure. The abbey has the characteristic of depending directly on the pope (exemption), not on the bishops, nor on the feudal lords; it also has the characteristic that the branches founded by it continue to depend on the abbot of Cluny (it is the first step towards the centralization of religious orders). Moreover, the Cluniacs are highly respected by all, since suffrages for the souls in Purgatory abound among their practices (they are responsible for the birth of the celebration of November 2nd). Finally, they are led at the beginning by very long-lived and holy abbots.

The missionary expansion ofthe Church continues: conversion of Poles, Eastern Slavs, Hungarians, Norwegians, and Danes.

**2. Odon of Cluny (d. 942)**

He is the second abbot of Cluny, a monastery founded in 910 by William the Pious, Duke of Aquitaine. In Cluny there is a fervent cultural activity, also because the *opus manuum* is reduced, in favor of the *oratio* and *lectio*. The first abbots are all monks of great intellectual and spiritual level: Bernone (910-927), Odone (927-942), Emardo (942-954), Maiolo (954-993), Odilone (993-1048).

Odon is a great ascetic and proponent of the reform of customs within the Church.

He writes the *Collationes* and the *Occupatio*, a biblical poem.

In his works the monastic life is presented as the best remedy to the evils and vices of the world. Pessimistic analysis of society, which leads him to one of the strongest forms of *contemptus mundi*. Monasticism prolongs in time and continues to offer men the possibility of participating in the fulfillment of the great ecclesial mystery inaugurated at Pentecost. It is a model that can be realized only far from a selfish, greedy, violent and lustful life such as the one we live in the world.

The Heavenly Jerusalem is reached through contemplation, and the latter through asceticism and liturgy (remember that in Odon's time at Cluny, monks recited 138 psalms every day).

The Church is presented as a vast monastery, in which there is the possibility of salvation for all those who live the mystery of Pentecost; the monastery is a sort of summary of the Church. Moreover, the abbey of Cluny supports the pontiff, and is itself a model of the centralized universal Church: in fact, the filial monasteries continued to depend on the abbot of Cluny.

In fact, Odone also writes a hagiographic work, the *Vita Geraldi*, about Count Gerard of Aurillac, who is a layman: the abbot of Cluny proposes the figure of a layman who, while remaining in the century, and in a socially relevant position, possesses and exercises the cloistered virtues.

Odone's opposition to the soteriological theory of the devil's rights over man is also worth mentioning.

**3. Acton of Vercelli (d. 961)**

Bishop of Vercelli. He writes the *De pressuris ecclesiasticis*, in which work he stigmatizes the miserable moral conditions in which the secular clergy is found and identifies the cause of this in ignorance. He proposes as a remedy the meditated reading of the Sacred Scripture, that leads to the *scientia fidei* and to the *discipline operum*. He reiterates the importance of ecclesiastical celibacy and deplores the participation of the clergy in shows, the frequenting of taverns, the practice of usury and simony.

In the *Expositio in epistulas Pauli*, we find a methodology that anticipates in some respects the scholastic one. There is in fact an initial *quaestio*, an exposition of the various *solutiones* of patristic literature, and finally the justification of the choice of only one of them.

In the field of ecclesiology, he advocates the less "Roman" line, that is, one that does not emphasize the powers of the pope much, but rather those of the bishops.

**4. Roswitha of Gandersheim (d. 973 ca. )**

Nun at Gandersheim Abbey, headed by Abbess Gerberga, who was a niece of Emperor Otto I. An example of the female culture of the time. She wrote a celebratory work on the emperor, the *Deeds of Otto*, and a series of hagiographic *Comoediae* in which she tries to bring together the style of the Latin playwright Terence and the hagiographies of his time. So he proposes effective models of holiness and virtue transported in dramaturgical form.

In this work he often makes allusions and references to the philosophical and theological culture of his time. Thus the virgin Constance, wishing the conversion of the Gallican leader to whom Constantine had promised her in marriage, turns to Christ with a synthesis of orthodox Christology and attributes of neoplatonic wisdom, moderator of the universe. The apostle John invokes, in order to bring the young Callimachus back to life, the uncircumscribed and uncircumscribable God, simple and priceless, who alone is simply *id quod est*, while man is composed *ex hoc et hoc.* The young girl Drusiana thanks God who has resurrected her, defining him as *vera et singolaris substantia et sine materia forma*. When Callimachus informs his friends that he is in love with Drusiana he answers their questions with an exposition of the logical terms that allow the correct definition of the concept.

**5. Raterio of Verona (887-974)**

He had a very adventurous and tormented life because of his strong desire to reorganize the ecclesiastical environment. From a noble family, he was born around 887 in the territory of Liege. He studied in the Benedictine Abbey of Lobbes, acquiring an excellent knowledge of Latin, the Bible and patristics, and then became a monk in the same abbey. In 926 he followed the abbot of Lobbes Induino in Italy, where in 931 Raterio received from the then king of Italy Hugh of Provence, cousin of Induino, the bishopric of the diocese of Verona.

Two years later, however, in conflict with the diocese and the king, Raterio was sent first to prison in Pavia, then in exile in Como from where he escaped in 939 to repair in Provence. Here he remained until 944, when he returned to the Abbey of Lobbes. Between 946 and 948 he was once again bishop of Verona and again had to flee to Germany because of the strong contrasts that emerged with the diocese and the city of Verona, represented by Count Milone.

From 953 to 955 he was bishop of Liege, but, driven out by a revolt of the local nobility, he had to recover in the Abbey of Aulne. In 962 he was again appointed bishop of Verona with the support of Otto I, but also this third time he was forced to leave in 968, repairing once again in the Abbey of Lobbes. From here, finally, in contrast with the then abbot of Lobbes Folcwin, was forced by the bishop of Liege Notker to repair in the Abbey of Aulne where he remained until his death on April 25, 974 in Namur.

He writes a *Dialogus confessionalis* and, his most important work, the *Praeloquia*: in them he wants to present a social ethic according to the states of life: kings, bishops, feudal lords, serfs. He opposes and condemns the mercantile economy, which was then moving its first steps, based on the accumulation of money and investment, contrasting it with the communitarian ideal of the *Acts of the Apostles*. He also believes that the restoration of the world derives from the rebirth of theology.

In the field of ecclesiology, he advocates the less "Roman" line, that is, one that does not emphasize the powers of the pope much, but rather those of the bishops.

In his writings and his preaching he deals a lot with the Eucharist, starting in Liege a tradition that will lead in the thirteenth century to make it the city of Corpus Christi.

In the same eucharistic line, always in the Liege area, we find **Erigerus of Lobbes** († 1007), who wrote a collection of eucharistic texts, the *Exaggeratio*, in which he tried among other things to settle the eucharistic controversy of the ninth century, supporting the orthodoxy of Pascasius Radbertus.

**6. Gerbert d'Aurillac (940ca.-1003)**

Benedictine. He entered the Monastery of Saint-Géraud d'Aurillac as a young man. From 967 to 970 he lived in Vich, in Catalonia, where he indirectly knew Arabic culture. He then stayed in Rome, where he made contact with the ancient culture. From 972 he taught at the Cathedral School of Reims; in 983 he became abbot of Bobbio; in 989 archbishop of Reims, then of Ravenna; finally, in 999, pope with the name of Sylvester II. Preceptor of Otto III with whom he shared the ideal of the *renovatio imperii*. Great scholar of his time, he wrote about astronomy, mathematics (*Geometria*), logic (*De rationale et ratione*), and grammar.

In theology he wrote a *De corpore et sanguine Domini*, in which he took up the themes of the Eucharistic controversy of the 9th century, defending the doctrine of Paschasius Radbertus from the criticism of Ratramnus of Corbie; it is ultimately an assertion of the real presence, despite the fact that Gerbertus, like everyone else in the early Middle Ages, calls the Eucharist *Corpus Mysticum.*

In the field of ecclesiology, he advocates the less "Roman" line, that is, one that does not emphasize the powers of the pope much, but rather those of the bishops.

He was a great scholar in the most diverse fields of knowledge: he knew the ancient classics very well: Virgil, Terence, Persius, Cicero, Sallust, Caesar, Seneca. He aspired to draw from the ancient authors teachings that could still be useful, and he polemicized against those who rejected them in the name of the Christian faith.

Moreover, he knew very well the whole Christian tradition. At the School of Reims he could read the *Categories* and the Treatise on *Interpretation of* Aristotle, the *Isagoge of* Porphyry, the works of Boethius, Raban Maurus, Incmarus of Reims, Alcuin, Scotus Eriugena, while in Spain he read Cassiodorus and Isidore of Seville. His wisdom, however, did not stop at the knowledge of the authors, but he wanted to develop the art of reasoning and synthesizing.

**7. Abbot of Fleury (d. 1004)**

Cluniac monk. After studying in Rheims and Paris, he taught at the Abbey of Fleury, of which he became abbot in 987. Friend of Odilon of Cluny and Gerbert of Aurillac. He taught for some time in the Abbey of Ramsey (England) and made numerous trips to Rome. Committed to the reform of the Church, he died in a riot during a mission of reform of a monastery.

His work represents a powerful attempt to do theology from a philosophy. For him, philosophy rises above common sense to reveal the conditions to which being is subjected in its less obvious manifestations.

It writes the *Quaestiones grammaticales* and the *Comment to the Calculus of Victor of Aquitaine* (mathematician of the V century). The latter is a treatise on the ability that mathematical studies have to produce effective theological knowledge in the mind, through the perception and conceptual representation of the harmony that God has established in creation. The reflection of this work begins with the concept of *unitas*, the first number from which all numerical multiplicity is derived and summarized. The one is immutable and for this reason it is placed at the beginning of the descending scale of being that closes, at the lowest level, with the multiple individual *unitates* that constitute multiplicity. Since the one is divine, everything that is not God is numerable, that is divisible and calculable. Therefore, the science of measurements, of which the *Calculus* is proposed as a useful tool, is in practice the foundation of wisdom. This wisdom is the science of things which, built on the teachings of the liberal arts, does not stop at the surface of knowledge and always seeks, as far as possible, to penetrate the intimate nature of things. True philosophy is characterized for Abbone by the process of ascent from a divided and complicated knowledge to an ever greater unification and simplification of the truth, with a continuous overcoming of divisions. Disciplined by discipline, Abbon strives to show how the study of each of the arts, rather than dispersing itself in the specific and the specialized, must lead to the recomposition in unity of the object of philosophy, which is the Supreme Good. For him, philosophy is the true love of wisdom that leads from accidentality to perfection, and helps us to rise from the knowledge of visible things to the unspeakable unity in the trinity of God.

Abbonian theology reconnects with Platonic teaching through Boethius and connects it with the Eriugenian theory of primordial causes. The unique theological intelligence of the abbot of Fleury stems from his scientific expertise, which suggests that he speaks of the God of the Christian faith through concepts borrowed from the great architect of the world of Platonic and neo-Pythagorean origin. His work is pervaded by confidence in the presence of a universal rationality, which is present in everything, determines everything, orders everything.

In addition, Abbone fought the millenarian tendencies of his era and wrote a treatise on logic on categorical syllogisms, in which he demonstrates an excellent knowledge of Aristotle's *Logica vetus.*

In the ecclesiological sphere, like the Cluniacs in general, he is an advocate of the "Roman" line, of support for papal authority and the enlargement of its prerogatives.
