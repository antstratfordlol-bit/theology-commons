## The 16th Century: Between Reformation and Counter-Reformation {#pioppi-storia-teologia.12}
**I. The general framework**

- Renaissance, which from Italy spread throughout Europe
- Geographical discoveries and first colonization: great development of catholic missions. Atlantic Revolution: the axis of the European economy passes from the Mediterranean Sea to the Atlantic Ocean: the decline of Italy begins.
- Rise of Spain and Portugal, which in 1580 to 1640 will be united under the Spanish crown: Habsburg attempt to create a universal Catholic empire, opposed by the Kingdom of France
- Advance of the Ottoman Turks: 1523, capture of Rhodes; 1529 siege of Vienna; 1565, siege of Malta; 1571, battle of Lepanto
- Protestant Reformation and Catholic Counter-Reformation: confessionalization and rupture of European unity: *cuius regio ejus religio* and wars of religion

a) Chronology of the Protestant Reformation

- **1483:** Martin Luther is born (Germany)
- **1484:** Ulrich Zwingli is born (Switzerland)
- **1505:** Following a vow made during a violent storm, Luther enters a convent in the Augustinian Order.
- **1509:** John Calvin is born (France)
- **1510:** Luther's trip to Rome
- **1513-1516:** Luther's inner travail about experiencing the reality of being in God's grace. *Turmerlebnis*. The following text belongs to the autobiographical preface that Martin Luther wrote on March 5, 1545, less than a year before his death, for the first volume of his *Complete Works*. Looking retrospectively at his younger years, specifically the winter of 1514-1515, that is, the months before his lectures on St. Paul, he writes the following:

"A wonderful desire had taken possession of me to know the thought of St. Paul in the letter to the Romans, which I had not done until then, not because of coldness of my blood around the heart, but because of that phrase in the first chapter: "the righteousness of God is revealed in him." For I hated that expression "the righteousness of God," which the usage and custom of all the doctors had taught me to translate philosophically as "formal or active righteousness," as they call it, by which God is just and chastises sinners and the unjust.

But I, who although I lived as a blameless monk always felt before God a sinner with a troubled conscience, knowing that I could not appease Him with my good deeds, did not love Him, indeed I hated the just God and chastiser of sinners, and I will not say with tacit blasphemy, but rather with tremendous murmuring, I was indignant with God saying: "as if it were not enough to keep us miserable sinners oppressed with all kinds of calamities by the law of the Decalogue, already condemned to eternal punishment by original sin, so that now God, through the Gospel, adds to one pain another pain, threatening us again through the Gospel with his justice and wrath!”. Thus terribly tormented, I raged and inopportunely consulted that passage of St. Paul, with a burning thirst to know what the apostle meant.

Finally, after waiting days and nights meditating on the connection of these words, "iustitia Dei revelatur in illo, sicut scriptum est: iustus ex fide vivit", I began to understand by "the justice of God", that is, that of faith..., the passive justice of God, by which God the merciful justifies us by faith, as it is written: "the just lives by faith".

Then I felt completely reborn, as if the doors had been thrown open to me and I was entering paradise itself. From that moment on, I began to praise the sweetest expression of 'the justice of God' with a love as great as the hatred with which I had previously hated it, so that that passage of St. Paul's was truly the gateway to paradise for me".

Luther begins to theorize about justification by faith alone, the total corruption of human nature by original sin (extreme Augustinianism), and the denial of free will.

- **1517:** Luther's personal problems intercept the socio-religious situation in Germany, where there was a strong discontent towards Rome for the many abuses present in the Church, especially in the areas of commissions and accumulation of benefits in the same person. In concrete terms, the question of indulgences takes shape: Leo X had proclaimed the preaching of an indulgence for the Fabric of Saint Peter's, to finance the construction of the new basilica. Albert of Brandenburg, who had accumulated three bishoprics: Magdeburg, Halberstadt and Mainz, was appointed as the collector for Germany. In the appointment, however, it was foreseen that half of the amount collected would go to the Fabbrica and the other half would be used by Albert to pay the heavy taxes to Rome concerning the dispensations for the accumulation of episcopates. The indulgence is sometimes preached in a very crude manner. Great scandal in Germany. 95 theses of Luther against indulgences, which make him famous. In general Luther begins to question many aspects of the mediation of the Church.

His ideas on justification will lead him to deny many aspects of the Church's visibility, specifically the mediation of the sacraments and the hierarchy. Moreover, his discourse makes it very difficult to find a balanced and harmonious relationship between grace and nature.

- **1518:** Albert of Brandenburg sues Luther before the Holy See. The Roman trial begins.
- **1520:** Luther writes three important polemical works:
- *To the Christian nobility of the German nation*, in which he hopes for a national Church
- *De captivitate babylonica Ecclesiae praeludium*, in which he denies transubstantiation in favor of consubstantiation, and criticizes the sacrificial character of the Mass
- *De libertate christiana*

Pope Leo X responds with the bull *Exsurge Domine*, in which Luther's ideas are condemned and he is invited to recant, under penalty of excommunication. Luther publicly burns the bull.

Zwinglius begins the reformation in Zurich.

- **1521:** Luther is excommunicated.

Leo X dies

With the Edict of Worms, Charles V condemns Luther, but he is saved by the prince of Saxony in the castle of Wartburg, where he remains hidden for several months, and where he elaborates better his thought and writes: against the monastic vows, ecclesiastical celibacy, the sacraments; he elaborates better the principle of *sola scriptura* and translates the Bible into German.

Henry VIII king of England, in controversy with Luther, edits in his name the book *Assertio septem sacramentorum* and for this he receives from the pope the title of *Defensor fidei.*

- **1522:** Socioreligious unrest begins in Germany with the Knights' War.

The Dutchman Adrian VI is elected pope, the last non-Italian pope before John Paul II.

- **1523:** Adrian VI dies and is succeeded by Clement VII

Ends the Knights' War

Open rebellion of Zwingli

- **1524:** Caused by the Reformation, the Peasants' War begins, with unprecedented violence. Luther calls on German princes to suppress the revolt with violence
- **1525:** Luther marries nun Catherine von Bora.

Albert of Brandenburg, Grand Master of the Teutonic Order, adheres to the reform, secularizes the order and founds the Duchy of Prussia on its territories

Abolition of the Zurich Mass (Zwingli)

German princes are divided into two camps: Catholic and Reformed

The peasants are defeated by the princes at Frankenhausen; their leader, the priest and reformer Thomas Müntzer is brutally tortured, then beheaded. The Peasants' War ends. Luther in his writings becomes increasingly intolerant and violent against anyone who does not think like him, even against other reformers, such as Ulderico Zwiglio (Colloqui di marburgo, 1529), Andrea Carlostadio, Martin Bucero, Giovanni Ecolampadio, Volfango Capitone, Stefano Agricola. The only one who always remained faithful and close to him was Philip Melanchthon, a humanist and much more moderate than Luther.

- **1526:** Luther begins to speak of the "supreme episcopate of princes," which gains him the favor of many German princes: these, by embracing the Reformation, assume total control over the Church in their domains, and, with the suppression of religious orders, forfeit their vast properties.
- **1527:** King Gustavus I of Sweden becomes Lutheran and imposes the Reformation in the country, but leaves many cultic aspects similar to the Catholic ones

Henry VIII of England, in love with the lady of the court Anne Boleyn, begins to ask that his marriage to Catherine of Aragon be declared null and void

- **1529:** the Imperial Diet of Speyer imposes tolerance for Catholics throughout Germany; 6 princes and 14 cities protest this decision: hence the reformers begin to be called Protestants. Charles V cannot intervene because the Turks are besieging Vienna.
- **1530:** At the Diet of Augsburg, Protestants present the *Confessio Augustana*, which will be one of the fundamental documents of Lutheranism.
- **1531:** battle of Kappel, in Switzerland: the Catholics defeat the Protestants and in the clash dies Zwinglio
- **1533:** Calvin becomes a Protestant. Henry VIII marries Anne Boleyn
- **1534:** John of Leiden, leader of the Anabaptists, seizes power in Münster and founds the Kingdom of Zion, with a millenarian flavor. In fact, he established an absolute, fanatical, theocratic government, where all forms of debauchery were allowed to flourish. Protestants and Catholics join forces to eliminate the Anabaptists from Münster and besiege the city.

Clement VII dies

Henry VIII, since he is not able to obtain from the Pope the declaration of nullity of his first marriage, with the Act of Supremacy separates the Anglican Church from the Catholic Church

Christian III imposes Lutheranism by force in Denmark

- **1535:** after 15 months of siege, Münster falls; John of Leiden is imprisoned
- **1536:** Christian III of Denmark imposes Lutheranism in Norway.

John of Leiden is executed among atrocious tortures

- **1541:** Calvin seizes power in Geneva and imposes a theocracy there.
- **1546:** The Smalcaldic War begins between the emperor and most of the German Protestant princes
- **1547:** Charles V defeats the Protestants in the battle of Mühlberg, which, however, is not decisive

Henry VIII dies and is succeeded by Edward VII, who transforms the Anglican schism into a Protestantism

- **1549:** Edward VII has the *Book of Common Prayer* published, in which the sacrificial character of the Mass is denied
- **1550:** The Danes defeat the army of Icelandic bishop Jon Arason, who is beheaded. Lutheranism is imposed on the island.
- **1555:** Pacification of Augsburg in Germany between Catholics and Protestants, based on two rules:
- *cuius regio eius et religio*: subjects must follow the confession of the prince
- the *reservatum ecclesiasticum*: if an ecclesiastical prince adheres to the reform, he must renounce the principality
- **1560:** after the preaching of John Knox, the Scottish Parliament adheres to Calvinism by voting the *Confessio Scotica*; nevertheless Queen Mary Stuart remains Catholic
- **1563:** Count Palatine of the Rhine adheres to Calvinism. Three confessions in Germany
- **1564:** Calvin dies
- **1566:** The Dutch War begins. Calvinist Dutch rise up against Spanish rule.

b) The Council of Trent and the Counter-Reformation

In the first decades of the 15th century, the Church seemed to succumb to the enormity of the crisis triggered by Luther. Yet she found unexpected energies that gave her the possibility of a stunning recovery. The Church will emerge from the test renewed, united and compact. The territorial losses in Europe were then compensated for by the great missionary expansion in America, Asia and Africa. The most important instrument for coming out of the crisis was the Council of Trent.

On the death of Clement VII, Pope **Paul III** (1534-1549), of the Farnese family, was elected. A lover of luxury, hunting and entertainment, and very nepotistic, he was, however, an intelligent man: he therefore realized the grave danger that threatened the Church, and understood that it was necessary to react to the crisis. To this end, he created in 1542 the Sacred Congregation of the Roman and Universal Inquisition, for the repression of heresy, thus restoring the medieval inquisition, which had been almost inoperative for more than a century.

On the other hand, the necessity of convening an ecumenical council, long requested by Charles V, by the Protestants, by the wisest Catholics, becomes more and more evident; however, Clement VII had always been opposed to the council, for fear of a resurgence of conciliarism. Paul III was convinced of the necessity of the council, but on the other hand the hostilities between France and the Habsburgs (Spain-Empire) did not allow it to begin until 1545. The city of Trent is chosen as a compromise solution. The Protestants wanted the Council in Germany, the Pope in Italy: Trent was an Italian city on German territory.

**1st period: 1545-1547.** The council begins with only 34 council fathers. The pope is not present in person. It is decided to address simultaneously the problems of Church reform (*decreta de reformatione*) and dogmatic problems (*decreta de fide*). It was also decided to abandon the system of voting by nations established at Constance and to return to individual voting. The Council Fathers were assisted by many theologians, among whom the Dominicans of the School of Salamanca (Domingo de Soto, Melchor Cano) were particularly important.

*Decreta de fide*:

- Importance of Tradition along with Scripture (vs. *scripture alone*)
- Fixation of the Biblical Canon (Protestants had eliminated the deuterocanonicals)
- The *Vulgate* is declared an authentic translation
- In interpreting the Bible, one must always keep in mind the common opinion of the Fathers and the judgment of the Church.
- Decree on original sin and justification: masterpiece of the council. Faith as the beginning, foundation and redeemer of salvation, but without denying the value of works.
- Sacraments: Baptism and Confirmation

*Decreta de reformatione*:

- Establishment of Scripture chairs in all major churches and monasteries
- Duty of preaching for priests with care of souls
- Mandatory residency for benefit holders (prohibition on accumulation of benefits)
- Qualities needed for bishop candidates

In 1547 a typhus epidemic in Trent forced the bishops to leave the city; Paul III took advantage of this to move the council to Bologna. Charles V, who had just defeated the Protestants at Mühlberg and therefore hoped they would come to the council, protested strongly. Paul III in 1549 suspends the council and dies shortly thereafter.

Card. Giovanni Maria del Monte, president of the council, who takes the name of **Julius III** (1550-1555). Nepotistic and worldly, he is nevertheless a very capable person. He immediately reconvenes the Council in Trento.

**2nd phase: 1551-1552.** There is participation by a small group of Protestants.

*Decreta de fide*:

- Eucharist
- Penance
- Anointing of the Sick

The council is suspended in 1552 for security reasons; in fact, due to the betrayal of Prince Maurice of Saxony, the military situation of Charles V becomes precarious and the Protestant armies advance in southern Germany, not far from Trent.

**Paul IV** (1555-1559). Elected at 79 years of age. Energetic and proud, he is, however, opposed to the Council and believes that he can only reform the Church through the Roman Curia. For this reason, he begins to reform the Curia itself, scourging its abuses very harshly. He strengthens the Inquisition. He creates the *Index Librorum Prohibitorum* in 1559. The Roman Index is not the first attempt in this sense. The first index is that of the Sorbonne (1544), then there is that of the University of Louvain (1546), then those of the Portuguese Inquisition (1549), of the Republic of Venice (1549), of the Spanish Inquisition (1551), of Milan (1554) and Florence (1554).

Paul IV is succeeded by **Pius IV** (1559-1565), conciliatory and diplomatic, but worldly and nepotistic (in 1560 he appoints as cardinal and archbishop one of his 21-year-old nephews, Carlo Borromeo, who will become the pope's right-hand man and good genius for some years). Pius IV reopens the Council

**3rd phase.**

*Decreta de fide*:

- Mass as Sacrifice
- Sacrament of Orders
- Marriage: *Tametsi* decree on the canonical form, against illegal marriages
- Purgatory
- Cult of saints, relics and holy images
- Indulgences

*Decreta de reformatione*:

- Prohibition of accumulation of ecclesiastical benefits
- Mandatory residency of bishops (eliminates accumulation of bishoprics)
- Obligation for prelates to be consecrated within three months of receiving the benefice
- Establishment of diocesan seminaries
- Diocesan synods (every year) and provincial councils (every three years)
- Bishop's pastoral visit to parishes
- Religious instruction of the people

It is also decided to prepare a new Index, Breviary, Missal and Catechism.

The new Index is published in 1564

In the same year, 1564, the Sacred Congregation of the Council was created to oversee the implementation of the Council.

In 1565 the council was solemnly closed: at the close there were 225 participants.

The council is quickly received in the Catholic world:

- Total recession in Portugal, Italy, Poland and Germany
- Recection "without prejudice to royalties" in Spain
- Reconstruction of dogmatic decrees only in France

Saint **Pius V**, Dominican (1566-1572)

- 1566 publishes the Roman Catechism
- 1568 publishes the new Breviary
- 1570 publishes the new Missal
- 1571 establishes the Sacred Congregation of the Index, which deals only with prohibited books, removing this function from the Inquisition

**Gregory XIII** (1572-1585)

- Reform of the Julian calendar: the astronomer Louis Lilius studies a correction of Julius Caesar's calendar: the new calendar will be called Gregorian, in honor of the pope. The Protestant and Orthodox states do not accept the Gregorian calendar and continue to use the Julian calendar (the Protestants will accept it throughout the 18th century, the Orthodox in the 20th century).
- Foundation of the German-Hungarian, English, Greek, Armenian and Maronite Colleges. Beginning of the history of the national colleges, very important to give cohesion to the ecclesial structure.
- He fostered the Roman College, which would later be called the Gregorian University in honor of this pope.
- Founds permanent nunciatures in Vienna, Paris, Madrid and Lisbon

**Sixtus V** (1585-1590), Franciscan

From very humble beginnings, with an incredible ability to work.

Organization of the Roman Curia into 15 congregations: broadly speaking, this is the current structure

*Editio Sixtina* of the *Vulgate*

**Clement VIII** (1592-1605)

He availed himself of the collaboration of learned cardinals, such as the Jesuit theologian Robert Bellarmine and the Church historian Cesare Baronius, an Oratorian, who wrote the *Annales Ecclesiastici*, in response to Flacius Illyricus' *Centuries of Magdeburg* (a history of the Church on the basis of anti-Catholic Protestant polemics).

*Editio Sixto-Clementina* of the *Vulgate*, which will last until the *Neovulgate* (twentieth century)

c) Catholic Reform and Counter-Reformation

In addition to the great work of doctrinal clarification and disciplinary reform of Trent, the recovery of the Catholic Church was also due to a very rich spiritual flowering, which led to the birth of many new religious orders. In particular, the canonical figure of the clerics regular (e.g. Jesuits, Theatines, Barnabites) was emerging, a further step in the history of consecrated life towards pastoral commitment. Already in the thirteenth century, in fact, there had been a shift from monasticism (monasteries outside the city, great space dedicated to the liturgy, vow of stability, independence of each monastery) to the structure of mendicant orders (centralization of government, convents in the cities, central pastoral commitment in the life of the religious). Clerics Regular go a step further: they dress like secular priests, have no choir obligation, and engage in a wider range of pastoral and social activities than do mendicant orders.

The main new religious foundations of the 16th century are:

- 1517: Observant Minors (reform in the Franciscan Order)
- 1524: theatines (Saint Gaetano da Thiene)
- 1525: Capuchin minors (reform in the Franciscan Order)
- 1530: barnabites (saint Antonio Maria Zaccaria)
- 1532: somaschi (Saint Jerome Emilani)
- 1534: Jesuits (Saint Ignatius of Loyola)
- 1535: Ursulines (Saint Angela Merici)
- 1540: Fatebenefratelli (Saint John of God)
- 1559: Discalced Minors (reform in the Franciscan Order; Saint Peter of Alcántara)
- 1562: Discalced Carmelites (Saint Teresa of Ávila and Saint John of the Cross)
- 1575: Oratorians (Saint Philip Neri)
- 1584: Camillians (Saint Camillus de Lellis)
- 1597: Scolops (Saint Joseph Calasanz)

In addition to this work of reform, which offers enormous energies to the Catholic Church, there is also the action of real Counter-Reformation, represented to a large extent by the reorganization of the Inquisition. The modern Roman Inquisition was created in 1542: however, it would function only in Italy (and not everywhere) until the 18th century. So it had a quantitative impact much less important than we think. It must also be said that it was a disorganized institution, which often had to suffer the competition or the hostility of the civil power and sometimes even that of the local bishop. It also had judicial systems that were more guaranteeing than those of the civil power.

Different from the Roman Inquisition were the Spanish and Portuguese, which were state institutions and depended on their respective kings. Even here, however, should be resized their work of repression (eg in all of Mexico, in three centuries, there are about forty death sentences by the Inquisition).

The term "Counter-Reformation" is rather recent: it originated in 19th century German Protestant historiography (Gegenreformation). It was probably the Protestant historian Leopold von Ranke who first spoke of the Epoch of the Counter-Reformation in 1843; the term was Counter-Reformation had already been used in 1776 by J.S. Putter, but it spread in academic circles especially in the decade 1860-70. It originally had a negative connotation for the Catholic Church, so Catholic authors often preferred to speak of "Catholic Reformation." It can currently be thought that in the Catholic response to Protestantism there are aspects of reform and others of counter-reform.

**II. Theologians of the beginning of the century**

**a) Erasmus of Rotterdam: 1467?-1536**

Dutch. Illegitimate son of a priest. He was orphaned of both parents at 14 years. All this contributed to develop in him a character quite restless. Although over time will develop, even in the cultural field, a love for peace and a distaste for the strong controversy.

To twelve years he enters in the Augustinian convent of Steyn. In 1492 he is ordained priest and becomes secretary of the archbishop of Cambrai (Flanders): in such condition he undertakes various trips in France, England and Italy (Florence, Padua, Venice, Rome); he tightens friendship with many Italian humanists and with Tommaso Moro. In 1506, in Turin, he received his doctorate in theology. He will always be on the move, between Flanders, England, Switzerland, Holland. In 1517 he abandons the religious habit to remain a secular priest.

It becomes one of the greatest and known European humanists of the first half of the century. Great Greek and Latin philologist.

His theological work is characterized by:

- criticism of scholasticism
- the revaluation of scriptural studies
- the launch of positive theology
- the anti-lutheran controversy

1516: critical edition of the New Testament, called *Novum Instrumentum*, dedicated to Pope Leo X. This work aroused great controversy, Erasmus had to defend himself and published many responses and apologia of the work, and in them he will clarify and refine his thinking about the theological method, and in it the place of philological studies.

He then writes the *Enchiridion militis christiani*, in which he advocates the alliance between humanism and Christianity, through a simple, accessible and pure Christian philosophy, the result of returning to the sources (Scripture and the Fathers: positive theology).

Critical work of scholasticism and customs of the privileged classes is the *Encomium moriae seu laus stultitiae* (*Praise of madness*), 1509, dedicated to Thomas More.

Critical editions of the Fathers of the Church, therefore positive theology (Origen, St. Jerome, St. Cyprian, St. Hilary of Poitiers, St. Ambrose, St. Irenaeus of Lyon, St. Augustine, St. Basil of Caesarea, St. John Chrysostom, Arnobius).

Erasmus advocates a renewal of theology through a new inculturation, in keeping with the sensibilities of his time, which consists in applying the philological method to Scripture in order to understand its true meaning. However, his project lacks doctrinal insights, being concerned only with exegesis. In the *Ratio verae theologiae he* also tries to give a hermeneutical doctrine, along the lines of St. Augustine's *De doctrina christiana.* In this project Erasmus proves to be a bit naive, believing that with only philological exegesis one can make a complete theology.

At first Erasmus looks upon Luther with some sympathy; in 1519 the German reformer writes to him to gain him to his cause, but Erasmus replies evasively.

Erasmus then wrote a work in which he went to meet Luther: the *Inquisitio de fide*, in which he emphasized man's smallness and the necessity of divine grace.

The break came in 1523, when Erasmus wrote *De libero arbitrio diatribé sive collatio*, divided into four parts:

- A premise in which we lay out the facts of the problem
- An analysis of Scripture that presents passages in favor of free will
- An analysis of Scripture that presents passages contrary to free will
- Solution in favor of the existence of free will: without grace man cannot take the path of good, nor progress in it, yet there is also his freedom which is expressed in the *studium et conatus* (research and effort) that are required of man to be virtuous. If human freedom is eliminated, man would become for Erasmus a slave, and God a tyrant.

One can appreciate how Erasmus tries to dialogue with Luther, using only the Holy Scripture as the source of his analysis. In 1525: Luther responded with *De servo arbitrio*, in which he reiterated his ideas in a contemptuous and arrogant tone. Erasmus wrote a rebuttal.

What separates Erasmus from Luther, in addition to the Catholic faith in which Erasmus, despite aspiring to a reform in the Church, wants to remain, is also his humanism, which clashed head-on with Luther's religious and intellectual attitudes, typical of a medieval man, with a strong negative and pessimistic charge towards human nature (balanced, however, by the individualism of the idea of free examination of the Bible). In the end, Luther's dark and pessimistic passionality is contrasted with rational harmony and humanistic luminosity.

Erasmus was, however, a sincere believer, a Catholic intellectual sensitive to the culture of his time; he was not, as he is sometimes presented, an Enlightenment *ante litteram*.

He also wrote, in 1516, an *Institutio principis christiani*, intended for Charles V as soon as he ascended the throne, and a *Letter to Francis I of France*: Erasmus is in total antithesis to his contemporary Niccolò Machiavelli; for the Dutchman public happiness depends on the moral perfection of the prince, and there are not two morals, one for the rulers (guided by the reason of state), the other for the subjects. The Christian prince must follow Christ on the path that He has traced for him, with sacrifice. In the *Querela pacis, he* also criticizes those who speak of the inevitability of wars, trying to expose the falsity of the idea that conflicts are fought out of necessity.

Erasmus's biblical and philological studies were the basis for the emergence of serious Catholic **exegesis** in the second half of the century, with the Italian Dominicans **Sante Pagnini** and **Sixtus of Siena**, and the Spanish Jesuits **Alfonso Salmerón** and **Juan Maldonando**; and, in the following century, with the Flemish Jesuit **Cornelius a Lapide**.

In many ways similar to Erasmus, but more profound spiritually and theologically, was the Frenchman **Jacques Lefèvre d'Etaples** (1460ca-1536ca).

**b) Tommaso de Vio, Cardinal Gaetano [or Caietano]: 1468-1534**

Dominican. Man of great culture and very active in the government of the Church. He studied in Naples, Bologna and Padua. He teaches at the University of Padua, then in Milan and Pavia. From 1508 to 1517 he is general of the Dominicans. He participates in the Lateran Council V, where he supports the urgent need for a reform in the Church. In 1517 he was created cardinal. In 1518 he was sent by the Holy See to convince the emperor to war against the Turks, but ended up dealing mainly with the question of Luther, with whom he had a famous meeting, in which he tried in vain to moderate his extreme positions with a benevolent and paternal attitude. He was then (1523-1524) sent as legate to Hungary, Bohemia and Poland. He was taken prisoner by the Lansquenets during the sack of Rome (1527) and freed after the payment of a high ransom. He then dealt with the divorce requested by Henry VIII of England. In September 1534, at the death of Clement VII, he was among the candidates for the papacy, but he died the following October. He wrote 157 works of theology, exegesis, and philosophy.

Great commentator on Aristotle and St. Thomas Aquinas; sometimes found closer to the former than to the latter.

A Thomist, he distances himself, however, from St. Thomas in some important matters: he ignores the theory of the *actus essendi*, so important in Aquinas' metaphysics; he does not have the same harmony between faith and reason as the Angelic doctor.

For example, on the immortality of the soul, he supports a kind of double truth: rationally it is not demonstrable, but we believe it by faith, thanks to revelation. He was probably influenced in this by Pomponazzi.

He also argues that Aristotle had conceived of the soul in the manner in which it was later interpreted by Averroes, namely, the unity of the agent intellect.

He also believes that the dogma of the real Eucharistic presence cannot be based on the narratives of the Last Supper reproduced in the Synoptics and *1Cor*: obviously, Gaetano does not deny the dogma, but the fact that it can be demonstrated from Scripture.

An important work of his is *De nominum analogia*. In it he rejects the position of those who believe that the sense of the expressions we use to speak of God is entirely different from the sense in which we use the same expressions to speak of creatures: such a conception would lead to agnosticism. But also to be rejected is the theory according to which the sense is instead totally equal: this second position would in fact lead to pantheism.

In controversy with Luther, he strongly emphasizes man's cooperation with grace through his works for justification, and grants great room for free will in human action: for this he was harshly criticized by theologians of Augustinian approach.

The Gaetano is very important for the diffusion of Thomism, preparing its triumph that will happen shortly thereafter thanks to the School of Salamanca; at the beginning of '500, in Paris, Peter Crockaert replaces the *Sententiae* of Peter Lombard with the *Summa* of Thomas Aquinas.

On the other hand, Gaetano conveys a Thomism that differs in some ways from the doctrine of St. Thomas.

His influence on later Thomists was very great. The edition that Pius V had prepared of the works of Thomas Aquinas contained the commentaries of Thomas de Vio. A great part of the authors of the second scholasticism and of the neo-Thomism were formed in the light of a Gaetanist reading of Thomism, with the limitation that often Gaetano comments on St. Thomas in a purely Aristotelian key, and not in his originality that surpasses the Stagirite.

He writes a *Commentary to the Summa Theologica*; of the *Commentaria super tractatum de ente et essentia Thomae de Aquino*. Besides *In Sacrae Scripturae expositionem*, and *Commentaria in tres libros Aristotelis de anima*.

In the ecclesiological field he is a decided anti-Conciliarist; regarding the infallibility of the pope, he distinguishes a personal error *in credendo*, which he considers possible, from a judicial error *in definiendo*, which instead is for him impossible.

It also raises issues that stem from recent geographic exploration:

- Whether one can baptize a child without the consent of the parents: Gaetano, following St. Thomas here, says no, in disagreement with Duns Scotus, for whom it would be permissible.
- Theory of enlightenment of children who die without baptism: they would have, at the end of life, a special enlightenment that would allow them to opt for God.

**c) Domenico Giacobazzi (1444-1527)**

Roman nobleman, bishop and cardinal, great canonist, died during the Sack of Rome. He participated in the Lateran Council V. In the theological field he distinguished himself in ecclesiology, with an important work *Tractatus de concilio* (published posthumously in 1538 by his nephew cardinal Cristoforo Giacobazzi). It presents an important definition of general council: "concilium est congregatio praelatorum ex universo orbe et aliarum personarum authoritate, et voluntate papae convocatarum ad aliquid communi intentione communiter tractandum in religione christiana, papa in ipso concilio praesidente vel alio loco ejus et aliquando Christi, sive ipsiusmet concilii authoritate suffulta". Giacobazzi, however, makes a distinction between ecumenical councils (those of the first millennium) and general councils (second millennium)

**III) Controversial Theology**

With the Protestant crisis, public disputes between Catholic and Reformed theologians became widespread. Hence, throughout this century, the theology of controversy with Protestantism developed, with the development of the *demonstratio catholica,* which - together with the *de locis*, forms the starting nucleus that will develop into today's fundamental theology. Sometimes these works are marked by a strong *vis polemica*, which is often seen in the titles of the works themselves: for example, the work of the Dominican Ambrose Catarino entitled *Apologia pro veritate catholicae et apostolicae fidei ac doctrinae adversus impia ac valde pestifera Martini Lutheri dogmata.* Catarino is also famous for his polemic against the Gaetano and the Thomists of strict observance (Bartolomeo Carranza and Domingo de Soto), in the name of humanistic culture.

**A) John Eck (1486-1542)**

A diocesan priest, he was a professor in Ingolstadt, where he distinguished himself in moral questions concerning the permissibility of lending with interest, an area in which he was modernly in favor of it within certain limits (this is why he was called the "Fugger theologian" by his detractors (the Fuggers were a large German banking family).

In 1517 he enters in controversy with Luther, writing the *Obelisks*; Luther answers in 1518 with the *Asterisks*. In 1519 a takes place in Leipzig a public dispute between Eck and Luther. In 1520 Eck writes the *De Primatu Petri adversus Lutherum*; in the same year he receives the task of managing the publication of the bull *Exsurge Domine* (the document of excommunication against Luther) throughout Germany, a task that he performs with dedication, sometimes risking his own life to accomplish it.

In 1522 he wrote *De poenitentia et confessione*, in 1523 *De purgatorio*, reaffirming the Catholic doctrine in these areas, against the denials of the same made by the Reformed.

In 1525 he published his most famous work: the *Enchiridion locorum communium adversus Ludderanos*, a response to Philip Melanchthon's work *Loci communes theologorum* of 1521. In it he adduces scriptural and patristic evidence on many controversial issues. The work is very useful and appreciated as a tool to prepare for public disputes, and has a huge success: it is reprinted in 90 editions.

In 1537 Eck, again to combat the Reformation, prepared a German version of the Bible.

**B) Giovanni Cocleo (1479-1572)**

John Dobneck, called Cochlaeus. Canon of Mainz, then of Breslau.

Culturally humanist, until 1520 looks at Luther with some sympathy, then becomes a fierce opponent. In 1521 he experienced a strong inner conversion, thanks to the environment of the Oratory of Rome. He wrote about 200 works against Protestantism, often with a very polemical tone. He protected the Society of Jesus in its beginnings. His main work is the *Commentaria de actis et scriptis Martini Lutheri* (1549).

His commitment to the controversy was such that he renounced the edition of his *opera omnia in* order to finance with his own money the works of Conrad Braun - another controversialist - which at that time seemed more useful to the Catholic cause.

**C) John Fisher (1489-1535)**

Englishman; chancellor of the University of Cambridge and bishop of Rochester. Died beheaded by order of the king for not accepting the Anglican schism. Saint.

- In 1520 Luther published *De captivitae babylonica Ecclesiae*, which contains a strong attack on Catholic sacramental doctrine.
- 1521: In response to that book, King Henry VIII of England published under his own name the work *Assertio septem sacramentorum*, which may have been written by Fisher. For that book, the king received the title of *defensor fidei* from the pope.
- 1522: Luther's retort: *German response to King Henry's book*.
- 1523: Fisher's rejoinder: *Lutheranae assertionis confutatio*.

He also wrote, again against Luther, the *Epistola de caritate*.

**D) Josse Clichtove (1472-1543ca)**

Flemish, disciple of Lefèvre d'Etaples, worked and died in France. Important for the reflection and clarification of the role of the priest in the Mass seen as a sacrifice. He wrote:

- *Antilutherus* (1524)
- *Propugnaculum Ecclesiae adversus Lutheranos* (1526)
- *De sacramento Eucharistiae, contra Oecolampadium* (1526)
- *Compendium veritatum ad fidem pertinentium contra erroneas Lutheranorum assertiones* (1529)

**E) Albert Pigghe (Albertus Pighius, 1490-1542)**

Dutch, was a theologian, mathematician, astronomer and geographer. He wrote the *De hierarchia ecclesiastica* (1538), in which among other things affirms the inerrancy of the pope even as a private person; and the *De libero hominis arbitrio et divina gratia* (1542).

**F) Ruard Tapper (†1559)**

Dutchman, chancellor of the University of Louvain. Supports, among other things, the infallibility of the Chair of Peter.

**IV. The School of Salamanca**

Resumption of Thomism, caused in part by the controversies with the Protestants. In fact, in the very exasperated theological contest of the time, the need was felt to have rigorous instruments on which to base one's theology; this caused the abandonment of many forms of Renaissance theology: Platonic, partially subjectivist, historical, in order to resume modes and forms of Aristotelian-Thomistic scholasticism.

This Thomist revival has its champions in a group of Spanish Dominican theologians, whose center was the University of Salamanca, founded at the end of the 14th century, and who succeeded in bringing together scholasticism and humanism. Three are the most important; two of them are the great theologians of the Council of Trent. In the 1500s Salamanca was one of the greatest theological centers in Christendom.

It should be borne in mind that in the sixteenth century Spain rose to the role of leading European and world power and was the political, cultural and spiritual epicenter of the Catholic revival in the face of the Protestant crisis.

**(a) Francisco de Vitoria (1483-1546)**

Dominican. He studies in Paris. He teaches in Paris, where he is a student of Peter Crockaert, then in Valladolid, and finally in Salamanca (1526), where he replaces the *Sententiae of* Peter Lombard with the *Summa of* Thomas Aquinas.

He left us the *Relectiones Theologicae*, his only theological work, containing 13 of his dissertations delivered at the university. They are:

1. *De potestate civili* (1528)
2. *De homicidio sive de fortitudine* (1530)
3. *De matrimonio* (1531)
4. *De potestate Ecclesiae prior* (1532)
5. *De potestate Ecclesiae posterior* (1533)
6. *De potestate papae et concilii* (1534)
7. *De augmento caritatis* (1535)
8. *De eo ad quod tenetur veniens ad usum rationis* (1535)
9. *De simonia* (1536)
10. *De temperantia* (1537)
11. *De Indis prior* (1539)
12. *De Indis posterior seu de iure belli* (1539)
13. *De Magic* (1540)

In them he deals extensively with ethical-political issues: for example, in *Relectio de Indis* we find the debate on the legal titles that justify the conquest of the Americas by the Spaniards. In that study he is very modern: he analyzes all 15 titles that were being produced at his time, and declares seven of them illegitimate, eight legitimate. It is interesting that he judges illegitimate the title of the papal cession of these lands: in fact - he argues - the temporal power of the pope is always indirect (through moral issues), never direct; therefore the pope had no right to cede the discovered lands to the Spaniards, because he had no power over them. He is the founder of the right of the people, as he argues that all men possess by nature a set of rights.

Already in *De potestate papae* and in the two *De potestate Ecclesiae* he had supported these ideas about the limits of papal power, following Thomas Aquinas' *De regimine principum.* The pope is not *dominus orbis*, and his power is always indirect, even if broad: this means that if the pope were to say that a certain administrative decision was wrong, he would be overstepping the limits of his power; instead, it is his duty to point out laws that are clearly unjust. But where the sphere of the salvation of souls ends, so does the pope's power.

*De potestate Ecclesiae posterior* enters into debate with Lutheran theories regarding power in the Church. Vitoria argues that ecclesiastical power does not reside in the community, as is the case with the state, which is based on natural law. Instead, the Church is based on positive divine law, and for it, power was vested in the apostles and their successors.

The *De potestate papae et concilii* addresses the issue of conciliarism: it reaffirms the superiority of the pope over the council, but denounces the frequent abuses of power by the Roman Curia and the pontiff.

In *De potestate civili* Vitoria argues that political power comes from God, but not directly, but through designation by the community. He applies the theory of the four causes to politics and the state:

1. Efficient cause: God
2. Material cause: it is the society that organizes itself in state
3. Formal cause: type of organization (monarchy, oligarchy, democracy)
4. Final cause: public good

In the two *relectiones de Indis* Vitoria intervenes in the debate going on at his time in Spain about the lawfulness or not of the conquest of the American dominions: in general he affirms such lawfulness, but at the same time rejecting the traditional arguments ("titles"); here are some examples:

| **Reject** | **Accept** |
| --- | --- |
| Sovereignty of the emperor over the whole world | The right to propagate the faith without obstacles |
| Authority of the pope giving the Indies to Spain | Protection of indigenous converts |
| Right derived from discovery | Tyranny of indigenous kings and inhuman laws |
| Indigenous rejection of Christianity | Lower level of civilization (doubtful) |
| God's special gift to Spain | Spanish right to unimpeded travel |
| Sins against nature of the natives | Voluntary indigenous choice |
|  | Conversion of most natives but not of the king |

So we find a Dominican theologian who disputes that the pope has universal temporal power and can therefore take territory away from one government and give it to another.

In these two *relectiones* Vitoria also sets himself as the founder of international law, when he speaks of the *jus gentium*, based on natural law and valid for all peoples, Christian and non-Christian; his incipient reflections in this regard will later be developed and theorized by Francisco Suárez and Hugh Grotius.

He is also the first scholar who was able to clearly formulate the law of war.

Vitoria also took part in some theological debates in the Spain of his time: among them, it is worth mentioning his moderate but censorious intervention on some aspects of the theology of Erasmus of Rotterdam (especially with regard to a certain underestimation of the patristic tradition compared to the sacred scripture); and the one on the preparation necessary to baptize the *Indians*: Vitoria advocates a long and careful preparation, so that the newly baptized person has the tools and knowledge to be able to live truly as a Christian.

**b) Domingo de Soto (1495-1560)**

Dominican. Thomist. Collaborator of Vitoria. Theologian in Trento, where he contributed in an important way to the drafting of the decree on justification and the one on original sin. He was also confessor of the emperor Charles V for some years. Worked for the Spanish Inquisition. Jurist, dogmatist and moralist. Studied in Alcalá and Paris, taught in Salamanca.

Works:

- *Summulae* (1529): in 4 volumes
- *In dialecticam Aristotelis* (1543): commentary to Aristotle
- *In libros Physicorum* (1545): comment to Aristotle
- *De natura et gratia* (1547): commentary on the decree *De iustificatione* of Trent.
- *De iustitia et iure* (1553): continues in the line of Vitoria
- *Tratado del amor de Dios*: a work of asceticism, in which he combats the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith alone, without works.

He intervenes in the dispute over the absolute certainty of one's state of grace; Soto thinks that one can have moral certainty, but not absolute certainty, unlike Ambrose Catarino, who argues the opposite.

He also intervenes in the dispute between Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda (totally in favor of the conquest) and Bartolomeo de Las Casas (very critical of the conquest) about the titles of Spanish dominion in America in the line of Vitoria, and arguing the importance of *jus gentium* to find solutions to the problem.

Although he was a Thomist, he was affected by the nominalist training he received in Paris: for example, he considered the distinction between essence and act of being in creatures superfluous.

In the field of ecclesiology, he considers the episcopate and also the duty of residence of bishops to be of divine right; at the same time he upholds the supreme power of the pope over the episcopate and the ecumenical council.

**(c) Melchor Cano (1509ca-1560)**

Dominican. Bishop. Theologian in Trent.

His most important contribution to theology is the treatise *de locis theologicis*, which, together with the *demonstratio catholica*, forms the starting point that will develop into today's fundamental theology. It is written against the principle of *sola Scriptura* advocated by Luther.

In the *Libri duodecim de locis theologicis*, that is, the sources of theology, Cano posits ten places:

- seven own, of which
- two own fundamentals:
- Holy Scripture
- Tradition
- five own declarative or explanatory
- Catholic Church
- Councils
- Church of Rome (popes)
- Church Fathers
- School Theologians
- three auxiliaries:
- Reason
- Philosophers and jurists
- History

He then proposes a well-balanced theological system, including scripture, patristics, magisterium, historical context, and scholastic philosophy.

He is very critical of the work of Pietro Lombardo, whom he considers, a little unfairly, very disordered in the disposition of the subjects, and to whom he reproaches for not having distinguished the treatises *de Deo uno* and *de Deo trino*.

Other works: *Relectio de sacramentis*, *Relectio de poenitentia*.

**V. Theological debates on grace**

The Lutheran doctrines on justification also push Catholic theology to question itself more deeply on the role and action of divine grace in Christian life, also in relation to human freedom. Moreover, the Protestant denial of created grace pushed Catholic theology to reaffirm this concept and to deepen it. The decree on justification envisages grace as something broader, and the bearer of a richer and deeper salvific value than Lutheran justification; on the other hand, the decree does not clarify the relationship between divine grace and human freedom. This renewed interest in the theology of grace gave rise to heated debates in the Catholic world, which all started from two sources: the Council of Trent and the works of St. Augustine (*doctor gratiae*).

**a) Michele Baio (1513-1589)**

Michel Le Bay. Originally from the Netherlands (in present-day Belgium), he taught at the University of Louvain, and later became its chancellor. He participated in the last phase of the Council of Trent as a theologian.

It is part of the great resurgence of Augustinian studies that took place in the sixteenth century, and of which Louvain was one of the main centers of thought. Baio claimed to have read 9 times the *opera omnia of* the Hippocrates, and 70 times his writings on grace.

Among his works: *De libero hominis arbitrio et eius potestate*; *De justitia et justificatione*; De *meritis*; *De prima hominis justitia et de virtutibus impiorum*; *De charitate, justitia et justificatione*.

Reasoning about grace, he arrives at statements that turn out to be heterodox.

He offers an interpretation of St. Augustine's most pessimistic version of man's natural possibilities.

He, in fact, argues that the integrity of man's first creation was not an undue exaltation of human nature, but a natural condition of it; in short, for him the elevation to grace would not be gratuitous, but due: it was an essential requirement of human nature. Before original sin, man was due eternal life. Before sin, without the need of any extraordinary gift, the progenitors could do everything necessary to achieve bliss.

Grace would come into play only after original sin. So for him, the innocent man has real rights before God.

Baio therefore exaggerates the rights of man in the supra-palapsarian state; but he also exaggerates the damage produced in him by sin. Without grace man can do nothing but sin. From this follows another assertion of his, that there are no virtues of the unfaithful, and that every act committed outside the grace of God would be sin. Moreover, for Baio without grace man cannot resist the smallest temptation. Unbaptized children, at the first awakening of reason, commit acts of rebellion, rejection and hatred of God.

In his theology, however, he takes care to save the free will of man, to avoid falling into predestinationism; but in the end, in man after original sin, the action of grace is granted a maximum space with respect to freedom.

His doctrines are condemned by the universities of Paris, Salamanca and Alcalá; then in the same Louvain; then by Saint Pius V, after the complaint to the Inquisition by the Jesuits and the appeal of Baio: 1567, bull *Ex omnibus afflictionibus.* The condemnation is reiterated by Gregory XIII in 1579 (*Provisionis nostrae*). Baio submits sincerely.

Two consequences of his teaching remain, however:

1. His ideas remain present in certain theological circles in Louvain, from which Jansenism will spring in the following century.
2. Catholic theology, to counter his theories, strengthens and structures the doctrine of the state of pure nature, already affirmed by Gaetano.

The controversy triggered by Baio prepared the ground for the Controversia *de auxiliis*.

**b) The Controversy *de auxiliis***

In the study of the action of grace in the life of the Christian, a controversy arose over the relationship between divine grace and human freedom. The Dominicans, in general, were more inclined to give more room to grace than to freedom, the Jesuits the opposite. The debate thus became a dispute between religious orders, which lasted for twenty years. In particular, the two theologians who were the protagonists of the controversy were Bañez OP and Molina SJ. The two denounced each other to the Spanish Inquisition: Bañez accused Molina of semi-Pelagianism, and Molina accused Bañez of Lutheranism.

- **Luis de Molina (1536-1600):** professor in Coimbra and Evora. He writes the *Concordia liberi arbitrii cum gratiae donis*: theories of average science, of simultaneous concurrence, and of prevenient and cooperating grace. He moves from a very optimistic interpretation of Saint Augustine, basing himself above all on the antimanic works of the Ipponate. The *Concordance* is however a difficult text, repetitive, cumbersome and difficult to understand.
- **Domingo Bañez (1528-1604):** professor in Salamanca. Ávila and Alcalá. He wrote the *Relectio de merito et augmento charitatis*: theory of prior contest and physical predetermination. Bañez was confessor of Teresa of Avila. Tries to hold a middle position between Baio and Molina.

Clement VIII, in 1597, appointed a commission to settle the matter. The commission had 120 sessions (about half of which were also attended by the pope), until, in 1607, Paul V closed the issue, prohibiting to continue to discuss it and to accuse of heresy those of the other tendency: "verum ab eodem Sanctissimo Domino serio admodum vetitum est, in quaestione hac pertractanda ne quis partem suae oppositam aut qualificaret aut censura quapiam notaret".

**VI. Ecclesiology: the notes of the Church**

In 1529 the first monograph on the four notes of the Church was published: one, holy, catholic, apostolic. This monograph - *De notis Ecclesiae*) was written by the Franciscan **Nicholas Ferber of Herborn**. After him, the subject was also treated by Melchor Cano, Domingo Báñez, Francisco Suárez, Card. Stanislaus Hosius, and by Saint Robert Bellarmine. This theory would come to full fruition with the works of the Hungarian Cardinal **Peter Pázmany**, in the early seventeenth century (Pázmany: 1570-1637, of noble Hungarian Calvinist family, converted to Catholicism as a student; Jesuit, Archbishop of Esztergom, Cardinal).

For a Gallican-style ecclesiology, on the other hand, it is worth mentioning **Pierre Pithou** (1539-1596): *Les libertés de l'Église Gallicane* (1594).

**VII. The birth of the separate treatise on moral theology**

The Catholic Counter-Reformation led to an appreciation of the Sacrament of Confession and also to the structuring of seminaries and curricula for candidates for the priesthood. There was therefore a need for instruments to form good confessors. As part of the *ratio studiorum of* the Society of Jesus, courses in moral theology were organized for the preparation of confessors: there was a *cursus maior*, more theoretical, and a *cursus minor*, more practical.

The best manual for these courses is written by the Spanish Jesuit **Juan Azor (1536-1603)**, under the title *Institutiones morales, in quibus universae quaestiones ad conscientiam recte aut pravae factorum pertinentes breviter tractantur*. Thus begins the treatise and teaching of morals separate from the rest of theology. The problem with such treatises is that, since they are aimed at the training of confessors, they soon slip into casuistry, losing sight of fundamental moral theology (I-II of Aquinas' *Summa*). In fact, Azor in his *Summa does* not treat the themes of I-II at all, intending that they would be studied in other instances and situations.
