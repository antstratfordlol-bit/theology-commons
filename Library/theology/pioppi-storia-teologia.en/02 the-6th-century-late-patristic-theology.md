## The 6th Century: Late Patristic Theology - a Silver Age {#pioppi-storia-teologia.2}
1. General Background

- We are in the late antique world, which is still a Mediterranean world, and not a European one in the current sense of the term.
- The fall of the Western Roman Empire has just occurred (476).
- Establishment of the Roman-Barbarian kingdoms: Visigoths, Ostrogoths, Vandals, Burgundians, Franks, etc.
- Religious duality in these kingdoms: Arian Germans and Catholic Latins, except for the Kingdom of the Franks, where there is a direct transition from paganism to Catholicism (this is why France is called la *fille ainée de l'Église*)
- Reign of Justinian 527-565:

- closure of the School of Athens, the last important center of pagan culture (529)

- reconquest of the west: Africa, Italy and Andalusia

- Corpus Iuris Civilis: 1. Institutiones (treatise on law); 2. Leges; 3. Digest (judgments of jurists: Paul, Ulpian, Papinian, Modestus, etc.); 4. Novellae (more recent laws)

- 568: Lombard invasion of Italy, which divides the peninsula into two: Romània (territories left to the Romans [Byzantines]) and Langobardia (territories conquered by the Lombards [who are Arians]).
- abandonment of the cities towards the *villae* (protohistory of feudalism); begins the decadence of culture and commerce

2. Ecclesial Context

- Development of monasticism: St. Benedict 480-547; foundation of Montecassino 529 (sacked by Lombards in 581). Monasteries outside the city.
- Semi-Pelagianism: no need for grace for *initium fidei*; resolved by the Second Council of Orange (529), which at the same time rejects extreme Augustinianism (predestination).
- Acacius Schism between East and West: 484-519. Caused by the *Henotikon*, promulgated by the Byzantine emperor Zeno, to try to settle the disagreement between Catholics and Monophysites.
- Second Council of Constantinople (553):

- Question of the Three Chapters: attempt of Justinian to bring Catholics and Monophysites back to unity.

- condemnation of Origen

- term "hypostatic union."

- Conversion of Visigoths to Catholicism (from Arianism): 589

3. Caesarius of Arles (470-543)

He is born in the Kingdom of the Burgundians. Becomes a monk in Lerino. Subjected to excessive penances, he fell ill and was dismissed from the monastery. He then studied rhetoric and philosophy. Then he became presbyter in the Diocese of Arles, in the Kingdom of the Visigoths, and administrator of a monastery in Trinquetaille; in 502 he became bishop of this city, which in 508 became part of the Kingdom of the Ostrogoths. Accused of treason in 512 he had to go to Ravenna by King Theodoric to exonerate himself.

He wrote:

- Preaching works: *Sermones*
- The *Regula monachorum* for the monastery of Trinquetaille
- Various works on virginity
- Works on the Trinity against the Arians (*De mysterio sanctae Trinitatis*, *Breviarium adversus haereticos*)
- *Opusculum de gratia*, the most important work, connected to the Council of Orange of 529; Caesarius reaffirms the strict Augustinian doctrine on grace against semipelagianism: in fact he affirms that grace, even initial, is given *nullis praecedentibus meritis*. On predestination, in the *Opusculum* he is a radical Augustinian, while on the subject he is a little more cautious in the *Sermons*: Pharaoh's heart (Exodus) is hardened both by the subtraction of grace *and* by Pharaoh's wickedness.

4. Fulgentius of Ruspe

Fabius Claudius Gordianus Fulgentius: born between 460 and 470, died between 527 and 533. Bishop in Vandal Africa. Born to a wealthy family of Carthage. He had an excellent education and was elected procurator of the city of Telepta. But around 499 he had a spiritual conversion and embraced the monastic life. In 507 the faithful and the clergy of Ruspe elected him bishop.

He wrote several anti-Arian works (including *Contra Sermonem Fastidiosi ariani liber unus*, *Contra arianos liber unus* and *Ad Thrasamundum regem vandalorum libri tres*) that earned him the exile imposed by the same king Trasamondo. Returned to his homeland, he was again exiled by King Ilderico. He was able to return permanently in his diocese but died a few years later.

He followed the doctrines of Augustine: in the area of the theology of grace he wrote the *Ad Monimum*.

In *De incarnatione et gratia* he made an excellent synthesis of Augustinian thought and of the theological evolution that took place between the Councils of Nicaea and Chalcedon.

He also wrote polemical works against monophysitism: *De Trinitate ad Felicem notarium*.

He wrote a very famous work in the Middle Ages, the *De fide sive de regula verae fidei ad Petrum*, which is a kind of synthesis of Christian doctrine, which had much success throughout the Middle Ages because it was believed to be a work of St. Augustine. In it, among other things, the formula *extra Ecclesiam nulla salus* is made explicit: "firmissime tene, et nullatenus dubites, non solum omnes paganos, sed etiam omnes Iudaeos, et omnes haereticos atque schismaticos, qui extram Ecclesiam catholicam presentem finiunt vitam, in ignem aeternum ituros".

Also in controversy with the Arians who denied the divinity of the Holy Spirit, he states that the Third Person proceeds from the Father and the Son (*Filioque*).

5. Neo-Chalcedonian theologians

After the Councils of Ephesus (431) and Chalcedon (451) and throughout the sixth century, there are important theological discussions in the East concerning the search for a concordance between the *Twelve Anathematisms* of Cyril of Alexandria ("mia physis tou Theou logou sesarkomene"), and the *Tomus ad Flavianum* of Leo the Great. John the Grammarian, with his *Apology of the Council of Chalcedon*, tries to adapt Cyrilian expressions to Chalcedonian ones, favoring the latter. On the other hand, Severus of Antioch (†538) tries to defend Cyril's terminology (one nature), even though he enters into controversy with the radical monophysites: in fact, after the incarnation, he speaks of a "composed nature" of the Word, and of two *ousiai* in a single *physis* (in the sense of concrete being).

6. Pseudo-Dionysius

This is a rather mysterious character, a Syriac Christian who lived in Asia Minor and/or Athens, probably towards the end of the V century and the beginning of the VI century. He wrote under the pseudonym of "Dionysius, Bishop of Athens" and was believed in the Middle Ages to be the disciple of St. Paul, and called "Dionysius the Areopagite": this identification with a direct pupil of the Apostle of the Gentiles gave him a great prestige among the theologians of the Middle Age, inferior only to that of St. Augustine. His works were translated into Latin in the ninth century by Abbot Ilduin of St.-Denis and John Scotus Eriugena, and in the twelfth century by John Saracen.

The Pseudo-Dionysius operates the first full-bodied experiment to create a theological system based on the application of philosophy to Christian doctrine: in particular he depends in good measure on the neo-Platonism of Proclus Lycus Diadocus (412-485), thought based on a triadic model: permanence-exitus-return (cf. *exitus-reditus* medieval). The existence of every entity is characterized by three moments:

1. that of the participation of the higher principle (permanence, *moné*)
2. that of difference from it (output, *proodos*)
3. that of aspiration towards the higher principle to acquire greater perfection (return, *epistrophé*).

This pattern will have a great influence throughout medieval theology, and especially in Hugh of St. Victor, Albert the Great, and Thomas Aquinas.

Dionysius seeks a deeper knowledge of God through a greater understanding of how human language works.

The doctrine of the Areopagite is at the same time:

- sapiential: deep intelligence of the divine mysteries
- apophatic: consciousness of divine sublimity
- mystical: belief that one comes to God above all through a deep spiritual life.

In his doctrine one can also appreciate, in addition to the Neoplatonic influence, that of the Alexandrian School and the Cappadocian fathers; but the only direct source he uses is scripture (neither fathers nor councils): scripture, however, is understood in an ahistorical way: not therefore as salvation history, but as a place of contemplation of God's self-revelation.

Apophatic theology (in it Dionysius follows very much Philo of Alexandria and Gregory of Nyssa): *De divinis nominibus* and *De mysthica theologia*

- God is seen primarily as goodness and beauty. "By this Beauty all beings have obtained to be beautiful, each in its own way, and because of the Beautiful there exist the agreements, friendships and communications of all things, and in the Beautiful all things stand together. The Beautiful is the beginning of all things as the efficient cause, which moves all things and holds them together with love toward its own beauty; and the Beautiful is the end of all things and is worthy of being loved as the final cause (in fact, all things come into being because of the Beautiful) and exemplary cause, because all things are defined in reference to Him. In fact, what happens to the Beautiful happens to the Good: all things in every way tend towards the Good and the Beautiful [...]. This one Good and Beautiful is in a unique way the cause of all things beautiful and good, which are many" (*De div. nom.* 4,7).
- Study of the possibilities and limits of theological discourse. "It is the custom of theologians to return to positive terms and deny them in order not to apply them to God in their negative aspect" (*De div. nom.* 7,1). God is the cause of creatures and they necessarily reflect his perfections. But all the perfections are possessed by creatures in a finite, limited way: this demands that our language, when applied to God, must be corrected. Therefore, the best way to speak of God is the apophatic way, that is, through negations; but these negations are not to be understood in a privative sense, but as transcendent: "we call the Light inaccessible and invisible Darkness, because it transcends the light we see" (De divine nom. 7,7). (*De div. nom.* 7,2). In short, the Pseudo-Dionysius through the negative way takes what scholastics will call "eminential way". This is how the contact between theology and mysticism takes place: "the higher we rise, the more our words contract for the overall vision of intelligible things. So now, penetrating into the fog that lies above the intelligence, we will find not the brevity of words, but the absolute lack of words and thoughts [...]. Now speech, ascending from the lower things to what is above everything, as it rises, it shortens; and at the end of the whole ascent it becomes completely silent and will be totally united to him who is inexpressible" (*De myst. theol.* 3). So there are three human ways of speaking about God:
- affirmative, *thesis*
- negative, *afáiresis*
- superlative, *though*.

*De coelesti hierarchia*: Pseudo-Dionysius identifies three degrees of angels, each of which is divided into three orders:

1. 1. Angels; 2. Archangels; 3. Principatus
2. 1. Potestates; 2. Virtutes; 3. Dominationes
3. 1. Throni; 2. Cherubin; 3. Seraphin

*De ecclesiastica hierarchia*: short ecclesiological treatise, in which are presented:

- the active hierarchy, with three priestly states: deacons, presbyters, bishops
- the passive hierarchy, with three common states: catechumens, common Christians, monks
- the instrumental hierarchy, with three sacraments: baptism, eucharist, confirmation

7. Boethius (480ca.-526)

Anicius Manlius Torquatus Severinus Boethius. Considered at the same time "the last Roman" and "the first scholastic". From a senatorial family of the *gens Anicia,* he studied in Rome, Athens and Alexandria; a married layman, he held important positions in the Ostrogothic Kingdom: president of the Roman Senate, sole consul in the year 510, palace master (*magister officiorum*) of the Ostrogothic king Theodoric. He fell into disgrace for suspicion of collusion with the court of the Eastern Roman Empire, was imprisoned and after several months executed.

He particularly studied the Neoplatonic (especially Porphyry) and Aristotelian philosophies.

Very important for the transmission of classical culture, for its translations into Latin of Greek philosophical works. In the Roman Empire, since the ruling class was bilingual, there was no need to translate Greek works into Latin (we begin to find a work of translation only from the end of the fourth century, with Jerome and Rufinus), so when in the Middle Ages the Mediterranean world was divided into three separate worlds (Europe, Byzantium, Islam), in the West was lost all Greek culture, especially in philosophy, except the works translated by Boethius, who can be rightly considered one of the masters of medieval culture. In fact, he translated and commented on the works of logic (what in the thirteenth century will be called the *logic vetus*), such as the *Isagoge* of Porphyry; he also wrote a commentary with a translation of the *Categories of* Aristotle, and two of *Perí hermenéias* (*De interpretatione*) of the Stagirita: an elementary in two books, and a scientific in six. In these commentaries on Aristotle, Boethius seeks to show that the Stagirite is in substantial agreement with Plato.

All this material, together with the *Menon* and the *Phaedo* of Plato and the Grammar of Priscius, will be the philosophical equipment of the medieval culture until the great translations of the second half of the XII century. Boethius is the main transmitter of Aristotle to the Middle Ages, contributing in a predominant way to the great prestige of the Stagirite as *philosophus* par excellence in monastic and scholastic theological literature.

Boethius was also very important for terminology, since his translations of Aristotelian terms have passed to medieval and modern Latin philosophical culture, some examples:

- enérgeia - actus
- eidos - species
- arché - principium
- universal→kath'ólou = concepts of genus and species, seen under their logical aspect
- katáphasis - affirmatio
- apóphasis - negatio
- aporia - dubitatio
- diaphorá - differentia
- division→diaíresis
- synbebekós - accidens
- endechómenon - contingens
- dynamis - potentia
- hypokeímenon - subiectum
- theoría - speculatio
- horízesthai - define
- homónymon - aequivocum
- antíphasis - contradictio
- contrary→enántios
- antistréphei - convertitur

Moreover, Boethius is one of the systematizers of medieval culture, dividing its basic principles into the arts of the *Quadrivium* (a term he himself forged), leaving a work for each of them: arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy (only the first and the third have survived).

He also wrote theological works, with a very philosophical theology (especially from Aristotle): for this reason he is considered the father of scholasticism. They are the *Opuscula sacra*, in five books:

1. *De fide catholica* or *Brevis fidei christianae complexio*: it is a kind of catechism, in which there is also the exposition of the errors of the Arian, Sabellian, Manichean and Pelagian heresies (on this book there are doubts about the authenticity)
2. *Treatise on the Person and Two Natures of Christ* (*Liber de persona et duabus naturis contra Eutychen et Nestorium*), in seven parts:

- Concept of nature
- Concept of person: traditional definition of person:

*individua substantia rationalis naturae*

- Person and nature from a linguistic point of view
- Nestorius' errors
- Errors of Eutiche
- Exposition of Catholic doctrine
- Christ is *of* and *in* two natures

1. *De hebdomadibus* or *Quomodo substantiae in eo quod sint bonae sint cum non sint substantialia bona*, of Neoplatonic influence. It poses the following problem: in what way are substances, by the fact that they are, good, although they are not substantial goods? In it we find two important concepts at the basis of scholastic theology: 1) the distinction between *esse* and *id quod est* (i.e. between being and entity); 2) the distinction between *per essentiam* and *per partecipationem* predication.
2. *Utrum Pater et Filius et Spiritus Sanctus de divinitate substantialiter praedicentur*: "since the persons are divided, while the substance is undivided, it is necessary that the name derived from the persons does not belong to the substance. It follows that neither "Father," nor "Son," nor "Holy Spirit" are substantially predicated of God, but according to relationship. Whereas God, truth, justice, goodness, omnipotence, substance, immutability, virtue, wisdom, are said substantially of the Godhead."
3. *De Sancta Trinitate*: on the basis of the homonymous writing of St. Augustine, Boethius examines the relationship between unity and trinity of God and illustrates it through the use of Aristotelian categories of relationship. Also in this work he makes the distinction between *esse* and *id quod est*: they are in God the same thing, but not in creatures; this distinction will be taken up and developed by medieval scholasticism, to reach the distinction between essence and existence, a distinction that Boethius does not reach.

The *Opuscula* were widely used throughout the Middle Ages: there are commentaries on them written by various authors: John Scotus Eriugena, Remigius of Auxerre, Gilbert Porretta, Thomas Aquinas. We also find them frequently cited in Alcuin of York and Hincmar of Reims.

In the *Opuscula* we already find *in nuce* methodological elements of scholasticism: for example, the statements: "fidem, si poteris, rationemque coniunge"; "ut igitur in mathematica fieri solet ceterisque etiam disciplinis, praeposui terminos regulasque quibus cuncta quae sequuntur efficiam". From them we see the intent to make theology a well-structured science.

Finally there is Boethius' best known work, written in prison, the *De consolatione philosophiae*. If there is God, whence come evil things? And if God is not, whence the good things? It is a treatise more philosophical than theological, which tries to solve the problem posed by the relationship between divine providence and human freedom, trying to fathom the complex issue of divine foreknowledge. It is based on mostly neoplatonic and stoic concepts. As already said, it is a philosophical work, not a theological one: in fact, it never speaks explicitly about Christ and Christianity. But it had a great fortune in the Middle Ages, it transmitted the love for classicism, the taste for a dry and rational style, as well as some conceptual definitions (of eternity, beatitude, providence) very useful to medieval theology. In order to make compatible the existence of God and the existence of evil, Boethius argues that what can satisfy the human desire for happiness are neither riches, nor honors, nor pleasures, but only God; then he explains that evil does not originate in God, but in creatures, because of their finiteness (physical evil) or of the bad use they make of freedom (moral evil); he then deals with the relationship between human freedom and divine providence: the latter does not fail in spite of the development of actions due to the former. Ultimately Boethius masterfully distinguishes the two concepts of predestination and divine foreknowledge: the latter is present in God, but does not compromise human freedom.

*De consolatione* is divided into five books:

1. Problem setting and question position
2. What happiness is not
3. What is happiness
4. Fortune works in the service of providence
5. How providence does not eliminate man's freedom

For all these works, Boethius is certainly considered one of the great masters to which medieval culture has drawn; as Battista Mondin wrote: "for the medievals he was much more than an intermediary between the ancient culture and them: literally he was placed at the same level of Virgil and Statius, philosophically at that of Augustine, Plato and Aristotle".

8. Cassiodorus (490-583)

Flavius Magnus Aurelius Cassiodorus. Calabrese. Senator. He succeeded Boethius in 523 as master of palace (*magister officiorum*) of the ostrogoth king Theodoric, and was then prefect of the praetorium. In 537 he retired from public office; he thought at first to create a school of theology in Rome, but the attempt was unsuccessful and Cassiodorus ended up founding in Calabria the monastery of Vivarium, in his estates near Squillace, and transferred there his rich library. This was a project at the same time spiritual and cultural (large *scriptorium* and collection of manuscripts), in which the contemplative ideal of classical *otium* was merged with the ideal of ascetic and mystical Christian.

He is another of the great masters of the Middle Ages, author of encyclopedic works, much used in the following centuries. It is he who, taking up the structure of the classical author Marcus Terentius Varro (116-27 BC), operates the subdivision of the teaching of the medieval arts in Trivium (grammar, dialectic, rhetoric) and Quadrivium, considered preparatory to theology, in a vision of harmony between faith and reason.

Here are his major works:

- *Institutiones divinarum et saecularium lectionum*: 1st part, biblical and Christian culture: Scripture and its major commentators; 2nd part, secular culture: trivium and quadrivium. He incorporates the ideas of St. Augustine (*De doctrina christiana*) for the use of secular disciplines for theological purposes. Widely used throughout the early Middle Ages, e.g. by Isidore of Seville, Alcuin of York, and Rabanus Maurus.
- *Historia ecclesiastica tripartita*: continuation of the *Historia Ecclesiastica* of Eusebius of Caesarea from three sources: the historians Theodoret, Socrates and Sozomenus. Cassiodorus is therefore the one who introduced the Greek historians of the Church into medieval culture.
- *Variae*: also contains many official letters written for the Chancery of Theodoric; written at the invitation of the king
- *Expositio Psalmorum*
- *Chronicon*: from Adam to the year 519
- *Historia Gothorum*
- *De anima*: philosophical treatise. Regarding the soul, it deals with: definition, direct creation by God, constitutive element, spirituality, union with the body, immortality. The body is presented as the temple of the soul. The soul is described as a light, "has for substance a certain light".

9. Gregory the Great (540-604)

He was one of the greatest popes in history, from 590 to 604.

Belonging to the noble family of the Anici, he was *praefectus urbis*; in 574 he had a conversion to monastic life and founded a monastery in one of his palaces at the Caelian. In 579 he was sent by Pope Pelagius II as apocrisary to Constantinople. In 586 he became secretary of the pope.

In 590 he was elected pontiff. With his pontificate, the temporal power of the popes began, out of sheer necessity in the face of the power vacuum caused by the impotence of the Byzantine exarch of Ravenna in the face of the Lombard advance; a very capable administrator, he reorganized the finances and properties of the Roman Church, using them to the advantage of the Church and the needy; he promoted the evangelization of the Angles, Saxons and Jutes by sending the mission of Augustine of Canterbury to England: the new English Church is closely tied to Rome and is the first to receive the pallium; sponsors reform of liturgical chant; turns Constantine's monument above St. Peter's tomb into an altar.

Controversy with John IV the Digiunator, patriarch of Constantinople, who assumes the title "ecumenical patriarch." In response to this claim, Gregory I assumes the title of *servus servorum Dei*.

His works are primarily moral:

- *Regula pastoralis*, vademecum of conduct for bishops, in 4 parts
- Sublimity and difficulties of the pastoral office
- Shepherd Conduct
- Preaching
- Daily spiritual renewal of the pastor's soul
- *Moralia in Iob*: vast synthesis of Christian life
- *In Ezechielem*: 22 homilies preached to the clergy following the example of Jerome and Augustine in similar situations, with Rome threatened by the advancing Lombards: very high and theological
- *Homiliae in Evangelia*: 40 homilies of a more practical and pastoral tone than those on Ezekiel
- *Dialogues*: written for the Lombard queen Theodolinda, Catholic, on the virtues and miracles of Italian saints
- *Register of Letters*
- *Sacramentary*: collection of liturgical prayers of the Church of Rome.

In soteriology, he supports the theory of the devil's rights over man, following in the footsteps of Origen, Ambrose, and Augustine.

Regarding the Church, Gregory distinguishes between the broader concept of the *Ecclesia universalis*, which includes all the righteous beginning with Abel and even the angels, and the more limited concept of the *Ecclesia electorum*, formed by the Catholic saints, that is, the Christians worthy of that title.

In the *Regula pastoralis he* also deals with the characteristics and duties of the Christian prince, to whom power has been given in order to open more widely the road to heaven to those who wish to do good, so that the earthly kingdom may be at the service of the heavenly one.

Throughout the Middle Ages, his works had a significant influence on pastoral care, mysticism, and scriptural exposition for edification purposes.
