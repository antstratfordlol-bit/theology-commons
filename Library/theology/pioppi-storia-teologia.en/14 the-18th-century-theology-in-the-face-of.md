## The 18th Century: Theology in the Face of the Enlightenment and Royalism {#pioppi-storia-teologia.14}
***Society and Culture***

**Policy**

- Great powers: France, England and Austria
- Powers in recovery: Spain and Portugal
- Powers in decline: Sweden, Holland and the Ottoman Empire
- Rising Powers: Prussia and Russia
- Holy Roman Empire: Germany and Italy, divided into a large number of small, de facto independent political entities

Colonization: Great Anglo-French Global Clash

In the Americas, there is a sedentary colonization; in Africa and Asia, a commercial colonization.

**Social system**

**Monarchical absolutism**, in the form of enlightened despotism, except in England, where there is a parliamentary system.

**Divine right of kings: the** king's power derives directly from God: overriding the people and the Church.

Creation of a royal bureaucracy and limiting the power of the nobles by creating lavish courts.

Society is divided into three classes: two privileged (nobility and clergy), and one (people, third state) on whom many of the burdens fall. The law is different depending on membership in one of the three classes. Social mobility is low: one can ascend socially mostly through the clergy; there is also a reduced phenomenon of entry into the nobility (nobility of toga, *noblesse de robe*). The high clergy in some countries (France, Germany) consists mostly of nobles. The nobility also holds the important positions in the army and diplomacy.

Europe is divided, from a religious point of view, into three worlds that have little dialogue: Catholics, Protestants and Orthodox. The phenomenon of **confessionalization**.

**Economy**

At the beginning of the century, **mercantilism** (Jean-Baptiste Colbert, 1619-1683) prevails: this doctrine identifies the wealth of a country with its availability of money and precious metals. It argues that the economic goal of a state must be a surplus balance of trade: for this reason, mercantilist economic policies advocate the subsidization of national manufacturing, the construction of infrastructure, the creation of state monopolies, the establishment of a strong merchant navy, the elimination of internal duties.

Against mercantilism, the **physiocratic theory** developed (François Quesnay, 1694-1774, doctor of Louis XV; Jacques Turgot, 1727-1781), according to which only agriculture is productive. It favors agronomic studies.

Liberalism: **classical political economy**: search for natural laws of economics that the state must respect: laisser *faire, laisser passer*; theory of supply and demand and equilibrium point.

Adam Smith (1723-1790): *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations*

David Ricardo (1772-1823): *Principles of Political Economy and Taxation*. Annuity Theory.

The **Industrial Revolution**: in the 1700s it developed only in England.

**Condition:**

1. population growth
2. Capital accumulation, due to:
   1. domination of the seas
   2. private property overrides feudal rights
   3. efficient banking system
3. entrepreneurship of the nobility and the bourgeoisie
4. inventions and technical discoveries

The Industrial Revolution developed from the textile industry:

invention and introduction of machines and division of labor industrial concentrations⇒urbanization⇒preparation of canals⇒

The machines used in the textile industry, with the invention of the steam engine run on coal, thus developing the industry of mining, processing and transport of that material.

Great increase in production, but very serious social costs caused by liberalist economic theory.

**Enlightenment culture**

**Kantian definition of enlightenment** (*Answer to the question: what is the enlightenment?* , 1784):

"Enlightenment is man's exit from the state of minority which he must impute to himself. Minority is the inability to make use of one's own intellect without the guidance of another. This minority is imputable to himself, if the cause of it does not depend on a defect of intelligence, but on a lack of decision and courage to make use of his own intellect without being guided by another. "Sapere aude": have the courage to make use of your own intelligence! This is the motto of the Enlightenment."

**Condorcet's definition**: "error and ignorance are the only cause of the evils of mankind, and the errors of superstition are the most fatal, because they corrupt all the sources of reason, and the fanaticism that drives them to commit the crime without remorse [...]. It is by enlightening men and softening them up that one can hope to lead them to freedom by the easy and safe path".

It is therefore a culture that exalts reason and nature, and at the same time makes a merciless critique of tradition. In addition, one notices a sort of secularization of certain Christian values proper to Western culture.

**The exaltation of reason** can be seen for example:

- from the currents of materialist thought, inspired to the English empiricism
- from the naive exaltation of the sciences
- From deism: Edward Herbert lord of Cherbury †1648: five basic ideas:

1. God
2. worship
3. virtues
4. sin
5. pay

- from naive optimism about the natural goodness of man (myth of the good savage)
- by the rationalist currents, which continue in the line of Cartesianism, but with a strong break from its religious thought
- from the extreme anthropocentrism, which takes a step forward compared to the Renaissance, sometimes reaching a real deification of human nature
- from the utopia of progress
- by the continuous search for uniformity in a constant process of reductionist simplification of the complexity of the world.

**The break with tradition** manifests itself, for example:

- hostility towards the Catholic Church (Voltaire's l'*écrasez l'infâme*)
- by the denial of the existence of revealed religions (Hermann S. Reimarus)
- irony towards the cultural, political and religious traditions of Europe (*Lettres Persianes* by Montesquieu)
- from disinterest in history
- from the negation of cosmocentrism and medieval theocentrism
- from an attitude of sufficiency and impatience with the past
- from the inability to accept, and therefore to understand, the complexity of reality

In the field of political doctrines, within this controversy against tradition, Enlightenment thought strongly criticizes the absolute monarchy, advocating the division of powers (***Ésprit des lois* by Montesquieu**), as well as the monarchy of divine right, arguing that the origin of power is the will of the people (***Contrat social* by Rousseau**).

Positive effects of the Enlightenment:

- Abolition of torture and particularly heinous death sentences
- Abolition of serfdom
- End of the repression of witchcraft
- Religious tolerance
- Widespread elementary education

All of these ideas have their expression in the French Revolution, which began in 1789.

**Other aspects**

- **Diffusion of disbelief among the upper classes**
- **Dissemination of panflectistic literature against religious orders**
- **Birth of modern Freemasonry** (1717: opening of the first lodge, in England, and rapid spread: Paris 1732, Florence 1733, Lisbon 1735, The Hague 1735, Rome 1735, Antwerp 1737, Berlin 1740, Vienna 1742). Condemned by Clement XII with the bull *In eminenti* of 1738.
- These phenomena affect mainly the upper classes: **the majority of the population (peasants) continue to live in a Christian** and hierarchical **world.**
- The official religion is protected by the state, which in theory can also use coercive measures for its practice (in truth more and more rarely), by virtue of the **throne-altar alliance**, typical of the *Ancien Régime.*

The century closed with the French Revolution, which opened an era of difficult relations between the Church and modernity.

**Theology in the '700**

**Alfonso Maria de' Liguori** (1696-1787): Neapolitan lawyer, priest at age 30, founder of the Redemptorists (1732), bishop of Sant'Agata dei Goti at age 66 (1762). Author of famous spiritual books, such as *La pratica di mare Gesù Cristo*, and religious songs in Neapolitan, such as *Quanno nascette o Ninno* (whose translation into Italian is the famous Christmas song *Tu scendi dalle stelle*). With him there was the **overcoming of the "morality of systems",** which borrowing from law the method of research of previous judgments, tried to solve the most difficult cases on the basis of judgments of the Church Fathers or great theologians. Depending on the number of judgments found in favor necessary to consider lawful an act, there were various systems: laxity, probabilism, probabiliorism, tutiorism, rigorism: all this causes a decline in moral science.

St. Alphonsus also builds a system: **equiprobabilism**, for which in the situation of doubt whether a law is obligatory or not, it is licit to follow the opinion contrary to the law if it is at least equally probable to the opposite one (but without a "mechanical" application). When one is in the state of doubt because of the presence of two opposing probable opinions, then the law prevails if it is certain; freedom prevails if one doubts that the law exists. When two opinions are in opposition, it is necessary that the opinion in favor of freedom be at least as strong - that is, equiprobable - as the opinion in favor of law.

St. Alphonsus, however, is important above all for having given seriousness and depth to moral theology, leaving the scheme of systems. He strives to put reason before authority, in which the solution to everything is not to be found, neither in Scripture nor in the Fathers; in fact, he wrote: "who among the Fathers has ever written treatises on restitution, on contracts of sale, on censure, on loans, on societies, on ecclesiastical benefits, on simony, on censure, on fasting and the like?" Liguori, while remaining in some ways linked to the schemes and methodology of the time, presents in his moral theology also the fruits of a lively pastoral experience and a deep sapiential knowledge; he shows that experience is very relevant for a genuine moral theology, and he also brings a method to process the data of experience and incorporate the results; he forges a method in which, always in the wake of the Catholic tradition, it is possible to incorporate the new perspectives in continuity with the tradition itself.

His main work is the ***Theologia Moralis*** (1748), in which he follows the **pattern of commandments** and not that of virtues (as St. Thomas Aquinas does).

St. Alphonsus was beatified in 1816, canonized in 1839, and proclaimed a Doctor of the Church in 1871. His moral theology was to be very important even after his death, throughout the 19th century, in order to counter Jansenist tendencies (harsh and rigorous); his proclamation as a Doctor of the Church should also be interpreted in this vein. In 1950 he became the patron saint of confessors and moralists (he is also the patron saint of lawyers).

Birth of **pastoral** (or practical) **theology** with the reform of church studies by Empress Maria Theresa in 1774; importance in this regard of Austrian Benedictine Franz Stefan **Rautenstrauch** (1734-1785).

In the field of apologetics, against deism and the denial of Christian revelation, the treatise of ***demonstratio christiana*** was developed. Antonino Valsecchi O.P. (1708-1791); Martin Gerbert O.S.B. (1720-1793); **Vitus Pichler** S.I. (1670-1736): *Theologia polemica*: Pichler is especially important for the "editing" of the treatise on fundamental theology (then called apologetics): he, in fact, in an attempt to bring together in a single work the issues that were the subject of controversialist theology, came to the conclusion that it was useful to distinguish between general and special or particular controversies and, as a result, he grouped, in the first part of his work, the treatise *de vera religione*, that is, the discussions on the rationality of the Christian faith (*demonstratio christiana*), and those on the divine origin of the Catholic Church (*demonstratio catholica*), together with the treatise on the sources of theology (*de locis theologicis*).

Gian Domenico **Mansi** and his monumental collection of conciliar texts.

The Enlightenment environment also influences theology and leads to the **division of** theological studies **into treatises** and to the abandonment of the *Commentary* in favor of the *Manual*: *De Deo uno*, De Deo *trino*, De *Deo creante et elevante*, *De Verbo incarnato*, *De Deo redemptore*, *De De Deo consummatore*, *De gratia*, *De Ecclesia.* This has the advantages of greater didactic clarity, but the risk, in the long run, of blurring the unity of theology.

**Jansenism**

Chronology of the Jansenist Controversy in the 1700s:

**1695**: Louis-Antoine de **Noailles,** pro-Joziansenist, Archbishop of Paris

**1695-1710**: Repression of Jansenism in the Spanish Netherlands (=Belgium)

**1701**: beginning of the ***cas de conscience*** question: whether a penitent can be absolved who, while condemning the Five Propositions, accuses himself of not attributing them to Jansen and does not believe in the sufficiency of the attrition.

**1703**: anti-Jansenist intervention of Bossuet, with the *Réflexions sur le cas de conscience*

**1705**: **bull *Vineam Domini***: condemnation of respectful silence and purely oral submission; resistance from French bishops (Episcopalist Gallicanism)

**1707**: **Jansenist Schism of Utrecht** (Holland)

**1708**: condemnation of the *Réflexions morales* of the Oratorian **Pasquier Quesnel** (1634-1719) with the *Universi dominici gregis* of Clement XI

**1709**: suppression and **destruction of the Monastery of Port-Royal,** and dispersion of the nuns, by order of Louis XIV

**1713**: the **bull *Unigenitus*** condemns 101 propositions extracted from Quesnel's *Réflexions morales.* Strong resistance from French bishops, despite Louis XIV's insistence: compromise agreement on a **Pastoral Instruction** to apply the bull in France.

**1715**: **death of Louis XIV**; regent (in name Louis XV, child) is indifferent to religious matters ⇒**Jansenist reaction**

**1717**: Jansenists **appeal to a council** against papal condemnations

**1718**: the Holy Office **excommunicates the "appellants"**. Noailles appeals **against the excommunication**. The appellants are disapproved by all other European episcopates, the majority of the French clergy and the court.

**1719**: death of Quesnel

**1720**: negotiations for an **accommodation** fail due to the bad faith of Noailles, who prepares two different texts, one to be sent to Rome, the other to be kept in France: Clement XI, having learned of the deception, rejects the accommodation.

**1721**: death of Clement XI: new Jansenist appeal; Jansenism begins to decline

**1727**: "Miraculous" healings at the **Cemetery of San Medardo**, which restore strength to the Jansenist movement

**1728**: Beginning of the publication of the clandestine Jansenist journal *Nouvelles écclesiastiques.*

**1729**: death of Noailles

**1731**: beginning of the phenomenon of "**convulsions"** (and consequent "rescues") in the Cemetery of San Medardo

**1732**: closure of the cemetery

**1733**: ordinance prohibiting public convulsions

**1749**: **Confession Ticket Crisis**: French bishops, now majority anti-Jansenist, require parish priests to request confession tickets before administering Extreme Unction, against the Jansenist practice of granting absolution even without jurisdiction. All this stems from the acceptance of the *Unigenitus* that could be requested in confession. Clash between the bishops and the Parliament of Paris who opposed the practice of tickets (parliamentary Gallicanism).

**1750**: the defeat of the Dutch Jansenists becomes clear: out of 200,000 faithful in Utrecht, only 10,000 are adherents to the Jansenist Schism in this year.

**1756**: the Holy See puts an end to the Question of Confession Tickets with the bull *Ex omnibus*: the tickets are replaced with the duty, on the part of the priest who administers Extreme Unction, to ascertain the true feelings of the dying person.

**1757**: the phenomenon of convulsions begins to diminish

**1763**: from this year it is clear that Jansenism has lost the battle and begins to die out; partly because of its moral rigor and condemnation of interest-bearing loans, which causes it to lose followers among the bourgeoisie. There remains only one last shot in the arm with the alliance between Jansenism and Richerist (or Presbyterian) Gallicanism, with the publication of the ***Code curial*** by Gabriel-Nicolas Maultrot (1714-1803), in which the claims of the lower clergy are vigorously supported.

Outside of France Jansenism has some development in Ireland, Scotland, and England (here there is some sympathy of Anglicans toward Jansenists); Holland and Belgium; little development in Germany and Austria; almost none in Spain and Portugal.

In Italy there is the episode of the **Synod of Pistoia in 1786**.

The background is the jurisdictionalism of **Peter Leopold I of** Habsburg-Lorraine, Grand Duke of Tuscany from 1765 to 1790 (brother of Joseph II of Austria; upon his death in 1790, Peter Leopold will become Emperor of the Holy Roman Empire, under the name of Leopold II).

The bishop of Pistoia **Scipione de' Ricci** (1741-1810) is a Jansenist and a jurisdictionalist. He advocates the autonomy of the bishops with respect to the Holy See, considering the pope only as a *primus inter pares*; he is against papal infallibility; he opposes devotion to the Sacred Heart, luxury in the liturgy, the multiplicity of altars, the canon recited in a low voice, simultaneous celebrations in the different altars of the same church, solemn Eucharistic expositions, indulgences, salaries for Masses, religious orders; he considers the civil marriage contract and the sacrament of marriage to be different things. All these ideas can be found in the texts of the Diocesan Synod of 1786, which was immediately disavowed by the other Tuscan bishops at a meeting in Florence (1787); in 1790 de' Ricci lost the support of Pietro Leopoldo, who left Tuscany to ascend as Leopold II to the throne of the Holy Roman Empire. The Synod of Pistoia is condemned by Pius VI in 1794 with the bull ***Auctorem fidei.***

**Regalism**

Throughout the eighteenth century there was an intensification of royalist tendencies, throughout the Catholic monarchies:

In Portugal, with Minister Sebastião José de Carvalho e Melo, Count of Oeiras and Marquis of Pombal, who initiates the anti-gesuitic policy that will culminate in the suppression of the order. 1750-1756: War of the *Reducciones* in Paraguay. 1759-1761: Repression of the Jesuits in Portugal and colonial dominions.

In Spain is also strong the Bourbon royalism with Charles III. 1767: expulsion of the Jesuits.

In France, Gallicanism. It is no longer presented only as a peculiarity of the Gallican Church, but as a persistence in it of the ancient customs of the Church, before the expansion of the Roman power. The Jesuits, damaged and weakened by the Lavallette affair (1761), are suppressed in the kingdom in 1764. Occupation of the papal territories of Avignon and of the Venasian county (1768-69) in order to pressure the pope for a general suppression of the order.

In Naples, Bourbon jurisdictionalism by the minister Bernardo Tanucci. Suppression of the Jesuits in 1767, and consequent occupation of the pontifical territories of Pontecorvo and Benevento (1768-69) in order to pressure the pope for a general suppression of the order.

In Parma, Bourbon jurisdictionalism by Minister Léon-Guillaume du Tillot. Suppression of the Jesuits in 1768.

1773: Clement XIV suppresses the Society of Jesus, without condemning it, with the bull *Dominus ac Redemptor*. The Jesuits survive somewhat in Lithuania, Poland and Silesia.

In the Habsburg dominions, Josephism: more moderately with Maria Theresa (1740-1780) and her minister Wenzel Anton von Kaunitz-Rietberg, who carried out a reform of ecclesiastical studies and religious orders, imposed government *placet* on papal decrees, and organized state control over the publication of books. With Joseph II, the trend increases, with decrees aimed at making the bishops independent of Rome, suppression of monasteries and confraternities, government centralization of ecclesiastical discipline, reform of diocesan boundaries, reduction and rationalization of the number of seminaries: Vienna, Pavia, Pest, Leuven and Freiburg of Breisgau remain. These measures provoke an insurrection in the Austrian Netherlands (=Belgium).

**Febronianism**

Nikolaus **von Hontein** (1701-1790), German bishop, wrote under the pseudonym of Justinus Febronius the work *De statu Ecclesiae et legitima potestate Romani Pontificis*: it is a mixture of conciliarism, episcopalism and regalism, expression of the great bishop-princes of the Holy Roman Empire (Liege, Trier, Cologne, Mainz, Speyer, Salzburg, Trent...).

1763: publication of *De statu Ecclesiae*

1764: *De statu Ecclesiae is* placed on the Index.

1778: Retraction of von Hontein

1786: Ems puntation: the attempt to severely limit the powers of the pope in Germany to the advantage of the major archbishops is blocked by the resistance of the minor bishops, who are not at all happy to see the prerogatives of their immediate neighbors increased.

In 1803, Napoleonic secularization of the German ecclesiastical principalities put an end to the Febronian trend.

The Italian priest Pietro **Ballerini** (1698-1769) responded to von Hontein's book with two treatises: *De vi ac ratione primatus Romanorum pontificum* (1766) and *De Potestate ecclesiastica Summorum pontificum et conciliorum generalium* (1768).

**The End of the Chinese Rites Question**

**1699: a cardinal's commission on Chinese Rites is created.**

In the meantime, the issue has become a topic of discussion "fashionable" ⇐sarcasms of Voltaire and Bayle. In France the dispute turns into a struggle between the Society of Jesus and the Foreign Missions, a struggle that intersects with the contemporary dispute between Jesuits and Jansenists.

1700: the Sorbonne condemns the Chinese Rites. The Belgian Jesuits of Peking (astronomers and mathematicians of the court) ask the emperor K'ang-hi for a declaration affirming that the Chinese Rites are purely civil and political ceremonies: the emperor makes the requested declaration official, ordering its publication in the empire. The Roman commission, in truth, takes little account of either the Parisian condemnation or K'ang-hi's declaration.

1701: Monsignor Carlo Tommaso Maillard de **Tournon from** Savoy is appointed papal legate in China; in 1703 he arrives in Pondichéry (India).

**1704: Inquisition decree prohibiting the Chinese Rites.** In India Tournon finds the same problem: the Indian Rites, with a struggle between Jesuits and Capuchins. In 1704 Tournon prohibits the Indian Rites, but with sanctions applicable from 1707. But the Archbishop of Goa already suspends in 1704 the effect of Tournon's censures, accusing him of not having respected the rights of the Portuguese *Padroado.* 1708: the Sovereign Council of Pondichéry refuses to recognize the powers of the Legate.

Meanwhile, in 1705, Tournon left India and, passing through the Philippines, Macao, and Canton, arrived in Peking. On December 31 he is received by K'ang-hi.

**1706**: In June, the emperor learns of the Roman convictions. In August he orders **Tournon and** Charles **Maigrot to be** expelled from Peking, and they **are confined to Nanking.** Maigrot is then banished from China.

1707: Tournon, in Nanking, begins to enforce the decree of 1704. Then K'ang-hi has him interned in Macau, under Portuguese supervision, and sends an embassy of two Jesuits to the pope. The two die on the voyage, in a shipwreck off Portugal, and Tournon dies interned in 1710.

In 1708 Maigrot arrives in Rome. The Jesuits simultaneously send requests to Rome for a revision of Tournon's decision.

1709: Clement XI has the 1704 responses of the Holy Office published; but he also writes a polite letter to K'ang-hi.

1710: new decree confirming the sentence.

1715: Constitution *Ex die illa*: further condemnation. But discussions continue, due to the impossibility of implementing the decrees.

1720: Monsignor Carlo Ambrogio **Mezzabarba** is appointed papal legate. He goes to Lisbon and makes agreements with the Portuguese. Then he goes to Macao and Canton. In 1721 he is received in audience by K'ang-hi. Back in Macao, he publishes a decree against the Chinese Rites, but at the same time he grants eight temporary permits.

1722: Mezzabarba returns in Europe, bringing back the body of Tournon. Emperor K'ang-hi dies.

The **Eight Permits, though** somewhat confusingly, allow the Chinese Rites to survive.

1737: the bishop of Peking wants to force the missionaries to make use of the permits; following an appeal to Rome against the bishop, **Benedict XIV definitively condemns the Chinese Rites** in 1742 ("non mala quia prohibita, sed prohibita quia mala").

In 1744 the Malabar or Indian Rites (*Omnium sollicitudinum*) were also prohibited, therefore also the Cocincina Rites, despite the opposition of Monsignor Pierre-Joseph-George Pigneau de Béhaine (Apostolic Vicar of Cocincina) to the condemnation.

All of these rites would be accepted again in 1939, by which time it was clear that no section of the population considered them religious rites anymore. The popular error of considering the rites as religious had to disappear before the Church could allow the rites to the new Christians. Contrary to the opinion of the Jesuits in Beijing, a declaration by the civil power was not enough to change this popular mentality: the secularization of the rites could only be the effect of a long evolution or, as the papal instruction of December 8, 1939, says, the fruit of "the changes that have taken place over the centuries in customs and souls".

The dilemma of the Chinese Rites was a **choice between practical pastoral effectiveness and the risk of syncretism and doctrinal confusion in the new Christian faithful**. However, the controversy was also complicated by the antagonism between Jesuits and other missionary orders, by the Jansenist question, by the ever present struggle between Rome and Lisbon, and finally by the anti-Jesuit controversy that was beginning to take its first steps. In any case, the difficulties in which Chinese Catholicism found itself in the second half of the 18th century cannot be attributed only to the prohibition of rites: these difficulties were also due to a change in imperial policy, which after K'ang-hi became more hostile to Christianity and to the crisis of the end of the century of Propaganda Fide, which occurred because of the French Revolution and the French invasion of the Papal States.
