## The 17th Century: The Great French Century {#pioppi-storia-teologia.13}
**1. General Situation**

**a) Protestantism and Confessionalization**

The Protestant Reformation is responsible for fracturing the unity of the European world. The principle of *cuius regio eius et religio* prevails: subjects are obliged to follow the religious confession of the monarch. At the end of the '500, there is a crystallization of the division from the geographical point of view. Europe remained divided into three worlds that had very little dialogue: Catholics, Protestants and Orthodox. The phenomenon of confessionalization. We no longer think in terms of Christianity, but in terms of denominations: Evangelical (Lutherans), Reformed (Calvinists), Anglican, Catholic and Orthodox. Religious intolerance grows throughout Europe, both in Catholic and Protestant or Orthodox countries: dissidents are often persecuted. This situation will change at the end of the 18th century and will be officially eliminated only in the 19th century (e.g. in England tolerance towards Catholics was sanctioned by law only in 1829; while the last death sentence of a priest for celebrating Mass on English soil dates back to 1767, even if the sentence was later changed to four years in prison).

Reorganization of the Roman Inquisition with the Council of Trent, which however will function only in Italy, and without the immense power that is often attributed to it; for example, in the valleys of Bergamo, in the middle of the '500 there is a great inquisitorial activity that however brings few convictions: often they are penances in terms of prayers and abjurations to be signed; in many cases, then, there are three powers competing for the inquisite: the inquisition, the bishop, and the state, with the result that the trials remain stalled; there are also many cases of escapes from the prisons of the Inquisition, which must not have been very organized.

Contributing to the increase in repression is the development of absolute monarchy: kings begin to really have a lot of power, with the creation of the modern state and centralized bureaucracy; monarchs want to use religion (Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, and Anglican) to reinforce their power. Protestant and Orthodox kings officially become the leaders of their national church, and Catholics, though to a lesser extent, also develop a strong caesaropapism.

Even in places where Catholics and Protestants coexist, as in the city of Augsburg, Germany, the two communities acquire distinct ways of living and behaving: from the clothing, the baptismal names, the pictures hanging in the house, one can immediately tell which community one belongs to. Members of one community try to avoid others as much as possible. For example, the merchants of the Bergamo valleys who had to go to Switzerland, in Protestant territories, had to get a permit signed by the parish priest, with the date of their return; if their stay in Protestant lands had to last longer than expected, they had to find a priest or a good Catholic layman in the place who would provide them with a document explaining and certifying the reasons for the delay in returning.

In the continent the last great religious conflict is the Thirty Years' War: it was born as a religious clash, but over time turns into a purely political war, ending in a major conflict between France and Spain. The war is divided into four periods: Bohemian-Palatine, Danish, Swedish and French. In 1648 the Paci of Westphalia put an end to the war.

1690: defeat of the Catholics at the Battle of the Boyne: the Wars of Religion in England end with the final rout of the Catholic Stuardians.

In all these wars the religious factor is not an element that aggravates the violence: in fact the armies are professional, and Catholics and Protestants fight together in mercenary armies, without great confessional concerns.

Although religious intolerance continued to dominate, a new cultural atmosphere began to develop in which Christianity was no longer understood as a unifying factor in European society: it began to be sought in reason alone; rationalism and empiricism; deism. Here, then, are found the roots of secularism.

Finally, it should be mentioned that in this century there was the last Ottoman assault on Europe: in 1683 there was the siege of Vienna, which ended with the Battle of Kahlemberg, in which the Poles rushed to the aid of the imperial army annihilated the Turkish army; from this moment the Ottomans lost the initiative and began to retreat, under the blows of the Holy League (Holy See, Venice, Holy Roman Empire, Poland, Tuscany, Savoy, Genoa, Spain, Portugal).

With regard to culture, we recall that the seventeenth century is in art the century of the Baroque; philosophy begins the process of separation from theology, which will be consummated in the following century: we recall the important figures of René Descartes (Descartes), Baruch Spinoza and Gottfried Willhelm von Leibniz.

**b) Spain**

Spain in the seventeenth century, while remaining a great power, however, enters a period of decline. It is due to:

- to the failure to create an entrepreneurial class and a productive economy, caused by two reasons:
- U.S. gold and silver provide continuous resource inputs that do not support domestic production
- the noble class continues to consider it unworthy to commit their assets to economic enterprises
- to the exhaustion of its political, military and economic power in an interminable series of wars:
- Dutch War: 1568-1648 (with a truce 1612-1618)
- 30 Years War: 1618-1648
- War against France: 1635-1659
- Separation of Portugal: 1640
- to competition and to the "race war" (a kind of piracy regulated by the states, which issued "race licenses" precisely for the "privateers") by the Dutch, English and French.

The Habsburg dynasty continues to reign, with Philip III, Philip IV and Charles II (the latter, probably affected by a certain mental retardation, symbolizes in some way the decadence of the nation). Charles II will die without children and with him will end the dynasty of the Habsburgs of Spain.

Spain remains Catholic because of the vigilance of the Inquisition. Statistics on the work of this state organ are difficult to calculate, due to the serious destruction of its archives in the 19th century during the liberal revolutions. It is estimated that in its entire existence (about 350 years), the Spanish Inquisition carried out between 100,000 and 300,000 trials, 2% of which ended in death sentences, not all of which were carried out.

**c) Italy**

Italy in the 1600's entered into full decadence; already in the 1500's it had lost its political importance: now it also lost its economic power and cultural supremacy.

Some events of some significance:

- 1605: exclusivity is used for the first time: the kings of France and Spain and the emperor have the possibility to exercise a right of veto in the conclave, through the cardinals of their domain. This custom was accepted by the Holy See because it guaranteed greater governability of the Church (it should be remembered that the pope outside Italy governed the ecclesial structure through the monarchs).
- 1606-1607: great clash between the Holy See and the Republic of Venice for reasons of ecclesiastical jurisdiction. Paul V throws the interdict on the territory of the Serenissima, which however fails: the people and the clergy of the Republic do not comply with the prohibitions of the Roman censorship and worship is not interrupted. In the end, a compromise agreement is reached.
- 1616: first trial of Galileo Galilei, which ends in nothing. It is important to realize that the "Galileo case" is not the struggle of a free thinker against the Church, but an internal struggle of the Catholic world, with great personalities in favor and against the great scientist, who by the way was very religious.
- 1621: Saint Robert Bellarmine, Jesuit cardinal and theologian, dies.
- 1633: Galileo's second trial, in which he is condemned (the punishment consists of house arrest in his villa in Arcetri, and the recitation for three years, every week, of 7 penitential psalms). It should be remembered that Galileo was not mistreated at all in his trial, but rather received with great respect.
- 1635: Roman Inquisition decides to further restrict the use of torture in trials
- 1655: Pasque Piemontesi: an attempt by the Savoys to extirpate the Waldensian heresy from their duchy with military operations; the diplomacy of England, Holland, Sweden and Denmark succeeded in stopping the operation. In the same year the Waldensians were granted the Patents of Grace, which established a limited tolerance.
- 1669: after a long siege by the Turks, the Venetians lose the island of Crete, in the southern Aegean Sea
- 1676-1689: Pontificate of Blessed Innocent XI

**d) France**

The seventeenth century is the century of France. France supplants Spain as a great military and political power, and replaces Italy as the center of European culture. It also develops a major colonial policy: Canada, Louisiana, the Caribbean, Senegal and India.

France is ruled by the dynasty of the Bourbons: Henri IV (†1610), Louis XIII (1610-1643), Louis XIV (the Sun King, 1643-1715). The political scene is also dominated by two prime ministers: Card. Armand-Jean du Plessis de Richelieu and the Italian Card. Julius Raymond Mazarin.

Development of the absolute monarchy (*absolutus* = released from all bonds), through the development of a modern bureaucracy that draws its ranks from the bourgeoisie, and through the creation of the sumptuous court of Versailles, where Louis XIV manages to attract the nobles, removing them from their domains, and making them harmless to his power, as they are under his direct control: in the life of the court everything depends on the favor of the king.

The persecution against the Huguenots: Louis XIV, in order to consolidate his absolute power, decided to reduce the strength of the Huguenot minority, which he did by the Edict of Nantes (1598). Starting from 1681 we have the *dragonnades*: Protestants were obliged to lodge in their houses soldiers of the dragon corps, famous for their violence and brutality. The soldiers behaved in the most vexatious ways possible, stealing, looting, destroying, committing a lot of violence. Those who converted to Catholicism were immediately exempted from the lodging of the dragoons. Many Huguenots became Catholics, and then the king, taking as a pretext their numerical decrease, revoked in 1685 the Edict of Nantes, and then the tolerance for Protestants. Several Huguenot revolts (Camisard revolts) broke out.

**e) Missionary activity**

The colonial powers of the period are:

- As early as the sixteenth century:
  - Spain: America, Philippines
  - Portugal: Brazil, Africa, Asia

From 1580 to 1640 union of Spain and Portugal under the Spanish Crown.

- From the 17th century:
- France
- England
- Holland
- Sweden
- Denmark.

In America there was a rapid establishment of the ordinary hierarchy: in 1610 we find in Spanish America 5 archbishoprics and 27 dioceses, as well as 400 convents. By the end of the sixteenth century, missions were already taking place only in the peripheral areas of the empire. There are three pastoral missionary institutions:

1. *doctrinas*: parishes of Indians, dependent on the bishop, but served by religious missionaries

2. *misiones: missionary* stations in the most peripheral areas: classic missionary establishments in areas not yet evangelized. Generally they do not give good results because they are located in areas inhabited by less civilized peoples, with an economy based on hunting and often nomadic. They were located in the north especially in the territories of Sinaloa, Sonora (Eusebio F. Kino), California (Junípero Serra), Arizona (missions among the Apaches), New Mexico, Texas and Florida. In the south in Guiana, along the Orinoco, in Upper Peru and in Patagonia.

3. *reducciones*: these are the most original experiment, created by the Jesuits; the most important were those in Paraguay, which functioned from 1610 to 1768, especially among the Guarani Indians. The reductions were large villages, some of which had 4-5.000 inhabitants, all built on an identical design; in the center, a large square, having in the middle a calvary or an image of the Virgin; on three sides of the square were aligned the houses of the Indians (one per family: a ground floor with a veranda) shaded by trees; in the middle of the fourth side stood the church, which they wanted to be always large and rich; in front of the church stood, on one side, the house of the Jesuit fathers with the school, in the other side were arranged warehouses and workshops; each family had a small garden. In each reduction there was an infirmary-hospital, a small hospice, an inn, equipped with a chapel, for people who were passing through. All around the village stretched cultivated land or pastures, whose exploitation was collective. The originality of the reductions lies above all in their juridical position before the Spanish crown and in the perfection of the theocratic paternalism under which they lived. The first reductions were established in territories that had not yet been occupied by either Spain or Portugal. The Indians who lived there, having freely accepted the sovereignty of the king of Spain, received from the crown a special status. They were immediately subject to the king, without their territory ever being ceded as a fief or *encomienda*. Only one office, that of the *corregidor real* (a kind of lieutenant of the Spanish governor of the province), is appointed by the governor; since the '700 the *corregidores* are Indians and no longer Spanish; all other magistrates are chosen by the Indians. The Christian republics of Paraguay enjoyed, therefore, a certain autonomy from the Spanish crown. In reality they were creatures of the Jesuits, who, having the government both spiritual and temporal, had made it a small closed world, where no European had the right to enter, except for the governor and the bishop and only for rare and fleeting visits. The whole life of the reductions was regulated down to the smallest details: religious instruction, marriage, work, music and entertainment. The suffocating meticulousness of these regulations, which seems to have caused the system to fail when it was applied to turbulent tribes, does not seem to have been too burdensome for the Guarani, people of such docile character as to accept a paternalistic organization of their lives. In each reduction there are ordinarily two Jesuit priests; one, the parish priest, is always in the midst of the population to assist the neophytes in all their needs, while the other travels the countryside, either to visit the sick, or to instruct and console those who, being obliged to care for the flocks or crops, could not come to church. On Sundays and Thursdays Christian doctrine is explained to all the people gathered in the church, and every day catechism is given to the children. The Jesuits established in each reduction a school for the children of the cacichi and notables and for the children destined to sing in the church. Beginning in 1640, to defend themselves against attacks by the Portuguese from Brazil, the Jesuits established a local militia. The official language is the indigenous one; access to the *reducciones* is forbidden to whites, with the only exceptions of the missionaries, the bishop and the governor. The period of maximum flowering is between 1650 and 1720.

In **Brazil,** too, the Jesuits tried an experiment similar to that of the reductions, but with much poorer results. In fact here, in order to remove the Indians from the harassment of the whites and to guarantee them a stable home, the Jesuits created villages, called *aldeias*, in which the Indians received technical education and religious formation. The *aldeias* differed from the *reducciones* of Paraguay in that they were Indian suburbs of the Portuguese centers, while the reducciones were independent nuclei far from the cities. The establishment of the aldeias triggered the reaction of the Portuguese colonists who, seeing themselves deprived of cheap labor, organized specialized teams to raid the Jesuit villages for Indians.

In **Japan** this work of inculturation was carried out by the Italian Jesuit Alessandro da Valignano, in **China** by Matteo Ricci, also an Italian Jesuit. In the seventeenth century great persecution with thousands of martyrs and the closure of Japan to foreigners (the only contact with foreign countries was the port of Deshima, where only the Dutch could dock); only a few groups of hidden Christians remained, without clergy, who were found by missionaries in the nineteenth century.

**In Indochina**, the Frenchman Fr. Alexandre de Rhodes, SI: 1591-1660, works and obtains about 6,700 conversions; he studies the Vietnamese language and gives it a grammar, using Latin characters adapted to local phonemes.

Presence of a thriving Christian community: pastoral care and missionary work are carried out by the Foreign Missions in Paris under the control of *Propaganda fide*.

In **India the** situation of the Church is very complex: there is a division according to the rites: Latin and Malabar; moreover, in the Latin rite there is a heated rivalry between the areas dependent on the *Padroado* and those dependent on *Propaganda*: important attempts at inculturation are made by the Italian Jesuit Roberto de Nobili.

In **Tibet**, in 1624, Fr. Andrade, a Portuguese, started a mission, but it did not prosper. In the eighteenth century, *Propaganda,* considering the mission in Tibet abandoned by the *Padroado*, took over its direction.

Beginning in 1623, missions to **Persia** were undertaken among the Mandeans.

In **Canada,** French colonization also brought with it the mission; generally speaking, the French-Canadian Catholic communities have a high level of Christian life and morality. The missions to the Indian tribes are carried out by Jesuits and Sulpicians (sometimes with contrasts between them); above all there are numerous conversions among the Uron tribe. There is also an Indian saint of this period: Saint Katarina Tekakwitha (1656-1680), very famous also in Europe: from 1717 onwards there are many French publications about this Iroquois girl. In 1647 there is the martyrdom of the Jesuit Isaac Jogues, in 1648 that of Jean de Brébeuf.

**In the current territory of the United States:**

In the early 1600s, the Spanish founded Santa Fe, **New Mexico,** which became a center of missionary activity in New Mexico, Arizona and Texas. In 1680, during a major Indian revolt, the city is destroyed. But in 1691-1693 the Spanish reconquest and rebuilding of the city takes place.

Foundation of the colony of **Maryland by** Catholic Cecil Calvert, Lord Baltimore. In 1634 the first Mass is celebrated there. In the colony, from the beginning, there is tolerance towards non-Catholics. There are also Jesuit missions among the Indians; Calvert granted the Catholic Indians 10,000 acres of land to organize indigenous communities directed by the Jesuits. In the 1650s the Puritans seized power by proscribing Catholics and Anglicans. In 1658 Lord Baltimore returned to power and the situation of Catholics became quiet again. In 1692, during the reign of William of Orange and Mary Stuart, the criminal laws against Catholics in force in England are introduced in Maryland. Persecution against Catholics; a certain number of them find refuge in Pennsylvania, where Quakers, the majority of the population, are on the whole tolerant.

**THEOLOGY OF THE SEC. XVII**

First of all, it must be said that in this century the separation between theology and philosophy begins to be consummated; we find ourselves in the century of philosophical rationalism, with Renato Descartes, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and Baruch Spinoza. Descartes and Leibniz are still theologians, as well as philosophers, but the separation between the two disciplines is now an almost accomplished fact.

**2. Jesuit theologians at the beginning of the century**

**(a) Francisco Suárez (1548-1617)** *Doctor eximius*

Spanish Jesuit. He teaches in Valladolid, Rome, Alcalá, Salamanca and Coimbra.

He is the first great systematizer of metaphysics, with his *Disputationes metaphysicae*. He is the founder of the treatise on metaphysics as a specialized science of philosophy.

It is also important for political philosophy and philosophy of law.

He takes up Scholastic theology and in part Thomism, but in many respects distances himself from St. Thomas:

- As for the demonstration of the existence of God, he follows a metaphysical way ("omne quod fit ab alio fit"), following Duns Scotus, and not a cosmological one ("quidquid movetur ab alio movetur"), unlike Aquinas
- Reject the *entis analogy*
- He abandons the concept of being as absolute perfection; being is for him something abstract
- Rejects the real distinction between essence and *actus essendi*, between essence and existence
- It supports an ilemorphism with three elements:
- matter
- form
- link between matter and form
- He has original theories about the divine attributes (which he distinguishes into three types), about the relationships between the divine persons (persons are for him relative subsistences and relative non-subsistences), about the human nature of Christ (his human nature is not a human person), about the real presence in the Eucharist (extensive local presence)
- Entifies both substance and accident, so the latter is not identified by the substance, but by itself
- On politics, dealt with especially in *De legibus ac Deo legislatore*, Suárez states that the existence of the state is of divine right, because it is necessary for man to attain perfection; the people choose the form of government and designate the holders of it. However, once a sovereign is chosen, he is the supreme vicar of God in the temporal order, even if the people retain a certain weight in the legislative power, and must supervise the exercise that the king makes of it, which must be directed to "political happiness", that is, the common good. If the monarch becomes a tyrant, the people can depose him. The Church, moving in the spiritual sphere, must be independent of state power (anti-regalism), but in matters of justice, and therefore moral, political power will sometimes be in occasional dependence on the Church. In *Defensio fidei*, on the oath imposed by James I of England (unacceptable to Catholics), he is even more extreme: it exalts the Church as a perfect society on a par with the state, but superior to it because of its spiritual purpose and universality; it deals with the right of resistance to the tyrant and goes so far as to justify tyrannicide (under certain conditions and with limitations that Suárez determines precisely); the Church has a *potestas indirecta* of intervention on the state when the civil power endangers the salvation of souls, which can go as far as the deposition of the sovereign.
- He also deals a lot with international law: among other things, he proposes compulsory arbitration to eliminate wars.
- In Spiritual Theology: Supports the existence of two situations of the state of perfection:
- monks and mendicants: *status perfectionis acquirendae*
- bishops (in which presbyters participate in some way: *status perfectionis acquisitus*)

Suárez exerts a great influence in Catholic philosophy and theology: his scholasticism in many ways will help to tarnish the genuinely Thomist one.

**b) Robert Bellarmine (1542-1621)**

Italian Jesuit. Cardinal. Beatified in 1923; canonized in 1930. Taught at Louvain, then in Rome at the Roman College.

His most important work is the *Disputationes de controversiis Christianae fidei adversus huius temporis haereses*, composed between 1586 and 1593. It is, as can be deduced from the title, a work of controversy, which was written as a response to the Centuriators of Magdeburg, a group of Protestant authors, led by Flacius Illyricus, who had written a history of the Church strongly polemic against the Catholic Church and tradition. Other works of historical-polemic scope are: *Chronologia brevis*, *Compendium de haeresi*, *De scriptoribus ecclesiasticis*.

Bellarmine, in his work, reiterates the role and importance of Tradition, against Luther's *sola Scriptura.*

Within the ecclesiology strongly emphasizes the visibility of the Church, and offers a definition that will have great fortune until the nineteenth century: "Ecclesia [est] coetum hominum, eiusdem Christianae **fidei** professione et eorundem **sacramentorum** communione colligatum sub regimine legitimorum **pastorum** ac praecipue unius Christi in terris vicarii Romani Pontificis".

Definition of Church as a collection of the faithful in unity of:

- faith (= vinculum symbolicum →munus docendi)
- sacraments (= vinculum liturgicum →munus sanctificandi)
- pastors (= vinculum hierarchicum →munus regendi).

Moreover, Robert Bellarmine affirms that the Catholic Church has the best form of government, namely a monarchy (the pope) moderated by an aristocracy (the bishops, who are not vicars of the pope, but have a power that comes from God) and democracy (in the Church anyone can access the episcopate, without limitations of social class or race).

In response to the Scottish Catholic William Barclay (1543-1608), who had published a *De Regno et regali potestate* in which he defended the divine right of kings, the Jesuit cardinal published a *Tractatus de potestate Summi Pontificis*, which supported instead the rights of the pope even on temporal matters, but only in extreme cases: the pope could, with all the cautions and warnings beforehand, depose a sovereign and dissolve his subjects from the oath of allegiance. Bellarmine's *Tractatus* had the misfortune to be published in the same year in which Henry IV was assassinated by a fanatical and deranged Catholic; therefore the book was also condemned and burned by the parliament in Paris (but the Sorbonne did not pronounce itself, as he was a cardinal) and Barclay's work was put on the Index in Rome. The nuncio in Paris, Roberto Ubaldini, protested strongly, threatening to break off diplomatic relations, and the parliamentary decree was withdrawn by the intervention of the Queen Regent.

Bellarmine is also important for having drawn up a list of ecumenical councils, which is the one actually adopted by the Catholic Church. This list consists of 18 councils: Nicene I, Constantinopolitan I, Ephesian, Chalcedonian, Constantinopolitan II and III, Nicene II, Constantinopolitan IV, Lateran I-IV, Lyons I and II, Viennese, Florentine, Lateran V and Tridentine. It therefore excludes the Council of Constance.

He intervenes in the Controversia *de auxiliis* advising Clement VIII against resolving it by taking a clear position for one of the conflicting views.

With that monumental work Petavio stands in practice as the founder of the modern treatise on dogmatic theology separate from the rest of theology.

His is a positive theology, based on the sources (Sacred Scripture and Catholic Tradition), with a historical approach to the problems. The influence of Melchor Cano can be seen in this approach. Therefore, his way of doing theology is very different from the scholastic one; at the same time, his is not only a history of theology, but a theology that remains close to the sources and to the historical development of the doctrines. His great merit is therefore that of having opened the way to a theology more sober in speculation and more nourished by history.

In this book, which is presented with calm and serene tones, also justifies the tyrannicide, when precisely the king becomes a tyrant. In 1610 de Mariana's book was condemned in Paris together with the *Tractatus de potestate Summi Pontificis*, shortly after the assassination of King Henry IV.

**3. Jansenism**

This movement draws its origins from the pious circles, heirs of the reformist aspirations of the pre-Tridentine period, which are not satisfied with the work of the Counter-Reformation, and therefore remain restless about the search, throughout the Church, for a higher evangelical perfection. Moreover, the Jansenists are critical of the social changes that in the 17th century bring to the fore the nobles and the bourgeoisie who enrich themselves through commercial activities, and who are the bearers of a new (more secular) way of dealing with reality. Finally, they think that scholastic theology has corrupted morality, favoring laxity, and that in this field it is necessary to go back to the fathers, in particular to Augustine.

Moreover, the controversy *de auxiliis*, and above all the survival, in Flemish and Lovanian circles, of Baio's ideas had an influence on its birth.

Main **dogmatic problems** were the claims that **grace is irresistible**, and that **Christ died only for those he wanted to save, and not for everyone**.

From the **pastoral** point of view, the problems are the **deferral of absolution of** confession, which is granted only when there is evidence of the penitent's change of life; and **the Eucharist conceived as the reward of** "perfect" souls.

In general, then, the Jansenists have a very negative view of the world: "the hatred of the world thus becomes the other face and measure of God's love; therefore the ideal condition for the Christian is that of the monk and only factual necessities, willed by God, can justify the presence of the elect in other conditions. The abandonment of the world means, in this case, also mistrust in the possibilities of improving it, both in social and political institutions and in the human aspects of the same religious institutions that are present in it, such as the Church" (Sofia Vanni Rovighi).

Thus, one can appreciate how Jansenism is an attempt to return to medieval practices and issues.

Within the Jansenist polemics, there was also one of an ecclesiological type: Martin de Barcos (nephew of the French Jansenist leader Jean Duvergier de Hauranne) took up, in more moderate terms, a theory of Marcantonio De Dominis (1566-1624): he had been a Jesuit, then Archbishop of Split, but had then switched to Anglicanism; he had claimed a primacy of St. Paul over all other apostles, including St. Peter. Barcos, in the preface to his uncle's book *De la fréquente communion* (1643), had made mention of the two heads of the Church (Peter and Paul) "qui ne font qu'un". This ambiguous assertion was criticized by Charles-François d'Abra de Raconis (†1646), and a controversy arose, which led in 1647 to the condemnation by the Holy Office of the theory of "two heads that form one".

Chronology of the Jansenist Controversy:

**1638**: death of Cornelius **Jansenius** (Cornelius Janssen) bishop of Ypres, and until 1636 professor of sacred scripture at Louvain.

**1640**: **publication of** Jansenius' ***Augustinus*** in Louvain: in this work, the Belgian bishop offers an exposition of Augustine's doctrine on grace, interpreted as a dogmatic foundation for a system of dogmatic and ascetic rigorism. The deleterious consequences of original sin are strongly emphasized, as well as the necessity of divine grace, presented almost as irresistible.

**1641**: publication of *Augustinus* in Paris.

**1642**: Bull *In eminenti* of Urban VIII, which condemns *Augustinus*.

**1643**: Death of Jean Duvergier de Hauranne (d. 1583), abbot of Saint-Cyran, friend of Jansenius and leader of the Parisian Jansenist party. Publication of the Jansenist work ***De la fréquente communion*, by Antoine Arnauld** (1612-1694). The spiritual center of Jansenism is the Cistercian women's monastery of Port-Royal, where Antoine Arnauld's sister, Mother Angélique (born Jacqueline-Marie) Arnauld (1591-1661), is superior. From 1633 to 1643 the abbot of Saint-Cyran had been spiritual director of the monastery of Port-Royal.

**1653**: **Bull *Cum occasione***, of Innocent X, which **condemns five propositions** contained in *Augustinus*, including: "grace is irresistible"; "Christ did not die for all" (the latter is considered by the bull false, reckless, scandalous, impious, blasphemous, slanderous, insulting to the goodness of God and heretical). Jansenists raise the ***quaestio facti*** (whether the five propositions are really contained in *Augustinus*) and the *quaestio iuris* (the five propositions are to be condemned).

**1654**: 40 French bishops against the *quaestio facti*: the sense of the five propositions is precisely that of Jansenius.

**1655**: absolution denied to the duke of Liancourt, Jansenist, supporter of the *quaestio facti*. Two works by Arnauld in defense of the duke.

**1656**: **condemnation of Arnauld** by the Sorbonne and his exclusion from the Faculty of Theology. Beginning of the publication of the ***Provincial Letters*** (1656-1657) **of Blaise Pascal**, Jansenist, strongly anti-Jesuit (the Jesuits were the main opponents of Jansenism): in them Pascal enters in polemic also with probabilism, Molinism and Protestantism. Healing of Jansenist Marguerite Périer upon touching a relic of the Crown of Thorns ⇒reinforcing Jansenist resistance. Bull *Ad sacram Petri*, of Alexander VII: the five propositions are in the sense of Jansenius. Preparation of an anti-Jansenist **Formulary by** the Assembly of the Clergy of France.

**1661**: obligation to sign the Formulary; resistance from the nuns of **Port-Royal**, who sign with restrictive clauses.

**1662**: Strengthening of the resistance of Port-Royal due to the miraculous healing of a nun of the monastery.

**1664-1667**: strong pressure on those who advocate **"respectful silence" towards the** condemnations of Jansenism and on the nuns of Port-Royal; the monastery is deprived of the sacraments; deportation of some nuns to other monasteries. New papal condemnation in the bull *Regiminis Apostolici* (1665).

**1667-1669**: Clementine **Peace**, advocated by Clement IX, even in the face of growing opposition from French bishops, who begin to argue that the Holy See has violated the king's rights in the Jansenist question (beginning of the intertwining of Jansenism and **royal Gallicanism**). Reconciliation of the nuns of Port-Royal.

**1669-1678**: peace. Activities of the Jansenist Oratorian Pasquier **Quesnel**.

**1678**: Peace of Nijmegen, ending the Dutch War, and conversion of Louis XIV.

**1679**: Beginning of Louis XIV's persecution of Jansenists and Huguenots.

**1687**: Publication of *Réflexions morales sur le Nouveau Testament*, by Quesnel.

**1693**: definitive rapprochement between the pope and Louis XIV, after the issue of the Corsican Guard (1662-1664) and the pontificate of Innocent XI (1676-1689), strongly hostile to the Sun King.

**1694**: Condemnation by the Holy Office of Quesnel's *Réflexions morales.* Beginning of Jansenist disputes in Holland.

**1695**: **Noailles**, pro-Jojanist, archbishop of Paris.

**1695-1710**: repression of Jansenism in the Spanish Netherlands (=Belgium); definitive papal condemnations with the bulls *Vineam Domini* (1705) and *Unigeniti* (1713).

**4. The morality of systems**

In the seventeenth century we find ourselves in front of a decline of moral theology, caused by an excessively legalistic vision of ethical science; in fact, the contact with the end of man and his happiness is lost and morality is reduced to an overall external law. Then the juridical method of recourse to previous judgments or to the doctrine of the jurists was applied to morals for the most complex cases; in the field of morals the sources were the judgments of the Fathers and theologians. Thus "systems" are formed to resolve doubtful cases, which differ according to the number of judgments needed to define the morality of a certain action, with extremes of laxity and rigorism.

All this is favored by the great development of casuistry: the moralist Antonio Diana (†1659) in his *Resolutiones* in 12 volumes, collected the study of about 20,000 moral cases.

The moral systems are as follows:

- Laxity: the Jesuits Antonio Escobar y Mendoza, Tommaso Tamburini and Étienne Bauny; the Cistercian José Caramuel; the theologian Antonio Diana.
- Probabilism: "Si est opinio probabilis, licitum est eam sequi, licet opposita probabilior sit." Hermann Busembaum, Jesuit: *Medulla theologiae moralis*.
- Probabiliorism: Tirso González de Santalla, Jesuit: from 1687 to his death (1705) he was provost general of the Society of Jesus, and in that position he tried to impose probabiliorism on the order, creating a great disconcertment and many tensions, since the majority of Jesuits were probabilists; before being provost he had written the treatise *Fundamentum theologiae moralis,* for which, however, he had not received from the superiors of the Society the permission to publish it: Having become provost, with the support of Innocent XII, he published it. Jean-Baptiste Gonet, Dominican.
- Tutiorism: Jan Opstraet.
- Rigorism: Jansenists: Jean Duverger de Hauranne Abbé de Saint-Cyran, Antoine Arnauld.

**5. Quietism**

**Miguel Molinos** (1628-1696). Spanish priest. In 1665 he settled in Rome, where he became a famous spiritual director. In 1675 he published the *Guida spirituale* (*Guía espiritual que desembaraza el alma y la conduce por el interior camino para alcanzar la perfecta contemplación y el rico tesoro de la interior paz*).

With the passage of time, he supports ideas that are considered heterodox: with spiritual ascent, the soul reaches a state of quiet, in which it lacks all desire, even that of salvation; the soul unites itself only with God; it is a state of total passivity, characterized by adoration without images, without words and without speech. In such a state the best thing is to abstain from any action, whether it be an attempt to converse with God or to resist temptation. In this state of quietness, what concerns the body has no repercussions on the soul.

From 1685 to 1687 a trial took place in Molinos, ending with his sentence to prison, where he died in 1696.

Innocent XI, in his bull *Coelestis Pastor* (1687), condemned 68 propositions attributed to Molinos: here are some of them:

1. It is necessary for man to annihilate his faculties, and this is his inner life.
2. To want to operate actively is to offend God who wants to be the one to operate: and for this it is necessary to abandon one's whole self and totally in God, and then to remain in him as a dead body.
3. Vows in order for something to be done are an impediment to perfection.
4. Natural activity is the enemy of grace; it impedes God's operations and true perfection. In fact, God wants to work in us without us.
5. Operating nothing, the soul annihilates itself and returns to its principle and origin which is the essence of God, in which it remains as if divinized.
6. The inner way is that in which one knows neither light nor love nor abandonment, and it is not necessary to know God, and so one proceeds righteously.
7. The soul must think of neither reward nor punishment, neither heaven nor hell, neither death nor eternity.
8. The soul need not wish to know whether it walks according to the will of God, or whether or not it remains abandoned to the same will; and it need not wish to know its state or its own nothingness; but it must remain as a dead body.
9. The soul must not reconsider itself or God, or anything else; in the inner life all reflection is harmful, even reflection on one's own actions and faults.
10. It is not necessary to reflect if by one's faults one scandalizes others, as long as there is no will to scandalize; and not being able to reflect on one's faults is a grace from God.
11. On the doubts that assail whether or not one is proceeding righteously, one should not ponder.
12. He who has given God free will, must not be concerned with any thing, neither with hell nor with heaven; and he must have no desire for his own perfection, nor for virtue, nor for his own holiness, nor for his own salvation, the hope of which he must eliminate.
13. Once we have submitted our free will to God, we must leave to God himself the thought and concern of all things; and let him do in us, without us, his divine will.
14. He who has submitted himself to the divine will must no longer ask God for anything; for asking is imperfection.
15. Just as he must not ask God for anything, so too he must not give thanks to him for anything; for both are an act of his own will.

**6. The controversy over pure love**

Controversy that takes place in France, beginning with the assertions of the wealthy widow **Madame Guyon** (Jeanne-Marie Bouvier de la Motte, 1648-1717), in part similar to Molinos' quietism, collected in her book *Moyen court et très facile pour l'oraison*, published in 1685. The publication of this work arouses much controversy, and Bishop Fénélon advises his widow to refer the work to Bossuet's judgment; the latter produces a document which, within a positive framework, reproves a number of statements contained in the work. Madame Guyon accepts the criticism and leaves the scene. The controversy continues between the two bishops, because Fénélon believes that the love of God in man must grow in purity and disinterestedness until it reaches a certain indifference with respect to one's eternal destiny; Bossuet maintains that man's aspiration to happiness cannot be denied. A controversy ensues.

- the Bishop of Meaux, **Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet** (1627-1704), with his *Instruction sur les états d'oraison* (1697).
- the Bishop of Cambrai, **François de Salignac de la Mothe Fénelon** (1651-1715), with the *Explications des maximes des saints sur la vie intérieur*.

A harsh dispute arose between the two; Bossuet induced Louis XIV to try again Fénelon who withdrew from the court to Cambrai.

On March 12, 1699, Innocent XII, with the brief *Cum alias ad apostolatos*, condemned 23 propositions extracted from the *Explications des maximes* (but none of them was reproved as heretical); Fénelon publicly submitted to the pope's decision with great obedience and humility.

Some of the propositions:

- There is an habitual state of love toward God that is pure charity, without any mixture with the motive of self-interest. Neither the fear of punishment nor the desire for remuneration has any part in it. One no longer loves God for merit, nor for perfection, nor even for the happiness one feels in loving Him.
- In the state of contemplative or unitive life, all interested motive for fear and hope disappears.
- In the state of holy indifference, the soul no longer has voluntary and deliberate desires in view of its own interest, except on those occasions when it does not faithfully cooperate with all its grace.
- In the same state of holy indifference, we want nothing for ourselves, but everything for God. We want nothing in order to be perfect and blessed with a view to our own personal advantage; but we want all perfection and bliss for the reason that it pleases God to make us want these things by the impulse of his grace.
- In this state of holy indifference we no longer want salvation as our salvation, as eternal deliverance, as a reward for our merits, as the greatest of all our interests; but we want it with fullness of will as the glory and satisfaction of God, as something that he himself wants, and that he wants us to want for himself.
- In the state of passivity, all the distinct virtues are exercised without thinking that these are virtues. At any time one thinks of nothing but doing what God wills, and jealous love at the same time makes one not want virtue for oneself, and one is never so endowed with virtue as when one is never attached to virtue.
- It can be said in this sense that the passive and disinterested soul no longer even wants love itself insofar as this is its perfection and happiness, but only insofar as it is what God wants from us.
- Pure love alone constitutes the whole inner life; and so it stands as the sole motive for all actions that are deliberate and meritorious.

**7. Apologetics**

From the middle of the century, as a reaction to the intolerance arising from the confessionalization and the development of monarchical absolutism, an anti-Christian thought developed: it is the group of rationalist libertines, or skeptics, who obviously had to move with great caution. The most important among them is Pierre Bayle. They will be one of the origins of the Enlightenment thought of the following century.

Libertine thought provokes a Catholic apologetic response, the two main exponents of which are French:

- **Blaise Pascal** (1623-1662), layman, Jansenist. Scientist, mathematician, philosopher as well as theologian. He died of an illness at the age of 39 years, among great suffering, which he endured with Christian resignation. Great writer, his apologetic work *Pensées* is still published in many languages. As a young man he received a broad education in both science and humanities. He was educated at home, by his father, without attending a school, in various areas of culture of the time: Latin, greek, logic, physics, philosophy ... Since very young showed a remarkable genius for mathematics, but in the study of which spent too much energy and ruined his health. He worked as a young man to a treatise on physics on the vacuum, which were published two extracts. After a first conversion in 1646, returned to a life a bit 'worldly. In 1654 he experienced a second deep spiritual conversion, thanks to the influence of one of his sisters, Jacqueline, a nun at Port-Royal. This second conversion brought him closer to Jansenist circles, and from then until his death he lived a life of prayer and Bible reading, poverty and mortification in the countryside. In 1656 he wrote the *Provincial Letters*, an anti-Jansenist work. The famous *Thoughts* are fragments that should have been used to write an apologetic treatise. In them Pascal criticizes the rationalist reductionism of Descartes and Montaigne, in the name of the complexity, depth and drama of human existence and soul. Man is an enigma: philosophers and religions have tried to find the solution, but the only true answer is Christ. Christianity presents itself as the philosophy that makes man know in a profound and balanced way his greatness and misery at the same time: unlike Stoicism that only exalts his greatness and leads to pride, and the skepticism of a Montaigne that lowers him to the level of animals. Therefore, he contrasts a more existentialist and anthropological approach to the rationalist one prevailing at the time: he opposes the esprit *de finesse* to the Cartesian *esprit de géometrie.* The latter cannot be used for philosophy, morals, religion, and in an absolute sense not even to the sciences themselves (except in geometry): in them usually the first principles are not at all clear and distinct ideas, but rather obscure. There is in this part also a harsh criticism of the inadequacy of logic (very close to that operated by Galileo Galilei, who moves, however, in another order of problems). The *esprit de finesse* is instead an affective method, for which we can understand in a deep, existential way, the origin of the principles. Along with rational demonstrations, Pascal leaves an important space for intuitive knowledge, which he calls "the reasons of the heart." Man lives in a condition at the same time of greatness and misery: Pascal underlines his cognitive limits (caused by his finiteness which is joined to an openness to infinity), and denounces also his cognitive presumption, that is the fact of standing up as judge of knowledge, typical of rationalism. Pascal's Jansenism can also be seen in a certain pessimism, when he describes man as a being dominated by errors, passions, sensuality, selfishness, lies, hypocrisy, who, in order to escape the discomfort that all this causes, seeks refuge in distractions, occupations, leisure, which allow him not to think about his condition, not to think about death; only grace can heal man from all this. At the same time Pascal underlines the greatness of man, which resides in thought, but this importance of thought is different from the metaphysical one of Descartes, it is more existential; therefore, morality has as its principle the effort to think well. The Christian religion is the only instance that manages to give an answer to man who perceives in himself the fracture of the binomial "greatness and misery" that characterizes his existence. On the existence of God: Pascal believes that it cannot be proved; at the same time it is a fundamental question to decide how to live; therefore we must bet, and it is much more profitable, in terms of losses/gains, to bet on the existence of God. God however remains a hidden God, with enough light for those who seek him, and enough darkness for those who are ill-disposed. In general, Pascal was able to propose the human condition in a modern way: man aware of his many limitations, but also able to overcome himself in a very consistent way through religious experience.
- **Nicolas de Malebranche** (1638-1715), priest of the French Oratory. In 1680 he published the *Traité de la nature et de la grâce*. In his studies and in his thought were important influences of St. Augustine and Renato Descartes: he realizes that the thought of the latter leads to a dichotomy between faith and philosophy, and tries to remedy this by trying to make a synthesis between Cartesianism and Augustinianism. Against Aristotelian scholasticism, he wants to propose an Augustinian theology. Following the Cartesian separation between extension and thought (matter and spirit), and also following Augustinian thought, he denies the concept of created causality in favor of a form of occasionalism: only divine causality exists, but it is neither despotic nor arbitrary, but rational (thus the possibility of science is maintained). He proposes again the ontological argument of Anselm of Aosta. He thinks that the concept of final cause is useless in the physical field, useful instead in the moral and spiritual one. He argues, in the line of Irenaeus of Lyon and John Duns Scotus, that the incarnation of the Word was the first divine plan, even before original sin. In the area of human knowledge, Malebranche advocates an ontological hymnatism of ideas, but different from both the Platonic and Augustinian: for him, we see ideas directly in God's intellect because we have an intuition of the divine mind; experience is a mere occasion that activates that intuition; the vision of ideas in God is possible because He is immediately present to our spirit. In general, Malebranche realizes the dangers of the Cartesian division between faith and reason, and he attempts to remedy it within the Cartesian philosophical construct, but he does not succeed well, and his remedy remains incomplete.

**An interesting and learned religious man: Mersenne**

Marin **Mersenne** (1588-1648) is an interesting religious figure of seventeenth-century France. He entered the Order of Minims and observed its strict rule with great fidelity. He wrote works of theology, such as a voluminous Commentary on Genesis, but with apologetic purposes, as can be seen from the title: *Quaestiones celeberrimar in Genesim. In hoc volumine Athei et deistae impugnantur et expugnantur, et Vulgatae editio ab haereticotum calumniis vindicantur*.

But Mersenne has gone down in history above all for the task he set himself of creating a network of intellectuals in Europe, putting them in contact with each other: he was a close friend of René Descartes and Pierre Gassendi, and managed to reconcile them after the harsh intellectual polemics between the two. The fact of being of a full proof orthodoxy did not prevent him from having a very open spirit: a lover of new scientific methods, he translated many works of Galileo Galilei; he was a friend of Thomas Hobbes. Leibniz said of him: "Fr. Mersenne was not as much Cartesian as it is believed: he divided himself between Robeval, Fermat, Gassendi, Descartes and Hobbes and was not afraid to take part in their theses and controversies; but he was available to all and encouraged them admirably.

**9. Gallicanism**

Gallicanism is the tendency existing in the French Church to claim its own specificity in relation to Rome and therefore, always within Catholic orthodoxy, a certain autonomy of the Gallican Church, which is governed by its own traditions and laws. The date of birth of Gallicanism is 1438, with the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges. In the first part of the seventeenth century this tendency was rather moderate, privileging the aspects of reform and unity, also in an anti-protestant function, but in the second half of the century, with the ascent to the throne of Louis XIV, Gallicanism regained strength and developed widely in the Kingdom of France.

Gallicanism, branched into four currents; once Gallican autonomy was claimed, each of them identified different subjects of power in the French Church:

- Gallicanism regalism: emphasizes the powers of the king of France
- Episcopalist Gallicanism: emphasizes the powers of the bishops
- Parliamentary Gallicanism or legist: defends the powers of the Sorbonne, the parliaments (courts) of the various cities and especially that of Paris
- Presbyterian or Richerist Gallicanism: claims more power for pastors within Gallican freedoms

Rome often succeeds in playing on these divisions of Gallicanism in order to dampen its scope; in fact Gallicanism will hardly ever be a heterodox or schismatic movement.

Edmond **Richer** (1559-1631), with his *De ecclesiastica et politica potestate libellus*, is an exponent of an ecclesiastical and presbyterian Gallicanism: he receives considerable influence from Marsilius of Padua and the conciliarist ecclesiologists. It supports the divine institution of the parish priests; moreover it affirms that the authority of Christ is transmitted to the community of the faithful, that in its turn transmits it the power of the priesthood to the *ordo sacerdotalis as a* whole, while the bishops have only a power of jurisdiction.

In 1639, brothers Pierre and Jacques Dupuy published a *Traitez des droits et libertés de l'Église gallicane*, with a Gallican royal tendency, but the work was reproved by a large group of bishops in the kingdom.

In 1641, the secular priest Pietro de Marca published the *Dissertationes de concordia sacerdotii et imperii seu de libertatibus Ecclesiae Gallicanae*, in which he sought a compromise between Gallican tendencies and Roman positions. The work was nevertheless put on the Index in 1642, although it had numerous reissues (1663, 1664, 1669, 1704, 1705) and the author became a bishop in 1648 and archbishop of Toulouse in 1652; then he was preconized archbishop of Paris, but died before taking possession of the see. Before becoming bishop, however, he had greatly moderated his Gallican theories and amended the *Concordance*.

Louis XIV (king from 1642 to 1715) carries on an extreme Gallican policy. In 1661-1664 the issue of the Guardia Corsa takes place: it was a police corps of the Papal State composed of men coming from Corsica. Because of a clash between the Corsican guards and the French ambassador's retinue, Louis XIV asks for humiliating reparations to the Holy See, occupies Avignon and threatens to invade Rome. The pope yields and accepts the humiliating conditions imposed by France.

In 1682 the General Assembly of the French Clergy voted in Paris on the Four Gallican Articles, drafted by Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet, Bishop of Meaux, and presented as vindications of Gallican liberties:

1. Popes have received from God only a spiritual power. Kings and princes are not subject to any ecclesiastical power in temporal matters; therefore they cannot be deposed by the ecclesiastical power of the keys, just as their subjects cannot be released from their oath of allegiance.
2. The fullness of power of the Apostolic See is limited by the decrees of Constance regarding the authority of ecumenical councils, decrees that have permanent value, not limited to the time of the schism.
3. The exercise of papal power is regulated by ecclesiastical canons; however, with them the longstanding principles and customs of the Gallican Church remain in force.
4. In judging questions of faith, the pope certainly has a preponderant part, but his decision is not irreformable if it is not supported by the consent of the universal Church.

Louis XIV immediately issued an edict in which he elevated these articles to universally valid doctrine, making them registered everywhere as law and making them compulsory for professors of theology and aspirants to the academic degrees of theology and canon law. The pope rejects the Four Articles, without effect; only later, at a time when France is isolated and engaged almost alone in a war against all the other European powers (War of the Spanish Succession, 1700-1715), does the pope succeed in bartering his support for France in exchange for the revocation of the Four Articles; they will then be restored by Napoleon I, with the Organic Articles.

Jacques-Benigne **Bossuet**, tutor to the Dauphin of France from 1669 to 1681, was also the theorist of the monarchy of divine right (the king derives his power directly from God, and only before Him is he accountable), with his posthumously published work *La politique tirée des propres paroles de l'Ecriture Sainte*; he also wrote a catechism (*Catechisme de Meaux*) a *Discours sur l'histoire universelle* (1681), and several works of anti-Protestant controversy (*Confutation of Pastor Ferry's Catechism*, *Exposition of the Catholic Faith*, *Histoire des variations des églises protestantes* [1688], *Diffide aux Protestants*).

**10. Theoretical and practical reflections on missions**

**The Congregation *of Propaganda Fide***

The Sacra Congregatio de Propaganda Fide is a dicastery of the Roman Curia, currently called the Congregation for the Evangelization of Peoples.

Its birth is due to a historical event of the second half of the 16th century. In fact, in 1568, Pope Saint Pius V, seeing the great fruits of the evangelization of Spanish America (a vast territory that stretched from California to Tierra del Fuego) wanted to send a papal legate to see the situation on the ground and to be able to make decisions for a better spiritual government of the young communities born from the conquest. However, he clashes with the refusal of the king of Spain, Philip II, who sees in this attempt of the pope a threat to the power acquired by the monarchy with the Patronage. Faced with the Spanish refusal, the pope creates a commission of cardinals to study the possibilities of action in the missionary field, and that has two sections: one for northern Europe (Protestant countries), the other for the West and East Indies.

At the end of the XVI century, Pope Sixtus V structured the Roman Curia on the system of congregations, and the idea of creating a congregation in charge of missionary work began to take shape. The definitive institution of the new congregation takes place on June 22, 1622, with the constitution *Inscrutabili* of pope Gregory XV (1621-1623): it is composed of 13 cardinals, two prelates and a secretary (the first, very important one is Francesco Ingoli). In 1626 the Tipografia Poliglotta is founded, specialized in the printing of books in various languages; in 1627 the Collegio Urbano di Propaganda Fide is established (named after the pontiff of the time, Urban VIII), which provided for the training of missionaries and would in time become the Urbaniana University.

The congregation was founded with two goals:

- 1. oppose and compete with the Spanish and Portuguese patronates, recovering territories from the direct government of Rome (in a certain sense this is the manifestation of the awareness of having made a mistake by granting patronage rights to the two Iberian Catholic monarchies)
  2. overcome, with a direction from above, the particularisms of the various religious orders, which sometimes caused mutual clashes, rivalries, attempts to create areas of "monopoly".

In this policy *Propaganda Fide* tries to lean on France (foundation of the Seminary of Foreign Missions in Paris, MEP) and uses many Italian, French and Flemish missionaries.

He begins to take control of territories that are "marginal" to the Spanish and Portuguese colonial empires: Canada and North America, Indochina and Siam; parts of China and India, where he comes into conflict with the Portuguese Patronate. Above all, given the greater weakness of the Portuguese patronage, he directs his efforts in Asia.

Constant Propaganda Policy Lines:

- 1. promotion of local clergy
  2. protection of colonized peoples
  3. independence from patronage
  4. avoid political compromises

In implementing this policy, the congregation must move forward always relying on a twofold scope of relationships:

- 1. *Propaganda Fide*⇔missionary orders: the congregation has no missionaries of its own and must turn to religious orders, which, however, also have their own dynamics of power and prestige within the Church (the same must be done by patronages).
  2. *Propaganda Fide*⇔colonial powers present in the various areas of missionary operations

In a memorial of 1625, Ingoli stigmatizes the "greed to have and acquire riches to take to Europe that reigns in many of the missionaries" and "the serious disagreements that multiply every day between the bishops of those parts and the religious and between the religious regulars and the fathers of the Company of Jesus missionaries. Moreover, he reproaches the superiors of the religious orders for sending to the Indies "for the most part unruly persons or persons touched by avarice from whom disputes and discords multiply". In order to remedy these inconveniences, Ingoli proposes to "prohibit religious from trading" and to send to the Indies "people fit for a good life and far from avarice".

On the local clergy: decree of *Propaganda Fide* of 28-XI-1630: "It is absolutely necessary to see to it that the most suitable among the Indians are promoted to holy orders up to and including the priesthood. They will have to be preliminarily instructed in a conscientious and precise manner. Their moral conduct will have to be tested for some years, and they will have to be trained in the piety and responsibility which the Christian religion imposes." The principles to which the document referred were the practice of the Apostles and the primitive Church, the trust that the indigenous priest would have enjoyed among his people, the awareness that such priests, knowing the language, customs, mentality, and culture of their compatriots, would have been better able to implement the incarnation of the Gospel message and dispel suspicions and mistrust of an anti-European type. This decree takes on great importance because the congregation, with the authority that comes from being a department of the Roman Curia, eradicates the widespread prejudice, according to which the natives were unable to access the priesthood, and reaffirms the full legitimacy of an indigenous priesthood. Ingoli, in a memorial of 1628 writes: "only very few Indians are promoted to the priesthood and with great favours and presents, so that as European priests the spiritual dominion is not lost to the Indians, nor the great riches that they obtain from them; for the same reason those few Indians who are ordained are not allowed to study the sciences, but only enough to be able to serve as chaplains in the parishes that the rectors of them, not knowing the language, need. In order not to promote the Indians to the priesthood and not to let them learn the sciences and because of the discords that arise among the religious for the above mentioned alternatives for other bad government of theirs and because there is no nuncio or judge who can put an end to them, there is no expectation or profit in the conversion of the Gentiles, the most important store that the Pope has in those parts and to whom he expects to procure their health ex officio". The remedies proposed by Ingoli: "the first is the ordinatione dell'Indiani atti. Nor should it be said that they are unfit, because it is not true, firstly because of natural reason, that it is impossible that in such a multitude of men there are not those fit for the priesthood; secondly because it is certain that the chaplains are ordained for the rectors of the parishes who do not know the languages; thirdly, the King of Spain makes use of the Indians in temporal governments, in Mexico there is a university where the Indians study and read, and the Indians who came to Rome have shown that this opposition is manifestly false and against experience; fourthly, the Bracmanians, the Chinese, the Japonese, the Corininese, the Tonquinese and all the Indians born of Spanish fathers are very apt for the priesthood. Nor is it worth saying that they get drunk, because this does not happen to everyone, but mostly in the lower classes who work all week, which also happens in Europe and in Germany passim even in serious people, and even these are ordained to the priesthood. Nor at last does it prevent them from being fickle and from easily falling into idolatry, because this is not generally true; on the contrary, the Indians succeed very well, because there is no family, however small it may be, which does not have a room set aside for the sole act of prayer and for the hospitality of priests and religious who pass by, and if they allowed themselves to be instructed in ecclesiastical things or if it were ordered that they be instructed, this inconvenience would not happen, if it even happens in some,. This remedy of the ordination of the Indians would remove the first cause at all, because they would attend with more charity and less interest to the spiritual government of their patriots, and it would not be necessary for Europeans to move to the Indies to dominate and enrich themselves under the pretext of administering spiritual aid to the Indians".

*In* 1649 *Propaganda Fide* issued the *Instruction to the Apostolic Vicars*, an extremely interesting document, in which one can see all the missionary ideology of the congregation. In it the missionaries are forbidden to intervene in political life and to participate in commercial activities: "the missionaries of the Sacred Congregation de Propaganda Fide have to consider that they are sent to convert the people to the Roman Catholic Apostolic Faith, and therefore they have to direct their every thought and action to this end, and leave out all things pertaining to the world, and they must not interfere in political and other affairs, which could hinder them, or cause persecution for anything other than the service of God and the health of souls, and therefore they must particularly refrain from writing new seculars to the same Sacred Congregation, or indeed to other public or private persons". In addition to not interfering in political affairs and not engaging in commerce, vicars apostolic should not ask for privileges, exemptions or special courts, aspire to public office, become advisors to princes, or indulge in nationalistic attitudes. Abstention from temporal affairs applies not only in cases where damage to missionary activity could result, but also when it is hoped that the influence of religion can thereby be increased and the faith propagated. The instruction strongly emphasizes that the Gospel message must spread without political or temporal support, but by its own strength, through the testimony of the evangelical virtues: detachment from earthly things, poverty, sobriety, patience, meekness, disinterestedness, charity. Missionaries must bear with serenity and patience the sovereigns even when they persecute the Church or are blameworthy, they must not burden the faithful for the sustenance of the mission because "the people appreciate nothing as absolute detachment and that evangelical poverty which does not accumulate riches except in heaven"; they must practice poverty remembering that riches and favors compromise freedom of speech.

The missionaries must avoid the territories occupied by Portugal or dependent on it, that is, the Atlantic route and the Cape of Good Hope; they must instead travel by land through Syria, Mesopotamia, Persia and India. Moreover, they must keep their departure hidden and travel incognito, revealing to no one their name, their country, their episcopal dignity (if any), or the purpose of their journey: all this to avoid being intercepted by the Portuguese.

Instruction entrusts to the vicar apostolics the primary task of training native priests. To this end they are to establish schools in which the Latin language and Christian doctrine are taught in the local idiom, and to promote the translation from Greek and Latin of many works of the Doctors of the Church.

The document strongly emphasizes that people should not change their rites and customs unless they manifestly contradict religion and good morals, because the missionary has the task of spreading not the Western culture, but the faith that transcends all cultures: "do not make any effort, do not use any means of persuasion to induce those people to change their rites, their habits of life, their customs, unless they are openly contrary to religion and good morals. For what could be more absurd than to transplant France, Spain, Italy or some other European country into China? It is not this that you must introduce, but the faith that neither rejects nor injures the rites and customs of any people, provided these are not evil, but rather intends to safeguard and consolidate them. And since it is inherent in human nature to surround the traditions of one's own country with greater esteem and great affection than those of others, so there is nothing that generates greater hatred or cause for resentment than to change the customs of one's own country, especially those learned from the concordant tradition of the fathers; and this is particularly the case when it is desired to replace abrogated customs with those of a foreign nation. Do not, therefore, ever compare local customs with European customs; rather, try with all your efforts to become accustomed to them. Admire and praise everything that deserves praise; if something does not deserve it, you must certainly not loudly exalt it as flatterers do, but you will have the prudence not to judge it or at least not to condemn it thoughtlessly and without reason. As for customs that are manifestly evil, it will be better to remove them by attitude and silence than by words, taking the opportunity to eradicate them slowly and almost insensibly, once minds are disposed to embrace the truth."

From these excerpts quoted above, it is possible to understand what ideas were present in Rome about the missions: it must be said, however, that they were often not applied, either because of the opposition of the colonial powers, or because of the resistance of the missionaries themselves.

**The Question of Chinese Rites (1645-1742): part one**

The question originates from the missionary ideas of Fr. **Matteo Ricci** S.I. (1552-1610); he understood that in China, a nation endowed with a very ancient culture, a wide work of **inculturation of** the faith was necessary: in this he was followed by the missionaries of his order. In concrete terms, the Jesuits thought that, unlike Buddhism and Taoism, which had to be fought, **Confucianism** was a **philosophy that could be "Christianized"** as had been done in the West with Plato and Aristotle.

In this line, however, the **Question of Terms** arose: is it better to use Chinese religious words, with the risk of not expressing the Christian concept perfectly, or to synthesize Latin terms?

More debated was the Question of **Rites:** the problem was this: is it permissible for a Christian to take an active part in a series of **traditional official ceremonies in honor of Confucius, the dead, and the genii of the** cities? In these ceremonies there was a mixture of religious and civil (but sometimes incense tablets were burned in honor of Confucius, etc.); on the other hand, public officials were obliged to take part. Summing up, the issue is a long debate between Jesuits on one side, Dominicans, Franciscans and the Foreign Missions of Paris on the other: the former believed that these rites were purely civil and therefore licit, the latter that they were religious acts and therefore illicit for a Christian. The different opinions reflected in part the social classes to which the different orders were directed in their mission: the Jesuits were directed to the upper classes, who in fact did not consider such acts religious; but the mendicants were directed to the popular classes, who instead considered them as such and whose converts were shocked to see a Christian mandarin participating in such rites. On the other hand, the Jesuits argued, prohibiting such ceremonies would lead to the end of the mission among the upper classes; but the mendicants retorted by showing the parallelism with the situation of Christians in the Roman Empire. Moreover, this debate was also an expression of a political clash in China between two colonial powers: France, which supports the Congregation *de Propaganda Fide* and is against the rites, and Portugal, which wants to reaffirm its rights of Patronage, in favor of the Jesuits and the Chinese rites.

Thus in the first half of the seventeenth century, Fr. Juan Bautista Morales O.P. asked *Propaganda Fide* for the condemnation of the Chinese Rites. In **1645**, indeed, this **condemnation** arrives.

The Jesuits commissioned Fr. Martino Martini S.I. to draw up a counter memorial, which was presented in 1654 to *Propaganda* and passed by it to the Holy Office.

**1656: The Holy Office allows rites**, but urges care to avoid superstitions; it states that rites look like civil ceremonies, and that if Christians missed them, social harm would result.

Juan Polanco O.P. asks the Holy Office how to harmonize the two decrees. In the same year, 1669, the Holy Office replies that both decrees must be applied: in fact, idolatrous ceremonies are prohibited by the first decree (1645), while purely civil ceremonies are permitted by the second (1656).

1693: the Vicar Apostolic of the Province of Fukien, Mgr Charles **Maigrot** (Foreign Missions of Paris) prohibits the rites in his territory: the missionaries agree, but groups of faithful rebel.

**1699: a cardinal's commission on Chinese Rites is created.**
