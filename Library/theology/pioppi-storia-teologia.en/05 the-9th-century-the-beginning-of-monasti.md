## The 9th Century: The Beginning of Monastic Theology {#pioppi-storia-teologia.5}
**1. General Background**

The Holy Roman Empire

The Holy Roman Empire, established in 800, brought together almost all of Christendom of the Latin rite; it included the territories of the following states or regions: France, Germany, Holland, Belgium, Catalonia, Switzerland, Austria, Italy, Slovenia, Croatia, Czech Republic, Hungary. Only the Anglo-Saxon and Celtic kingdoms of the British Isles and the Asturian principalities remained outside.

This fact gives rise to the idea of the *respublica christiana,* or *christianitas*, that is, of a single political entity that gathers all Christians, with a single authority (the emperor) called by God to lead the Christian people towards happiness, both earthly and eternal, together with the pope. Therefore, the idea of two sacred powers in theChristian world developed: papacy and empire.

There was therefore a sacralization of imperial power, in which the idea of *christianitas* and the ancient Roman imperial-universal tradition converged, a tradition that had never died in the West even after the dethronement of Romulus Augustulus in 476. This sacralization is shown in the almost sacramental ceremonies of the taking possession of the title: the emperor is crowned by the pope; he receives a sacred anointing. In the Carolingian court, it became fashionable to give each person a nickname taken from the Bible, and Charlemagne called himself David, like the holy king of the Jews, chosen by God and anointed by the prophet Samuel. This is a new phenomenon in the Christian West: neither the Christian emperors of the dominated, nor the kings of the Roman-Barbarian kingdoms had operated such a mixture of sacred and political; in the West, however, this phenomenon, although consistent, never reached the levels of the Eastern Roman Empire, where a more extreme caesaropapism was in force.

A concomitant phenomenon to the sacralization of political power is that of the clericalization of officials: the latter is dictated by mostly practical reasons, since clerics are often the only men of culture.

In short, in this world, Church, society and state come to be confused; this attitude can be seen from the formulation that is sometimes made of the famous formula of Pope Gelasius on the distinction between religious and political power; the original formula read: "duo sunt quibus principaliter mundus hic regitur"; in the ninth century we often find this formula written as follows: "duo sunt quibus principaliter ecclesia regitur": the word *ecclesia* has therefore taken on the meaning of a synonym for *mundus.*

During the reign-empire of Charlemagne (768-814)and the empire of his son Ludwig the Pious (814-840) there was a period of calm, prosperity, peace. The center of the empire is Aachen. The Christian world is well defended by the imperial armies, there is a certain economic recovery, as well as a cultural renaissance. This positive phenomenon is called the Carolingian Revival:

- Palatine School, which brings together scholars from all over Europe: Alcuin of York, Witto and Sigulfo, English; Dungalo, Irish; Paolo Diacono, Italian; Theodulf of Orleans, Spanish; and Eginardo, first biographer of Charlemagne. Further on we find Rabanus Maurus, Walafridus Strabo, Amalarius of Metz, John Scotus Eriugena.
- emendation of Sacred Scripture, to eliminate deformations introduced over time by copyists.
- introduction of the Carolingian script, which brings Europe back to a writing unity; in the early Middle Ages, in fact, national scripts had developed, so that each geographical area tended to create its own alphabet starting from different modifications of the Latin alphabet (thus, Merovingian script in France, Visigothic script in Spain, insular script in England and Ireland, Benevento script in Italy).
- organization of instruments for the education ofchildren and social assistance for the poor, handicapped, etc., through ecclesiastical structures (monasteries and cathedrals).

Feudalism

In the Carolingian world, the feudal system was being organized, the result of a development that had begun earlier: at the base of the system were the peasants: they, no longer able to defend themselves, entrusted themselves to the local castellan: they gave their land to the lord and cultivated it for him, sending him a part of the products, and pledged not to abandon it; in return, the lord pledged to protect them in case of need (serfdom). In turn, the castellan, for similar reasons, swears allegiance to a lord of higher rank, which acquires obligations of protection to the lower and so on until the emperor.

In the feudal system, as a result of the ancient Germanic mentality, the Roman distinction between public and private law is lost; the feudal lords have fundamental relations with each other for the structure of the state, but they are governed by personal oaths between private individuals.

The crisis of the Carolingian system

With the death of Ludwig the Pious (840), the empire fell into chaosfor two reasons:

1. Ludovico has three sonsand, according to the Frankish conception, where there was no difference between public and private law, divides the empire between them; but the partition does not satisfy any of the three. It begins a long period of wars between Lothair (Italy, Lorraine, Rhineland, Netherlands), Charles the Bald (France) and Ludwig the German (German areas) and their descendants, which plunge the state into a condition of feudal anarchy.
2. In addition to this, there are highly devastating external attacks by Vikings (North), Hungarians and Saracens: these peoples have no interest in invading the empire (which then implies its administration), but only in systematically plundering it with continuous raids. The Christian world plunges again into chaos and barbarism. These harsh living conditions lead to a further barbarization of the European population. The Saracens, who in 827 had begun the conquest of Sicily, in 846 besieged Rome, which resisted within the Aurelian walls, and sacked the basilicas of St. Paul Outside the Walls and St. Peter's, which were outside the city walls. In 849 they return in force to conquer and sack Rome, but the Urbe is saved by the pope, who in the absence of other authorities, organizes a fleet and defeats the Arabs in the naval battle of Ostia. In the same period the Saracens sacked the abbey of Montecassino. The Viking invasions put an end to the flourishing civilizations developed since the 7th century in England, Scotland and Ireland, which were devastated by the Scandinavians. The Hungarians devastate all of Central and Eastern Europe, often reaching as far as Italy and France.

The Papacy

In the meantime, the pope, in the 8th century, saw the opportunity to formalize his dominion over central Italy, which would have allowed him greater independence from political power, and thus avoid the risk of becoming an instrument in the hands of a monarch (it should be remembered that even at the end of the 7th century there were unsuccessful Byzantine attempts to kidnap the pope and take him to Constantinople in order to make him fulfill the will of the emperor of the East. There are three steps in the formalization of temporal power: the donation of Sutriin 728, made by the Lombard king Liutprand; the decision of Pope Adrian I to use in the dating of documentshis years of reign and not those of the emperor of the East; the donation of Charlemagne.

At the beginning of the crisis of the mid-ninth century, the power and prestige of the pope increased, in correspondence with the decrease in imperial power. Especially with **Saint Nicholas I** (858-867), a great pope (he won the friendship of his enemy, Anastasius the Librarian, an antipope who became his main collaborator; he defended Tietberga from her husband Lothair II who wanted to leave her to marry Fastrada: to this end the pope deposed the archbishops of Cologne and Trier, who had been too complacent with the emperor).

But over time, the dramatic political and social situation would also have an adverse effect on the papacy: in 896, in fact, with the *Synodus ad cadaver* against the late Pope Formoso by Stephen V, the Iron Age of the papacy began. Without imperial protection, little by little the papacy fell into the power of the feudal lords of Central Italy.

The theorization of papal monarchy:proponents of a pastoral monarchy of the pope are the pontiffs Nicholas I and John VIII (872-882), who begin to use the title "vicar of Christ". It should also be considered that their papacies took place in the period of the serious crisis of the Holy Roman Empire, devastated by internal discord among the various pretenders to the crown, and by external invasions: this situation gave more prominence to papal power. In the same period the false *Pseudo-Isidorian Decretals* were drawn up, which attributed to popes of antiquity attitudes and rights proper instead of the theories of this time on the power of the pontiff in the Church. However, the Decretals were not made in Rome, but probably in France, with the intention of giving more strength to the claims of independence of priestly authority from the laity.

Missions

Conversion of the Czechs (845), Moravians (850), and Bulgarians (866).

The missions in Scandinavia begin with St. Ansgarius (d. 865).

The Eastern Roman Empire

Emperor Leo V(813-820) believed that the defeats suffered by his predecessors against the Arabs were divine punishments for the idolatry of the cult of images present in the Christian world: he thus opened a second iconoclastic crisis: 815-853**.** The Empress Theodora, an iconodist, brought back peace and union with Rome. But in the meantime there was another forty years of separation between East and West.

Shortly afterwards another incident occurs: the Schism of Photius: during the reign of Michael III the Drunken, in 858, the patriarch Ignatius is illegitimately deposed by the emperor at the instigation of his uncle Barda; as new patriarch is installed Photius, a very cultured man, but still secular (in five days he is given all the orders). Ignatius appeals to Rome, which gives him reason for the schism, which will be resolved by the Fourth Council of Constantinople. The furrow between Latins and Greeks deepens more and more; Photius writes a series of violently anti-Latin works. The two worlds no longer understand each other: the Greeks despise the Latins and consider them barbarians, ignorant and heretics; the Latins, hurt by this attitude, elaborate a representation of the Greek as intriguing, untrustworthy, cunning and cowardly. At the end of the crisis Photius will return to be patriarch, and in communion with Rome, but in the meantime he has fabricated a whole anti-Latin paraphernalia that will remain for centuries and comes to this day.

**Monastic theology**: characterized by a great respect for tradition. It uses ancient sources but without knowing how to blend them into something very original. As M. Grabmann wrote: "the profane studies [trivium and quadrivium] were seen as preparatory to theology, without, however, an intimate connection being made between the two fields of knowledge. The formal advantages inherent in the seven liberal arts, especially dialectic and didactic aids were, in general, little used to define and study the content of revelation. Theology consisted above all in the exegesis of the sacred scriptures, an exegesis which, as regards its aims, had a moralizing and allegorical character, while from the point of view of form, it had the character of a compilation. For the explanation of scripture, neither historical-grammatical interpretations nor independent research on concepts and connections of the biblical texts were of almost any importance, but rather collections of passages extracted from the fathers. On the other hand, this character of receptivity, of transmission of contents not subject to the revision of an autonomous thought, so typical of the teaching of the High Middle Ages, can also be found in the literary productions of this period".

**2. The question of the Filioque**

It seems that the origin of the addition of the Filioque (i.e., the procession of the Holy Spirit from the Son as well as the Father) first occurred in Spain (Council of Toledo in 589) and from there passed into the Kingdom of the Franks. After the Council of Frankfurt in 794, Charlemagne decided to have the Creed sung with the Filioque in his chapel. Pope Leo III gave his approval but refused to introduce it into the Roman liturgy. In fact, it was not used in Rome until 1014, when Benedict VIII, at the insistence of the Emperor Saint Henry II, accepted it into the liturgy of the Roman Church.

The Greeks accuse the Latins of heresy for this use: Paulinus of Aquileia defends the Filioque in the Synod of Cividale del Friuli (796). Charlemagne has it sung in the Credo of the Palatine Chapel. The Frankish monks of the Mount of Olives in Jerusalem use it. Pope Leo III (795-816), while recognizing the formula as orthodox, out of regard for the Greeks disapproves of its use in the Creed, and advises the Franks to remove it.

During the Schism of Photius, criticism of the addition of the Filioque became one of the workhorses of Greek polemicists and of Photius himself. The latter, in fact, understands the term "proceed" in a very narrow sense: proceed = proceeding from a principle without a principle. On this basis the Filioque would lead to two consequences:

- either the Father and the Son are one person
- or you falsify the genuine meaning of the term "proceed".

The Latins respond by stating that the Father and the Son are different persons, but that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son as from one principle.

**3. Theodulf of Orleans (760ca-821)**

He is, after Alcuin, the most eminent cultural figure of Charlemagne's time. Of Visigoth origins, he was born in Saragossa or in Aragon. He wrote the works *De Spiritu Sancto* and *De ordine baptismi*, as well as commentaries and explanations of the Mass and the Creed. He possessed a great patristic culture. He was also the most important Latin poet of his time.

**4. Godescalco d'Orbais and the Predestination Controversy**

Godescalco is a Saxon monk, born in 805 from a noble family (he was son of Bernone, count of Saxony), who enters the Abbey of Fulda, where Rabano Mauro was abbot (who in 847 becomes archbishop of Mainz); then he passes in the Abbey of Orbais. Godescalco, basing himself on some anti-Pelagian polemical works of St. Augustine, comes to hypothesize a double predestination, *similiter omnino*, one to evil and the other to good; therefore postulates a salvific will of God only particular and not universal, coming in substance to deny human freedom. These theories provoked the intervention against him by Rabano Mauro, bishop of Magonza and of the metropolitan Incmaro archbishop of Rheims. Judged in the synods of Mainz (848, presided over by Ludwig the German) and Quierzy (849), Godescalco is condemned to flagellation and then to monastic prison for life. Soon protests for this harsh treatment arrived: from Ratramnus of Corbie, Remigio of Lyon and Lupo Servato of Ferrières. In time the matter reaches Pope Nicholas I, who wants to intervene in favor of Godescalco, but it is not possible because the death of the Saxon monk occurred in 866. However after the condemnation Godescalco continued his theological activity, participating in the Eucharistic controversy.

There is a theological reaction against Raban and Incmarus in Lyon: Lupus Servatus and Prudentius of Troyes accuse of heresy the position of the party of Reims; Incmarus asks for help to John Scotus Eriugena, who however produced a writing - the *De divina praedestinatione* - that resulted practically pelagian. In 859, at the Frankish diet of Savonnières, there was another clash between the two parties; they appealed to Pope Nicholas I, but the death of Godescalco (866) put an end to the controversy.

**5. The Eucharistic Controversy**

The controversy arises on the occasion of the writing of the first specific treatise on the Eucharist in history, the *De corpore et sanguine Domini* (between 831 and 833, but made public in 844) by the Benedictine **Paschasius Radbertus** (†850), abbot of the Monastery of Corbie. This is the first attempt to organize a synthesis of the patristic heritage on this sacrament. Up to this time, theological reflection on the Eucharist had not yet gone into depth in an attempt to explain the mystery. Two traditions existed in general:

- a more realistic-metabolic one, inspired by Saint Ambrose and Saint Isidore.
- a more spiritualistic-dynamic one, inspired by Saint Augustine

Paschasius Radbertus fits into the former. The problem is that he over-emphasizes the identity of Christ's historical body and his Eucharistic body, making too little of the diversity of the way he is present; he is therefore accused of Capernaitism. In fact, Pascasius argues that there is more than a real presence of Christ in the Eucharist, speaking of a material presence. "The Body of Christ is found on the altar in a local manner, if one can say so, with a spatial arrangement of its members, shrunken to the size of the host, but nevertheless existing apart from each other. The species are like a veil thrown over this reality, without Pascasius daring to affirm that they have an objective existence. As for the manner in which this presence is realized, our author still hesitates on the choice of words to use; his terminology oscillates between the term *creation* and that of *transformation*, to which he would more willingly adhere. If we then add that Pascasius, at least in the way of expressing himself, supports the idea that in the Mass a real immolation is realized, a real killing of the holy victim and as a passion from which it would suffer, it will be understood how - in spite of its fundamental orthodoxy, in spite of its undeniable piety and the happy anticipation on the ideas of the future - the treatise *De corpore et sanguine Domini* could not gather all the consents. To be a true treatise on the Eucharist, it lacked adequate theological terminology" (Fliche-Martin, *Storia della Chiesa*).

The reaction was in a spiritualistic sense: against Paschasius intervened **Ratramnus of Corbie** (*De corpore et sanguine Domini*, 859): he accused his confrere Paschasius of cafarnaitism, and affirmed that the conversion of bread into the Body of Christ can happen only *spiritualiter* and not *corporaliter,* and therefore it must be asserted that it happens *figuratively*; one cannot speak of *veritas* for the Eucharist, but of *figure.* John Scotus Eriugena, Godescalco (who wrote, already imprisoned, the *Dicta cujusdam sapientis*) also intervened. Rabanus Maurus himself opposes Pascasius, with different arguments: for him there would be an identity *naturaliter*, in the substance, and not *specialiter*, in the way of being.

Paschasius replied to Ratramnus, moderating his terminology a bit, in a Commentary on the Gospel of Matthew and in a writing to Fredgard of S. Riquier: above all he denies ever having claimed that a shattering of the Body of Christ takes place in the Mass.

In the field of soteriology, we should also remember Pascasius Radbertus' theory according to which the necessity or expediency of redemption derives from the rights of the devil, which he acquired over men through original sin and their other sins, and from the justice that had to be rendered to the devil for these rights.

**6. Rabanus Maurus (776-856)**

He is called the *primus praeceptor Germaniae*, because of the importance of his work in the spread of ecclesiastical culture in this country. Benedictine and disciple of Alcuin of York. He directed the Palatine School. Author of the hymn *Veni creator Spiritus*. Abbot of Fulda from 822. Archbishop of Mainz since 847.

His works include:

- commentaries on Holy Scripture: Rabanus Maurus is an exceptional exegete, and his commentaries on the *Corpus Paulinum* are of particular value.
- *De universo*: encyclopedic work, which follows the style of the *Etymologies* of St. Isidore of Seville (books I-V: theological subjects; books VI-XXII profane sciences). There is also an ecclesiological treatment: *Ecclesia Catholica quae mystice corpus est*; in it he dwells much on episcopal collegiality and less on the Petrine primacy.
- *De institutione clericorum*: for candidates for the priesthood; written in 819, Rabanus there expressed his views on the method of teaching, and the importance he accorded to the *artes liberales* as a propaedeutic to theology. It depends heavily on Augustine (especially on *De doctrina christiana*), Cassiodorus, and Isidore. It is divided into three books:

1. Church Offices, Baptism, Eucharist
2. Liturgy of the hours, fasts and feasts, liturgical hymns, compendium of the Catholic faith
3. Scripture, Preaching.

- *De praedestinatione*

We recall his participation in the Eucharistic controversy (see *above*), and also some criticism of the soteriological theory of the devil's rights over man.

**7. Amalarius of Metz (d. 850)**

Disciple of Alcuin of York. Founder of medieval allegorical liturgy.

*De ecclesiasticis officiis ad Lodovicum Pium*. Not free from exaggeration.

"For him, the celebration of the Mass is first and foremost a "sacred representation," a dramatic reliving of Christ's life and in particular his passion, death and resurrection. According to Amalarius, the Introit (as the cantors announce the arrival of the celebrant) refers to the chorus of the prophets who announced throughout history the coming of Christ into the world. The Kyrie refers to the prophets' immediate preparation for Christ's advent. The Gloria recalls the choir of angels who announced to men the birth of Christ. The Collect refers to Jesus' preaching in the temple in the twelfth year. The Epistle refers to the preaching of John the Baptist. The Responsory refers to the prompt response of the apostles, who were called by Jesus and followed him. The Gospel is linked to the preaching of the Lord. The Preface to his prayer at the Last Supper. The first three prayers of the Canon, to the Lord's threefold prayer in Gethsemane" (A. García Ibañez).

On the real presence in the Eucharist, Amalarius relates the parts of the host, to the *fractio panis*, with the threefold body of Christ: the body born of Mary, the church militant, and the church triumphant.

In the same line stands **Aimon of Halberstadt** (†853), a friend of Rabanus Maurus.

Against such exaggerations, **Florus of Lyons** (†860) reacted; among other things, he criticized the devaluation made by Amalarius of Metz with regard to the common priesthood of the faithful; in fact, Florus wrote in his *De actione Missarum*: "quod enim adimpletur proprie ministerio sacerdotum, hoc generaliter agitur fide et devotione cunctorum".

**8. Walafridus Strabo (d. 849)**

Disciple of Rabanus Maurus, abbot of Reichenau and tutor to Emperor Charles the Bald.

Great exegete, liturgist, and hagiographer.

He wrote a compendium of liturgy, entitled *De exordiis et incrementis quarundam in observationibus ecclesiasticis rerum*.

For a long time he was attributed the authorship of the Glossa Ordinaria.

**9. John of Orleans**

Bishop of Orléans, successor of Theodulf, he wrote a treatise against iconoclasm, entitled *De culti imaginum*; also a work of moral and pastoral theology - *De institutione laicali* -, and another of political theology based on the thought of the fathers of the Church, entitled *De institutione regia.*

**10. Remigius of Auxerre (d. 908)**

He carried out a conspicuous activity of glossator and commentator, above all to the S. Scripture and to the works of Boethius.

**11. Incmar of Reims (806-882)**

He is the most important canonist of this century. We have already seen his intervention in the controversy over predestination. In the field of ecclesiology, he, like Rabanus Maurus, deals a lot with episcopal collegiality and does not seem too interested in the Petrine primacy. From 845 he was archbishop of Reims. He also wrote a historical work, the *Annals of the Kingdom of the Franks*, which cover the years from 861 to 882.

**12. John Scotus Eriugena (d. 877ca.)**

Born in Ireland, he moved to France in 847. He directed the Palatine School under Charles the Bald (who reigned from 843 to 877). There is no more certain news of him from 870 onwards. The name Eriugena is used since 1681, as it appears in the edition of his works edited by Thomas Gale (Oxford). He knows Greek (quite an extraordinary fact at that time in the Latin West) and translates in Latin the *Celestial Hierarchies* of Pseudo-Dionysius, the *Quaestiones ad Thalassum* of Maximus the Confessor, various works of Gregory of Nyssa. He is very influenced by Platonism, through Augustine and the Pseudo-Areopagite. He also comments on the *Opuscula sacra* of Boethius.

He is a very original thinker, with a great speculative capacity; Martin Grabmann says about him: "as in the middle of an undulating and well-cultivated countryside suddenly rises a steep and lonely mountain, so from the scientific production of the ninth century, traditionalist and mostly compilatory, emerged an extraordinary figure of thinker: John Scotus Eriugena".

He writes a *De divina praedestinatione*, with which he intervenes in the controversy aroused by Godescalco.

His *De divisione naturae* (or *Periphyseon*), finished around 866, is in the line of a consistent application of Platonic and Neoplatonic theories to the theological discourse on creation and in general on the main Christian themes: represents a kind of *sum*, but *avant la lettre*; this work is strongly influenced by Maximus the Confessor and Pseudo-Dionysius, of whom the Irishman was the first Latin translator: in fact, the works of the Aeropagite had been brought as a gift, in 827, by a Byzantine ambassador to the court of Ludwig the Pious.

In fact, Eriugena draws his fundamental idea from the Areopagite, that of God as principle and end of all things, which come out from God and return to God, in a process of multiplication and one of intercommunion and return. Eriugena too, however, suffers from the philosophical ambiguity of the Pseudo-Dionysius, since, in using the neo-Platonic theory (especially Proclus') he does not reject - as clearly does Augustine of Hippo - a conception with pantheistic accents.

Eriugena then makes a distinction of the existent in: *quae creat et non creatur* (God as cause); *quae creatur et creat* (archetypal ideas); *quae creatur et non creat* (entities)*; quae nec creatur nec creat* (God as end).

Therefore, between the first cause and creatures, Eriugena places created and creating nature: all creatures derive from God through intermediaries, through a causality that has the character of a hierarchical participation. These intermediaries are the primordial causes, in which other things participate. There are ten of them: goodness, essence, reason, life, intelligence, wisdom, virtue, bliss, truth, eternity; all that is good participates in goodness in itself, and so on. These primordial causes extend their action only as far as it is established by the Creator, and their multiplicity is brought back to unity in their principle, namely, in the Word. One finds in this the attempt, along the lines of Dionysian thought, to compose the idea of the plurality of participations with the revealed datum of God as the only Creator. To the above mentioned theory of the realism of participation, Scotus adds that of exemplarism, according to which the ideas and the primordial causes are eternal: in order not to deny the creation *ex nihilo* Eriugena affirms that the ideas are eternal, and not coeternal, wanting with this to mean that God precedes them as a cause, but he can not get out of the ambiguity and insufficiency of these statements. From here John Scotus passes to the most problematic statements of his thought: according to his exemplarist doctrine, with the ideas as predestinations through which God has arranged all things that are and must be, he cannot separate the will of God from his essence: God is therefore identical to his will, that is identical to everything he does. Hence come a series of more than ambiguous statements, such as the one according to which "Deus itaque omnia est et omnia Deus", or the other that reads: "et omnia creans in omnibus creatus, et factor omnium factus in omnibus et aeternus coepit esse et immobilis movetur in omnia et fit in omnibus omnia". God's action is theophany, and all that is, is theophany. These ambiguous positions, which could be interpreted in a monist and pantheist sense, earned Scotus a first warning of his doctrines by the Council of Valence (855), which also condemned his predestinationist theses and more explicit condemnations at the turn of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when some exponents of the school of Chartres tried to revive his doctrines.

In reality Eriugena does not seem to be a pantheist: for him God is the being of things insofar as their idea is their true essence; but Eriugenian ideas are the wills with which God creates himself in things; there is therefore a priority of the will, and therefore of divine independence and transcendence, which gives the full meaning to the formulas of monistic accent.

Man is seen as *officina mundi*. Man's highest dignity consists in being made in the image and likeness of God. Before original sin, the human soul was all image: the fall caused animality. Sexuality, too, derives from original sin; without it, human generation would have been similar to that of the angels.

Ultimately Eriugena continues the direction of the theology of the Pseudo-Areopagite by offering a Christian interpretation of Neoplatonism. Being is thought of as a function of will, goodness, and grace. The difficulty of his thought is found in the fact that he identifies essences with ideas, ideas with the divine will and therefore with the very essence of God.

In any case, it is undeniable that Eriugena represented an exceptional character in the theological panorama of his century; the originality of his thought derives above all from his knowledge of Dionysian writings. The main sources of *De divisione naturae* show well his originality: besides pseudo-Dionysius and Maximus the Confessor, Plato, Aristotle, Eratosthenes, Cicero, Virgil, Ambrose and Augustine, the three Cappadocians, John Chrysostom, Hilary of Poitiers and Boethius. Above all, Eriugena is an interesting attempt to apply philosophy to the knowledge of faith, even if not entirely successful. In him is clear the confidence in the harmony between faith and reason, between philosophy and theology.

Scotus had a certain influence in the theological thought of the 12th century; at the beginning of the 13th century Amalric of Bène (†1206), inspired by him, crossed the border of heterodoxy, which led to the condemnation of Eriugena's *De divisione naturae* first by a Parisian council (1210), then by Pope Honorius III (1225): with this last condemnation, it was ordered to burn the existing copies of the work, which therefore did not have an important influence on the great scholastic teachers of the 13th century. XIII; yes, however, were widely used by scholastics, his comments and glosses to Boethius. The Irish thinker was much revalued in the German Catholic theological and philosophical world in the first half of the nineteenth century, at the time of the great rise of idealism and Hegelianism.

**13. Theodore Studita (759-826)**

Monk and then igumen in the Monastery of Studion in Constantinople. He composed the *Studite Rule*, which was later adopted by many Eastern monasteries; it was inspired by Pachomius and Basil.

He committed himself to the anti-iconoclastic struggle and wrote the *Antirreticos*.
