### Chapter 2 God Speaks to Man: Divine Revelation and His Offering of Salvation {#tanzella-rivelazione.1.chapter-2-god-speaks-to}
#### 2.1 Religion, question about God and Revelation
It should not be forgotten that, in addition to philosophy and theology, religion also speaks of God. While *philosophical* reflection on religion is well developed (philosophy of religion, phenomenology of religion, etc.), a *theological* investigation of religion is generally less present. Today it is more easily a theology *of religions*, but it reflects less on a theology of religion, in the singular. If philosophy of religion is interested in studying how religion speaks of God, a theology of religion should first of all be interested in studying whether and how God speaks to man through religion; that is, what is the theological value of the "religious sense" that has always accompanied the human condition.

There are many reasons that suggest including a study of religion at the beginning of a study of fundamental theology. Patristic apologetics, and in part also mediaeval apologetics, have generally placed religion and philosophy side by side as two great areas of preparation for the faith. Important in the works of the Fathers was the clarification aimed at separating religion from idolatry, the true religious sense from superstition, the work of the Logos from the works of the devil. Christianity was presented by them not only as true philosophy (Justin, Clement of Alexandria), but also as true religion (Lactantius, Eusebius of Caesarea, Augustine). From a theological perspective one can speak of religion as a preamble to faith; as an anthropological constant that manifests the human being's openness to a divine revelation, recognizing his condition as "hearer of the Word"; as the context of a revelation of God - of the one and only true God, creator of heaven and earth - in the spectacle of nature and in human consciousness. The religious sense is of interest to theology because it involves both the transcendental and athematic dimension of the relationship with the divine (religious existence) and the categorical dimension (religious language). The study of the relationship between religion and revelation, between religion and faith, is thus decisive for the theology of religion. A third relationship, that between religion and reason, is equally important: philosophy, *recta ratio*, and therefore to a certain extent also scientific knowledge, have the task of purifying religion from idolatry and irrationality, of healing it from contradictions and errors, bringing the religious phenomenon back into the realm of authenticity, of human experience as human beings, which manifests our condition as creatures in the image of God.

Finally, religion is not only the beginning of the human journey that questions things and oneself, but in a certain way it is also the conclusion: "When *the whys and wherefores* *of things* are fully investigated in search of the ultimate and most comprehensive answer, then human reason touches its summit and opens up to religiosity. Indeed, religiosity represents the highest expression of the human person, because it is the culmination of his rational nature. It springs from man's profound aspiration to the truth and is the basis of his free and personal search for the divine".1

The place of a fundamental theology path where the relations between philosophy, religion and revelation can be developed with greater theological extension, is the course of *Introduction to the Christian mystery*. In this course, *Revelation and its credibility*, we will recall only a few essential concepts.

##### 2.1.1 Religion as a fundamental anthropological constant
**The human creature can rightly be defined as a *naturaliter religiosus* being.** Religiosity accompanies the development of the whole of human culture as **a fundamental anthropological constant**.2 Cultural anthropology, the phenomenology of religion and, more recently, paleoanthropology, have asked themselves **whether the religious sense and the sense of the sacred belong to the original structure of human experience or whether they are rather the product of cultural choices linked to the increasingly evolved and complex development of human society**.

**The schools that originate from an Enlightenment and positivist perspective have interpreted human religiosity in "evolutionist" terms, as a passage from an absence of God, to fetish-totemism (worship of nature, animals and things), to idolatry, to anthropomorphism and finally to an idea of transcendent divinity.** These are, however, forms of belief destined to be surpassed with time, according to an evolution that, begun with magic, would mature in structured religions and **eventually lead to atheism**. Well known is the positivist Auguste Comte's thesis, according to which humanity would pass from the primitive religious state to a metaphysical philosophical stage, to finally move towards a scientific stage, in which knowledge of nature and the human condition would be entrusted only to the sciences. There have been attempts to interpret religiosity in "functional" terms. The diversified ideas of the divinity would accompany the historical-social growth of men, alternating according to their social organisation: from hunting, to agriculture, to pastoralism, up to more organised societies.

These interpretations, which almost always **contain precise preconceptions** (sometimes ideological presumptions) about the nature of man, and therefore about religion itself, **are** **opposed by a phenomenological approach, which seeks to understand when and why religious meaning appears in human history** (M. Eliade, J. Ries). In this case the object of research is **not man as a subject of evolution or social or cultural transformation, but man in as much as he is man**.

##### The appearance of homo religiosus
Paleoanthropology confirms the presence of a religious phenomenology since prehistoric times. Although it is not possible to deduce a coherent picture of the primitive belief systems from the oldest human remains, nor, above all, to deduce the canons of the relationship between human consciousness and the transcendent dimension, i.e. what the sense of God was for the first humans, the human remains that have come down to us show sacred symbolism in burials, artistic representations, ritual forms.3

The first testimonies of some form of belief, manifesting a certain "**religious sense", are provided by the ritual aspects present in the burials of the deceased, which, since about 90,000 years ago,** represent a common practice not only for *Homo sapiens*, but also for other human forms that have then interrupted their evolutionary path, such as the Neanderthal man. However, the presence of a sacred vision of life and nature even before this time cannot be excluded, but the corresponding evidence can only be put into an indirect relationship with a sacred, magical or religious sense, as in the case of the findings of bone deposits, or ordered deposits of skulls and special ways of embellishing or preserving a human skull. The religious and not merely hygienic or social significance of the burial of the corpses of the deceased is revealed by the geometric-spatial arrangement of their bodies, the outfit that accompanies the burials, the red ochre tinctures on the bones, an easy symbolic reference to blood and life. The burials reveal the belief in a life that transcends the earthly dimension, and therefore in a certain transcendent dimension, desired or at least imagined. Already in the Middle Palaeolithic (40000-50000 years ago), the presence of rituals in funerary deposits accompanies *Homo sapiens* in Asia and Europe.

**The first structured *religious concepts* date back to the Upper Paleolithic, about 30,000 years ago. Proof of this are sophisticated ritual practices carried out inside caves, with proto-mythological artistic representations and findings of objects used exclusively for ritual purposes**. During a very long extension of time, up to about 10,000 B.C., the sanctuaries of the caves have hosted objects and paintings that revealed Homo sapiens' deep-rooted conviction about a "cosmic-dualist" vision of nature and life, in which it is not the struggle between opposites or a dialectic logic to be highlighted, but rather an idea of complementarity: male and female, light and darkness, life and death, heaven and earth. In the sacred sphere, animals gain importance, to which highly symbolic roles are attributed, because they are part of human life itself.

Abandoned after the thaw of the last great ice age (about 12,000 years ago) the great caves as sanctuaries of worship, the religious-rite practice does not seem to leave traces or significant developments. Instead, it will be from about 6,000-5,000 B.C. that human history will record the progressive origin of great organised systems of beliefs, first in Central Asia and then in the Mediterranean basin,while other areas of the planet, such as America and Oceania, will continue to preserve more primitive forms of worship.

##### Aspects of the phenomenology of the sacred
In the study of religion and its appearance in man's journey it is appropriate to distinguish three specific areas: the experience of the sacred; the acts proper to religiosity; the conceptualization of the notion of God.

The experience of the sacred, of more archaic origin, appears as something intrinsic to human experience. According to Mircea Eliade it is a fundamental element of the structure of consciousness. Acts of religiosity already imply a social dimension of the sacred vision: the most ancient ones, as previously mentioned, have come to us essentially in the context of ritual burials; they are acts that manifest belief in an afterlife, a belief that could in some cases even include a primitive idea of retribution or punishment. The idea of God, on the other hand, needs more articulated cultural forms in order to be made explicit: it is the Absolute (Supreme Being) on whom the religious sense expresses a fundamental dependence; it is the divine who is called to accompany the essential steps of human life; it is the Cause that presides over the phenomena of nature and dispenses them as it pleases. Philosophical reason subsequently theorises the notion of God and that of some of his attributes: Founding of the world, Providence, Other from man, answer to cosmological and existential questions about the origin and meaning of the whole reality.

Classical thought, which seeks to link philosophical reflection to an even more original experience, approaches the term *religio* by recognizing two etymological roots: one can be traced back to the verb *religere*, which, as opposed to *negligere*, would indicate the act of paying attention, observing, diligently carrying out duty (Cicero); the other is linked to the verb *religare*, which would indicate the link between man and the divinity, the object of reverent adoration (Lactantius, Augustine). Both highlight, respectively, the subjective and objective aspect of religion.

An obligatory reference for classical reflection on religion is the work *De natura deorum* by Marcus Tullius Cicero (106-43 B.C.). He reports that the notion of God - or rather of the gods - was accepted from the earliest antiquity in the human soul because of four factors:4 a) the desire to know future things; b) gratitude and wonder at the beneficial things we receive from nature; c) the presence of disturbing atmospheric phenomena capable of causing feelings of fear and inferiority; d) the admirable order found in the cosmos, particularly in the starry sky. Before Cicero, Aristotle (384-322 B.C.) warned that men had already derived the notion of "god" essentially from two sources, namely, the human soul and the orderly movement of the stars, when Greek philosophers, in later epochs, subjected it to a sort of "critical revision".5

There are clear links between *religiosity and* *man's relationship with nature*. The latter makes man perceive his contingency and fragility, arousing insecurity and feelings of fear, but also feelings of reverence and astonishment; both the aesthetic experience and the experience of the fundamentals/elements,both responsible for raising "ultimate questions", are thus stimulated. In particular, religiosity finds a stable connection with the contemplation of heaven; not only because the uranic/heavenly expressions of worship are among the most archaic, but above all because heaven has the same attributes as transcendence. But there are also clear links between *religiosity and the various phases of human life*, such as birth, entry into adult life, work and subsistence, marriage, old age, death. Finally, there are links between *religion and the ethical-social practices of the human community*, both because what is proposed as ethical law is traced back to a divine origin, and because the relationships established by the laws of the ethical-religious sphere structure social organisation in a decisive way.

As noted above, theology is interested in the religious sense because the latter determines the experience, the categories and the language with which man can express the divine. Sacred Scripture, after all, collects the religious experience of the people of Israel, and it is from this experience that theology elaborates its formulations. It is therefore useful to recall some of the most important categories of the phenomenology of religion.

With the ambit of the *sacrum* we indicate, and in part we conceptualise, something that transcends the human scope; an ambit that is pertinent only to *numen*, that is, to a subject "other than man", to be considered as the cause and source of sacredness itself. The horizon of knowledge and life that belongs properly to numen and the divine is called *mysterium* and represents, in general, a space precluded from man, because it is the expression, precisely, of what is sacred. But there are manifestations of the *sacrum*, both in nature and to a certain extent also in man, which we can indicate with the term "hyerophanies". The presence of the sacred leads man to experience, on a subjective-personal level, a feeling of fear and veneration. He is faced with a *mysterium* that is simultaneously *tremendum* et *fascinans*. If the adjective *tremendum* refers largely to the ethical-remunerative dimension associated with the presence of the sacred and its relationship with man, the adjective *fascinans* refers rather to the aspect of veneration and contemplation that the manifestations of the sacred always imply.

The most foundational religious experience is probably, for the human being, the perception of his own contingency, of his own inferiority and fragility in relation to natural phenomena, whose overpowering force is feared, but whose grandeur is also admired. It thus takes the shape of that sense of dependence that most authors consider to be the basis of every religious experience, the premise of a relationship with the Absolute, on which, in the end, one recognizes oneself dependent. The human being is the protagonist of events of which he cannot be the arbiter, in relation to his own physical life and survival: richness or scarcity of the fruits of the earth, availability of means of subsistence and, above all, the encounter with pain and suffering, with the final survival of death.

It is against the background of these life experiences of contingency and dependence that human beings perceive their individuality, the desire to preserve "their own self" and to survive. To think, however, that the perception of one's own self is entirely originated by the sense of dependence and the commitment to survival would be reductive. In fact, there is the experience of the encounter with the other, the experiences of love and communion, all marked by precise religious and ritual phases,which strengthen one's personal identity in the face of a "you", both in the context of sexual intercourse with regard to procreation, and in the context of parental relations and cooperation with one's fellow human beings. In a more literal way, rereading the "principle of dependence" in the light of a "principle of creation", the fundamental religious experience becomes that of creaturality: one's own self is then perceived before a "You" by which one recognizes oneself as founded, the gift of a personal life whose origin is rooted in a Creator.

It is also on the basis of a relational experience that man's relationship with the idea of a Supreme Being is established. The latter is grasped by common sense and spontaneous philosophy, following a cosmological and/or anthropological route, before being thematized by reflected philosophical thought (Absolute, Unconditional, first Cause, etc.). It goes beyond the material elements, according to a more original ontological foundation than the material elements, as the phenomenology of religion has shown, recognizing the astral and uranic/heavenly cult in general as late derivations. To satisfy the most radical questions, the Supreme Being must be placed in a sphere completely different from the human one, and therefore as "totally Other" and "totally different".

Finally, religiosity can and must be distinguished from magic. If the true religious sense manifests itself as a conscious and reverent recognition of an absolute reality, the sacred and the divine, on which man knows he is existentially dependent, in the idea of magic there is instead the will to make the divine obedient to man and manipulable by him. Religion grasps the sacred and the divine as something unavailable and worships it; magic tends to possess it as a source of knowledge and power, as something to be managed at will.

##### 2.1.2 The theological status of religious experience
When theology reflects on religion and the religious experience, it makes some important epistemological clarifications in a propaedeutic way.6 First, theology shifts attention to the *cause of religion*, thus avoiding confining the deeper question of what religion is to religious phenomenology alone. Why does the human being behave in this way? Can the idea of God arise from a totally subjective experience linked to fear of natural phenomena? Can the sense of dependence arise from the sole experience of fragility and limitation? Here also comes into play the relationship with philosophical thought, which associates the Absolute with a causal role (Necessary Being, Intelligent Cause, Foundation of Being, etc.) that goes well beyond the traits that the Supreme Being of religion would assume if it were the simple result of man's existential expectations or projections. Secondly, theology reminds us that the personal and relational dimension of the human being with God - which concerns the life *of the spirit* - cannot be deduced, interpreted or solved only from the *material traces* that religious experience has produced: burials, objects, artistic representations, social organisation, etc. In order to then qualify the relationship between man and God as a *moral* relationship - that is, one that includes the possibility of merit and sin - theology points out that the human being must have known the true God and have placed himself in relationship with Him, not stopping at incomplete, fragmentary or deceptive images of the divine.

When he speaks of religion, Thomas Aquinas introduces it in reference to the theological notion of God, stating that, in a proper sense, it concerns man's relationship with God: *religio propri importat ordinem hominis ad Deum*.7 Aquinas makes its requirements derive from the statute of natural law, capable of making us recognize a Creator and bind us to Him through appropriate acts of worship, not only internal but also external. Of the religious phenomenon, theology therefore emphasises the *dialogical and personal dimension* of the religious phenomenon, and it is a consequence of this that it cannot be structured only as the place of human aspirations towards the divine, but must also involve the divine's forms of response to these aspirations.

The relationship with God, the *ordo hominis ad Deum*, is not only about the constitutive dependence that human beings have on God, their Creator, but also about the expressions of what human beings ask of God and what they expect from Him. It is in this relational dimension that the fundamental religious attitudes of prayer and sacrifice assume consistency and truth.

Theology highlights that man's religious experience includes, in reality, a real aspiration for redemption, a deep "desire for salvation". Salvation and redemption, however, are theological categories: how can they fit into the study of religion? Religious life is intertwined with some important anthropological situations, of which man recognizes himself as a protagonist and from which he asks to be in some way "redeemed".

The religious man perceives the "problem of evil".He asks the divine to be protected from evil, he wishes to ward off evil, to exorcise it. For this he prays, he offers sacrifices. Evil is captured in its physical-natural dimensions, but also in its moral dimensions, through the perception of guilt and the correspondent of divine judgement. All this translates into expectation and hope of salvation. This is shown, in particular, by two precise existential situations.

The first is that the human being observes in himself an acute gap between the *self-transcendent condition* of his spirit (intellectual activity, will, self-reflection, freedom, desire for eternal life and love) and the *transient*, finite and corruptible condition of his corporal dimension. If, on the one hand, man glimpses the plausibility of the immortality of his spiritual component (a conviction expressed by practically all religious traditions), on the other hand he remains deeply dissatisfied with the fate of the body, that is, ultimately the fate of the whole person, as a unity of the two dimensions, material and spiritual. This uneasiness is greatest in the face of death, but it manifests itself in a broad way in every experience of pain and suffering, both physical and moral. The religious man grasps his limitations and vulnerability as a "deprivation". The second situation that requires healing concerns the dichotomy that man perceives between the good he wishes to do, perceived by his moral conscience, and the evil in which he feels trapped; between altruism, as an aspiration towards which reason would lead him, and selfishness as a measure of his practical work: *video meliora, proboque; deteriora sequor*.8 Religious man knows the experience of guilt and approaches the idea of the Supreme Being as judge and redeeming Lord.

There is, in a profound way, an existential situation to be healed: the human being feels made to love, because he notices that only giving love and receiving love can make him happy, but he finds himself at the same time incapable of giving his aspiration an ethical fulfilment and an eternity in time: his love is imperfect, weak, mortal. He feels the need for a stable and lasting relational relationship, where truth conquers falsehood and love is stronger than hatred and hypocrisy, but he feels incapable of reaching it with his own strength. All this means that the question about the Supreme Being or, in its philosophical formulation, the question about the Absolute, is not only guided by the desire to know the truth about the origin of things and one's own existential condition (why the world? Who am I, where do I come from, where am I going?...), but alsotakes on the character of an *invocation of salvation*. One seeks the answer to the why of a suffering condition that asks to be healed.

Human life in its most essential structure is relational life, openness to a personal dialogue as a place where one gives and receives: religious experience is then directed towards the divine as one who is in *search of a face*. The possible answer to questions about causes and foundations remains insufficient: man asks God also the *meaning* of things, what the meaning of his life is. He asks himself why, unlike other animals, he aspires to truth, goodness and love and why, by virtue of his self-transcendence, he aspires to them in an *infinite* way, even though he recognizes himself incapable of reaching them in fullness. If man asks to be saved from this discomfort, his relational nature implies that the salvation to which he aspires must be able to identify with a personal being, with a subject *capable of love*.

The anthropological notion of religion therefore naturally opens up towards a personal-salvific dimension,leading to a typically theological notion: "the essence of religion does not consist simply in a *situation* of existence or a *state* of consciousness; but in the re*al transcending towards God in the horizon of the problem of salvation*, and therefore in an *affirmed interest in salvation* and in the courage to follow a *path* in the corresponding praxis".9

Theological reflection thus recalls that religion cannot be defined solely on the basis of the experiences of our religious sense or the manifestations of the divine in which we come across. Its definition also implies a salvific praxis that seeks redemption, and as far as possible finds it, *in* and *through* that reality which is theologically called *God*. This is also the reason why the fulfilment of a certain personal praxis and the request for a corresponding ethical behaviour, beyond the cultic activity *sic et simpliciter*, has always played an important role, both in the personal religious sense and in the structured aspects of each religion.

##### 2.1.3 Religion as man's openness to revelation
The notion of the divine, as we have seen, is associated with the category of *mystery*, an area of knowledge and life precluded to man. The Supreme Being, or the philosophical Absolute, are in themselves this mystery. The personal nature of the Absolute - which the religious sense glimpses and desires - tells us that in order to have access to this mystery it is necessary for the Absolute himself to *decide to reveal himself*. Here is rooted the importance that the revelatory fact possesses in every religion. The way in which reason recognizes the existence of the *mysterium* and religion signals it as *sacrum* are aspects of an ascending motion from the human to the divine; the revelatory fact is presented instead as a descending motion of God towards man.10

On closer inspection, the idea of "revelation" is already in some connection with the religious phenomenology outlined above. After all, a pre-experience of revelation are the feelings of hope and expectation that the human being feels in the face of existential situations that ask to be healed. If he expects something from the divine - and for this he prays, invokes, offers sacrifices - it is because he believes the divine is capable of this response. Religious man does not base his attitude (with everything that accompanies it) on a purely subjectivist and psychological perception: it refers to a deeper justification, to an expectation that he knows to be founded.

##### The presence of revelations in the religious phenomenon
The intrusion of the divine into nature and into the various spheres of human existence, that is, every *hierophany*, is in part already a revealing manifestation of the presence of the sacred. A "revelation" is, after all, already present in religious experience itself *as an experience*: the human being "experiences the divine" because the divine comes towards him, or at least man perceives in some way his presence and his action. If this were not so, religious experience would be something totally subjective, exclusively confined within human psychology, a relationship that is only apparent and deceptive, without any openness to Being, to the Foundation, to the Absolute.

The phenomenology of religion places the term "revelation" in continuity with that of hierophany, both indicating the manifestation of something hidden and mysterious. Theologically speaking, it is a communication of God to man, a self-communication to which religion aspires in a more intense (and necessary) way the greater the distance that separates the human from the divine. The phenomenology of religions knows many ways to indicate the type of such communication: God manifests Himself to man through inner experience, through symbols, signs hidden in animals or plants; through natural phenomena and what they mean; through dreams and visions, but also through direct illuminations and revelations. Very frequent is the delivery of the divine will through the word of intermediaries, such as founders, prophets, priests and in general the classes used for cultic activities. The phenomena of divine revelation can be linked to the sacredness of the place or the sacredness of the person.

The phenomenology of many of these signs indicates the need for man to retract himself in order to offer space to the divinity, to be able to prescind from his knowledge and will, to enter into a relationship with the divine knowledge and will. It is to this logic that communication through dreams or through ecstatic visions responds, but also through the use of casting fortunes or experiencing divine judgement (ordeal) on man. In the revelatory fact the initiative can be God's, but also man's, when the latter, in order to "question" God, seeks the signs of the divine and dedicates himself to interpreting them.

The revealed contents are often fixed by means of a written text, of a literary or cultic nature, through which the divine is believed to manifest his will, revealing part of his mystery and thus bridging the distance that separates him from man. It is frequent to trace back to an explicit divine revelation the dictates of a *law* of an ethical-moral order or the prescriptions of cultic practices, containing a set of criteria useful to social life, the effects of which are not only socio-normative but also personal-salvific. The historical memory of the contents revealed in such laws or norms is generally entrusted to the religious transmission of books, received by the community as sacred.

In more philosophically and socially structured religions, the contents revealed mainly concern the communication of a key to interpreting reality, nature, history, something that would be inaccessible to simple reason. In a more general way, such revelations are presented with the character of *gnosis*. Their content does not say anything about the personal life of God (unlike, as we shall see, what happens in biblical revelation), but rather concerns a knowledge about nature and a praxis of life for man, on which his fortune and his "salvation" (salvific gnosis) depend.

As much in primitive natural religiosity as in more structured religions, the divine is not easily available to man, because the divinity is the *jealous guardian of the mystery*. Knowledge of this mystery is, for man, the result of a struggle; to access it he must dare, risk and sometimes resort to stratagems. The divine mystery is never dispensed in a simple and free way: those who know it often use it as a source of power, privilege and profit, thus giving rise to a priestly or prophetic "class".

Although the examination of Jewish-Christian revelation, when evaluated against the background of other religious traditions and natural religiosity in general, will highlight all its specificity and originality, it should come as no surprise that Sacred Scripture, in its language and concepts, also expresses itself with the categories proper to religion and the accompanying revelatory phenomena. In the biblical texts too, God's presence is manifested through extraordinary natural phenomena, especially atmospheric ones; God communicates his will through dreams, visions or by drawing lots; his will is dispensed through the ministry of intermediaries and prophets. The narrative contexts of many biblical theophanies closely follow the canons of the most frequent hierophanies.

##### Revelation and belief recall each other in the phenomenology of the religious fact.
The noetic scope of the signs associated with a divine revelation necessarily refers to a *supra-rational* sphere and therefore requires a certain existential option of the subject. In order to accept what the divinity reveals, it is necessary for the subject to go beyond the limit of his direct experience, it is necessary to give signs a value that goes beyond what they would seem to say, it is necessary *to believe* in a will different from one's own. In this way the role of *religious belief* comes into play, which can be traced back to the genre of faith, here understood in its broadest sense, not as theological faith, but as an option through which one relies on the new knowledge communicated by the divine mystery, in the light of which nature and one's own life are now re-read.

For this reason, revelation and faith are concepts which, on the phenomenological level, certainly refer to each other. Religious belief does not represent the simple emotional acceptance of something that goes beyond reason or that cannot be proven, but implies a behaviour, an existential involvement, a trusting trust in the divine will. Religion therefore always implies a certain faith, in the sense indicated above. For Thomas Aquinas, religion and faith appear to be closely linked, to the point that "every religion, or cult of God, is a manifestation of some form of faith".11 Religion necessarily carries with it a relational dimension between the subject and the divine, between the subject and the members of the religious community, a relationship that implies an existential option of the believer *as a believer*.

In other sections of this volume we will deal with the specificity of the theological faith, by which the believer adheres to the Word of the One and Triune God revealed in Jesus Christ, and also with the specificity of Jewish-Christian Revelation (which we always capitalise) when compared to other revelatory phenomena. Here it is enough to recall that the encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998), and then especially the declaration *Dominus Iesus* (2000), are concerned to distinguish, in the theological discourse, the "theological faith" - personal response/adhesion to the God of Israel and Jesus Christ, Word of the Father - and the "beliefs present in the various religions". Similarly, when referring to non-Christian religions, the Second Vatican Council prefers to speak in the declaration *Nostra aetate* of precepts, doctrines, etc., not using the term "revelation", which in the Council documents always takes on a very definite meaning. However, these are precepts and doctrines, it is added, which "not infrequently reflect a ray of that Truth which enlightens all men" (NA, 2), thus implicitly referring to a Truth given by God to man, which therefore reveals through his light.

A final consideration concerns the relationship between *natural* religions and *revealed* religions. In classical Apologetics, the former referred to those religious phenomenologies in which the divinity would make its presence and will known essentially through the phenomena of nature, while the latter referred to those religious traditions in which there was historical mediations, sacred books or other documentary evidence. In the first case the relationship with the divinity takes on a more subjective and implicit dimension, while in the second this relationship is more objective and explicit, to the point of taking on historical importance.

Since the second half of the twentieth century, the history of religions and the phenomenology of religion have indicated that such a distinction can never take on such clear-cut contours. First, it should be noted that the notion of revelation, as we have seen, is associated with every religious experience as such; moreover, the perception of the divine through nature is itself a form of revelation. In fact, there are no religions where the relationship with the divinity is regulated by purely natural canons: there is almost always a "reflection" on these natural data, an interpretation entrusted to mediators, which is elaborated and transformed into a historical tradition. Moreover, even revealed religions, such as the Jewish-Christian one, recognize a manifestation of God through nature as an indispensable part of their content. Finally, mediators and prophets are present both in religions once called "natural" and in those traditionally referred to as "revealed": on a mere phenomenological level, even the difference between revelations of a public-historical type and private illuminations is sometimes less defined than is thought.

In essence, all religions are based on forms of revelation and belief. They differ, however, in the way in which they justify the involvement required of the subject: because of the action of natural phenomena subject to a greater or lesser dose of interpretation by individuals or the community; because of the authority of mediators or visionaries who are the protagonists of an enlightenment reserved for the few and of the obedience due to them; because of the intervention of the divine into history through the completion of works carried out in front of everyone, etc.

The study of Jewish-Christian Revelation is not hindered by a greater deepening of the phenomenology of religion and that of religious experience. Religion is an integral part of man's life and his way of speaking of God. The Fathers of the Church were not afraid to present Christianity as the true religion, and Jesus Christ as the fulfilment of every religion.

#### 2.2 Introduction to the notion of Revelation
The Jewish-Christian religious tradition delivers to the world the announcement that *God has truly revealed Himself*. Referring to this tradition, we will always refer to the religious experience transmitted to us by the Scriptures and the living faith of the people of Israel, the repository of a history of Promise and Covenant which Christians proclaim to have been fulfilled in the history and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth. The aspiration of salvation that the human religious sense addresses to the Absolute has received a response from the Word of God coming to man. The dialogue between God and man has its fulfilment in the Word made flesh and in his Paschal Mystery: entering into relationship with this *mystery*, man is certain that God is a subject capable of love and that, in Christ, we can contemplate his face. The revelation of the Absolute ceases to remain a possibility and is now *preached as a story of salvation*: a supreme and scandalous story, because until now it has not been heard; a story of death and resurrection, because it is a story of love.

The theological category of Revelation therefore acquires a central role for the Jewish-Christian faith. If from the linguistic-conceptual point of view it is a notion which, as we shall see, certainly has limits and requires careful, never completed hermeneutics, from the religious-salvific point of view it continues to show all its value and therefore justifies its use. Thanks to his self-revelation the personal being can be known, with him one can establish a dialogue and recognize his credibility, from him one receives the invitation to participate in his intimate and personal life. Thanks to the Revelation of the God of Israel in Christ, "in His immense love [He] speaks to men as friends and entertains them, to invite them and admit them to fellowship with Himself" (DV, n. 2).

The theological notion of Revelation certainly takes on the meanings proper to common language and the phenomenology of religion, but it goes beyond them. Above all, it associates with it the salvific dimension, which goes beyond the instructive-conscious aspect typical of common language, and enriches, qualifying it with a profound personalism, the relational aspect already present in religious language.The theological contents of the notion of revelation are, however, the result of reflection on different biblical concepts, which identify a wide and polyvalent semantic area. With it one can indicate, in an overall way, *the free and gratuitous communication of God and his saving mystery to man*. We can recognize some important articulations:

- this communication is also a donation, because in revealing Himself, God offers man something inaccessible to reason alone, that is, the mystery of His will and His intimate life;
- this self-communication and self-donation mainly *take place in and through history*;
- Revelation, communication and the gift of God to man, have their culmination and their interpretative key in the *mystery of the Incarnate Word*;
- God himself is as much the *subject* as the *object of* Revelation; it is in fact the fruit of his free initiative: we are offered his personal mystery, that of a love accomplished and immanent in his *intra-trinitarian* life, and that of a love given in a free economy, *ad extra*, in favour of man;
- In this manifestation of God, the objective content of Revelation and the personal dimension of the Revelator-revealed *are inseparable, as* are, in the human response, the acceptance of the revealed content and the reception of the Revelator.

The notion of Revelation can therefore be accessed through multiple verses and biblical-theological concepts such as: manifestation, communication, gift, love, participation, mystery; but also, word, dialogue, preaching, doctrine, proclamation, witness, gospel. Revelation is, in itself, salvation, truth, light, life.

##### 2.2.1 Modalities and contexts of God's Revelation in the Old Testament
There is no specific term in the First Testament to indicate the theological notion of *Revelation*. The Hebrew verb *galah*, which means "to reveal" or even "to discover", is almost always used in apocalyptic literary texts (announcement of future upsetting events), often in connection with the announcement of the "day of Yahweh", definitive revelation of God and final fulfilment of His justice. The verbs *ra'ah* and *yada'* emphasize the cognitive aspect of revelation, but when they have God as subject they are coloured with relational and salvific connotations.

God's communication to man, His being present and His condescension are expressed by OT in various ways. Let's see the main ones.[12](about:blank)

The OT does not know a direct vision of God, but recognizes his closeness to man through a certain manifestation of his presence, that is, through *theophanies*. The theophanies often involve extraordinary and impressive natural phenomena (fire, thunder, earthquakes, strong wind or other phenomena: cf. *Ex* 3:1-6; *Ex* 19:16-18; *Ex* 24:16-17, etc.), but sometimes even more ordinary and discreet, as when God makes the prophet Elijah feel his presence through a gentle breeze (cf. *1 Kings* 19:11-13). Revelation by theophanies is frequent in *Genesis*, but especially in the *Book of Exodus*(cf. *Ex* 3:14; 19:16-20; 24:16-18; 40:34-38).

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Exodus* 19.16-20

The God of Israel "cannot be seen", nor should any image or representation be made of Him. Among the many biblical passages that speak of God's invisibility, the episode of *Exodus* 33:18-23 is very significant, where Moses asks God to "show him his glory", obtaining as an answer only the possibility of an indirect vision, "from behind" (however, in an atmosphere of prayer, before God Moses speaks "face to face", cf. *Dt* 34:10-11). With the precise intention of avoiding interpretations that could admit direct visions of God, the translation of the LXX of *Exodus* 24:10 - the theophany of Sinai which speaks of God's particular closeness to Moses and the elders of the people - made the original Hebrew meaning "they saw the God of Israel" blurring it into Greek "they saw the place where the God of Israel stood".

The visions through which God reveals are generally *allegorical visions* or through the presence of intermediaries who interpret or preach certain events. As expected, the OT retains the classical references of religious language to know the will of the divinity, such as the use of *dreams* (cf. *Gen* 20:3; *Gen* 28:11-15; 37:5-10; *cc.* 40 and 41; *1 Sam* 28:6). Frequent in revelation to patriarchs (we will find traces of it in the NT, for example in Matthew's gospel), recourse to dreams decreases in later times and is seen with some mistrust by prophets, who will accurately distinguish between dreams sent to authentic prophets and dreams of false prophets (cf. *Jer* 23:25-32; *Is* 28:7-13). Unlike in Egypt and then in the neighbouring countries of Israel, the OT never proposes magic divination techniques, such as hepatoscopy (inspection of the internal organs of animals).

A singular manifestation of the God of Israel is the descent/presence of his *glory* (cf. *Ex* 16:10; *Nm* 14:10), associated with an experience of the numinous, which however does not belong to the genre of "direct vision". The glory of God (eb. *kabod Jahweh*) appears as a cloud covering the tent that Moses had prepared during the Exodus to guard the ark of the Covenant and the tables of the Law. The presence of a pillar of firewhich guides the people in the desert and which stands near the tent (cf. *Ex* 33:10) also has a theophanic significance.

God also manifests Himself through *vicarious figures*, in particular through "*the Angel of Yahweh*", a character who represents Him in an often anthropomorphic form (cf. also *Gen* c. 18 and 32,25-31). In general, the recourse to the Angel and the angels acts itself, in the OT, as the theophany of God's presence and action (cf. *Gen* 16:9; 22:11; *Ex* 14:19; *Jn* 6:11-24; 13:13-21; etc.).[13](about:blank)

Among the modes of revelation employed by the First Testament, there is particular emphasis on the *prophetic ministry* (to be understood in a broader sense than classical prophecy, typical around the time of Babylonian exile), whose task is, to a large extent, to announce the course of history, which Jahweh directs according to his saving plans, and to recall the demands of the Covenant. The archetype of the prophetic ministry remains, however, the story of Moses, within the context of the fundamental experience of God's condescension for his people, that of liberation from the land of Egypt. Thanks to this *saving revelation* Israel understands that God is capable of proclaiming and directing history; of this experience Israel is called to make a constant and reverent memory (cf. *Dt* 4; *Ps* 78:136).

If these are all "ways" through which God reveals Himself, at their base and in their hearts there is the religious experience that the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, communicates with man, manifests to him what He is and what He wants, mainly through His *word*. The God of Israel is essentially *a God who speaks*. The expression "word of God" (Heb. *dabar Yahweh*) is the most common and at the same time the most important and solemn way in which Scripture describes the communication and manifestation of God to mankind. The religious man, the believer, the prophet, reacts to the perception of the divine presence by declaring: "Speak, Yahweh, for your servant hears you. Example of the *1 Sam 3.1-10* episode. Through His word, God progressively introduces man into the sphere of his intimacy, into self-knowledge, to the supreme gift, now in the economy of the NT, of His *Word made flesh*. Unlike Greek thought, which is more attentive to knowledge through the vision and conceptualization of the idea, for Israel the main way to know is to listen to the word: starting from it, everything else will be a *recognition.*[14](about:blank)[[1]](#footnote-0)

*[Embedded Image]*
 *1Samuel* 3.1-10

Even in the case of theophanies and prophetic visions, the most important thing is not so much to *see* God as to *hear His word*(cf. *Ex* 16:10-11). The relationship between the way of revelation in the word and that which is given for example in dreams, in the theophanies themselves and in the many other signs of divine presence, indicates that all the other modes of revelation *point towards listening to the word,*for example by introducing it with solemnity, as shown by the continuation of the Sinaitic theophany of *Exodus* 19,16-20.

With regard to the relationship between revelation and religious experience, as much in the solemn theophany of Exodus as in other biblical episodes, we cannot exclude that the literary recourse to the mention of external signs typical of religious language - such as smoke, fire, earthquake, trumpets sounding and proclaiming, etc. - may be used in a literary way - expresses the intention of the sacred author to reproduce, in those to whom he was directed, the feelings and experience of the protagonist of divine revelation in approaching the God of Israel, with the aim of introducing attentive listening to His word. The people of Israel do not recognize God's will by interpreting the natural signs that accompany His manifestations (thunder, storm, signs in heaven, etc.), but by listening to what He says.

The economy of the word dominates all the theology of the revelation of OT, to become then, in the NT, the saving economy of the incarnate word. The expression *dabar Yahweh* appears in the First Testament about 250 times. The word of God is addressed to the patriarchs, Abraham, Moses, the prophets. Its meaning is richer than what the term "word" takes on in modern languages: it includes both a conceptual and a dynamic aspect. The word of God does not only communicate a content, nor does it only inform: it is always also an effective word that works what it says. It's a powerful, irresistible, creative, transforming word. The well-known passage of *Isaiah* 55:10-11, which also admits a Christological reading in the light of the mission of the Incarnate Word, is instructive in this regard.

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Isaiah* 55,10-11

The word of God creates, guides history, performs wonders, saves. It also ensures the provident unfolding of the seasons and other natural events useful to human life, which are also part of the same saving economy. It is a demanding word that demands attention and asks for adherence, a word that moves man to action and gives him the strength to do what he asks. That's a *promising* word. It is a *faithful* word, on which both the laws of the cosmos and the history of the chosen people depend (cf. *Jer* 31:35-37; *Ps* 136). Finally, it is a word not only addressed to man, but also *entrusted to man to* transmit and live it.

The word of God is manifested through specific *actions*. It is a word that creates (creative word), calls, blesses and promises; it offers a saving covenant (word of the Covenant), announces and judges (prophecy), resounds in the heart of man and makes him reflect (sapiential word), prays together with man and on the mouth of man.

The patristic tradition, within the framework of the propaedeutic and pedagogical understanding of salvation history, has recognized in Revelation the historical succession of different *stages*: a primitive revelation handed down to the ancestors; a patriarchal revelation, centred above all around the world vocation addressed to Abraham; a mosaic revelation, characterised mainly by the power of a word that frees from slavery and delivers the word of the law; a prophetic revelation, in which the divine word continues to keep hope alive in its promises; a wise revelation, in which existential reflection and prayer become the place of a word that expresses the conversation between God and man.

##### 2.2.2 Revelation in the New Testament
At the heart of the concept of revelation present in the NT, in continuity with what the OT had affirmed on the word of Yahweh, there is now the awareness that "God, who had already spoken in ancient times many times and in different ways to the fathers through the prophets, has recently spoken to us through the Son, who has been heir to all things and through whom he has also made the world" (*Heb* 1:1-2). The concept of Revelation in the NT appears *all marked by the economy of the Word made flesh,* as the supreme and most perfect communication of God to man, which has in itself the characteristics of condescension, of donation, of salvific encounter. In an economy of revelation centred on the Incarnation of the Word, both the objective-contained and the subjective-personal aspects are brought together: Jesus Christ reveals not only through his teachings and his doctrine, but also through the mystery of his own person.

The terms that in the NT appear most involved in the idea of "Revelation" are still those connected with the notion of "word": preaching and preaching; teaching, proclaiming, proclaiming the Gospel; witnessing the word, welcoming it, living it.[15](about:blank)

##### Synoptics
The verbs that describe the revelatory action of Jesus Christ are above all "to preach" (gr. κηρύσσειν) and "to teach" (gr. διδάσκειν), which together with the activity of healing represent the most important aspect of Jesus' action; it should not be forgotten that the action of "healing" (gr. θεράπειν) also has an important revelatory value. In the Synoptic Gospels Jesus of Nazareth reveals the word of God as Master/Teacher who preaches and Doctor who teaches, but there is no lack of passages in which He reveals Himself as Son sent by the Father: "No one knows the Father except the Son and the one to whom the Son wishes to reveal Him (gr. ἀποκαλύπτειν)". (*Mt* 11,27). He reveals something of his true identity by declaring himself "Lord of the Sabbath"(cf. *Mt* 12:8) and attributing himself the power to carry out the Law (cf. *Mt* 5:17). Jesus Christ is the definitive Messiah in whom the promise made to Abraham and his descendants is fulfilled (cf. *Lk* 4:14-21) and a new and eternal covenant is ratified (cf. *Lk* 22:20).

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Luke* 4,14-21

Christ, with his earthly event, "reveals" the coming of God's kingdom and what must be done to enter it; he "makes manifest" the hearts, the true feelings of man, revealing to the lost man what he must do to save himself; Jesus of Nazareth "reveals" that the opportune moment of conversion, of orientation to God with all one's heart, has come. This is the "moment": the good news is proclaimed to the poor (cf. *Mt* 11:5) and the announced Messiah exercises his ministry of peace and reconciliation (cf. *Lk* 4:14-21). Through his teaching in parables and their explanation, he allows the listener to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven (cf. *Mt* 13:10-12; *Lk* 8:9-10). The authority of his word is in continuity with that of God (cf. *Mt* 7:28-29; *Mk* 1:22; *Mt* 24:35), his doctrine is in authoritative continuity with the divine law given to Moses (cf. *Mt* 5:17-48).

Therefore, according to the synoptics, Christ is revelatory and reveals "as he proclaims the good news (gr. εὐαγγέλιον) of the kingdom of heaven and teaches with authority the word of God. Ultimately he reveals why he is the Son who knows the intimate life of the Father. The essential content of revelation is the salvation offered to men in the image of the kingdom of God announced and established by Christ [...]. The times are fulfilled: in Jesus Christ the Kingdom of God is present and active. Christ is both the one who proclaims the Kingdom and the one in whom the Kingdom is fulfilled".[16](about:blank)

##### Acts of the Apostles
This NT book insists on the notion of *testimony* (gr. μαρτύριον) and *preaching* (gr. κήρυγμα). In the narrative which the *Acts* make of the Ascension, the mandate to teach entrusted to the Apostles reported by the Synoptics is enriched by the following specification: "You will be my witnesses in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria and to the ends of the earth" (*Acts* 1:8). In a special way they are witnesses to the Resurrection of Jesus. The Apostles have the task of making manifest to them what Jesus Christ revealed to them *by publicly proclaiming the Good News* through preaching (cf. *Acts* 8:5; 8:35; 10:42; cf. also *Mt* 10:27).

The object of this teaching, in the light of the risen Christ, is all that concerns Jesus Christ, his reality as promised Savior and Messiah, who died for our sins, rose again and sat at the right hand of the Father. It also announces the economy of the Kingdom of God which he established (baptism, remission of sins, sacramental action in general), the historical realisation of which he entrusted to the Church. That of the apostles-witnesses is a *service to the word* (cf. *Acts* 6:4). The proclamation of the Gospel is accompanied and confirmed by saving signs (cf. *Acts* 4:30; 5:16; 8:6-8) and, above all, by the constant action of the Spirit.

##### The Pauline corpus
The concept of Revelation in Saint Paul moves along two main lines:

- the proclamation of Jesus Christ as the revelation of the true meaning and sense of the mosaic revelation, previously delivered to Israel;
- the development of a theological reflection around the fundamental Gospel-Mystery (gr. εὐαγγέλιον - μυστήριον) binary: the Gospel is the revelation of the Mystery of the Father in Christ.

The Mosaic Law was seen by Israel under a veil, symbolised by the veil that covered the face of Moses during the pilgrimage of Exodus. It is Christ who has *revealed* the will of God and the meaning in which the Mosaic Law was to be lived, that is, according to the Spirit (cf. *2 Cor* 3:12-18). There is therefore a *dynamic* of divine revelation, which moves from the economy of OT to that of NT. God, calling Paul, "revealed Christ to him" (*Gal* 1:16) and with him everything else. The definitive revelation that Israel awaited and expressed with the apocalyptic genres is now revealed as waiting for the definitive manifestation of Jesus (cf. *2Ts* 1:7; *1Cor* 1:7).

*[Embedded Image]*
 *2 Corinthians* 3.12-18

Although he has been the protagonist of a mystical experience reported many times, Paul does not attribute to this experience the primary meaning of a revelation: the "revelation" par excellence remains Christ and his mystery. Paul's experience on the road to Damascus is revealing because it allowed him to come to the knowledge of Christ. What leads to faith is not a vision or revelation in the psychological sense, but preaching, a word that reveals and transforms. The Gospel of Paul is the proclamation of the *mystery of Christ* (cf. *Rom* 16:25; *Col* 1:25-26; *Eph* 1:9-13; *Eph* 3:5-6).

Paul wants to draw attention to the existence of a hidden divine mystery, now proclaimed precisely by "the message of Jesus Christ, according to the revelation of the mystery which has been silent for everlasting centuries, but now revealed [...] by order of the eternal God to all nations" (*Rom* 16:25-26). The *content of* this message, of which Paul feels a minister among the Gentiles (cf. *Eph* 3:9-10; *Col* 1:27), can be summed up by saying that God the Father "has made known to us *the mystery of his will ...* the plan, that is, to recapitulate all things in Christ (ἀνακεφαλαιώσασθαι), those of Heaven and those of the earth" (*Eph* 1:9-10). Explicit developments of this divine plan are the solemn Christological hymn of the *Letter to the Colossians* (cf. *Col* 1:13-20), where Paul still describes it as a "mystery hidden for centuries and generations, but now manifested to his saints" (*Col 1:*26), and the similar hymn of the *Letter to the Ephesians* (cf. *Eph* 1:3-12).

It is part of this plan to make in Christ of all peoples one people (cf. *Col* 3:9-11; *Eph* 2:16-18) and of the Church one Body (cf. *Col* 1:18; *Eph* 1:22-23), because all have been elected in the Son and called to conform to his image (cf. *Col* 1:28; *Eph* 1:4-5; cf. *Rom* 8:29); Paul feels responsible for making everyone understand the greatness and sublimity of this call (cf. *Eph* 1:18-19 and 3:17-19). It is a *salvific*, gratuitous plan, the sole fruit of the Father's condescending benevolence, to which the Apostle's thanks and praise is directed (cf. *Col* 1:12; *Eph* 1:3).

At the centre of this mystery is therefore Christ himself, the role he played in creation (cf. *Eph 1:*4-5; *Col 1:*16-17; cf. also *Heb 1:*3), in the redemption brought about through his blood (cf. *Eph 1:*7; *Col 1:*14) and in the new creation which this entails (cf. *Eph 1:*10-11; *Col 1:*20). Precisely because Christ Jesus himself is "revelation", that mystery of salvation is not only revealed and fulfilled *through Christ,* but Christ himself, in his person, *is this mystery* (*Col* 2:2), He "in whom are hidden all the treasures of wisdom and science" (*Col* 2:3). This wisdom is the logic of the cross, a logic dictated by the spirit and therefore exceeding the reason for the letter and purely human wisdom (cf. *1 Cor* 1:18-25).

It also belongs to the *Pauline corpus* the idea that what has been revealed to us by God in Christ, once delivered to the apostles, constitutes a deposit (gr. παραθήκη), a content intended for faithful transmission(gr. παράδοσις) so that it can be lived in its entirety. The Apostle's exhortations to Timothy are well known so that with the help of the Spirit he might guard the deposit of truth and doctrine entrusted to him (cf. *1 Tim* 6:20; *2 Tim* 1:12-14). Moreover, Paul exhorts the Church to preserve the traditions he has transmitted (cf. *1 Cor* 11:2), both those learned by word and those known in writing (cf. *2Ts* 2:15), avoiding those who detach themselves from them and fleeing false prophets who preach a doctrine other than the Gospel (cf. *Gal* 1:9-12; *2Ts* 3:6).

##### The Johannine corpus
With a Semitic language and an expositive structure that distances the divine Logos from the characters of the Greek Logos, to place it in continuity with the characters of the *Dabar Yahweh,* the prologue of the Fourth Gospel constitutes a solemn summary of the Johannine conception of revelation.

The Son is the "revelator of the Father", the life and salvation brought by the Son "reveal the plan of the Father". "The Word (λόγος) became flesh and came to dwell among us [...] - God no one has ever seen him: the only Son, who is in the bosom of the Father, he has revealed him" (*Jn* 1:14.18). The radical nature of this revelatory action is also found in the original Johannine use of the formulation "I Am" (Εγὼ ἐιμί), reported as many as four times by the Evangelist (cf. *Jn* 8:24; 8:28; 8:58; *Jn* 13:19) and which expresses the solemn claim of the Revelator to place himself in visible continuity with the invisible presence of God in the midst of his people.

The revelation brought about by Christ consists above all in the testimony which the Son gives to the Father. Only the Son knows the Father because he has been sent by him (cf. *Jn* 6:46; 7:29; 8:55; 16:27; 17:8). He knows the Father as the Father knows him, because he and the Father are one (cf. *Jn* 10:15.30; 17:21.23). Christ reveals a fount of knowledge, that which he has from God himself: "We speak of what we know and testify to what we have seen" (*Jn* 3:11); "I say what I have seen from my Father" (*Jn* 8:38); "He who sent me is true, and what I have learned from him I tell the world" (*Jn* 8:26.40). The Son reveals the love that the Father has for the world (cf. *Jn* 3:14-17) and dialogues with Him within a plan of salvation and communion that involves the whole human race (cf. *Jn* 17). In John, Jesus reveals the intimate life of a God in three Persons, the gift of the Spirit, and makes all this known to the apostles as friends (cf. *Jn* 15:15).

In the language of John**,** the words of the Greek language that express the revelatory character of Jesus and his doctrine are word (λόγος), life (ζωή), truth (ἀλήθεια), light (φῶς), glory (δόξα). All these terms are *substantiated in the person of Christ*: He is truth, life, light, glory and the word of the Father. Christ is the witness par excellence: in the mystery of his person, he bears witness to the truth, to the light, to the word that leads to eternal life. In John a singular *epistemology is* taking shape: to believe is also to know, to know as believers: a close link between faith and knowledge (cf. *Jn* 6:69). Faith is the beginning of knowledge, knowledge is the perfection of faith.

In John the theme of welcoming revelation is also present with particular force, indicated by the frequent use of the terms "listen" (58 times) and "believe" (98 times)**.** This is also confirmed by the rather dramatic tone that accompanies the exposition of his prologue: "the world was made through him, yet the world did not recognize him. He came among his own, and his own did not receive him" (*Jn* 1:10-11). The struggle between light and darkness, between the truth of the sent word and the lie of sin that rejects it, is a constant in the *body* of John.

Saint John himself seems to place himself in the flow of the testimony that the Son gives to the Father. He testifies that he has heard, seen, touched the Word of life (cf. *1 Jn* 1:1-4); he is the eyewitness to the supreme event of the Cross (cf. *Jn* 19:35-37); he testifies that the Father sent his Son as the Saviour of the world (cf. *1Jn* 4:14).

##### The Book of Revelation
The book that closes the NT is entitled "Revelation", inspired by his opening words: "Revelation of Jesus Christ (᾿Αποκάλυψις ᾿Ιησοῦ Χριστοῦ) which God gave him to make known to his servants the things that must soon happen" (*Rev* 1:1). In it we find categories and modes of revelation in continuity with the prophetic and apocalyptic language already present in the OT: visions of great narrative effect, interpretive angels, numerical symbolism. However, the scenario is dominated by the great figure of Christ the Redeemer and Judge, Alpha and Omega, the only one able to open the seals of the book of history and, therefore, the only one able to reveal in a definitive and complete way.

In revealing "the last things", the Lamb sacrificed, the Christ with the signs of his passion, presents himself as the Lord of a cosmic history which he directs according to his justice and his will. The book of *Revelation* has had a great influence in the "theology of history" of all times contributing to the understanding of revelation as the *history of salvation*: what in the mosaic revelation was essentially memory and expectation, and in that prophetic interpretation of present and future events, in the Christian *Revelation* becomes definitive fulfilment in the *escathon* of the heavenly Jerusalem.

##### Inner revelation and action of the Spirit
Finally, there is a further specification of the action of revelation that runs through all the books of NT: there is also an "inner revelation". After Peter's solemn Christological confession at Caesarea Philippi, Jesus himself says of Peter "Blessed are you, Simon the son of Jonah, for neither flesh nor blood have revealed it to you, but my Father in heaven" (*Mt* 16:17). And again, Jesus in the Gospel of John: "That is why I have told you that no one can come to me unless he is granted by my Father" (*Jn* 6:65; cf. 6:43-44). We speak of a revelation granted to the little ones (cf. *Mt* 11:25). Above all, there are various contexts in which it is understood that the word preached externally is understood and made its own through interior enlightenment, an action of the Spirit, a gift of God (cf. *Acts* 16:14; *2 Cor* 4:6).

In sacred Scripture there is a recognized action of the Holy Spirit in the hearts and hearts of believers, as well as a certain association between Spirit and word. This in a certain way establishes a correspondence between the Holy Spirit and inner revelation. In the biblical language of OT, when we speak of the word of God which is directed to men, it frequently takes on the characteristics of breath, of the Spirit of God: God who speaks, acts, carries out his works with the power of his Spirit.

With the notion of "inner revelation" we do not want to indicate so much the communication of a private content as the gift of a disposition that allows us to welcome the word and recognize it as meaningful. If in the objective order revelation as preaching has a certain priority over interior revelation, in the subjective order it is the latter that has priority so that the word announced finds a well-disposed heart (cf. *Mt* 13:18-23). Although they do not constitute two different revelations, but two moments or dimensions of the one divine revelation, exterior and interior revelation can occur in separate moments: one can listen to the word without immediately welcoming it interiorly, or possess the appropriate dispositions to welcome what one does not yet know.

The word heard outwardly corresponds to the inner action of the Spirit, the gift of the Gospel is accompanied by the gift of grace. The gift of grace is indeed a gift of the Spirit, but also the presence of Christ. Referring to Christ and his presence in us through faith, Saint Augustine asks: "If he who is in my interiority does not reveal the truth to me, what can I say, how can I pronounce myself?".[17](about:blank)

It also belongs to the genre of inner revelation and to the action of the Spirit the reference to the presence of "revelations" in the nascent Church. The Church of the NT presents itself under the gradual revelatory action of the Spirit: it has its prophets and receives revelations (cf. *1 Cor* 14:29-33). Yet, whatever these terms mean, everything takes place in order, respecting authority and a tradition already received.

##### 2.2.3 Revelation in the Fathers of the Church
The Fathers of the Church do not propose to elaborate a theological reflection on the concept of Revelation.[18](about:blank) The latter is scarcely used in the Greek environment, also because of its possible Gnostic value: Tertullian will be the first to use the Latin terms *revelare* and *revelatio* in a sensitive way. Jerome will be the first to make it clear that Christians have received the term αποκαλυψις from Sacred Scripture and not from Greek writers. The term "Gospel" is used in a broad sense to indicate the totality of the revelation given in Christ. The categories of patristic thought are generally the same as in Scripture: the historical character of God's communication to men, the universal character of salvation, the centrality of Christin this saving economy, the normative value of the teachings collected by the apostles and evangelists. Insights into the logic and contents of the revealed Word appear when the Fathers propose to fight a particular heresy.

The main themes about the nature and implications of Revelation are, in the works of the Fathers, the following.

##### The continuity between the two covenants of OT and NT
The *novelty* of the Gospel does not mean a break with the revelation made to the Patriarchs and the prophets, but leads that word/promise to fullness, giving it a definitive fulfilment**.** The divine plan of salvation, continuity and articulation between the two Testaments are presented using the categories of "pedagogy"(Fathers Apologists, Clement of Alexandria) and "economy". (Irenaeus, Origen). The relationship between the First and the New Testament is seen by some Fathers as a passage from partiality to fullness, from enigma to solution, from darkness to light, but above all *from promise to fulfilment*. Saint Basil, Saint Gregory of Nyssa, Saint Augustine will write about the divine pedagogy of Revelation.

The full correspondence between the two Testaments was denied both by those who underestimated the Gospel novelty (Jewish heresies) and by those who exalted it by making the God of Jesus Christ a different God from the one revealed in the history of the people of Israel (Marcion). In the first case the patristic production focuses on the exegesis of OT (e.g. Justin, *Dialogo con Trifone*; Origen, *Contra Celsum*), in the second one it is directed to strongly reject any opposition between a Creator God and a Redeeming God, between Logos and salvation (*Irenaeus*, *Adversus haereses*).

The continuity/correspondence between the two Testaments can be well summarised by the well-known Augustinian expression "Novum in Vetere latet, Vetus in Novo patet", taken up by the Council Constitution *Dei Verbum* (n. 16).

##### The relationship between philosophical thought and Christian message
With a cultural operation that will mark the whole history of the West, a good number of Fathers of the Church and Christian authors will show the continuity existing between the Gospel and philosophy, to the point of proposing it as the *true* philosophy(Justin, Clement of Alexandria, Lactantius, Meliton of Sardis). The God revealed in Jesus Christ is the same God manifested in nature,whose recognizability was available in a common itinerary to both classical Greek thought and stoic thought. Philosophy is then assigned the role of a "preparation for the Gospel"(Eusebius of Caesarea), almost an alliance established with the pagans on the model of that offered to the chosen people (Justin; Clement of Alexandria). Indeed, Christianity is the true philosophy, the true knowledge, because it is also the *true* religion (Tertullian, Lactantius, Augustine). It should not be forgotten that, for the first time, the category of "truth" is thus introduced into a discourse on religion, until then dominated by the categories of the sacred and the profane.[19](about:blank) The only judgments that classical culture knew about the truth or falsehood of the gods belong**ed** to a discourse formulated by philosophy, not religion, and were proposed within the dialectical confrontation between *physic theology* and *mythical theology*.

Christianity can thus address all cultures, assuming and enlightening what was implicitly part of Christian truth in them.This is the itinerary followed by Justin, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Lactantius, Eusebius of Caesarea. In this reflection, which developed especially in the Greek cultural environment, as well as in the fight against Gnosis, Revelation is seen primarily, but not exclusively, under the aspect of *knowledge***.** Its historical character and salvific-existential dimension will be addressed at a later date.

##### Centrality of Christ on the historical-salvific level
The ineffability of the Old Testament God is put by the fathers in relation to the visibility of the Word made flesh, obtaining from it the great revelatory scope. The mystery of God appearing in the flesh is a *condescending mystery of piety*. God continues to be in Himself an inaccessible mystery, yet everything we really know about this mystery comes to us only from Christ. Only Christ is "the true revelator and the true pedagogue". The Fathers thus responded to the rationalism of Eunomius, according to whom the divine essence, now revealed in the NT, presented no mystery, and to that of the Gnostic Docetists, who, denying the true humanity of Christ, also compromised its meaning as a revelation of what belongs to the life of God.

All the saving works of God can be read as works of Christ: everything from the Patriarchs to the prophets, and even before that the whole creation, speaks of Him and points towards Him.In the mystery of the Incarnate Word are contained the ultimate and definitive answers on the meaning of the world and of man, according to a perspective already dear to Irenaeus and Athanasius. The Incarnation constitutes the full and definitive *salvific mediation*,the greatest that history can know.

The fundamental role of the Incarnation not only concerns the hermeneutical aspect, for the purposes of understanding the mystery of God, but involves the whole of history, beginning with the personal history of each one: Jesus Christ is in fact the fulfilment of the moral life and reveals all the dignity of the human being. In fact, those who recognized the Logos and lived according to reason were already worshippers of Christ, before the Christians (Justin). However, it is the theme of the fulfilment of prophecies that provides the Fathers of the Church with the main context in which to present Jesus Christ as the centre of Revelation (Origen, Eusebius of Caesarea). And Jesus Christ also becomes the centre of the credibility of the word, because it is to his passion, death and resurrection that the Fathers suggest we look.

##### Revelation also involves the transmission of a deposit
The Fathers of the Church do not conceive Revelation as a set of separate knowledge and information, but as "a whole" received from God. This means that Revelation must be defended, correctly interpreted, preserved in all its multiple implications and transmitted intact to successive generations. It therefore concerns a *content*, a deposit, although the latter is closely linked to a living testimony. Criterion of fidelity to this content is the teaching received from the Apostles. The College of Twelve and its teaching are increasingly seen as *regula fidei* (Irenaeus of Lyons), fidelity to their Gospel as the responsibility of a transmission involving the whole Church. The call to an apostolic tradition and to a *public* reading and interpretation of Scripture takes on importance from an anti gnostic point of view (Irenaeus). The rule of faith "the Churches received it from the apostles, the apostles from Christ, Christ from God" (Tertullian).

Thus a well-known text by Saint Vincent of Lerins (4th century) on the immutability of the content of the deposit received: "What is the deposit? It is what is believed by you, not what you have found; what you have received, not what you have devised; not the fruit of wit, but of doctrine, not private appropriation, but public tradition [...] of which you are not the author, but you must be the guardian".[20](about:blank) Opposition to the idea of new revelations after the apostolic epoch was a constant in the teachings of the Fathers against Gnostic heresies.

At the same time it should not be forgotten that Revelation, knowledge of the message of salvation, is not seen as something crystallised or lifeless. If proximity to the event of Jesus Christ imposes normative criteria for the transmission of the deposit, the living presence of the Spirit throughout history makes the life of the Church a *continuous revelation*, that is, a divine word constantly addressed to mankind. The attention paid to the idea of transmitting a repository does not imply that, for the Fathers, Revelation is identified with Scripture, precisely because of the surplus due to the role of the Spirit, which develops and makes alive what He himself inspired in the written text. In Augustine's thought, as in that of other authors, interior revelation, which has as its protagonists both the Son and the Spirit, assumes a decisive importance.

##### 2.2.4 Understanding Divine Revelation in Medieval Theology
With mediaeval theology begins a systematic reflection on certain aspects of Revelation, even without calling it such. This is treated with preferably philosophical rather than historical-salvific categories. Among the main themes that attract the attention of theologians are: **t**he relationship between what we know through revelation and what reason alone could know by its own forces; the ways in which the charisms of inspiration and prophecy act; the stages of divine condescension and pedagogy towards man. Also of interest is the study of the relationship between Revelation and the Church.

##### Revelation and Scripture
While in Patristic times the contemporary notion of Revelation was closer to the notion of Tradition, in mediaeval times it tended to identify with the notion of Holy Scripture. The mediaeval Masters insist that only (or at least mainly) from Scripture do we receive the communication that God makes of Himself. Scripture thus becomes the *regula fidei* of theological discourse, although always read in the Church and with the Fathers. It is the Apostles who have received the divine word for "revelation": it is the Church's task to expose it. Revelation thus loses part of its dynamic aspect and the emphasis shifts to the objective content of the Word: when some subjective aspect is discussed, it almost exclusively concerns the prophetic charism and, in general, the theme of inspiration. It should also be considered that all the culture of the time revolved around the Bible and that, in the religious instruction of Christians, there was now an almost total identification between Scripture and knowledge.

##### Revelation and reason
With the entry of Aristotelian philosophy into the first universities that emerged from the diocesan episcopal schools, the theme of the relationship between philosophical knowledge and theological knowledge of God, common to the Fathers but no longer developed in the early Middle Ages, becomes topical again. It is generally the whole issue of the role of reason that comes to the fore. Between Revelation and rationality a certain gnoseological boundary ends up being established: Revelation is above all that set of knowledge inaccessible to reason.

It is not a question of a separation between faith and reason, but rather of an impoverishment of the concept of Revelation, due to the fact that, when we speak of Revelation, the gnoseological problem clearly prevails over all the others. However, the relationship between faith and reason remains harmonious: there are reasons which faith possesses in the face of philosophical thought and there is a role for reason within faith. Anselm, Bonaventure and Thomas teach and live a profound unity of life between knowledge and prayer, between the work of intelligence and affection of the heart.

##### Revelation and inner experience
Revelation, in fact, also emphasises the inner dimension and spiritual experience, to the point of recognizing in prayer and love the place of a true self-communication of God.Well rooted in the monastic tradition, which takes up the patristic heritage, explicit in Saint Bernard and Saint Bonaventure, it is not foreign to the thought of Saint Thomas. For Bernard of Clairvaux the way in which God reveals Himself is that of a spiritual experience, to which is subject both the mediator to whom the Word to be transmitted is delivered (apostle, prophet, hagiographer) and the one who receives it; and this also regulates the truth of our relationship with Scripture, whose effective and correct reading is possible only through an action of the Spirit and according to a life lived in the Spirit.[21](about:blank) The patristic and anti heretical expression that Scripture could be understood "only in the Spirit in which it was written", the first reference to which was its necessary reading *in the Church* - the seat of the Spirit because it is the seat of apostolic continuity - now makes more explicit a further content: the Holy Spirit is the cause, in those who proclaim and those who listen, of a unifying spiritual experience. God reveals not only through signs and words perceived by the external senses, but also through signs grasped by the internal senses and through a word which is communicated to the human spirit.[22](about:blank) Faith is acceptance both of the word spoken outwardly and of the word heard in the depths of the soul; indeed, faith is above all interior listening, because the action of the preacher is vain if not accompanied by the enlightenment of the inner Master.[23](about:blank)

##### Revelation, prophecy and inspiration
The main contexts in which the mediaevals use the term "revelation" remain, however, prophecy and inspiration. To indicate the objective dimension of the revealed content, expressions such as *Gospel doctrine* and *saving truth* are used. For Bonaventure, for example, the terms *revelation*, inspiration and illumination are almost synonymous, in a way not far from what Thomas himself intended, making them converge in the expression *lumen revelationis*. Christ's teaching is seen as an illumination for all humanity. Medieval Masters are attracted by the supernatural dynamic with which God communicated His word to the prophets and the Spirit inspired the hagiographers. Of such divine-human operations they strive to offer a convincing systematics.

Thomas Aquinas interprets prophetic knowledge as a divine light that allows the prophet to formulate a deeper judgement on the reality of things. The charism of hagiographers' inspiration is considered inferior to the prophetic one, because hagiographers were not always qualified to know the full scope and implications of what they wrote, but only to transmit a divinely inspired content.

##### Some cues from the doctrine of Thomas Aquinas on Revelation
The Angelic doctor does not develop a specific treatise on Revelation. His main beliefs about what we today would consider the object of a theology of Revelation can be summarised as follows.[24](about:blank)

The nature of revelation is presented since the opening of the *Summa Theologiae in* close connection with the purpose of man. Since the end and salvation of man is participation in the very intimate life of God - something absolutely superior to the forces of human nature - it was necessary that the means of knowing and achieving this end, divine Revelation, should come from a divine initiative and therefore be absolutely free (cf. S.Th. I, q. 1. a. 1, resp.). The Angelic specifies that the revelation of certain truths of a natural kind, although not necessary for the purposes of their direct knowledge, because they can be reached by reason without the light of faith, becomes in fact *morally* necessary if we consider the state of men after the fall of original sin. The truths that can be known naturally are revealed by God in order to make them known to all, in a speedy and unmixed way:

Even on what around God man can investigate with reason, it was necessary for him to be trained by divine revelation, for a rational knowledge of God would not be accessible except to a few, after a long time and not without error; yet on the knowledge of these truths depends all man's salvation, which is placed in God. Therefore, in order to provide for the salvation of men in a more convenient and more certain way, it was necessary that they be instructed about divine realities by divine revelation (S.Th. I, q. 1, a. 1, resp.; cf. also C.G. I, c. 4; IV, c. 1).

The theme of "revelation", as already observed, will receive its main theological development in the study of the prophetic charism (cf. e.g. S.Th. II-II, qq. 171-174; C.G. III, c. 154; *De Veritate*, qq. 12). However, the "revealed truth" is first and foremost that contained in the teaching of Jesus Christ. The prophets and apostles were the direct recipients of the revelation. To us revelation comes through the mediation of the Church, that is, through preaching (cf. S.Th. II-II, q. 6, a. 1; q. 1, a. 10, ad 1um). Basically, the "revelation" is the revelation given by God to the apostles and prophets, who have placed it in writing in Sacred Scripture, which is the foundation of our faith (cf. S.Th. I, q. 1, a. 8, ad 2um).

Revelation is understood by St. Thomas above all (though not exclusively) as knowledge. The cognitive action corresponding to it does not fall within the natural faculties or *habitus of* man, but is compared to a *lumen,* for which, however, different stages, or as many degrees of knowledge of God, are possible. Faith and Revelation are ordered to the vision of God in the beatific life. To the *lumen fidei* necessary to know and welcome Revelation on the *way*, will correspond a *lumen gloriae* necessary to enjoy the vision of God at *home*. There is in man a threefold degree of knowledge of God: in the first, one rises up to God through created things; in the second, God descends to man with the gift of Revelation; in the third, the blessed are raised up to know perfectly what had been the object of revelation, to a degree called "perfection of divine revelation" (S.Th. II-II, q. 171, a. 4, ad 2um).

In man there are three types of knowledge of divine things. The first is the one by which, through the natural light of reason, one arrives at the knowledge of God through creatures. The second is the one in which divine truth, which transcends the human intellect, descends in us in the form of revelation, but not of visual order, but of discursive order so that we can believe it. The third type is that in which the human mind will be elevated to see things revealed perfectly (C.G. IV, c. 1).

Therefore, in an active sense, *revelation* is an action of God (*lumen revelationis*); in a passive sense are such revealed truths (*revelatum*), understood, as already mentioned, above all as truths inaccessible to reason, although there are some that do not surpass reason but are revealed by God for reasons of salvific convenience (*revelabile, revelatum per accidens*).

The Church infallibly interprets Scripture and authoritatively proposes what is revealed, what we must believe. The junction point between Scripture and the authoritative interpretation of the Church is the Holy Spirit. If Scripture is *regula fidei*, the Church is its *regula infallibilis* (cf. S.Th. II-II, q. 2, a. 6). The interpretation of Scripture is linked to the authority of the Church, but the believer formulates his act of faith in relation to the content of Scripture, which always remains the foundation of everything, including the Magisterium (cf. S.Th. II-II, q. 5, a. 3).

Revelation gives rise to a discipline, namely theology, called *sacred doctrine*, which acquires its specificity precisely because, unique in the panorama of the sciences, its source of knowledge is precisely the revealed truths, from which it also derives its regulatory principles, just as the subordinate sciences derive them from knowledge of a higher hierarchical order (cf. S.Th. I, q. 1, a. 1).

In accordance with what has already been affirmed by the Fathers, Revelation is given to men according to a pedagogy that highlights the gradualness of divine condescension. This is seen according to a great hierarchical structure, according to three great stages of humanity: the one that extends from the revelation made to the ancestors to the patriarchs, the mosaic and prophetic one and, finally, the one realised by Jesus Christ. They show a growing condescension and a growing depth in the revelation of the mystery of God's intimate life (cf. S.Th. II-II, q. 2, a. 7; q. 174, a. 6; I-II, q. 103, a. 3).

Even for Thomas, Revelation has an inner dimension: our heart would not turn to God if God did not draw it to Himself (cf*. In Ep. ad Romanos,* c. 8, lect. 6). The call to faith is threefold: that of the teaching and proclamation of the Gospel; that of the signs that confirm preaching, such as miracles (external dimension); and finally that of the inspiration of the Spirit (internal dimension). Often referred to as *motion*, *inner instinct* or *attraction*, the inner dimension of revelation is often assimilated to the action of God's grace and faith, as an intellectual light that elevates the mind to perceive things to which reason alone would not reach (cf. C.G. III, c. 154). Unlike human masters, the divine Master teaches both through words and through inner inspiration(cf. *In Johan. Ev.* c. 13, lect. 3; c. 3, lect. 1).

##### 2.2.5 Nature and forms of Revelation in the teaching of the Magisterium of the Church up to the Second Vatican Council
In Patristic times the Magisterium of the Church had no need to clarify the concept of Revelation or to deal with it directly, because it was rather committed to fighting heresies in the Christological and Trinitarian fields. In mediaeval times the relationship between Revelation and Scripture or between Revelation and the Church, although theologically not yet well defined, had never been seen in a conflictual key. It is since the Council of Trent that the theme of Revelation has necessarily begun to be dealt with in greater depth, especially because of the crisis introduced into the Church by the separation of the Reformers.

##### The Protestant crisis and the Magisterium of Trento
Among the main statements of the Reformers that influenced the conception of Revelation was the principle of *sola Scriptura* which denied the existence of an unwritten Tradition binding on the faith of believers. Moreover, according to Protestants, the interpretation of Scripture would have been guaranteed by the action of the Holy Spirit upon each believer, without the need for authoritative mediation by an ecclesial Magisterium.

The Council of Trent intends to respond to the position of the Reformers. Although the Council does not offer an explicit definition of Revelation, it can be identified in general terms by reference to the notion of "Gospel" understood globally. The purpose of the Council, in this specific area, was to clarify the two errors mentioned above. The most significant text in this regard is that of the *Sacrosancta* decree, formulated during the IV session of the Council:[25](about:blank)

The Holy Council of Trent, always having before its eyes the intention of preserving in the church, eliminating errors, the same purity of the *Gospel* which, after having been previously promised by the Prophets in the Holy Scriptures, was made known first by the mouth of Our Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, then by his apostles to whom he entrusted the mission of proclaiming it to every creature *as the source of every healthy truth and every rule of morals*; and considering that *this truth and this moral rule are contained in the written books and unwritten traditions* that have come down to us, or received by the apostles through the mouth of Christ, or transmitted as from hand to hand by the apostles to whom the Holy Spirit had dictated them; The Council, therefore, according to the example of the Orthodox Fathers, receives all books, both Old and New Testament, since God himself is the author of both, as well as traditions concerning both faith and customs, since they come from the same mouth of Christ or dictated by the Holy Spirit and preserved in the Catholic Church in a continuous succession: the Council receives them and worships them with the same respect and pity.

The term Revelation is not used, but it speaks of a *Gospel* proclaimed by the prophets, promulgated by Christ and preached by the apostles, as the only source of saving truths concerning faith and morals. These saving truths (Gospel) are contained in written books and oral traditions. The Church welcomes with equal devotion not only the OT and the NT, but also the traditions taught by Christ or the Holy Spirit, written or transmitted.

It is significant to note that the use of the term "Gospel" was dear to Luther, although it was invoked by him in some opposition to the notion of the Church. Moreover, even in accordance with what the Lutheran reformers wanted to emphasise, the Catholic position did not neglect the action of the Spirit in believers. A comment by one of the Council Fathers, Cardinal Cervini, the future Marcellus II, will in fact affirm that there are three principles in our faith: the holy books, written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, the Gospel promulgated by Christ, who did not write it, but rather engraved it in hearts, and finally the Holy Spirit who, not being able to remain the Son of God visibly with us forever, reveals in the hearts of the faithful the secrets of God and teaches the Church the whole truth.[26](about:blank)

In order to better understand what happened at the time of the Reformation, it should be noted immediately that the notion of "Tradition", as we know it in modern and contemporary theology, was not yet well established at the time: rather, it was spoken of as "traditions", in the plural with the lower case. It should also be noted that the Churches of the Reformation today understand the Lutheran principle of *sola Scriptura* less radically than they did in the 16th century. This is due to the development that originated above all thanks to the hermeneutical philosophy of the twentieth century, which favoured an understanding of the relationship that generally exists between text and interpretation, that is, between each text and its corresponding context. Something similar must be said about the notion of Tradition, today very different from that of *traditiones* common at the time of the Council of Trent.

##### Vatican Council I between rationalism and modernism
In the epoch preceding Vatican Council I, the two major philosophical-cultural currents of the time, rationalism and idealism, gave rise to not inconsiderable consequences on the way of conceiving Revelation. The first, especially in its materialistic resonances, questioned the possibility and the very fact of a supernatural revelation, contributing to a reductive understanding of knowledge. One of the temptations of idealism was instead to see Christianity as a particular and provisional moment of a wider historical development; but idealism itself detached the reality of Christ from history, transforming him into the prototype of a moral man (Kant, Hegel), and Revelation itself was seen as a gnosis susceptible of being assimilated to the laws of the Hegelian Spirit.

In the Constitution *Dei Filius* (1870), entirely devoted to themes in some way connected with Revelation, after recalling that there is a knowability of God from created things (*per ea quae facta sunt*) - which is not here called "revelation" - the First Vatican Council states that

However, it has pleased the wisdom and goodness of God to reveal (*revelate*) to mankind by another way, and supernatural, Himself and the eternal decrees of His will; this is what the Apostle says: after having on several occasions and in numerous forms already spoken to the Fathers and Prophets, God in these last days has spoken to us in the Son (*Dei Filius*, ch. 2, DH 3004).

These statements emphasise the following concepts: Revelation is a fact, given in history; it is a word of God addressed to humanity, distinct from the witness of God offered by creation; Revelation is a free gift, due to God's free initiative. The object of Revelation is a certain objective content ("the decrees of the divine will"), but also the personal mystery of God ("Himself"). The explicit insertion of the passage from the Letter to the Hebrews recalls that Revelation is a personal dialogue, addressed by God to men and women and which culminates in the incarnation of His Word. Even if the perspective with which it is presented is still that of *knowledge* (in relation to the errors to be clarified), the link between Revelation and salvation is unequivocal.

With regard to the truths of the natural order contained in Revelation, the Council reiterates the doctrine of Thomas Aquinas, according to which such revelation would be *morally* necessary so that these truths "may be known by all, without difficulty, with firm certainty, without mixing errors" (DH 3005).

The same words of the Council of Trent are also repeated to reiterate that the content of Revelation consists of written books and traditions which, "received by the apostles through the mouth of Jesus Christ in person, or having been transmitted, so to speak, from hand to hand by the same apostles to whom the Holy Spirit dictated them, have reached us". (DH 3006).

The Council also establishes a close correspondence between Revelation and the *deposit of faith*: "The Holy Spirit has been promised to the successors of Peter so that they may faithfully preserve and expose the revelation transmitted by the apostles, the deposit of faith" (DH 3070).

Post-Vatican I neoscholastic theology often used the Council's statements in a dialectical key against dominant errors, thus contributing to spreading an image of Revelation unbalanced towards a certain extrinsicism, more attentive to its truthful content than to its Subject. In this sense, the relationship between faith and reason was presented by circumscribing it in a purely intellectual and existentially insignificant sphere, as was often the case with the treatment of the virtue of faith. In fact, a balanced historical judgement shows that Vatican Council I did not have this vision of Revelation and faith, even though its arguments and defining language were certainly coined in the light of the doctrinal clarifications it was intended to bring about.[27](about:blank)

At the turn of the century, the modernist movement introduced new conditioning on the terrain of understanding Revelation and faith. Although animated by the desire to bring the Christian faith closer to the cultural needs of the moment, modernism ended up identifying the idea of "revelation" with that of "religious experience"; the notion of revelation as deposit and objective content was rejected in favour of more dynamic notions, open to historical development and the richness of personal experience. The documents of the Magisterium in this period following Vatican I (the decree *Lamentabili, the* encyclical *Pascendi*, the anti-modernist oath contained in the motu proprio *Sacrorum antistitum*) highlight further aspects of the concept of Revelation. Precisely because of the errors to be clarified, these anti-modernist documents highlight the *objective* and *transcendent* character of Revelation. In the enc. *Pascendi*, when speaking of faith, it is said that it is the assent with which we believe to be true, based on the authority of God himself, "all that has been said, attested and revealed by the personal God, our Creator and our Master" (DH 3542).

The Catholic theology of the time was not mature enough to constructively gather the provocations of modernism and recover some valid elements present in that movement. This will only become possible later, thanks to the mediation operated by philosophical personalism, which will allow theology, by now at the end of the first half of the 20th century, to return to talk about religious experience and the dialogical-personal dimension of revelation in a balanced and convincing way.

##### 2.2.6 The nature and characteristics of Revelation according to the dogmatic constitution of the Second Vatican Council *Dei Verbum
The dogmatic constitution *Dei Verbum*, promulgated on 18.11.1965, still represents today the widest magisterial discussion on the nature of Revelation.[28](about:blank) With respect to the historical context of the First Vatican Council, the more serene historical-doctrinal period in which it was born favours a balanced exposition of doctrine, without the need to respond primarily to errors or deviations. In fact, however, the constitution had a very complex history.

The approach of the initial scheme proposed by the Preparatory Theological Commission to the *Fathers*, *De Fontibus Revelationis*, moved in the line of an apologetic defence against contemporary errors. Almost 40% of the Council Fathers (822/2209) did not like the scheme, and John XXIII, with prudent decision, had it withdrawn. The drafting of a new scheme began, which, experimenting with as many as four successive drafts, was then finally promulgated as *Dei Verbum*. Another scheme drawn up in the run-up to the opening of the Council, called *De deposito fidei anche custodiendo*, did not even come to be discussed in the Chamber. According to the intention of the Fathers of the Council, the Church should not present how and why a series of more or less widespread ways of understanding Scripture, Tradition and the Church's Magisterium itself should be considered erroneous; instead, her main task should be to expose in an organic way Christian Revelation, her message of salvation addressed to the world, focusing its coherence and credibility on the mystery of the Word incarnate, crucified and risen.

The most significant change of perspective is that Revelation is no longer discussed only in its objective aspect of *content*, but above all in its personalist aspect of *self-communication of the living God to man,* to invite him to participate in Trinitarian communion. In so doing, the notion of Revelation regains a strong Christocentric character, capable of assuring and making explicit the personalist aspect, its historical character and its eminently salvific content.[29](about:blank) The response of faith to this divine communication moves not only on the level of knowledge, but on that of the whole person, since both divine Revelation and human response are moments of a person-to-person relationship.

With regard to the nature and object of Revelation, the most significant text in terms of density and content is the one set out in No. 2:

It pleased God, in his goodness and wisdom, to reveal himself and make known the mystery of his will through which men through Christ, the Word made flesh, in the Holy Spirit have access to the Father and are made sharers in the divine nature. With this revelation, in fact, the invisible God, through his immense love, speaks to men and women as if he were friends and entertains them, to invite them and admit them to communion with himself (*ut eos ad societatem secum invitet in eamque suscipiat*).

This economy of revelation takes place with events and words intimately connected (*fit gestis verbisque intrinsece inter se connexis*), so that the works performed by God in the history of salvation manifest and strengthen the doctrine and reality signified by the words (*doctrinam et res verbis significatas*), and the words declare the works and clarify the mystery contained in them.

The profound truth then, both of God and of the salvation of mankind, through this revelation shines forth to us in Christ, who is both the mediator and the fullness of all revelation (n. 2).

This Council passage contains important elements which, in constituting progress in the understanding of the gift of Revelation, are linked in some themes to the biblical-patristic categories. Among the main elements of interest are the following.

1. There is an explicit link between Revelation and the *salvific offering of the mystery of God*, which consists in the invitation to men to participate in the intimate life of the Trinity. The *content of* Revelation and the *purpose of* Revelation are two categories that cannot be dissociated (the expression "*ut* eos ad societatem" was preferred by the Fathers to "*et* eos...").

1. The *sacramental character* of Revelation is widely presented. It manifests itself in the mutual interpenetration of words and deeds. God reveals by intervening in history and, speaking, reveals the meaning of events. The word *gesta* was preferred by the Fathers to the word *facta* because it was more personal, since the works with which God reveals are events caused by a personal agent.Thus a certain manualistic conception that he had easily sustained as "revelatio naturalis fit per facta, revelatio supernaturalis per verba" was overcome.[30](about:blank) The fact that the couple *gestis verbisque is* immediately followed by that *doctrinam et res verbis significatas*, underlines that there can be no opposition between the idea of a *Revelation-Knowledge* and that of a *Revelation-Adventure*. Linked to the sacramental character of a Revelation accomplished with words and works is the sacredness of history itself, which can reasonably give expression to Revelation as "the history of salvation". The opening of the Revelation on history will be linked, in No. 8, to the action of the Holy Spirit: "God, who has spoken in the past, never ceases to speak to the bride of his beloved Son [...]. The Holy Spirit, through whom the living voice of the Gospel resounds in the Church, and through her in the world, introduces believers to the whole truth".

1. The *mystery of Christ* is placed at the centre of Revelation, the exposition of which precedes that of the natural knowledge of God (cf. nos. 3 and 6). Christ not only leads Revelation to its fullness, but He *is* this fullness: of Revelation He is both mediator and fulfilment.The biblical New Testament perspective according to which preaching the word is preaching Jesus is thus fulfilled: Christ cannot be separated from the Gospel. This identification opens the way to a better understanding of the specificity of Christianity: it is the only religion whose founder proposes himself as a way, truth and life personified.

1. The *personal and testimonial character* of Revelation is highlighted by the use of the categories of *word* and *dialogue* as key notions to explain the nature of Revelation, according to a personalist vision which will also affect the exposition of the nature of faith (cf. n. 5).

#### 2.3 The Revelation that God makes of Himself and the mystery of His saving love: the tradition and religious experience of the people of Israel
Unlike the treatise on the "Mystery of God", where the content of the image of God presented by the Hebrew-Christian Revelation is deepened, the treatise on Fundamental Theology is mainly concerned with the *modalities of* this Revelation, its historical phases, the great biblical categories involved.

In the Constitution *Dei Verbum*, the Old Testament Revelation is framed in a brief panoramic view that embraces the whole history of salvation, from creation to the birth of Jesus Christ, thus considering it, as a whole, the preparation of the Gospel Revelation:

God, who creates and preserves all things through the Word, offers to men in created things a perennial witness of Himself (*perenne Sui testimonium*), and moreover, wanting to open the way of supernatural salvation (*viam salutis supernae*), from the beginning He manifested Himself to his ancestors.

After their fall, with the promise of redemption, he lifted them up in the hope of salvation, and took assiduous care of the human race (*et sine intermissione generi humani curam egit*) to give eternal life to all those who seek salvation with perseverance in the practice of good.

In his time he called Abraham to make him a great people, who after the patriarchs he instructed through Moses and the prophets, so that they might recognize him as the only living and true God, Father, provident and just judge, and wait for the promised Saviour, thus preparing the way to the Gospel throughout the centuries (n. 3).

In this text we recognise some well defined content:

- a perennial witness that God gives of Himself through creation;
- a "primitive revelation" made to the ancestors of mankind;
- a fall of the ancestors as a result of a moral test;
- a condescending divine attitude, after the experience of man's fall, consisting of a promise of redemption, providential care and a saving intention to lead those who practise goodness to eternal life;
- the constitution of a people formed by the lineage of Abraham;
- a revelation of his saving will, fulfilled through Moses and the prophets, linked to a teaching about the recognition of the true God and aimed at welcoming the promised Saviour.

The ways in which God reveals Himself in the First Testament can be expressed through some great *biblical categories*, which, going through the entire salvific history, cannot be traced back to simple *historical stages*. Let's see the main ones:

*Revelation or manifestation of God through the work of creation*. The God of Israel, known by his people as the Lord and God of the covenant, is also revealed as the Creator of heaven and earth: all things speak of him. It is the message developed particularly by the sapiential literature (Psalms, Job, Sirach, Wisdom), in addition to what is implicitly transmitted by the narratives of Genesis. A well-defined expression of this awareness is the constant reference to the God of Abraham and the God of Israel as "He who made heaven and earth".

*Revelation in blessings and Promise*. Divine *blessing* and *promise* are two categories that embrace the whole of Scripture. God blesses his ancestors and patriarchs (cf. *Gen* 1:28; *Gen* 9:1), Abraham and his descendants (cf. *Gen* 12:1-3; *Gen* 14:19), through *Jacob* he blesses his twelve sons (cf. Gen *c.* 49); Israel and the righteous (cf. *Gb* 42:12; *Is* 61:9, etc.). But God is also subject to precise promises, the promise of a large lineage to Abraham, the promise to Moses to inherit a land and enter it, the promise of a stable kingdom to David. Above all, God is the author of a Promise par excellence, which extends from the Genoese Proto-Evangel to the prophetic ministry and then enters the New Testament: it is the promise of salvation, of a Messiah, always placed on a special plan of solemnity and gratuitousness. This *Promise* should not be absorbed within the logic or content of the Alliance, because it is rather the premise and foundation of the Alliance.

*Covenant revelation*. Already in the story of Adam, God offers a first covenant to man, inviting him to a special intimacy with Him on condition that he obey His law; later, He makes a covenant with Noah in the cosmic realm, promising the providential care of creatures after the flood: "I establish my covenant with you: no more will any living person be destroyed by the waters of the flood, nor will the flood devastate the earth" (*Gen* 9:11); however, it is essentially with Abraham that a revelation of God *as the explicit subject of a covenant* begins. From that moment on, the category of the covenant became firmly established in biblical language, especially during the experience of the Exodus, thanks to the mediation of Moses and the handing over of the Law, and then took on a more spiritual and interior dimension in the time of the prophets.

*Revelation in prayer and praise.* God comes to meet man to converse with him. Man prays and God answers him. God himself inspires man with the words of praise and prayer and is revealed in this word. Abraham and Moses talk to God and pray to him (cf. *Gen* 18:16-33; *Ex* 33:11). The righteous of Israel address fervent and heartfelt prayers to God (Tobias, Esther, Judith, etc.). God welcomes the prophets' prayer and responds by revealing Himself in the fire that consumes sacrifice (cf. *1 Kings* 18:30-39). Man dissolves his praise to God in the Psalms, making himself the voice of the whole of creation and in a certain way responding to God who reveals himself in the cosmos.

*Revelation through a wise word*. There is a mode of revelation of the one God, adored by Israel, which goes beyond the boundaries of this people and reaches the common experience of mankind and every culture in the face of the great themes of existence. God reveals himself through man's reflection and sapiential meditation on the meaning of the cosmos and life (*Book of Wisdom*), on the questions about death and what will follow it (*Qoelet),* on the why of the experience of pain (*Job),* on the meaning of human activity on earth (*Qoelet*, *Proverbs*). God also reveals himself in human reflection on family and social relationships, on the virtues that must regulate them in order to live according to God's plans, on what is necessary to reach the fullness of one's humanity, living according to the truth of one's condition as a creature in God's image (*Proverbs*, *Sirach*). The wise revelation will accompany in every age the life of Israel, reaching as far as the teachings of Jesus of Nazareth proposed by the NT.

*Revelation in the Prophets*. Similar to what was said for the covenant, although the idea of the prophet as a man of God or the announcement of future events is present even before the saving experience of Exodus, it is from Moses that God's revelation is manifested through the prophetic ministry of *men who speak in the name of God*. However, it will be from the deportation to Babylon that prophecy will represent the main form of communication of the Word of God.

With regard to the *historical relaxation of* divine Revelation, in accordance with what the Fathers of the Church already pointed out with regard to divine economics and pedagogy, biblical theology recognizes three great *epochs or stages of* Old Testament revelation, in substantial agreement with what is expressed in *Dei Verbum*, n. 3:

1. a primitive revelation;

1. a revelation that develops from the formation of a people (Israel) and the offer of a saving alliance linked to the observance of a law;

1. a revelation related to the ministry of the prophets among the people of the covenant.

Sometimes the classical scheme also foresaw the first stage of a revelation of God in creation, but it seems clear that this cannot be considered a "stage", since the awareness of a revelation/manifestation of God in the cosmos (as well as the revelation of God in prayer or that through a wise word) accompanies, with continuity, the whole history of salvation.

##### 2.3.1 Primitive revelation in the context of “origin narratives”
The content of the "primitive revelation" is represented by the answers that Revelation provides to that set of fundamental questions about the origin of the world and man that have accompanied human thought since its beginnings and are commonly referred to as *the problem of origins*. It is customary to place the content of this revelation in the teachings presented by the inspired author in the first chapters of the *Book of Genesis*, but it should not be forgotten that they also deal extensively with wisdom books and prophetic literature, even when they do not reread or comment on the pages of Genesis. The recipients of this primitive revelation were the ancestors of mankind and, later, all humanity up to Abraham.

##### Contents and specificities of the primitive revelation delivered to Israel
Comforted by divine inspiration, the people of Israel put their oral tradition in writing in historical-cultural moments in which humanity already possessed many other traditions on the problem of origins. They formed the backbone of as many specific cultures, especially in the two geographical areas that had the greatest relations with Israel, namely Egypt and Mesopotamia. The "narratives on the origins" presented by Scripture, while using elements of the language of other traditions that already exist, *have their own specificity* that distinguishes them from other traditions of the same kind.

Among the original elements of this primitive revelation is certainly the position of the human creature, placed at the apex of visible creation, as the subject of a special dignity, that of being the image and likeness of God (cf. *Gen* 1:26-31). In God's image**,** the human being has a free and relational structure, he is a *person*.From the beginning, he is presented as *God'*s *interlocutor,* created by God, able to recognize God's word and called to put it into practice.

In the biblical narratives, God reveals Himself to man with the characteristics of a solemn transcendence, but also with a paternal and provident image: He creates and differentiates the universe in such a way as to be able to welcome man, He places him in a situation of happiness allowing him to fully realise his humanity, He entrusts him with the task of guarding and humanising the earth and He puts him in a position to realise it intelligently (cf. *Gen* 2:8-9.15.18-23).

Also belonging to this primitive revelation is the narration of a sin that took place "in the origins": the suspicion of divine paternity and its goodness, which generates a disobedience to its law, moved by pride and instigated by another creature, a serpent who introduces the figure of an adversary of God, Satan(cf. *Gen* 3:1-6).[31](about:blank) This original guilt changes man's relationship with God, from a situation of friendship and intimacy to a situation of separation; it changes the original position of the ancestors and upsets the relationship between man and woman, as well as that between them and nature (cf. *Gen* 3:14-20). With man's sin death comes into play (cf. *Gen* 3:3; *Wisdom* 2:24), especially in its anthropological-existential value even before on the physical or biological plane.[32](about:blank) The sinful condition of mankind is proposed several times, at least in two other important "original" contexts, according to narrative plots where sin of origin and personal sins intertwine: in the corruption that mankind experiences at the beginning of its history because of interpersonal relationships now dominated by violence and selfishness (cf. *Gen* 3:20; 5:1ff; *Gen* 4:8; 6:5), and in the bold construction of the Tower of Babel, presented as an implicit act of pride against God (cf. *Gen* 11:1-9). Both the sin of the progenitors and the corruption of the first men and their subsequent decision to ascend to heaven to Babel generate normative consequences for the human race as a whole: a new state of painful life, far from friendship with God (progenitors), the propagation of evil that reaches all men (first men), the dispersion of tongues and the loss of unity (Babel).

The precise understanding of what has been entrusted to Israel through the "revelation of origins" is a difficult and delicate task for theology, as it must enter into dialogue with history, biology, paleoanthropology, ethnology and cultural anthropology. Theological Anthropology seeks to thematize the human condition against the background of the relationship between nature and grace, while biblical hermeneutics seeks to deepen the meaning of the texts transmitted to us. Fundamental Theology, on the other hand, has the task of deepening the modalities and credibility of this primitive revelation. Fundamental Theology points out that the narratives of the origins, also because of their normative value, have a meta-historical as well as historical scope, as it is not always easy to distinguish between the two. It recalls, with the theology of many Fathers of the Church, that to understand the human condition one must look not only at the origin but also at the end, the *eschaton*, towards which creation is certainly oriented (Gregory of Nyssa, Maximus the Confessor). It should also be kept in mind that the revelation of origins has not only a categorical aspect (texts collected in writing from the religious experience of a people), but also a transcendental and athematic aspect, available at the bottom of every human being in every age, and therefore also to our ancestors. Finally, Fundamental Theology is called to show that, when understood through correct hermeneutics, certainly not literal, the contents of the "revelation of origins" do not contradict the knowledge acquired on the level of history and science and remain significant for people of all times.

Even after the moral trial sustained by his ancestors, man does not cease to be God's interlocutor (cf. *Gen* 3:9-10; 4:9; 6:13ff). He continues to turn to man, reveals His will to him, does not deny him the gifts of His Providence. Of great importance is the revelation of a constant struggle between the lineage of the woman and the *serpent* (cf. *Gen* 3:15), a struggle that patristic exegesis and the *analogia fidei* tell us ends with the defeat of the latter. The human person remains the object of special dignity, not having lost the seal of his divine likeness, and God remains the subject of a special fidelity to him (cf. *Gen* 4:1; 4:10; 5:1-3; 9:5-7).

Still in the "context of the origins", the narration of the universal flood reveals the personality of God, whose holiness/separation prevents him from tolerating the corruption of humanity (cf. *Gen* 6,6-7), but whose condescension makes him the subject of a solemn blessing-covenant with Noah, with the characters of mercy, forgiveness and fidelity (cf. *Gen* 9,12-17). The covenant made with Noah and his descendants takes the form of a unilateral gift extended to the cosmic plane of creation, rather than that of a true pact with moral demands that man must fulfil, although the latter seem implicit in the story that gives rise to a new humanity, replacing a corrupt and sinful generation.

*Dei Verbum* necessarily expresses in a synthetic way the long connection of time that links the narration of the origins to Abraham's vocation. And it does so in an extremely respectful way of the whole religious history of humanity that preceded the formation of the people of Israel. We read: "After their fall [of the progenitors], with the promise of redemption, he lifted them up in the hope of salvation and took constant care of mankind, to give eternal life to all who seek salvation by perseverance in the practice of goodness. (n. 3).

We can summarise the essential contents of this primitive revelation as follows:

- The Creator of heaven and earth is one God, transcendent over nature and all things, omnipotent and provident; He creates the human being, man and woman, in His image and likeness.
- God manifests to man not only his creative power but, above all, his condescending love, offering him to share in his intimacy and beatific communion.
- Alongside this invitation, God asks the human being to respond with filial trust to his goodness, a request represented in a paradigmatic way by a moral test: the meaning of this test was not to demand a counterpart to the divine gifts, but to reveal man's freedom and the respect that God has for it.
- There is a difference between the present historical condition of human beings and humanity intentionally desired by God's creative plan: this difference was caused by man's sin, which introduced universal consequences into the original relations between man and God, between man and woman, between man and his fellow human beings, between man and man with himself.

Finally, it should be noted that, in general, primitive revelation speaks to us not only of the condition of our ancestors, but also of the condition of each one of us, of the history we have already built and the history we will still build: it reveals something of our origins but also of the *eschaton* to which we are called. Understanding the relationship between these two perspectives, historical and meta-historical, of primitive revelation, between its categorical component and its transcendental component, is not an easy subject for theology and can be considered an open question. Its treatment is entrusted primarily to Theological Anthropology, which examines the biblical datum and presents the teachings of the Magisterium of the Church on the relationship between nature and grace and on original sin.

##### The significance of biblical narratives against the background of history and science
With regard to the "contents" of primitive revelation, the word of God is urged to confront other sources of knowledge that attempt to focus on the problem of the origins: from natural sciences to natural history, from paleoanthropology to cultural anthropology, from the phenomenology of religion to the history of religions.[33](about:blank)

The comparison with the natural sciences and history shows the symbolic-narrative value of biblical chronology about the origins of humanity and its early geographical development. Thanks to appropriate hermeneutics, it is also possible to specify which questions biblical Revelation intends to answer and which the sciences must answer. The first is primarily interested in highlighting the *original relationships* between man and God, between God and creation, between the human being and his fellow human beings, not the chronology or physical description of events. Knowledge from the sciences maintains its value, because it helps the exegete to understand which literary genres to apply to biblical narratives.

Anthropology and the history of *religions* tell us that the human race has known its own "religious history" which is intertwined with its own origins: the biblical text cannot be used to reconstruct in detail the itinerary and phenomenology of this history, but nevertheless it traces its essential coordinates: the human being is *naturaliter religiosus*, what he does on a personal level has repercussions on a social and collective level, he experiences in himself a dichotomy between good and evil, approving the former but giving in dramatically to the latter. To affirm that God, Creator of heaven and earth, has revealed Himself in a personal and gratuitous way to the human being, does not contradict the analysis of the sciences, whose method does not deny that the human being has, in the panorama of our planet, an irreducible singularity. This singularity can be legitimately interpreted by theology as the effect, on an empirical level, of a privileged relationship with God, of the human person being a creature made in His image and likeness. It is plausible that this privileged relationship is interpreted as an *encounter of revelation*. Supporting it remains significant even in the contemporary scientific context, regardless of whether it provides a complete description of how God's creative causality operates in the context of the biological, genetic or psychological causes that have led to the human form as human. If theology must confess it's ignorance about the historical-factual ways in which God has laid his gaze on his creature, the empirical sciences must also confess their ignorance about the inability to interpret human phenomenology in the context of a materialistic naturalism: the human being is certainly an animal, but he is not *just* an animal.

As for the plausibility of an "original revelation" directed to the first men, what the biblical text exposes in anthropomorphic and categorical form may also have been the object of a transcendental revelation. This revelation could take place where the human being, at different times and places, has reached the awareness, sustained by divine grace, of transcending nature, because it is the image of God, recognizing that he has a responsible task before others, nature and himself. Always in the transcendental sphere, the human being may have grasped for the first time the meaning of love, spousal and charity, in the transmission of life, in the intelligent care of offspring, in the building of social community. Such an original revelation should not only be seen as a form of inner revelation, but also as a revelation of God through created things, through the face of the other. Listening to nature around him - from the starry sky to the laws of sowing and harvesting, from the perception of one's own contingency to amazement at the fascination of creatures, from the face of one's spouse to the joy of procreation - the human being may have received and accepted divine revelation about his role in the world and the task entrusted to him by the Creator.

In the context of a scientific examination of the origins of man, the narrative of Scripture about the moral test borne by the ancestors and the consequences it generated does not lose meaning either. The reasonableness of this content should not be justified by trying to specify more and more precisely the circumstances of space and time in which this evidence occurred and what the actors were involved. The existence of moral evidence and its consequences should rather be based on empirical and phenomenological grounds.[34](about:blank) It is in fact undeniable that there is a dramatic dichotomy between the intelligence of the human being on the one hand, capable of greatly increasing the quality of his or her life on the planet through an ever more sophisticated knowledge of nature, and the use he or she makes of it on the other, all too often directed towards war, oppression and destruction. While it is difficult to understand what original sin has been in the evolutionary history of the human species, it is even more difficult to understand who man is regardless of it, not accepting sin as a possible explanation that decodes the condition of the human being and reveals the outcome of an original call. With regard to those "responsible" for the sin of origin and its consequences, whose narrative is basically representative of sin generally understood, the sciences do not deny in an apodictic way the possibility of an original first couple, and the Magisterium of the Church is also oriented towards this solution.[35](about:blank) It will be future scientific knowledge which will suggest whether and in what way theology and the Magisterium should eventually propose a different interpretation of the biblical text, as was done in past ages with regard to other comparisons with the sciences (heliocentrism, biological evolution of the living).

With regard to the archaic appearance of monotheism or polytheism, and their possible relationship with the context of a primitive divine revelation, the history of religions can also be heard. The historical approach initiated in his time by W. Schmidt (*Origin and Development of Religion*, 1930), suggested that all religious cultures could be traced back to an original monotheism, explicit or implicit, i.e. belief in a single Higher Being with similar manifestations from people to people (archaic monotheism). This original monotheism could have been matched by a primitive revelation, with the same constants in all peoples. The sociological-evolutionary approach of the school of E. Durkheim (*The Elementary Forms of Religious Life*, 1912), considered monotheism and its characteristic contents as the point of arrival of an evolution whose beginning was animism and totemism, which, as social organisation grew, conceptualised a divinity with more precise contours, associated with legal contents and more transcendental moral references. In reality, there is no apodictic evidence in favour of one or the other interpretation as regards the greater archaicism of monotheism or polytheism, although today, in the light of ethnological studies of older Aboriginal populations, the former would seem more likely. From the point of view of their connection with the primitive revelation of which Sacred Scripture speaks to us, the idea of an original monotheism could suggest a certain preservation of the content of that revelation, while an archaic polytheism would put the accent, as a historical starting point, on the degradation of humanity and then on the consequent, laborious reconstruction of that original content. Both solutions therefore admit a possible reading within the history of salvation, as we have received from the biblical text.

Finally, the comparison with the natural sciences and history sometimes poses the problem of the chronology involved in the narratives reported by Revelation.[36](about:blank) In fact, the very long time elapsed between the appearance of man on earth and the fixation of primitive revelation in writing should not come as a surprise. It must be considered that the long journey of *Homo sapiens* from prehistory to history is compatible with the situation of slow historical development in which our ancestors found themselves and which the inspired author may have put in writing, precisely because inspired, much more than human historical memory was able to preserve.[37](about:blank) Moreover, the sacred text could also have included the contents of a transcendental revelation available in every age, whether interior or linked to the observation of creation. If the judgement of surprise then concerned the long time elapsed from the apparition of *Homo sapiens* to the Incarnation of the Word, it must be remembered that the judgement on the historical significance assumed by a certain interval of time is always relative to the global extension of the phenomenon under consideration. But this extension is in fact unknown to us because we do not know the final end of the story.[38](about:blank) The fullness of the revelation given in the Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ could be both the chronological culmination of a long historical evolutionary process that has already seen its longest fraction pass, and the moral summit of a historical journey that is destined to extend much further into the future.

##### 2.3.2 Creation as cosmic revelation of the Word
To indicate that the world and its creatures speak in some way of the existence of their Creator, the Magisterium of the two Vatican Councils had not used the term "revelation" in the strict sense, preferring terms such as manifestation, attestation, testimony. Beginning with the encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998), and then in the ordinary Magisterium of John Paul II, the term revelation begins to appear more frequently in this context. From a theological-fundamental point of view there is no lack of reasons to consider the created world as an integral part of the economy of Revelation.39 Let's see some of them:

- Creation is presented by Scripture as the effect of the Word of God; thus in the account of Genesis (cf. *Gen* 1:3.6.9, etc.) and in various places in the wisdom books: "Through his word the heavens were made, from the breath of his mouth all their hosts [...]. He speaks and all is made, he commands and all is made" (*Ps* 33:6.9);
- Scripture explicitly mentions the possibility of knowing the existence of God starting from the consideration of created things: cf. *Wisdom* 13:1-9; *Rom* 1:18-20; *Acts* 14:15-17; *Acts 17:*26-27;
- Creation is capable of *revealing something* of its author, because Scripture invites men to give glory and praise to God through contemplation of his works; thus *Ps* 19: "The heavens narrate the glory of God, and the work of his hands proclaims the firmament. The day to the day he entrusts the message and the night to the night he conveys it" (*Ps* 19:1-2); similarly in *Ps* 104, *Gb* cc. 38 and 39, *Sir* cc. 42 and 43, *Is* 40:25-26;
- Finally, the NT will present Jesus Christ, the Word of God made flesh and the fullness of Revelation, as *Logos* mediator of God's creative project and meaning of creation itself: cf. *Jn* 1:1-3; *Col* 1:1-18; *1Cor* 8:6; *Heb 1:*2-3.

In this regard, it should not be forgotten that faith in the fact that the universe is the effect of a divine word and that the cosmos is therefore an expression of rationality and has a meaning to be transmitted, has historically had - and continues to have - important consequences on the philosophical level, even in relation to scientific thought.40

##### Position of God's cosmic revelation in Israel's faith: creation and salvation
When the religious experience of the people of Israel is put in writing, the revelation of a God as Creator and his knowability through the works of creation is not among the first contents to be themed or developed. Rather, it constitutes a basic awareness, something on which is reflected *from the covenant*, from its meaning for man, whose weight in the historical formation of the people of Israel is greater than that of the revelation of a God who was also Creator. Israel presupposes an understanding of the world as created and accesses a faith in creation almost as a consequence of its faith in the liberator God, thus encompassing creation in relation to the same covenant.41 For the mentality of the Jews, the world's total dependence on God does not need to be *believed*, because it already belongs to their way of thinking: God is the *Creator* (cf. *2Mac* 1:24-25; *Gdt* 9:12).

At the same time it would be a mistake to consider the revelation of God as Creator (and the faith that corresponds to it) an entirely derived or late element. Faith in God the Creator, for example, sustains Israel at times when the covenant is challenged by adverse historical events. If the role of the covenant was decisive for the religious formation of Israel, that of faith in a God the Creator was certainly decisive for the religious formation of the human race as a whole, and this is witnessed to by Sacred Scripture itself, as can be seen from the image of God transmitted by the wise books. In many pages of the books of Job, Proverbs, Syracuse or Wisdom, the revelation of God through creation remains an original experience. That it must be a foundational revelation, it also reveals an episode of Abraham's vocation. Immediately after leaving the land of Ur of the Chaldeans to go to the land of Canaan at the command of the "Lord" whose name they do not know, when they meet Melchisedek king of Salem, both of them will recognize themselves as worshippers of the one God through a cosmological reference to the "Most High God, creator of heaven and earth" (*Gen* 14:19). A correct position of faith in (or revelation of) creation must finally be evaluated by remembering that the affirmation that the God of Israel "made heaven, earth, sea and all things therein" actually runs through all of Scripture. It is like a constant refrain in its pages (cf. *Ex* 20:11; *Ne* 9:6; *Dn* 14:5; *Jer* 32:17; *Est* 4:17), especially in the *Psalms* (cf. *Ps* 24:1-2; 89:12; 146:6; etc.). In the NT Jesus himself will use this same expression to address the Father: "I bless you, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth...". (*Mt* 11:25; *Lk* 10:21; see also *Acts* 14:15 and 17:24).

Throughout Scripture, God's works in creation maintain a close relationship with His saving works. In *Psalm* 89 God creates and saves, because he is the same God who acts in the cosmos and in history. There is, in essence, a significant *correspondence between the word creator and the word prophetic*: the word that created all things is the same word that interprets the meaning of history and guides the course of events towards its salvific fulfilment. *Psalm* 136 assures us that there is a common language capable of proclaiming God's wonders in creation and God's works in the history of salvation; there is a musicality capable of rhythmic praise to God "because he created the heavens with wisdom" and a praise to God "because in our humiliation he remembered us", a praise for "having made the moon and the stars" and one "for having given the promised land as an inheritance to Israel his servant", intercalating each of these praises with a single incessant justification: "for his mercy is eternal."42

##### Creation moves man to give glory and praise to God...
The observation of heaven, the archaic place of transcendence, but for Israel also, inseparably, the work of the Creator, moves man to give glory to God.

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Psalm 8*

Similar teachings will be proposed by Sirach (*Sir* 42,23-24 and 43,1-2.9-10), Isaiah (*Is* 40,25-26) and the Psalms of praise of creation, such as *Psalm* 104. In referring to God as "He who made heaven and earth", one wants to indicate his uniqueness, the universality of the extension of Jahve's causality and dominion over all that exists. He guarantees the stability of the world and governs its natural laws wisely, a sign of his fidelity and his love for mankind, through which everything is gently conducted towards its end (cf. *Prv* 3:19-20; *Wisdom* 11:20-21; *Is* 61:11; *Jer* 31:35-36).

Turning one's gaze to heaven as the basis of an argument that leads one to recognize and praise the one God is a religious and intellectual operation of a certain originality, if one takes into account the historical-cultural context in which it is born and the recipients to whom it is directed. From the observation of heaven and its phenomena, the Mesopotamian world and later the Greek world had in fact inferred the existence and action of several gods: while not ignoring the rich cosmic and natural context of which the human being is a spectator (cf. also *Gb* 9,9-10 and 38,31-33; *Bar* 3,34), when Israel observes heaven it does not multiply the gods, but worships one God, the creator of heaven and earth.

##### From knowledge of creatures to knowledge of the Creator
In the *Book of Wisdom we* find the affirmation of a "way" of knowledge that creatures can lead back to their Creator, through an explicit reference to "analogy": creatures *resemble the* Creator, and therefore *reveal Him*.

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Wisdom* 13.1-9

*Psalm* 14 will call the man who positively denies the existence of God a fool, that is, a fool, because he is considered an adequate object of human intelligence.

To affirm that starting from created things one can recognize the existence of a Creator is not the exclusive teaching of Jewish-Christian revelation. This deduction belongs to classical, mediaeval and modern philosophical thought, and there are widespread traces of it in natural religiosity prior to philosophical reflection proper. In the patristic sphere, this conviction will give rise to the use of Nature's metaphor as "Book": "Let your book be the divine page you must listen to; let your book be the universe you must observe. In the pages of Scripture only those who can read and write can read, while everyone, even the illiterate, can read in the book of the universe".43 In the philosophical-cultural tradition of Christian inspiration, this ascent from creation to the Creator will be referred to in modern times as the *Cosmological Topic*.

Today there is a need to keep alive, at the centre of philosophical wisdom and culture, the ultimate questions which refer to the origin of the whole, to the meaning of the world, to the meaning of human existence; and there is also a need to reaffirm the capacity of human reason to trace back from the knowledge of creatures to the knowledge of a Creator. John Paul II's encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998) stresses the importance of God's revelation in nature as a basis for comparison with philosophical thought and as an implicit basis for dialogue with the religions of the earth, suggesting that the Book of Nature is destined to play an extraordinarily valuable role in this regard in the future.44

##### Creation reveals the right relationship between man and God
The *Book of Job* presents us with an element of great interest. The call to contemplation of nature has the important effect of "re-centering the right relationship between man and God"; this call arouses faith and abandonment in his provident fatherhood, disposes man to accept what comes from God even when reason does not understand him. Job is convinced that he must accept the sufferings due to his creaturely condition when God, showing him creation, solemnly asks him *Ubi eras?*

When I was laying the foundation of the earth, where were you?
Tell me, if you're so smart!
Who fixed its size, if you know, or who stretched the rope over it to measure?
Where are its bases fixed or who laid its cornerstone, while the morning stars rejoiced in the choir and cheered all the children of God? [...] Have the gates of death been revealed to you and have you seen the gates of the dark shadow?
Have you considered how far the earth stretches? Say it, if you know all this! (*Gb* 38.4-7.17-18)

The protagonist of the narration accepts this reproach and recognizes it as fully reasonable: "Well, I'm very petty: what can I answer you? I put my hand over my mouth" (*Gb* 40,4).

Part of the revelatory value of creation lies in its appeal to realism, a fundamental element not only for a correct approach to religious relations that bind man to God, but also to support a gnoseology that opposes an idealism of Cartesian origin and knows how to distance itself from any form of ideology. Realism lets things talk and makes the man talk about God again.

##### The human person, God's revelatory creature
Therefore the universe, as we have seen, is the effect of the very word of Yahweh (cf. *Ps* 33:6; *Prv* 3:19), it is the voice of a cosmic word which announces and narrates the glory of its Creator (cf. *Ps* 19:1-7). In *Psalm* 19 we find praise for God for the creation of the universe and for the sublimity of a salvific law present in the heart of every man: cosmic law and moral law are united in the same praise of divine wisdom:

The heavens narrate the glory of God, and the work of his hands announces the firmament. Day to day it gives the message, and night to night it gives the news. Without language, without words, without their voice being heard, their proclamation spreads throughout the earth and their message spreads to the ends of the earth (*Ps* 19:2-5).

The law of the Lord is perfect, it refreshes the soul; the testimony of the Lord is stable, it makes the simple wise. The Lord's precepts are upright, they make the heart rejoice; the Lord's command is clear, it enlightens the eyes. The fear of the Lord is pure, it remains forever; the judgments of the Lord are faithful, they are all just, more precious than gold, much finer gold, sweeter than honey and a dropping honeycomb (vv. 8-11).

There is a witness that God gives of Himself, and therefore a revelation of Himself, in human consciousness (cf. *Rom* 2:12-15), because it bears the seal of His eternal law. The testimony that the Creator gives of Himself through His works reaches its apex precisely in the creation of the human person, male and female. The solemnity of the whole Genesistic narrative (cf. *Gen* 1:26-27) places the human person in the position of those who can most reveal God as man and woman created in His image and likeness. Alongside contemplation of material creation, consideration of the human person (spirituality, freedom, transcendence over the rest of creation) is itself a way to recognize the existence of God (cf. *Ps* 8:4-8). And so is the existence of a moral law, inscribed in the heart of man, which judges his works and urges him to do good and avoid evil.

##### 2.3.3 The Word of the Covenant
In the history of religions the case of an alliance between a monotheistic people and its supreme divinity is unprecedented outside of the religion of Israel: "what great nation has the divinity so close to it as the Lord our God is close to us? (*Dt* 4.7). Together with the experience of liberation, the awareness of his covenant *with Yahweh* **-** of which liberation is after all a consequence - is one of the constitutive religious experiences of the people of Israel and, therefore, one of the most significant words of the Old Testament revelation. However, it should not be forgotten that the theme of the covenant is always preceded by the theme of *vocation-election*, which very often introduces a solemn *Promise*. God first calls, blesses and promises. Once he has established a personal relationship with man, calling him and converting him to Himself, God offers him his condescending covenant. As is well known, the latter takes on different forms in salvation history: implicit in Adam, incipient with Noah, now explicit with Abraham and Moses. The Promise prepares and crosses the covenant: a veiled promise of intimacy with God in the Garden of Eden becomes a promise of redemption after the fall;45 again: promise of provident care of mankind to Noah, of a numerous offspring and a universal blessing to Abraham, of a land to settle in for Moses, of a stable kingdom forever to David, of a new and eternal covenant in prophecy. Finally, it is always in prophecy, although already overshadowed in previous eras, where we encounter the Promise par excellence, that of a Messiah, who will introduce the NT as the biblical place of its definitive fulfilment.

In contemporary theology of Revelation it is the progressive development of the Alliance that is generally commented on - perhaps leaving the Promise in the shadows - probably because of its easier historical unfolding. We will therefore follow the same route in this manual.

In a general sense, the term *covenant* indicates a bilateral pact between two persons, stable in time, established with a character of solemnity before God and the people; in its practice we often find a common meal, a banquet, associated with an act of worship, or a sacrifice, which becomes a way of sanctioning the sacred character of the covenant itself.

In the language of OT, what we understand theologically today as "covenant" is an expression of the more general concept of "commitment", indicated by the Hebrew term *berith*,although the context in which this term appears is largely that of the relationship between God and his people, between God and man. The noun *berith* is attested in the OT 287 times; its most numerous occurrences are in Genesis and Deuteronomy (27 times), in Jeremiah (24 times), in Joshua (22 times), in the Psalms (21 times). The etymology of the term is uncertain. It could derive from "bond" or "chain" (*biritu*), from the preposition "between" (*birit*), or from the root *brh,* which, although in its primary meaning would refer to "see" and "look", as derived action would indicate determine, fix, establish.46 The idea of reciprocity is undoubtedly present in the term, although references also to a vassal agreement between a king and his vassal cannot be excluded, so that it is compatible with a certain asymmetry between the parties.47 Even following this reading, the idea that a divinity, whose image in the faith of Israel certainly transcends that of an earthly king, has the initiative to enter into an alliance with a people, does not cease to be, in the panorama of religions, a singular novelty. The translation offered by LXX almost always uses the term διαθήκε, or "testament/will", highlighting above all the aspect of mediation and union, opting only rarely for σπονδή, which means "community of the table", although the latter is used in the plural in classical Greek to indicate pacts, agreements, and even truce or armistice.

##### The image of the God of the Covenant
The fact that Yahweh is the protagonist of an alliance with men reveals the image of a *loving* God, addressed to man, eager to enter into dialogue with him. At the same time he is the image of a powerful God, *Lord* of history and omnipotent over nature because he is capable of doing what he promises; a God absolutely *faithful* to his promises, and precisely because he is determined to enter into relationship with man, capable of waiting and forgiving, of punishing in order to convert and save; a ***Holy*** God, because allying himself with Him implies for man to live a law and respect a moral norm.48

The God of the covenant is a "merciful" God, subject of goodness and mercy (Heb. *hesed*). Indeed, the very possibility of the covenant rising and remaining is based on the presence of *hesed*: he keeps the covenant *because he keeps mercy* towards his people. One of the dimensions of mercy is therefore fidelity, that is, the property of being *loyal* to the covenant entered into. The fidelity-truth of God (eb. *emet*) is experienced by Israel precisely in the context of its mercy-loyalty in relation to the covenant. Many of the terms that express "the good provisions of Yahweh for the covenant" have as their root *rhm*, which refers to the mother's womb (eb. *rehem*), to the feelings of a mother who generates and protects. A summary phrase of the nature and dispositions of the God of the covenant is the one frequently proposed by the OT: "Yahweh! Merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, rich in grace and fidelity" (*Ex* 34:6, cf. also *Na* 1:3; *Gl* 2:13; *Wisdom* 15:1; *Ps* 86:15; *Ps* 103:8, etc.).

God's disposition towards man (covenant) and the world (creation in the light of the covenant) lies at the heart of biblical revelation, constituting the "good news" announced to the righteous who believe. "The foundation that makes this disposition of God possible is its transcendence over the world and its absolute personality and freedom. They form the biblical *mysterium tremendum* on which takes its unprecedented prominence and its ecstatic splendor the *mysterium fascinosum* of God who bends towards the created being".49

The covenant that God offers to his people in the OT is not comparable to the concepts of a "horizontal" covenant made between men. It's always a *free gift* from God. This gratuitousness is manifested in God's initiative in the covenant, in its connection with gifts as gratuitous as the blessings and election of its recipients (Noah, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, David, Mary), in the fact that these divine choices often correspond to criteria that invert human expectations (sterile women giving birth, modified birthright, election of the weak or the young). To the fidelity of God's gift, to his initiative that he elects and chooses, corresponds also man's fidelity: it is very often that of isolated characters representing the whole people in a certain way, that of a small remnant who represents the people and intercedes for them: Noah, Abraham, Moses, Elijah, Jonah, David... In the NT we find again the fidelity of Mary and John at the moment of the flight and the bewilderment caused by the drama of the Cross.

##### The covenants with Noah, Abraham and his descendants...
By abstracting from the primitive relationship with God of our ancestors - in some way also traceable to the category of the covenant - it is with the figure of Noah that Revelation introduces this category more clearly. In the context of the new cosmic order immediately following the universal flood, God's covenant with Noah takes on the character of a great blessing which will sanction the faithfulness of the cosmos to the order established by God. However, Noah is not called to observe a specific divine law in order to fulfil this covenant, which is presented above all as a gratuitous gift from God. The idea that humanity should now no longer have to walk paths of corruption and evil, the cause of divine punishment, remains, however, an implicit commitment on the part of mankind (cf. *Gen* 9). Of Noah's three sons, Sem is the subject of a special fatherly blessing.

It is, however, with Abraham's vocation that the covenant takes root, and his promises and demands become progressively more explicit. The Covenant is linked to Abraham's name-election as the forefather of a great people. The context is not so much that of a mutual agreement as that of a command, of a choice that will have important consequences.50 God elects Abraham and promises to make him a great people in whom all the families of the earth will be blessed (cf. *Gen* 12:1-3).

The commitment made by Abraham to go to the land indicated to him by God then becomes an explicit commitment to worship, from generation to generation, and a commitment to practice circumcision as a sign of the covenant:

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Genesis* 17.4-11

The oath-promise-election-covenant of God with Abraham is therefore renewed and confirmed on several occasions (cf. *Gen* 15:5-6; 17:1ff; 22:15-18). Abraham obeys the command of the Lord and will show him his obedience at other times, especially in the dramatic trial that saw him willing to sacrifice his son Isaac (cf. *Gen* 22:1-18). The divine covenant is renewed with Isaac (cf. *Gen* 26:2-5) and his son Jacob (cf. *Gen* 28:12ff; 35:9-12), always in the character of an oath-benediction-election. Revealing himself to Isaac, God praises Abraham for having obeyed his voice and kept all that he had prescribed for him, his commandments and his laws (cf. *Gen* 26:5). The echo of such a promise-benediction will cross the entire NT, introduced by the words of the *Magnificat*: "He has helped Israel, his servant, remembering his mercy, as he promised our fathers, Abraham and his descendants, forever". (*Lc* 1.54-55).

##### The Sinaitic alliance with Moses and its solemn religious-salvific context
The deepest and most solemn biblical context of God's revelation as *God of the covenant* is that of Exodus. With his revelation to Moses and the liberation of Israel from the slavery of Egypt, the divine promises progress towards their fulfilment and are in continuity with the oaths made to Abraham, Isaac, Jacob (cf. *Ex* 2:24; *Lv* 26:42). The content of the alliance-promise is enriched by the commitment to introduce Israel into a promised *land*. Here appears for the first time, solemnly and explicitly, the association of the covenant with the handing over of a *Law*: the Israelites will commit themselves to live the *Decalogue handed down* by Yahweh, and Yahweh will commit themselves to pave the way for them to Canaan (cf. *Ex* cc. 19-24). The covenant stipulated between God and his people is ratified by the sacrificial feast of the Passover supper which precedes the departure from Egypt (cf. *Ex* 12:1-14) and, in the context of the great theophany which accompanies the delivery of the law, by a meal eaten on Mount Sinai (cf. *Ex* 24:10-11).

This is how Deuteronomy sums up the value of the law in the Sinaitic alliance:

These are the commandments, laws and regulations which the Lord your God has commanded you to teach, that you may put them into practice in the land into which you are about to enter to take possession; that you may fear the Lord your God by keeping all the days of your life, you, your son and your son's son, all his laws and all his commands which I give you, and thus prolong your days. Listen, O Israel, and take care to put them into practice, that you may be happy and become very numerous in the land where milk and honey flow, as the Lord, God of your fathers, has told you (*Dt* 6:1-3).

The law delivered on Sinai remains essentially linked to two basic data: the *love* on which it is based and the *holiness* towards which it is directed. But the Law itself remains a gift from God: it is for Israel a source of freedom and a distinctive sign that characterises it as the People of God, before God. The observance of the law is not a means or a counterpart to gain a relationship with God, but rather the way to live this relationship. The law linked to the covenant is truly welcomed by Israel as "a gift from God", as a manifestation of his goodness and mercy, a source of joy and fullness of life (cf. *Ps* 19:8-11).

The handing over of the Mosaic Law constitutes an important specificity of the Jewish-Christian Revelation, which places the chosen people in a condition of absolute singularity with respect to other peoples; consequently, it places the image of Yahweh in a condition of absolute originality (transcendence and holiness) with respect to the divinities of other religions. As with Abraham, the covenant between God and his people will be renewed several times by Moses and his successor Joshua. Thus after the episode of the golden calf and the destruction of the first tablets of the law (cf. *Ex* c. 34), and after entering the promised land (cf. *Gs* c. 24).

Also in the context of the covenant with Moses, the *revelation of the name of God* plays a very important role (cf. *Ex* 3:7-15). Jahve can rightly call himself "the divine name of the covenant".51 The Semitic sense of the root *hajah* indicates the verb "to be" in its dynamic dimension; in its most genuine meaning - in addition to the offer of intimacy represented by "giving the name in order to a personal relationship"- the name of God JHWH is meant to signify the fact that God is present permanentlynext to Israel and in favour of Israel: "I am here, I am he who is here". If the name of God is at the heart of God's nature as it is *addressed to man*, of this nature it expresses the free and gratuitous desire to be close to man and with man, forever. It is not surprising that in the NT the name of God made man, Jesus - that is, "Yahweh is salvation" or "Yahweh is salvation" - is in continuity with the biblical divine name of the covenant.

##### The historical development of content associated with the alliance
Once Israel's presence in the promised land is consolidated, David becomes the protagonist of the renewal of Jahweh's alliance with the people (cf. *2 Sam* c. 7). Here too the characteristics of gratuitousness stand out, both in the way David is elected (cf. *1 Sam* 16:10-12) and in the disproportion between what David will offer and what God will promise him (cf. *2 Sam* 7:1-16). Since David's reign, the divine promises-benedictions are explicitly linked to a dynasty, that of David. It is the promise of a descendant and a lineage by which the kingdom of Israel will never end and will be stable forever. God's revelation and His new blessing are placed in continuity with the liberation from Egypt and the constitution of Israel as the People of God (cf. *2 Sam* 7:22-25).

With the prophetic ministry of Jeremiah and Ezekiel during the Babylonian exile, the theme of the covenant returns to the forefront, but this time with the promise of a new, inner covenant, linked to the fulfilment of the law in one's soul and the renewal of hearts (cf. *Jer* 31:31-33; *Ez* 36:26-27). Even in these contexts there is no lack of the characteristics of divine gratuitousness: it is Yahweh who will write this law in hearts, it is Yahweh who will remove a heart of stone to give a heart of flesh and infuse in his chosen ones his own Spirit.

##### 2.3.4 The Word of Wisdom
As evidenced by the contents of divine revelation related to Exodus and the Covenant, an important part of the revelation of the God of Israel has *moral life* as its object and context. The moral dimension of Revelation, which can also be found in various pages of Genesis or the Prophets, is however expressed in a particular way in the wisdom books. Beyond the deuterocanonicity of some of them – *Sirach* and the *Book of Wisdom*, which the Christian tradition has, however, accepted and made its own - it is possible to identify a divine revelation through a word or a *sapiential* tradition. This tradition seems to arise from the confluence of two sources:

- the value of human moral life experience;
- the fruit of theological reflection on creation and divine gifts (including the sanctity of moral life itself).

This is a twofold aspect which makes the sapiential tradition particularly suited to the study of the relationship between nature and grace, between creation and alliance, between virtuous ethics and moral holiness, the fruit of divine grace.52 Sapiential revelation, therefore, possesses important virtues for the evangelization of the contemporary world. In the inter-religious and intercultural context such as the present one, it may not be easy to approach the idea that God reveals Himself to man mainly through the religious history of a people that progressively acquires its political and national identity, experiencing divine intervention at the moment of its liberation and the wars necessary for its subsequent settlement. Although the events of war in Israel have a consolidated spiritual reading in the history of biblical exegesis and should always be read in the context of the contrast between idolatry and worship of the one true God, the new peoples who come to faith today may not immediately understand them in this light.53 On the other hand, contents linked to the recognition of God in creation, to reflection on the great existential questions that open up the search for meaning, on the moral virtues that make human life good and worthy, are more easily framed in a *religious* context capable of unifying different peoples and cultures.

The two sources of the sapiential tradition - the experience of moral life and theological reflection on the meaning of life and creation - have a certain chronological articulation. "In ancient wisdom, the emphasis was placed on the acquisition of wisdom through diligent research conducted on the world of experience. With the passage of time, however, another aspect of wisdom has increasingly developed, which, without denying the role of human experience, considered wisdom as the mystery that God himself had introduced into creation. Therefore, all wisdom is a gift from God, who in active self-revelation calls all men and women to life.54

Through the sapiential word, divine Revelation seems to reach man and express itself according to three great areas:

- the revelation in and through prayer;
- the revelation of the moral conduct that one must follow in order to live one's humanity to the full, that is, God's creative plan for man;
- the revelation of the answers to the great themes of human life: life, death, evil, etc.

The sapiential tradition should be considered as a coherent and unitary mode of transmission of the divine Word: it is not identified with the law and, at least for a certain part of the books in question, it boasts an antiquity comparable to that of other great historical traditions of Israel. There are also theological perspectives according to which the first chapters of *Genesis* could themselves be considered part of "sapiential literature".

The central idea that justifies the originality of sapiential revelation is that the Word of God can also be understood by listening to things. Such listening requires recollection and reflection, indeed the word itself that is heard proceeds from such reflection. Unlike the prophet, the sage is not given or entrusted with a word, but is required to engage in meditation and research in order to find what God means to mankind: "I have set out to seek and explore wisely all that is done under heaven" (*Qo* 1:13). "I have watched and I have thought, I have seen and I have taken this lesson" (*Prv* 24:32). The word of wisdom knows the dynamic of freedom and conversion proper to every word of God addressed to man: in the face of the revelation of God in his works and in the conscience of the one who reflects, man can open himself to the gift of meaning offered to him by the Creator or close himself and harden his heart, not listening to the voice of God.

A "canonical" approach to biblical books will, however, show that theological reflection within Scripture complements and clarifies how much philosophical-sapiential reflection acquires through life experience. On the contrary, with the passage of time we witness a certain "development" of the ways in which the contents of wisdom revelation become accessible to man, because "wisdom", while not losing its universality, begins to bind itself prevalently to Israel and the Holy City. According to the most recent tradition of Sirach, wisdom finds its dwelling place and place of rest in Jerusalem (cf. *Sir* 24:11); it was given to Israel to keep it as part of God's willed covenant with his people. A wisdom which, as in chapter 3 of the prophet Baruch, appeared in Israel and lived among men (cf. *Bar* 3:38).

##### God reveals Himself in the contents of His dialogue with man
From the very beginning of creation man is called to dialogue with God. He is able to listen to its voice and answer it. This dialogue takes the form of sapiential reflection and prayer. God communicates to man the words of his dialogue with him, what He wants man to ask of him, what contributes to his holiness and salvation, but also what contributes to a righteous and honest life lived in praise and respect for his Creator.

In the encounter with the man who prays to him, God reveals himself to be just and merciful (cf. *Gen* 18:16-32; *Ex* 34:5-8), eager to descend to the level of man to converse with him (cf. *Ex* 33:11). In the prayer of the Psalms, the word of God becomes the word of man: man manifests his faith in the just and provident God, from whom he asks protection and comfort; at the same time, by praying to him, man praises God together with all creation and God reveals himself as magnanimous, wise, glorious.Prayer reveals man's faith in God's faithfulness and the love that God has for man, His compassion for humility and contrition of the human heart.

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Psalms* 40.2-6.17-18

As in Psalm 51, prayer becomes a place of revelation of the moral value of man's actions, because God reveals himself in man's conscience and moves him to conversion.

**The sapiential tradition of moral life**

The word "sapiential" [“sapienziale”] is a revelation of a specific moral conduct, which exhorts people to use the earth's goods correctly, to set up relationships between people inspired by both justice and charity, to fulfil the obligations of their state. Among these, the obligations of parents to their children and of children to their parents are important (cf. *Sir* 3,2-16 and 7,27-28; *Prv* 6,20-22). Also significant is the reference, especially in *Proverbs*, to the duty to work well and responsibly, fleeing laziness and foolishness. One blames the figure of the *lazy*, who considers work always too tiring, not worthy of sacrifice and diligent application, and thus ends up in a situation of scarcity and contempt on the part of society (cf. *Prv* 6:6-11; 24:30-34 and 26:12-15). Also reprehensible is the figure of the *intriguer*, who devotes himself with satisfaction to sowing discord among people, in order to gain some personal advantage, resulting in the rapid and catastrophic precipitation of the situation: "for this, suddenly his ruin will come, in a moment he will collapse without remedy" (*Prv* 6:15; cf. 6:12-14).

It is thus possible to delineate a true biblical ideal of wisdom, in which all the elements of a true humanism are being elaborated. The concept of wisdom involves both concern for a practice attentive to earthly facts and a high notion of human dignity and its promotion. Wisdom books declare that wisdom begins with a confident and reverent obedience to God: "the fear of the Lord is the first stage of knowledge" (*Prv* 1:7), or even "the beginning of wisdom is the fear of the Lord" (*Ps* 111:10; cf. *Jb* 28:28). But they also indicate that this is only the starting point. The attainment of knowledge and wisdom implies a growing willingness to share in the Wisdom of God himself on the part of those who apply it. In obedience to God one can then understand, almost by experience, that the whole of reality is imbued with a profound meaning, the greatness of which exceeds the full understanding of the human intellect (cf. *Ps* 104:24; *Prv* 3:19; *Sir* 42:21).

Wisdom can only be obtained as a free gift bestowed by God himself (cf. *Wisdom* 8:21; *Sir* 1:10). He has given human beings the great honour of making them guardians of the whole of creation (cf. *Ps* 8:3-8); but they will be equal to their task only in so far as they remain united to the Wisdom of God (cf. *Wis* 9:1-2.17-18). The gift of wisdom must be fervently asked of God (cf. *Wisdom* 9:4-6:10-17; *Sir* 39:5): to ask for it alone places us in greater intimacy with Him (cf. *Wisdom* 7:27) and transmits to man an unshakeable security, that of living in perennial communion with Him (cf. *Wisdom* 15:3; *Ps* 16:8-11; 48:3-15 and 72:23-28). In short, obtaining wisdom coincides with the full development of one's own dignity (cf. *Sir* 25:10), as is attested to by the many examples, taken from the history of salvation, that these books of Scripture report (cf. *Sap* c. 10; *Sir* cc. 44-50).

Both in the life of relationship with others and in personal life, the characteristic traits of the moral virtues are recognized in the Word of Wisdom: justice, prudence, fortitude and temperance. It is, ultimately, the revelation of what constitutes, for man, wisdom. Wisdom revelation is fully inscribed in the relationship between revelation and salvation, indeed it makes it explicit. The moral life of man, in fact, built on the commandments of the Law but open to a progressive expliciting/completion through human and moral virtues, is capable of leading to union with God, it is life according to God and, therefore, life that leads to salvation. The search for Wisdom is itself a search for God; to possess Wisdom is to live according to God. For this reason, wisdom must be sought more than anything else and, by possessing it, one achieves the purpose of one's existence.

It is not difficult to see the multiple applications of this perspective, especially with regard to the evangelization of culture and the intellectual world: Scripture assures us that whoever honestly seeks truth and wisdom is seeking, whether or not he knows it, God; whoever loves truth and wisdom is loving, whether or not he knows it, God. Thus Christian authors of the past and present, from Saint Augustine to Saint Edith Stein, have pointed it out.

##### The word sapiential and the revelation of the meaning of human existence
The biblical sapiential tradition also includes reflection on the central themes of existence, such as how to deal with life and death, the right value to be given to material goods, reflection on evil and the travail of man who seeks an answer. From the sapiential revelation we are also instructed about the true nature of human feelings, knowing about which it is possible to direct one's emotional life, which becomes knowledge of oneself. The wisdom invoked by wise men lies "in understanding one's own way" (*Prv* 14:8) in the midst of the agitation of things, and therefore in "following it" (cf. *Prv* 28:26). The numerous "rules of life" present in *Proverbs* and *Sirach* are intended to warn man against the seductions of life and make him master of his feelings.

*Qoelet* [Ecclesiastes] deals with describing the condition of the human person in a realistic and even pessimistic way by reflecting on it: starting from the experience common to all, it underlines the finiteness of the human creature and the transience of all things (cf. *Qo* 2:1-11), leaving implicit the invitation to seek something stable and higher. The great existential questions of man all come to the surface: the meaning of life and activity that man carries out, the earthly destiny of the just and the ungodly, the constant search for happiness, which ends up being always frustrated. Man perceives the infinite distance that separates him from God and from the true knowledge of all things, resigning himself to a finiteness that, however, never degenerates into despair, but remains constitutively open to God's gift, the only one who possesses the answers that man needlessly seeks. It is a reflection that is in the wake of other extra-Biblical sapiential reflections, with its own religious perspective, that of a silence of God that even man believes to be present and continues to confess Creator of the world.

The question of the meaning of evil finds ample space in the *Book of Job*, whose non-closely belonging to the Jewish people highlights even more the parallel between the sapiential revelation and the questions that the righteous of all times addresses, seeking an answer. To physical evil, beyond the temptation of pessimism and despair, one can respond by letting God's Providence act, provided that one humbly accepts the proof of one's human condition and remains capable of recognizing God's presence in creation. The experience of divine Providence with regard to created things becomes a guarantee that providence also exists for man, even though it may remain in the shadows at certain times in life.

##### The sapiential Word between Revelation and reason
As already noted, the sapiential tradition is able to link the Revelation of Yahweh to a search for and adoration of God made from creation, that is, taking its cue from nature and the great themes of human existence. Since these realities are before everyone's eyes, it therefore possesses important virtualities of dialogue between the different religious traditions of the earth. The link between the God of Israel and the tradition of wisdom - a tradition to which men could in a certain way access even without an explicit knowledge of the "history of revelation" delivered to Israel - is but an implication of the agreement between the revealed word in history and the revealed word in creation. The revelation of God in Wisdom belongs to the logic of the relationship between faith and reason, as highlighted by *Fides et ratio*, in particular in nn. 16-21.

How deep the link between the knowledge of faith and the knowledge of reason is already indicated in Holy Scripture with surprising clarity. This is mainly documented in the *Sapiential Books*. What is striking in reading these pages of Scripture without preconceptions is the fact that not only the faith of Israel is contained in these texts, but also the treasure of civilizations and cultures that have now disappeared (n. 16).

In some of the important pages of the wisdom books it is possible to glimpse a *biblical counterpart to the* great ontological questions about nature and its rationality that the philosophical approach, especially in Greece, had theorized reflecting on the concept of Logos. It is in this sense that it could be read, for example, the role of Wisdom-Logos as a wise architect who assists God in the work of creation, revealing the intimate joy that such rationality takes with it. Here is also rooted, at least in part, the subsequent reflection of Eastern Christian theology which will see in Wisdom-Logos a fount and original mediation of God's self-revelation in the world. In general terms, it is precisely the successful link between wisdom tradition and philosophical-religious reflection on creation, that is, the mediating role of Wisdom in the work of creation, that justifies the fact that Wisdom can truly be spoken of as the mediator of God's revelation in the world. In fact, this encounter between philosophical reason and divine revelation has already been operative in the formation of some sapiential books, those written in Greek. The sacred author has sought in some established philosophical categories, such as the Logos and its properties, but also the virtues and the good life, the appropriate language to express the word inspired by God. "Wisdom becomes an instrument by which Israel's very different approaches to divine reality - through divine revelation on the one hand and human experience on the other - can be harmonized in a profound way, avoiding eliminating each other. It cannot therefore come as a surprise that wisdom provides new tools to relate the human spirit to the divine spirit.55

The revelation of a word of wisdom thus provides an extraordinary instrument for a fundamental synthesis, not only within the biblical message but also in its projections towards philosophical thought, between Revelation and reason, between faith and reason, recovering every human effort of ascent towards God through the transcendental of the true, the good and the beautiful, to place them at the service of knowledge/revelation of the One God.

##### 2.3.5 The Prophetic Word
The memory and deepening of the Word of the Covenant, especially the one related to the delivery of the Sinai Law, is realized mainly through the Prophetic Word. The term "prophet" (Heb. *nabi*) means "he who is called", "he who speaks in the name of another". The extra-Biblical religions knew the figure of prophecy, but it was mainly linked to the characters of clairvoyance, oracles, ecstatic possession or even magic. The idea of a moral recall, of the historical memory of a relationship with the divinity or of a saving mediation towards the divinity itself is very little developed. Extra-Biblical prophecy, in fact, does not express a relationship with the word of God, but rather relates to the events of history, the cosmic order and the decoding of its laws. In the version of the LXX, the term *nabi* is translated as the Greek word προφήτης, a concept linked to the idea of the proclamation of knowledge, and not as μάντις, a word reserved for magical clairvoyance as such, and that the LXX will correspond to the Hebrew word *qosem,* equivalent to 'magician'. Even before prophecy in the strict sense, Abraham and especially Moses can be regarded as prophets, since they fulfilled the true mission of a prophet, that of speaking in the name of God (cf. *Dt* 34:10).

##### The relationship between the prophet and the word of God
God manifests his designs to the prophet through visions, dreams, communications of images and ideas. However, these manifestations all point to the delivery of a Word, the divine Word, which makes the prophet the protagonist of a unique experience, experiencing the strength with which it is revealed and imposed: "You seduced me, Lord, and I let myself be seduced; you made me violent and you prevailed... I said to myself: "I will think no more of him, I will speak no more on his behalf! But in my heart there was like a burning fire, held in my bones; I tried to contain it but I could not" (*Jer* 20:7.9; cf. *Am* 6:7-8). It is the moments of the prophet's vocation that highlight the power of the divine word, its demands, its effectiveness and the power with which it is able to direct history (cf. *Ez* c. 2; *Gen c.* 1; *Jer c.* 1, etc.).

The prophet therefore has a very special relationship with the Word of Yahweh: the Word is in him (cf. *Jer* 5:13), it has been placed in his mouth (cf. *Jer* 1:9; 5:14), it is given to him as nourishment (cf. *Ez* 3:1-3), with an energy and effectiveness that do not depend on him (cf. *Jer* 1:4-10.18-19); the Word was received not to be kept in private but to be transmitted to the people (cf. *Ez* 7:1). He has heard the word of God directly (cf. *Is* 8:11; *Jer* 11:21; *Ez* 2:2), and he is like the mouth of Yahweh (cf. *Jer* 15:19).

##### The personality and ministry of the prophet
The prophet is a man of God and by his very life offers a religious witness of fidelity to the Word. He is moved by the Word of God towards a preaching that privileges three contents:56

- to manifest to the people the knowledge of Yahweh;
- to remember the loyalty of the alliance;
- exhort the fulfillment of divine law.

His personality, totally conditioned by his relationship with the divine word, can thus be summarized.

First, the prophet can be considered an *expert on God*. He transmits to the chosen people the knowledge of Yahweh not as an abstract conceptual doctrine, but because he is a living witness of an experience. He experiences the *gratuitousness* and *intimacy* of the divine call (cf. *Am* 7:14-15; *Hos* 2:16), witnessing to the power of God's glory (cf. *Ez* 1:26-27), the fascination of his holiness (cf. *Is* 6:1-8). His closeness to God is such that he witnessed the counsel of Yahweh (cf. *1 Kings* 22:19-23; *Jer* 23:18.22).

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Isaiah* 6,1-8

Secondly, the prophet is sent to *remind the people of the divine love* manifested in the call and covenant and to exhort them to be faithful to the covenant established with God. The monotheistic consciousness of the people of Israel is definitively strengthened by the prophets themselves, leaving no more room for doubt about the presence of other gods alongside Yahweh. The prophetic ministry becomes particularly pressing in times of corruption and crisis (Elijah, Amos, Hosea, Jeremiah, Jonah), during the trials of deportation and exile (Ezekiel, Isaiah, Daniel), at the time of undertaking the reconstruction of the temple in Jerusalem and restoring worship and the law after the return home (Nehemiah, Haggai, Zechariah, Joel, Malachi).

Finally, the ministry of the prophet *takes on a real moral task* of denouncing iniquity, which not infrequently will cost the man of God the price of his life. It is through the word of the prophets that God condemns fraud (cf. *Jer* 22:13; *Am* 8:4-8; *Mic* 6:10), usury (cf. *Am* 2:8), injustices against the poor, slaves, the defenseless (cf. *Is* 3:15; *Am* 4:1-8; *Jer* 34:8-22).

##### The prophet, memory and announcement of history
An important peculiarity of the prophetic word is its link with history. The word of God is addressed to the prophet at a precise moment and time: "In the eighth month of the second year of Darius' reign, this word of the Lord was addressed to the prophet Zechariah, son of Barachiah...". (*Zac* 1:1; cf. *Jer 1:*1-3; *Am* 1:1, etc.). We will find an echo of this in the NT at the time of the Baptist's preaching: "In the tenth-fifth year of Tiberius Caesar's empire, while Pontius Pilate was governor of Judea, Herod the tetrarch of Galilee, and Philip, his brother, tetrarch of Iturèa and Trachonitis, and Lysania the tetrarch of Abilène, under the high priests Anne and Caiaphas, the word of God descended on John, son of Zechariah, in the wilderness" (*Lk* 3:1-2).

But of history the prophet becomes almost the living memory, a history whose decisive stages had always been preceded by the divine word: the creation, the flood, the vocation of Abraham, the continuity of his descendants in Isaac, the vocation of Moses and the liberation from the land of Egypt, the wandering to the promised land, the election of kings and prophets, the division of the kingdom of Israel, the deportation to Babylon and the return home, the ruin of Jerusalem, the appearance of the Messiah. Progress in the knowledge of God and His designs towards His people is so inextricably linked to the events of Israel's history, events that the word of God announces, realizes, interprets.57

Through history God guides his people, but he also punishes and purifies them through the difficulties and misfortunes they face, often presented in a paradigmatic way as a consequence of infidelity to the true God and forgetting his love. The prophet's task is to show the meaning of this history so that it becomes pedagogy and allows the people to convert. A classic example is the solemn prayer of the prophet Daniel.

##### The messianic promise and the progressive internalisation of the law and the covenant
Finally, it will be precisely through the prophets that God will confirm the Promise to his people, revealing ever more explicitly the coming/presence of a Messiah and the announcement of a future salvation (cf. *2 Sam* 7:1-16; *Ps* 2; *Ps* 110; *Is* 55:1-5; *Is cc.* 40-55; *Mic* 5:1-3; *Ez* 34:11-16.23-25). What in past ages had been outlined with essential and sometimes uncertain traits, now takes on clearer contours, to the point that the Christian faith may indicate in the Servant of Yahweh prophesied by Isaiah the passion and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth.

Part of the preparation to accept the definitive Promise, which will introduce the logic of the Incarnation, is the fact that the memory of God's truth-fidelity of the Sinaitic covenant now becomes the preaching of a *new and eternal covenant* (cf. *Is* 55:3; *Is* 61:8).

 *Jeremiah* 31:31-34

Salvation is now understood as purification from sin and as the capacity to fulfill God's law; the proclamation of the Messianic age, together with the promise of eternal stability for Israel, points towards the progressive revelation of a state of new and definitive intimacy of God with his people and with all the peoples of the earth (cf. *Ez* 36:23-28; *Ez* 37:26-28; *Mic* 4:1-5). This definitive presence of Yahweh in the midst of his people is particularly explicit in Ezekiel: "For the Lord God says: 'Behold, I will seek my sheep myself and I will review them. The best introduction to the centrality of the Incarnate Word in the economy of divine condescension and its revelation to man is represented by the solemn Prologue of the Gospel of Saint John, read in the light of its Old Testament resonances:

 *John* 1,1-18

*Dei Verbum* thus exposes the Christological centrality of Revelation:

After having God, on several occasions and in many ways, spoken through the Prophets, "in the end, in our days, he has spoken to us through the Son" (*Heb* 1:1-2).

For he sent his Son, the eternal Word who *enlightens* all men, so that he might dwell among men and explain to them the secrets of God (*ut intima Dei enarraret*).

Jesus Christ, therefore, the Word made flesh, sent as "man among men", "speaks the words of God" (*Jn* 3:34) and brings to completion the work of salvation entrusted to him by the Father.

Therefore He, seeing the Father, with the very fact of His presence and with the manifestation of Himself, with words and deeds, with signs and miracles, and especially with His death and resurrection from among the dead, and finally with the sending of the Holy Spirit, completes and completes Revelation (*Revelation complicating perficit*) and corroborates it with the divine witness, that is, God is with us to free us from the darkness of sin and death and raise us up for eternal life.

The Christian economy, then, as a new and definitive covenant, will never pass and no other public revelation is to be expected before the glorious manifestation of our Lord Jesus Christ (n. 4).

Divine Revelation has in the event of Christ, in his Paschal Mystery, its fullness, both in the way it is realized and in the scope of its content.58 Jesus Christ is the definitive and most perfect word that God directs to humanity. Not only because, as an incarnate Word, it is the most condescending word pronounced by God on behalf of man, but also because this Word interprets, decodes and reveals the true meaning of every other word pronounced by God. In Jesus Christ, the divine word finds not only its *noetic fullness* (i.e. the highest revelation of its intelligence and meaning), but also its *hermeneutical fullness* (i.e. the revelation of its true meaning and the interpretation of its content). It is a centrality that embraces the whole historical-salvific horizon, because it concerns all the great categories of biblical revelation, from vocation to promise, from covenant to law, from creation to redemption.

This very singular position of Christ in the economy of Revelation makes it possible for the logic of a *universale concretum* to be realized in him: a normative knowledge and salvation, of a universal character, are accomplished in the concreteness and visible frame of a determined historical event. The ineffability, unknowability and universality of God are mysteriously concretized in history and time in the mystery of Christ. What for philosophical thought would remain a paradox, that is, the coincidence of "universal" and "concrete" becomes the fundamental law of the economy of Revelation.59 The prologue to John's Gospel is the solemn summary of this economy: "No one has ever seen God: the only Son, who is God and is in the bosom of the Father, he has revealed him" (*Jn* 1:18).

##### 2.4.1 The Incarnate Word, fullness of the creative word, center and end of the history of the cosmos
What God manifests through creation - always manifested in a close relationship with the Word - also reaches its fullness in the event and in the gift of the Incarnation. Although less intuitive for us than in the fulfillment of the word of the covenant and the word of prophecy, Scripture also presents Christ-Jesus as the fullness of the creative word. In addition to what Scripture already makes explicit about the role of the Son, made visible in human nature, as the exemplary cause of creation (cf. *Jn* 1:3; *Heb 1*:2; *Col 1:*16; *1 Cor* 8:6), the biblical-theological contents which manifest the fulfillment and fullness of the creative word in Christ concern at least the following four aspects:

- creation exists in Christ and was made in *view of Christ*: the manifestation of God through the cosmos is a certain preparation of the incarnation of the divine word, and therefore preparation of Christ;
- *Christ's humanity is* the *fullness of creation*; it is the most perfect and most revealing created reality of God's love and greatness;
- In Christ the logic of a *new creation is* already available, which he inaugurates and mysteriously leads to its eschatological fulfillment through his glorious Resurrection;
- creation, in its historical development, has reached in the mystery of the Incarnation the *fullness of time*, its focal point: starting from Christ it is possible to read the meaning that history tends towards, to interpret its past and to understand the logic of its future.

This connection, in Christ, between the fullness of the creative word and the fullness of the Redemption does not lead to a necessary connection between creation and the Incarnation, nor does it take away gratuitousness from the reality of the Redemption; it merely highlights the fact that, in the only historical economy we know, the one marked by our original sin, there must exist a correspondence already foreseen in God's plans between creation and Incarnation. Having chosen us, created and elected in His only-begotten Son, brings with it the free willingness of the Most Holy Trinity to restore in us, precisely through the created humanity of the Word, the divine image obscured by sin, and to reveal for us, precisely in the visible mission of God made man, what was all its original beauty.60

A history of salvation that calls into question the logic of the Incarnation of the Word and its consequences on the level of creation before the development of a history of sin and redemption, respecting the gratuitousness and unfathomability of the divine will, highlights the logical coherence of God's plan, God's fidelity to Himself, to His free plans of love, to His promises; it highlights the logic of revelation that is associated with it, that of an infinite love, because it is also a love of mercy and forgiveness.61

##### Creation subsists in Christ and was made in view of Christ
One of the biblical places of greatest interest regarding the role of the Incarnate Word in creation is that of the Christological hymn collected in the *Letter to the Colossians.*62 This page presents Jesus Christ in the heart of the divine plan of creation and salvation according to a threefold involvement: "in Him (εν αὐτω) all things were created" and, again, "all things were created through Him and in view of Him (τὰ πάντα δἰ αὐτὸῦ καὶ εις αὐτὸν ἔκτισται)". (*Col* 1.16). There is a certain similarity with a similar formula of faith present in *1st Corinthians*: "There is only one God, the Father, from whom (ἐξ οὗ) everything comes and we are for him (εἰς αὐτὸν); and one Lord Jesus Christ, by virtue of whom (δἰ οὗ) all things exist, and we exist through him (δἰ αὐτοῦ)". (*1Cor* 8.6).

To affirm that all things were done *in view of* him seems to reveal that creation points towards the humanity of the Word as it points towards an apex, towards his most perfect expression. The dynamic character of this expression is present in Greek (είς αὐτόν), which the Latin of the Neovolgate translates as accusative: *omnia in ipsum creata sunt*. We are faced with an indication of unity and coherence of the whole divine plan, of how the person of the Man-God is capable of expressing and revealing this coherence in Himself: it is the world that is for Christ and not Christ for the world.

From the theological point of view, admitting a normative role of the Incarnate Word on creation, a centrality not only exemplary but also final (*primum in intellectum, ultimum in esecutionem*) implies the affirmation of the "pre-existence" of Jesus Christ, that is, the presence of the mystery of the Incarnate Word already at the dawn of divine creation. It is a pre-existence preached in the first place by the uncreated Person of the Son, and therefore certainly not functional to the being of the world, but we can also refer secondly to his true humanity, which has always been present in the mystery of the divine plan of God One and Triune over creation.

Scripture affirms that He who makes visible what in God is arcane and invisible, "is before all things (πρὸ πάντων)". (*Col* 1,17) and "is generated before any creature (πρωτότοκος πάσες κτίσεως)". (*Col* 1.15). These expressions do not affirm that he was created first, nor that he is the temporal principle from which the series of all creatures originated; rather, they affirm his foundational-ontological originality, his pre-eminence as universal mediator of all that is called to be with creation.63 The Christ preached by John preexists at creation because "he is in the beginning" (cf. *Jn* 1:1; *1Jn* 1:1); because the love which binds this Son to God his Father "is before the foundation of the world" (cf. *Jn* 17:5.24). Also for Peter, witnessing with John of the glory of Tabor, he "was predestined even before the foundation of the world, but he manifested himself in recent times for you". (*1Pt* 1.20).

The position of Christ as the final meaning of the divine work of creation means that the mystery of his Incarnation is celebrated not only in reference to "what was in the beginning" (cf. *Jn* 1:1-3), but also in what will be in the end (cf. *1 Cor* 15:28; *Col* 3:11). In the plan of creation, only he can be, in some way, in *the beginning and the end*, in the Alpha and the Omega, as indicated by the apocalyptic vision of the Lamb (cf. *Rev* 1:8; 21:6). His mediation *in principle* is such that "everything was made through him, and without him nothing was made of what exists" (*Jn* 1:3), because he "through whom [God] has also made the world ..., he sustains everything with the power of his word" (*Heb* 1:2-3). His mediation *in the end* manifests itself in his role as judge and, as we shall see a little later, in that of the final heir to a redeemed creation recapitulated in him.64

##### The humanity of Christ, the fullness of creation
If everything has been done in view of Christ, then the created humanity of the Word presents itself as the most perfect divine work**.** The author of the *Epistle to the Colossians* expresses this by saying that "in Christ corporally dwells all the fullness of divinity(πᾶν τὸ πλήρωμα τῆς θεότητος)". (*Col* 2.9). In the glorious body of Christ the whole divine world is revealed, to which the uncreated nature of his divine Person belongs; but the whole cosmos is also revealed and recapitulated, to which his created human nature belongs, because the fullness of the divinity dwells in him *corporaliter*, in the concreteness of his corporeity. It is not a matter of a cosmological fullness, as in the Greek tradition, where the cosmos constitutes the very body of the divinity, but of a soteriological fullness, which expresses gladness, peace, reconciliation.

The Scriptures had already presented a certain superiority of man over creation. He is placed at the top of the divine work of the six days, the most perfect creature because he is the image of God and capable of summing up in himself the coexistence of the two spiritual and bodily dimensions, almost a synthesis of the whole created world. Thus the Lateran Council IV had expressed it: "[I believe in unum Deum] Creator omnium visibilium et invisibilium, spiritualium et corporalium: quia [...] de nihilo condidit creaturam, spiritualem et corporalem, angelicam videlicet et mundanam: ac deinde humanam, quasi communem ex spiritou et corpore constitutam" (DH 800). "Unity of soul and body - he would say seven centuries after *Gaudium et spes -* man synthesizes in himself, by his own bodily condition, the elements of the material world, so that these, through him, touch their summit and take voice to praise the Creator in freedom" (n. 14).

Such an "anthropocentric" superiority is assumed and brought to fullness in a Christocentric key, because Christ is preached not only as the true and perfect image of the Father, but also as the image of the perfect man(cf. GS, 22, 41) of whom the first Adam was a figure (cf. *Rom* 5:14). In him it is no longer a synthesis of spirit and matter to be expressed, but the hypostatic and unconfused union of a divine Person with a corporal and spiritual human nature: "Nam sicut anima rationali et caro unum est homo, ita Deus et homo unus est Christus" (*Pseudo-Athanasian Symbol,* DH 76).

The revelatory value and significance of the humanity of the Incarnate Word are therefore those of those who, by revealing the face of the perfect man in God's plans, also express in a perfect way the whole of creation, at the head of which man was placed. This fullness is exercised not only through his divinity, that is, through his creative mediation as the Word generated before all things, but now also through his true and perfect humanity.

##### The new creation in Christ
Christ, in whom all things were made, becomes for us universal mediator also of a *new creation*, the one inaugurated with his glorious Resurrection. It is a renewal that is mysteriously *already given*, already present in the Church as a sign and universal sacrament of salvation, *but not yet* realized, because it will commit the mystical body of Christ in a sacramental economy that will last until the end of time.

The correspondence between the first creation and the new creation is well expressed by *Easter Sunday***,** the dawn of the Church and the first day of Genesis. Having as its reference the transfigured humanity of the risen Christ, the new creation does not destroy, but transforms the previous creation, bringing it to completion. Strictly speaking, we are not faced with two creative projects, the second of which perfects the first, but with one and the same plan of God the Creator, which from the beginning aimed at the Sabbath of glory and which, in a story of sin and redemption, passes through the mystery of the Cross and the Resurrection.

Between the notions of first and new creation there is certainly discontinuity, but also (and perhaps above all) continuity: it is the reconstruction of an original order of which we now discover what the true meaning was. To a "new creation" in Christ are associated essentially two contents:

- the definitive submission of all creation to Christ, with special reference to victory over death;
- a universal recapitulation whose purpose is to reorganize, establish and lead all things to the Father in the Spirit.

With regard to the first content, Scripture states that Christ was "made heir to all things" (*Heb* 1:2; cf. *Rom* 8:17); all things were destined for this submission (cf. *Eph* 1:22) because it is necessary that at the end of history "God be in all things" (*1 Cor* 15:28). Firstborn of those who rise from the dead, by his resurrection he achieves a cosmic primacy that has the sense of victory over death. It is a markedly saving capital, which extends over the body of which He is the head (gr. κεφαλή), that is, the Church (cf. *Col* 1:18).

Manifestation and definitive guarantee of this submission of creation, which has the character of a universal reconciliation, is therefore its glorious resurrection, whose saving fruits are already revealed in the lives of believers:

With him he also raised us up and made us sit in heaven, in Christ Jesus, to show in future centuries the extraordinary richness of his grace through his goodness towards us in Christ Jesus (*Eph* 2:6-7);

If therefore ye are risen with Christ, seek the things that are up there, where Christ is, sitting at the right hand of God; turn your thoughts to the things that are up there, not to the things of the earth. For you are dead and your life is hidden with Christ in God! When Christ, your life, is manifested, then you too will appear with him in glory (*Col* 3:1-4).

It is by virtue of his resurrection that he can make the first fruits of this new covenant economy live in his Mystical Body, enabling him to return to the Father a renewed creation in the Spirit (cf. *Phil* 3:20-21; *1 Cor* 15:28; *Rom* 9:5; *Col* 3:11; *Eph* 4:6). The latter, impatiently awaits a final transfiguration, in which the victory over the corruption of death will be extended to every creature, and the image of a universal sonship will be definitively revealed and brought to fullness (cf. *Rom* 8:19-22).

The second content associated with the "new creation in Christ", that of the recapitulation, has its main scriptural passage in the prologue of the *Letter to the Ephesians:*

[He has made us] to know the mystery of His will, according to the benevolence which in Him He had proposed for the government of the fullness of time: to bring back to Christ, the one head, all things, those in heaven and those on earth (*Eph* 1:9-10).

In the noun "ricapitolazione" (gr. ἀνακεφαλαιώσις), whose verb in an infinite way is rendered in Italian with "ricondurre a Cristo, unico capo", in addition to the preposition ανα indicating "nuovo" or "verso l'alto", one can recognize the term κεφάλαιον (cima, capitolo) but also κεφαλή (capo). Therefore, in the biblical expression "to recapitulate all things", rendered by the Italian Bible "to lead back to Christ, one head all things" (ἀνακεφαλαιώσασθαι τά πάντα ἐν τῶ Χριστῶ) two meanings converge, to *give back one head* and to *erect*, probably not in an alternative way, but indicating both things in the same event.65 The expression means that in Christ:

- are contained, summed up in summary form, all things, first and foremost the saving works of God;
- all things are restored or established, that is, founded;
- everything finds a leader or must be placed under his sovereignty (universal submission before explicit).

This universal recapitulation of the Incarnate Word as the Redeeming Word has a "cosmic" influence: it includes "all things in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible" (*Col* 1:16; cf. *Eph* 1:11), "not only in the present time but also in the future". (*Ef* 1:21).

##### Christ, fulfillment of time, enters history but transcends history
The mystery of the Incarnation - more generally the Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ -, the fullness and fulfillment of Revelation, is preached by Sacred Scripture as the fullness of the meaning of all history. In fact, the *Revelation*, in which the glorious Christ is indicated as the Alpha and the Omega, the beginning and the end, He who was, who is and who comes (cf. *Rev* 1:8; 21:6); the *Letter to the Hebrews,* which presents us with an **eternal priesthood, capable of exercising a definitive mediation because it is eternally relevan**t (cf. *Heb* 13:8; 9:24-26); the Christological hymns of the *Letter to the Ephesians* and the *Letter to the Colossians*, which present the mystery of Christ as the mystery to which history points and which is itself capable of summarizing the entire history of the cosmos (cf. *Eph* 1:9-10; *Col* 1:16-18).

What is the relationship between the chronological-progressive nature of time and the fullness of time brought by the Paschal Mystery of Christ as a salvific event that is manifested and accomplished in a specific place and time? In order to understand this relationship it is necessary to reflect on the fact that **the fullness and fulfillment of which we speak not only depend on the chronological flow of temporal events, but are determined above all by the fact that God himself breaks into history, giving it meaning**. The fact that history belongs to God and that God enters, in Christ, into history, makes time available to receive a *sacramental form,* because it is intercepted and dragged by the Paschal Mystery.

In the study of the relationship between Jesus Christ and history it must be kept in mind that the mystery of the **Incarnate Word enters history with the temporal concreteness** of the annunciation to Mary, of her human life in Israel at the time of King Herod, of her passion and death under Pontius Pilate, **but at the same time transcends it**, just as the glorious body of Jesus, who rises from the dead and ascends/seats at the right hand of the Father, transcends it.

In the language of the NT the term "time" is indicated with **χρόνος** (corresponding to the Hebrew word *eth*) or with **καιρός** (corresponding to the Hebrew *zemán*). **The first indicates a quantitative time, i.e. the simple duration of time, while the second indicates a qualitative time, i.e. an opportune, propitious time, as is a time especially salvific.**

With the event of the Incarnation, **Christ *completes the fullness of time,* both qualitatively and quantitatively.** The *qualitative* fulfilment is witnessed by the words of Jesus in the Gospel of Mark at the beginning of his public life: "the time (καιρός) is fulfilled" (*Mk* 1:15); it is also the fullness of time that corresponds to the economy of the saving plan of the Father to recapitulate all things in Christ (εἰς οἰκονομίαν τοῦ πληρώματος τών καιρῶν: *Eph* 1:10). The *quantitative* fulfillment is present in the Pauline teaching: "when the fullness of time came (τό πλήρωμα τοῦ χρόνου) God sent his Son, born of woman" (*Gal* 4:4); but it seems to allude to it also in the *Letter to the Hebrews,* in a passage which, even if it does not use the Greek word for "time", but "eon", or "century", speaks of a convergence of the centuries, almost a precipitation of time: "now, once and for all, in the fullness of time (ἐπὶ συντελεία τῶν αἰώνων), he appeared to undo sin through self-sacrifice" (*Heb* 9:26).

##### Christ, qualitative fulfillment and chronological fullness of history
The affirmation that in Christ time reaches its *qualitative* fullness descends fundamentally from the consideration that, **in Him, God has told us everything, because in His Son made flesh *God has said Himself*.** In Christ, *fulfilment of* the Promise, **the definitive covenant is expressed**, because after Him there is no other divine revelation to wait for. **In Him the divine offering and response become the greatest possible.** The silence of Christ Jesus on the Cross, who died for our love, is in a certain way the Father's most eloquent word to man and the world; but it is at the same time the greatest word that a created humanity, that of Christ-Son, can express in obedience and abandonment to God the Father. The mission of the Spirit will not have as its task to add other words to Revelation, but to lead to the depths of truth the Word delivered in Christ: for this reason, the mission of the Spirit will be to make constantly present the relationship that the Son has with the Father (cf. *Rom* 8:15; *Gal* 4:6), to place the word of the Son in the mouth of believers (cf. *1 Cor* 12:3), and to repeat to the Church-Bride, until the end of time, "come Lord Jesus" (*Rev* 22:17.20).

The Word made flesh also carries with it a chronological, i.e. *quantitative*, fullness of history. **This does not mean that time and history ended 20 centuries ago, but rather that the history following** **the earthly event of Christ does not look to Him as a past, but as a continuous present**. Christ, who "it's the same yesterday, today and always" (*Heb* 13:8), **thanks to the redemption accomplished in his blood, he becomes present in every age and contemporary to every man who comes to life.** **Every man, in his life and in his death, has Jesus and his mystery as his travelling companion because "by the Incarnation, the Son of God has united himself in a certain way to every man"** (GS, 22).

The sacramental perspective of Christian times is fully in accord with **the *sacramentality of the Church,* whose aim is precisely to make Christ present in every age,** through the relevance of the preaching of the Word and the salvific relevance of the sacraments. **Having Christ always with her and being His mystical body, the Church is together *in* time and *out of* time**, actualizing with her life the eternal present of her Spouse-Savior (cf. *Eph* 1:22-23). This tension between present and future is paradigmatically summed up in the sacrifice of the Holy Mass. The Eucharist celebrated on our altars is not a sacrifice different from that made by Christ on the cross, nor does it involve a different priest or a different victim. Through the re-proposal of the gestures of his mysterious-sacramental anticipation made by Jesus on Holy Thursday, the Mass re-actualizes and re-presents in time the same salvific sacrifice of the Cross made on Good Friday, illuminating all the preceding and following history with the relevance of that salvation. The author of the *Letter to the Hebrews* highlights with extraordinary clarity the over-temporality and perennial relevance of Christ's sacrifice: "For Christ did not enter a sanctuary made by human hands, the figure of the true one, but in heaven itself, to appear now before God on our behalf. And not to offer himself several times, like the high priest who enters the sanctuary every year with the blood of others: in this case he should have suffered many times since the foundation of the world. But now, in the fullness of time, he has appeared once, in the fullness of time, to undo sin through self-sacrifice" (*Heb* 9:24-26). The new covenant, unlike the old, is eternal, and is fulfilled in a blood that is not only sprinkled on Israel, but shed for all.

The time that separates us from the Sabbath of glory, from the Parousia, is not time for progress, but time for eschatological fulfillment. At the end of time we will manifestly achieve what has already been given to us once and for all and what Christ has already conquered through his death and resurrection. The novelty of Parousia will not be essential. The new creation, in fact, does not depend on any mystery other than that of Christ (cf. *Rev* 21:5-6). The new heavens and the new earth do not belong to a new saving economy, but are the eschatological fullness of the messianic era; they are the state of a creation reconciled with the Father by Christ in his blood (cf. *Col* 1:20); they are the condition of a creature already conceived that must come to light (cf. *Rom* 8:22-23); they are the situation of a world already recapitulated in Christ, which must be brought back to the Father by the Son in the Spirit (cf. *Eph* 1:9-10); they are the first fruits of a Spirit who has already been given to us and who belongs to us as a deposit of our inheritance (cf. *Eph* 1:13-14).

##### 2.4.2 Christ, fulfilment of the word of the Covenant
##### A new and eternal alliance
In the event of the Incarnation, the idea and offering of a Covenant between God and mankind find their highest conceivable expression. God not only comes to meet man but joins him, taking on a created human nature. The place in the NT where such fullness is particularly exposed is the *Letter to the Hebrews*. The logic of this document is to reaffirm the superiority of this new covenant by basing it on the superiority of the priesthood of Jesus Christ, the only one capable of fully satisfying the praise and worship due to God. His mediating sacrifice is put in explicit and direct relation with the *new* covenant prophesied by Jeremiah:

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Jews* 8.6-13

Just as the name of Yahweh was par excellence the name of the saving covenant, so that of Jesus, whose meaning is "Yahweh saves", inherits all the strength of its content.

Among the effects of the Sinaitic alliance was that of placing the people who were its repositories in a new relationship with God: they were revealed to him a *sonship*, which could still be understood, at that moment, with the sense of a metaphor: "You are *children* for the Lord, your God [...]. For you are a people consecrated to the Lord your God, and the Lord has chosen you to be his particular people among all the peoples who are on earth" (*Dt* 14:1-2; cf. *Ex* 4:21-23; *Dt* 8:5). The awareness of this sonship is reconfirmed in the renewal of the promise of salvation made to David (cf. *2 Sam* 7:14) and it acquires in the wisdom literature the characteristics of goodness, generosity and mercy (cf. *Sir* 23:1, 51:10; *Wisdom* 2:16), and then reaches tones of great tenderness in the prophets (cf. *Jer* 31:20; *Hos* 11:3-4). However, it is in the NT where the intimacy of **a true filial adoption is offered to believers, a participation of the natural sonship of the Son of God made man**, based on the sharing of his own Spirit (cf. *Rom* 8:23; *Gal* 4:4-6).

Similarly, the *spousal analogy,* typical of the offering of the covenant in the OT, violating which was considered infidelity and prostitution for the people of God (cf. *Ex* 20:5; *Is* 43:4; 49:15; 54:7-10; *Jer* 31:20; *Hos* 2:21-25), also finds its full meaning in the Incarnation of the Word. Christ is the true bridegroom of the Church, a bridegroom who embodies all the love of God Israel for his people and reveals to what extreme God's condescending love was able to go (cf. *Eph* 5:22-32). **Ultimately, the covenant between God and his people is now as close as the union between Christ and his own body, which is the Church** (cf. *1 Cor* 12:12-13.27).

The consummation of *sacrifice*, an act that sanctioned the ancient covenant and its renewals, finds in the word made flesh a new reason for fullness and fulfillment. Sacrifice external, but also internal, as was from the beginning the moral demand to live the Law associated with the covenant. This is underlined, once again, one of the main themes of the *Letter to the Hebrews* (cf. *Heb* 10:1-24):

- true sacrifice are not those of the ancient law, but the cross of Christ; to this sacrifice corresponds a new law, the only one that has the power to lead to perfection (cf. v. 1);
- is sacrifice of one's own body, not like the extrinsic body of goats or lambs (cf. vv. 10.5-8);
- is a unique sacrifice, with a definitive value: it will no longer be necessary to multiply the sacrifices, as in the economy of the ancient alliance (cf. vv. 10,11-12);
- Those who are sanctified by this sacrifice are associated with it, live it in their own lives, thus obtaining forgiveness of sins and purification of heart (cf. vv. 10:19-24).

The sacrifice was linked to the *outpouring of blood*, especially the blood of the flawless Easter lamb, which on the eve of the first Easter ensured salvation from the exterminator and heralded the beginning of the Exodus (cf. *Ex* 12:5-7 and 12:21-24). The blood of sacrifice was also shed on the people in the renewals of the Sinaitic alliance made during their wanderings in the desert (cf. *Ex* 24:6-8). The covenant made by Jesus Christ takes on the character of a reconciliation *in his blood* and is now called "new and eternal" (*Lk* 22:20; *2 Cor* 5:18-21; *Col* 1:19-20). The strength of such an alliance lies in the capacity to renew-reconcile all creation and to make man himself a new creature. Christ Jesus is the Lamb immolated at the centre of John's apocalyptic vision, who keeps the signs of his sacrifice and who, in making all things new, renews the *status of* the relationship between God and mankind (cf. *Rev* 21:4-5).

In Christ, then, not only the form but also the very content of the covenant reaches its fullness: God's truth-faithfulness, his mercy and forgiveness, his justice and his love for mankind (cf. *Jn* 3:16-17). Giving the world its only Son, God gives Himself.

##### Christ fullness and fulfillment of promises in the Promise
Jesus Christ manifests in his person and in his work of redemption the figure of that *promised inheritance of* which the people of Israel was the depositary, exceeding expectations. The NT reveals to us that our inheritance is now the same as that of the Son (cf. *Rom* 8:17), indeed this inheritance is the Son Himself, with whom we receive everything else, because He who did not hesitate to spare for us His only Son, has committed Himself to give us everything else together with Him (cf. *Rom* 8:32). It is a fulfilment and an inheritance worthy of us thanks to his Paschal Mystery: "Therefore he is the mediator of a new covenant, so that, having intervened his death as ransom for the transgressions committed under the first covenant, those who have been called may receive the eternal inheritance which has been promised" (*Heb* 9:15).

In the economy of the Old Testament alliance there was the promise of a numerous lineage, as extensive as the stars of heaven, in which all the peoples of the earth would be blessed. By his universal redemption, Christ gives final fulfillment to this promise through the formation of God's new people, uniting every race, lineage and nation in the sacrifice of his blood. The *Letter to the Ephesians* will speak of the mystery accomplished in Christ of reunifying the two peoples (heirs of the promise and Gentiles) into one people (cf. *Eph* 2:10-22; cf. also *Col* 3:9-11), to the point of bringing them together in one body which is his own Body, that is, the Church (cf. *Eph* 1:22-23; *Col 1:*18; *1Pt* 2:9-10). The scope of this unifying plan is absolutely universal because all, without exception, have been created and elected sons in the Son to form the new Israel, according to a lineage where the circumcision that sanctions their belonging will be that of the heart.

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Ephesians* 2.10-18

The experience of liberation, so strongly connected to the consciousness of Israel as a people and its covenant with Yahweh, now becomes an experience of forgiveness and liberation from sin and death. Jesus Christ, Truth in person, free from ignorance and error (cf. *Jn* 8:31). In the letters to the *Romans* and the *Galatians*, it is liberation from slavery of an extrinsic observance of the Law, to move definitively towards a "new law" that can only be realized in the freedom of love. Experience of forgiveness and liberation for those whom sin had led to no longer being God's people.

The Levitic priesthood and the special relationship that it implied between a part of the people and God, is now assumed and transfigured into the new priesthood of the new law, extended to the whole people of God, participation in the one priesthood of Christ: "you, on the other hand, are a chosen lineage, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people that God has acquired [...]. Once you were non-population, now you are the people of God (*1Pt* 2:9-10)". (see also LG, 9).

Finally, the promise of a Redeemer, who did not enter directly into the economy of the covenant, is fulfilled in Christ in a way unexpected for Israel, with the very presence of God in the midst of his people. It is God himself this Redeemer and this Shepherd, it is God himself, in his only Son, who satisfies for the sins of all men.

The second, taken from the *Baptismal Catecheses* of St. John Chrysostom, explains how the promises of the ancient covenant, led by Moses, are now brought to fullness in the new covenant made in Christ:

##### Jesus Christ reveals the fullness of the Law in the precept of charity
With its Incarnation, the Word perfects and completes the Law in many ways. First, Jesus Christ adds to the Sinaitic Law the inner grace necessary to live it. The justice of which the Decalogue proposed to remember and codify was not fully practicable by the sinful man, but now becomes a possible path thanks to the example of Jesus of Nazareth and the inner gift of the Spirit: "What was impossible for the Law, rendered powerless by the flesh, God made possible: by sending his Son into a flesh similar to that of sin and because of sin, he condemned sin in the flesh, so that the justice of the Law might be fulfilled in us". (*Rm* 8.3-4). The "new law" is not only teaching, but also grace to fulfill it. According to the expression of Thomas Aquinas: "Lex nova est ipsa gratia Spiritus Sancti, quae datur fidelibus" (S.Th., I-II, q. 106, a. 1, c). Here, therefore, the prophecy of a new law placed in the heart of man is fulfilled, that of a new heart acting under the action of the Spirit of God (cf. *Jer* 31:31-34; *Ez* 36:27; cf. *Rom* 8:14-15; *Gal* 5:18).

The solemn, ancient promulgation of the Decalogue is now accompanied by the living proclamation of the Gospel beatitudes (cf. *Mt* 5:1-12; *Lk* 6:20-22). Christ does not come to abolish the ancient law, but to fulfill it (cf. *Mt* 5:17), he comes to indicate its observance in a spirit of authenticity and sincerity, before God and not in the eyes of men. The Sermon on the Mount is also proclaimed with an authority that places Jesus Christ on the same level as Yahweh (cf. *Mt* 7:28-29). Christ himself sets himself as the source and end of the judgement operated by the Law: the judgement of blessing or condemnation, of acceptance or refusal does not have as its reference an abstract norm, but his person, being "what has been done to him" (*Mt* 25:40.45).

The centrality of Jesus Christ in the economy of the "new law" is seen by the NT in the light of the theological virtues, *faith*, but first of all *charity*. It is through faith in Christ that we are saved, without which the legalistic observance of the works would have no meaning, since the meaning of the whole Law was that of a preparation for the Gospel: in this regard, the law *ends* with Christ (cf. *Lk* 16:16; *Rom* 10:3). In the Johannine vocabulary, what God asks of men as the beginning and summary of their moral action is "faith in him whom the Father has sent" (*Jn* 3:14-18; 5:24; 6:29; etc.).

The true observance of the Law finds its fullness in a single precept, that of charity (cf. *Gal* 5:13-14). But if the whole Law can be summed up in one word, this is possible because Christ, the Father's one and final word, has the capacity to sum up the Law in Himself as *His law*: "bear one another's burdens and thus you will fulfil *the law of Christ*" (*Gal* 6:2). In revealing the primacy of charity (cf. *Mt* 22:34-40; *Lk* 10:25-28), Christ teaches the need to live the Law in an interior way, with sincerity before God, and not according to an extrinsic justice linked to legal works (cf. *Mt* 5:20). This is what leads to the true fulfilment of the moral Law and makes those who fulfil it perfect (gr. τέλειοι) as the heavenly Father is (cf. *Mt* 5:48). In Christ one can understand that the Law has a filial character, because the law of children is the law of love and not of fear (cf. *1 Jn* 4:18). The new evangelical law can live only in the *freedom of charity*: this perfection of the Law is most in conformity with the divine image in man, an image of which Christ is the true restorer.66

A moral reflection on the Christian message therefore shows that charity and freedom converge: it is the meaning of the Augustinian aphorism "Dilige, et quod vis fac" or the Thomistic aphorism "Quanto aliquis plus habet de caritate, plus habet de libertate". The NT sees them both connected to the filial condition and to the presence of the Spirit (cf. *2 Cor* 3:17; *Rom* 5:5; *Rom* 8:2.14-15:21-23:29).

##### 2.4.3 Christ, Wisdom of God
The moral and sapiential teaching transmitted by the Old Testament revelation also reaches its fulfilment in Christ the Master. Jesus' preaching reveals the "way" of a life practice that leads to true wisdom, and with it to an encounter with God. Moreover, He Himself is this way and the place from which every rule of wise behaviour emanates. He is "more than Solomon's Wisdom" (cf. *Mt* 12:42; *Lk* 11:31) and this justifies why we must tend to Christ with a force greater than that which the righteous man in the OT placed in the tendency to wisdom.67

Already in chapter 3 of the prophet Baruch, and in accordance with a progressive specification of the link between Israel and Wisdom, a long eulogy of divine Wisdom had resulted in declaring her dwelling place in Jerusalem and her apparition among men:

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Baruc* 3.13-15.22-24.32-38

Although the Christological title "Wisdom of God" (*1Cor* 1:24) will be affirmed especially in the post-Neotestamental period, the NT contains important passages that make it possible to associate the Incarnate Word with the fullness of divine Wisdom, attributing to it some of the characteristics that OT typically attributed to it. In the Christological hymn of the *Letter to the Colossians* we find a reference to Christ as "the image of the invisible God, begotten before all creatures" (*Col* 1:15), He, the firstborn son in whom all things were created (cf. Col 1:16), similarly to what is said about the Wisdom of God (cf. *Wisdom* 7:26 and 9:9; *Prv* 8:22-25; *Sir 1:*4 and 24:9), He in whom all things find subsistence (cf. *Col 1:*17; *Wisdom* 1:7). However, the NT does not realize any explicit identification between Jesus Christ and Wisdom, either because it wants to avoid associating directly to the Incarnate Word those subordinate notes that the Old Testament Wisdom possessed with regard to Yahweh, or because it wants to show that the Incarnate Word surpasses the wisdom to which the righteous of OT aspired.

Many of Jesus' parables and discourses are part of the wisdom tradition of Israel, as reported, for example, in the *Book of Syracuse*. The invitation to assume the gentle and light yoke of Christ, the source of peace, expressed in the Gospel of Matthew (cf. *Mt* 11:28-30), recalls the same invitation made by Syracuse: "Come closer, you who are uneducated, take up residence in my school. Why do you want to deprive yourself of these things when your souls are so thirsty? I opened my mouth and said, "Buy it for you without money. Put your neck to its yoke, and your soul accept the instruction: it is close to those who seek it."" (*Sir* 51.23-26; see also *Sir* 6.23-25.27-28). There can also be parallels between the "banquet" which wisdom provides for those who receive it (cf. *Prv* 9:1-6; *Sir* 24:19-21) and the Eucharistic banquet in which Jesus Christ gives himself as nourishment to his disciples. The fact, then, that the liturgy of the Church has attributed to B.V. Mary the well-known passages of *Prv* 8 and *Sir* 24 must be interpreted from the point of view of a certain inseparability between the Incarnate Word and she who, generating him as man, is rightly called the Seat of Wisdom.

In Christ, above all, a "wisdom of the Cross" is accomplished which surpasses all human knowledge and which gives, to those who know how to accept it, a perfect understanding of God's plans for creation and for man, becoming salvific wisdom. Wisdom that can only be recognized and accepted through the action of the Spirit. Clear and explicit, in this regard, the Pauline teaching on the insufficiency of human wisdom which, in the context of *1Corinthians*, does not indicate the Wisdom of OT but a proud reason, unable to rise above the human, the sensitive, because blinded by its own self-sufficiency:

*[Embedded Image]*
 *1 Corinthians* 1.20-25 and 2.1-7

In Jesus Christ the moral virtues preached by the sapiential tradition of *Proverbs* and *Syracuse are fulfilled, the* aspirations and prayers of the *Psalms are fulfilled,* the paradoxical novelty with which God Himself wished, in the Incarnate Word, to assume upon Himself the finiteness and transience complained of by *Qoelet*. Finally, in Christ the questions about the meaning of human suffering and innocent pain posed by the *Book of Job* will be answered. Job himself will be presented as a figure of Jesus Christ, as happens for example in the moral commentary on the book of the same name by Gregory the Great and in the reflections of Antonio Rosmini.68

##### 2.4.4 Christ, fullness and fulfillment of the prophetic word
In the OT the prophetic ministry was the living experience of a special relationship with the divine word; the prophet presented himself to the people as a witness to the Law and the holiness of God, manifesting a unique relationship with history as upright and guided by Him who is Lord of history. In Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word, the prophetic word reaches its fullness in each of these aspects. The mystery of God made man brings to fruition what was announced by the Patriarchs, by the prayer of Israel and by the Prophets; especially the messianic promise of which the prophecy of the OT had made itself announcer and interpreter. But Jesus Christ is also the true Master, not only the one who receives the word of Yahweh to transmit it to his people, but *the very Word of Yahweh* made flesh. Christ does not speak to men in the name of God, but it is God himself who speaks to them in human words. In Him the relationship between prophecy and history will also find its fullness: just as the history of Israel was foretold and interpreted by its prophets, so universal history finds the definitive announcement of its eschatological meaning in the Paschal Mystery of Jesus of Nazareth, who is judge and lord of history.

##### Jesus Christ is the very word of Yahweh made flesh...
But in Christ the prophetic ministry ceases to be a mediation with the Truth to become the offering of truth in person (*Jn* 14:6). His mediation is as immediate as possible because in this way the hypostatic union of God made man is realized. In Christ all Revelation is recapitulated in him as the Revelator.

In the mystery of the Incarnation the word is not delivered to a prophet as nourishment and disruptive force of divine proclamation, but is incarnated (cf. *Jn* 1:14). What makes itself visible is not a generic divine word, but the pre-existing Word, that which is at the beginning and through which the world was made (cf. *Jn* 1:1-3).

It is the Father himself who bears witness to the Son in the solemn theophanies of baptism and transfiguration: it is a greater witness than that given by God to Moses, because - precisely in this last theophany - it is at the centre and at the summit of the revelation of the Law and the Prophets (cf. *Mt* 17:5). The centrality of Jesus between Moses and Elijah is more than a simple vision transmitted by the evangelists: it is the visual instruction on the value of the fulfillment of all the Scriptures in Jesus, a fulfillment which is realized, precisely, in his Paschal Mystery: "Moses and Elijah, appearing in glory, spoke of his exodus, which was about to take place in Jerusalem" (*Lk* 9:30-31).

The authority with which Christ teaches, finally, as already observed, is the same authority of God, both for the expressions used and for the object involved, that is, the Law, the Sabbath, the Temple (cf. *Mt* 5:22-28 etc.; *Jn* 8:51-58). Christ is more than Jonah (cf. *Mt* 12:41), more than Solomon (cf. *Mt 12:*42), more than the temple of Jerusalem (cf. *Mt* 12:16). The uniqueness and authority of his word flow into the explicit revelation of his divinity, into his being God and being one with the Father. The divinity of Jesus, which John will testify especially through the speeches in which Jesus speaks of the Father, the Synoptics bear witness to it just as explicitly by emphasizing the transcendence of his authority, as it emerges in his teachings as Master to the crowd.

##### Jesus Christ fulfills the contents of the word given to the Prophets
Jesus is not a prophet, but his whole life is presented as a prophecy, a *prophecy of the Father,* the word of the Father addressed to men, a historical word that has in itself the features of what is definitive and will not be removed.69 Jesus Christ in fact represents, in the scan of salvific history, that "Prophet who is to come," a character whom Israel awaited as preacher of the coming of the Messianic era. This is how his contemporaries speak of him (cf. *Mt* 21:45: *a* prophet; *Jn* 7:40: *the* prophet who must come) and this is how the Baptist himself seems to understand him (cf. *Jn* 1:21).

Jesus acts like a prophet: he teaches, interprets the Scriptures, reveals future events, performs miracles, exhorts people to faithfulness to the covenant, announces God's mercy and judgment. Although Jesus never applied the title of prophet to Himself, because He was not a prophet among others, the crowds and individuals considered Him as such (cf. *Mt* 21:10-11; *Mk* 6:15; *Jn* 4:19). In fact, the prophecies of the Servant of Yahweh (Isaiah) are fulfilled in him, those of a universal shepherd in whom God himself feeds his people (Ezekiel), that of a stable kingdom, of Davidic descent, which will never end (Samuele-Natan), those relating to the inauguration of a new kingdom where the law is lived in the heart of man (Jeremiah) and harmony and peace reigns (Isaiah); in him are fulfilled above all the prophecies concerning the coming of the Messiah, or Christ, literally the *Anointed One of God,* the "son of David" (Psalms, Prophets in general).

In particular, Jesus Christ is the *fulfillment of the messianic promise*. He applies to himself, in a solemn way and at the beginning of his public life, the messianic prophecy of Isaiah (cf. *Lk* 4:16-21). This is how Philip speaks to Nathanael: "We have found him of whom Moses was written in the law and the Prophets, Jesus, son of Joseph, of Nazareth" (*Jn* 1:45). To the Samaritan woman who says "I know that the Messiah must come, when he comes, he will announce everything", Jesus himself will answer: "it is I who speak to you" (*Jn* 4:25-26). The Christological confession of Peter, on which the three synoptics are unanimous (cf. *Mt* 16:16; *Mk* 8:29; *Lk* 9:20) and that of Martha reported in the Fourth Gospel (*Jn* 11:27: "I believe that you are the Christ, the Son of God who must come into the world") are equally precise messianic confessions.

##### Christ is the definitive interpreter of history
The relationship between prophecy and history takes on special importance in Christ, since he is the true interpreter of history. If the prophetic word had as its characteristic to announce and interpret the history of Israel according to the saving plans of Yahweh, in the Word made flesh these plans will have their definitive unveiling. His prophecy about history has the character of an eternal word, more stable than heaven and earth (cf. *Mt* 24:35). The event of his Incarnation is the qualitative fulfilment of the times (cf. *Mk* 1:15). The whole scene of the world, created, fallen and redeemed, finds its definitive salvific interpretation in the Paschal Mystery of the dead and risen Christ. Man and the cosmos find in Christ their complete and deepest exegete. All the history following his Easter event will be irresistibly marked by the grace of his cross and resurrection, and guided by the sending of his Spirit into the world.

The role of Jesus, judge of history, emerges in the eschatological discourses of the Synoptics (*Mt* cc. 24-25; *Mk* c. 13; *Lk* 21,5-36). His prophetic judgment involves the whole universe, visible and invisible things. He is the figure of the *Son of Man*, judge of humanity, presented by the eschatological speeches of the synoptics and the visions of the Redeemer of the Book of *Revelation*. History must open itself to its definitive revelation: on the acceptance or rejection of Jesus, the Son sent by the Father, the world will be judged. He is the God *who returns on the last day*, the day of Yahweh. On this day, and before Christ the judge, the thoughts and works of all will be revealed, that is, revealed.

##### 2.4.5 The Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ: the articulation between death and resurrection as the culmination of Revelation
Jesus brought Revelation to completion with his Incarnation, in the fullness of time, and through his entire earthly life. Every gesture, word, action or sign of Christ is always revealing. However, the word and the testimonial gestures par excellence are those of his *paschal mystery,* the events that is, and at the same time the mystery, of his passion, death and resurrection.

The whole evangelical narrative is presented as a *great preparation of* the account of the "events that took place in Jerusalem". The entire apostolic preaching will be nothing more than the proclamation of the death and resurrection of Christ as the fulfilment of what God has willed to communicate/donate to humanity. Apostolic preaching will be the proclamation of the decisive importance that such events have for the salvation of humanity. Paul illustrates the Paschal Mystery in all its meta-historical dimension, the fulfilment of a silent sketch through previous centuries and now revealed/realized in Christ, recapitulation and reconciliation with an absolutely cosmic scope.

The evangelizing mission of the apostles consisted from the beginning in presenting in a living and existential way the announcement (gr. κέριγμα) of an event with salvific consequences, that is, that Jesus had died *for our sins* and had *risen from the dead* (cf. *Acts* 10:39-43). They claim that they cannot silence what they have seen and heard (cf. *Acts* 4:20), what they have contemplated and what their hands have touched: the Word of life, eternal life who was with the Father and who made himself visible to us (cf. *1Jn* 1:1-4). The density of meaning and salvific energy of the Paschal Mystery is such that Paul can declare himself satisfied with "knowing nothing in your midst but Jesus Christ, and him crucified" (*1 Cor* 2:2).

The Incarnate Word, the fullness of the Word of the Father, reveals by his very presence all that the Father has communicated to us: as we have already seen, in him converge the noetic and hermeneutic fullness of Revelation. The fullness of divine revelation resides, however, in the crucified and risen word, not in the simple word as embodied. It is in the passion, death and resurrection of Jesus that the condescension of the God of Israel achieves its highest figure and the Father's saving plan its definitive revelation. The crucified and risen word is the only word capable of deserving entry into the history of the stable gift of the Spirit.

In the Paschal Mystery, therefore, we encounter both the hermeneutical fullness of Revelation, because the wisdom of the Cross offers the supreme principle for interpreting *the meaning of what God has to tell us*; and its noetic fullness, because the Cross and the Resurrection of Christ reveal once and for all the *figure of what it meant* for God to create and love in his Son the creature made in his image and likeness.

Following Luther's initial intuition, the Reformists' thought emphasized that God's weakness and concealment on the Cross express the greatest and most abysmal divine revelation, the revelation of his greatest power, that of being able to annul himself (*kénosis*) in favor of man. Thus a *theologia crucis* takes shape, sometimes seen in opposition to a *theologia gloriae, to the* point of believing that the summit of Revelation is touched in the paradox of annulment (Moltmann) and, for reason, in the scandal of contradiction (Barth). Catholic thought has recently made a certain recovery of the positive values contained in such a *theologia crucis* (e.g. with H.U. von Balthasar), without opposing it to a perspective that sees in the resurrection the definitive fulfillment of the divine logic of revelation and salvation.

Two points need to be kept in mind here. First of all, the death and resurrection of Christ, for the purposes of the theology of revelation, are *inseparable*: it is the divinity of Jesus, definitively manifested in his resurrection, that makes us understand the whole depth of revelation *in the scandal of the cross*. Without resurrection the cross remains foolishness, and what God would like to communicate to man, unfinished. Paul's thought, which does not discount the scandal and paradox of the *kénosis* of the cross (cf. *1Cor* cc. 1 and 2) is, on this point, explicit (cf. *1Cor* c. 15). Secondly, the main biblical categories, from creation to salvation, from God's faithfulness to his covenant with men, from divine sonship to the realism of the promises associated with that sonship, would not find their full fulfilment except in the resurrection of Jesus. Moreover, if it is true that it is the sacrifice of the cross that constitutes, from an objective point of view, the accomplished figure of divine love for man and man's love for God, that is, the love of the Man-God Jesus Christ for His Father-God, it is equally true that the full revelation of *Who is the subject of* this sacrifice, and therefore of how much it costs to his victim, even objectively, is only had in the Resurrection. The divinity of the Subject, and therefore the *high sensitivity* poured into his humanity, give Christ's passion and suffering an unprecedented intensity, which rightly make it the greatest sacrificial offering ever made.70

Even the relationship between revelation and faith, which at first might appear privileged when one stresses the scandal of the cross and the abandonment of death, because it requires believing in relying on a faithful God who does not betray, finds in reality an equally strong link in the resurrection, because it is *in the resurrection* that the Christian is called to believe on the word of a few witnesses, not primarily in *the death of* Jesus on the cross (but yes in its soteriological value for us), because it took place before everyone's eyes. It remains clear, however, that the articulation of the two supreme moments of human life of the Incarnate Word, that is, his death and his resurrection, maintain a *different relationship with history*: death can be circumscribed by history, while the resurrection, though passing through history, transcends it. As we shall see in chapter V of this volume, the resurrection of Christ crosses history leaving traces in it, but at the same time transcends it. In any case, the Paschal Mystery of Jesus must be considered in its unitary logic, only by keeping it firm can one fully understand its revelatory and soteriological scope, its appeal for faith, its value as a foundation for the credibility of Christianity.

#### 2.5 The Holy Spirit and his mission in the Church, in the economy of Trinitarian Revelation
Divine Revelation, God's self-communication to the world which reaches its fullness in the Incarnate Word, can only be understood within a Trinitarian framework. Therefore, it is important to examine in depth the role played by the Holy Spirit. It should also be remembered that God's revelation/communication to the world is realized precisely through the missions of the Son and the Spirit in history. If the former is rightly at the center of the theology of revelation because of the Incarnation of the Word, the latter may run the risk of remaining a little in the shadows. If the economy of the Word made flesh necessarily dominates the logic of the whole Revelation - as visually offered by the mosaics of Christ Pantocrator in the Byzantine basilicas - the economy of a Word entrusted to the Spirit makes it current and operative throughout history, making it reach all places and all peoples. **As recalled by *Verbum Domini*, "there is no authentic understanding of Christian Revelation outside the action of the Paraclete**" (n. 15).

Talking about the role of the Holy Spirit in Revelation is not an easy thing. On the one hand, there is a need to re-evaluate and deepen the many aspects in which **he is the protagonist: the inspiration of Scripture, inner revelation in people's hearts, the relationship with tradition and with the holiness of the Church, charisms given to the Magisterium regarding the authentic interpretation of the Word**, etc. On the other hand, it must be recognized that the word transmitted by the Spirit goes beyond concepts and definitions, moves along an interior and spiritual dimension, acts beyond the visible boundaries of the Church, arouses revelation and gives inspiration. A revelation in the Holy Spirit can then encounter tensions, as in the relationship between charism and institution, or misunderstandings, as in the lives of the saints, or even imply a discernment that clarifies what belongs to the faith and what is foreign to it, as has happened throughout history for those movements that, believing they follow the Spirit, have lost the path of truth and ecclesial communion.

The relationship between Revelation and the person of the Holy Spirit involves two major areas:

- his relationship with the revelation that the Father has brought to completion in the Son, of which the Spirit is the privileged witness;
- his relationship with the Church, fruit and at the same time guardian of the revealed word.

No. 15 of Benedict XVI's Apostolic Exhortation *Verbum Domini* (2010) offers a concise but effective summary of the many aspects in which the action of the Holy Spirit is carried out, in the gift of Revelation and in the history of salvation:

##### 2.5.1 The Holy Spirit in the logic of Divine Revelation
So far we have spoken of the Spirit almost exclusively in relation to the inner dimension of Revelation, to its acceptance in the heart of man. But the Holy Spirit is also the protagonist of Divine Revelation on other important levels. The following can be highlighted:

- The word of Revelation has in the first place a Trinitarian articulation: it is pronounced by the Father, coming out of the silence of his mystery, revealed to the world by the Son and in the Son, **made present in history and internalized thanks to the gift of the Spirit**.
- The Holy Spirit, "who has spoken through the prophets," is considered in a certain way the **author of Holy Scripture, responsible for the inspiration that led to its formation and transmission**.
- The Spirit, who in the immanent Trinitarian life proceeds from the Father and the Son, in the historical economy of the Son's mission **is the protagonist of the gift of the Incarnation**, which is also realized through Him, the source and reason for every divine gift in the order of creation.
- The Spirit plays a significant role in **guiding the whole earthly mission of Christ**, that is, of Him who is Anointed in the Spirit, thus **cooperating with what the Incarnate Word works and reveals**, making it possible to speak of a "**pneumatological Christology**".
- The Spirit is **present in the Paschal Mystery** of Jesus Christ: he is present in the fulfillment, on **the Cross, of the Son's love for the Father**, which reaches as far as his *tradidit spiritum*; and he is the Spirit who, according to the primitive Christian proclamation, makes Jesus rise again.
- Already in Jesus' promises, the mission of the Spirit with regard to Revelation involves a special relationship with the "group of twelve", the Apostles; this mission effectively guides the formation and development of the Church, a community called by the Word to serve and spread the Gospel.
- The Holy Spirit, whose invisible presence has accompanied the whole history of salvation, inaugurates with Pentecost a visible mission which flows from the fullness of the Paschal Mystery of the Son and which has as its declared purpose the custody, the deepening and the perennial re-presentation in time of the revealed word and its fruits of salvation.
- **The Holy Spirit enriches the Church with those gifts and charisms** that ensure the indefectibility of her mission, that of making salvific Revelation present in time, through a sacramental economy that visibly structures the Church and invisibly vivifies her.

The Holy Spirit therefore possesses its own logic of revelation; He is not Jesus in "spiritual form", nor does He limit Himself to showing it, but He teaches, after the earthly event of these, to live according to Jesus, because He is *His* Spirit. **It makes sense to affirm that God continues to communicate himself to the world in the Holy Spirit, precisely because it is in the Spirit that Revelation *remains open* through the life of the Church**. The Spirit completes, completes, a work that is not only His, nor belongs exclusively to Him, because it is the work of God carried out in/by His Son, but a work of which He is a true Subject in the sacramental economy of the Church's time. Just as the Word is, in Christ, the word of God made flesh to come and dwell among men, so the Spirit is, in the Church, the presence of a soul which founds the communion of men with God and among themselves.71 These are not different stages of Revelation, as if the Son or the Holy Spirit guided and realized divine revelation in historical periods especially reserved to them; rather, they are different *logics* of revelation in relation to the new sacramental logic by which history, after the Paschal Mystery of Christ, makes the one Word delivered forever to men resound, live and progress.

##### The Spirit and the Word
In the study of the relationship between the Holy Spirit and Revelation, the major categories in the Old Testament related to the Spirit of Yahweh play an important role. Such are, for example, the *wind* as an expression of divine power, the spirit as a *breath and breath of* life, the source of life for man and for the whole of creation. In relation to the prophets, the *Spirit of Yahweh* has very similar, almost interchangeable, characteristics to those of the word: he chooses the prophets, descends upon them, guides their ministry with energy, transports them from one place to another of Israel. The Spirit is also *Spiritus creator*, that is, it has a special relationship with creation.

If it is true that the Father reveals through the Son, "who is his Word coming out of silence" (Saint Ignatius of Antioch), it is equally true that Revelation could not take place except through the Spirit: the Son and the Holy Spirit, according to the well-known expression of Saint Irenaeus of Lyons, are like "the hands of the Father". The Holy Spirit was present in the first word which gives rise to God's gift in creation (cf. *Gen* 1:2), expresses the last created word which the Church will utter to her Spouse who comes (cf. *Rev* 22:17). The *Spiritus creator* is the *Spiritus Dominum et vivificantem* "who has spoken through the prophets". He inspired the word mass in writing and vivifies that unwritten word which is the very life of the faithful, without which the letter would remain sterile.

But the spirit is not confused with the word: what characterizes it does not automatically follow the properties of the word but possesses its own characteristics.72 Like this:

- the word can be indicated and circumscribed, while the Spirit remains ineffable;
- the word almost penetrates from the outside, while the Spirit lives almost from within, without being seen;
- the word manifests a directionality and a precise recipient, while the Spirit descends and expands;
- the word reveals by making itself understood, the Spirit reveals by transforming.

Revelation is thus accomplished through the Son and the Spirit; thus its visibility and ineffability, its coming to meet man and its remaining *beyond* his predictions or representations are expressed simultaneously: if the Son is image, the Spirit surpasses every possible image; if the Son is spoken word, the Spirit is impulse and motion which, although it inspires the written word, cannot be fully translated into words. The Son expresses better the *exitus* from God towards the creature, the Spirit guides more clearly the *reditus* in the bosom of the Trinitarian life.73

The divine Word given to the world is certainly that of His Word, that is, the Son, but this word is spoken and given because of divine gratuitous love, and the condescension of Revelation therefore has in the procession of the Spirit of love its model and its norm. Just as the Spirit closes the two Trinitarian processions by expressing in his person the eternal communion of the Father and the Son, so through his temporal mission in the world, he moves believers to the inner acceptance of the word, to generate a new communion, that between the creature and the Trinity, giving access to the adopted children to the communion that they can have, with the Father, in the natural Son.

We can say that the Son is the word of the Father and that the Spirit has no other word than that of the Son. The revealed Word is primarily the word of *the Father*, and the fullness of this word addressed to man is presented in the visible mission of the Son, that is, as an extension in the history of that procession with which the Father, within the Trinitarian life, *says the Son*. But it is also a Word spoken *in the Spirit*, that is, through Love, and this already in the immanent Trinitarian life, where the Word generated responds to the generating love in an eternal dialogue of love.

##### 2.5.2 The Holy Spirit and Revelation in the Constitution *Dei Verbum
The references to the role of the Holy Spirit in the economy of Revelation presented by *Dei Verbum* of the Second Vatican Council are numerous: let us now see a systematic summary of the main ones.

- His **sending to the world** is presented as that which, in continuity with the Paschal Mystery of the Son, **accomplishes and completes Revelation**: "Therefore he [Christ], seeing the Father also, with all his presence and with the manifestation of himself, with words and deeds, with signs and miracles, and especially with his death and the glorious resurrection of the dead, and finally with the sending of the Spirit of truth, accomplishes and completes (*complicating perficit*) the revelation and corroborates it with divine witness" (n. 4).
- **His invisible mission in hearts** is necessary in order **to welcome Revelation with faith** and **to reach an ever deeper understanding of it:** "For this faith to be given, it requires the grace of God who prevents and helps, and the inner help of the Holy Spirit, who moves the heart and turns it to God, opens the eyes of the mind, and gives everyone gentleness in allowing and believing in the truth. So that then the intelligence of revelation may become ever more profound, the Holy Spirit himself continually perfects faith through his gifts" (n. 5).
- The **Apostles transmitted to their successors not only what they saw and knew through their direct experience of Christ, but also what was suggested to them by the Holy Spirit**, and it is under the **inspiration of the Holy Spirit that apostles and evangelists wrote the books of the NT**: "This was faithfully carried out, both by the apostles, who in oral preaching, by examples and institutions transmitted both what they had received from the mouth, from living together and from the works of Christ, and what they had learned by the prompting of the Holy Spirit, and by those apostles and men in their circle, who, under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, put in writing the proclamation of salvation" (n. 7).
- The **transmission (*tradere*) of Revelation continues to take place in the Church under the assistance of the Holy Spirit**: "This tradition, which originates from the Apostles, progresses in the Church under the assistance of the Holy Spirit" (n. 8).
- The word of **Revelation was put in writing with the inspiration of the Holy Spirit**; indeed, he himself entrusted this word to the apostles together with Christ, and it is **through his light of truth that this word can be preserved, exposed and spread**: "For sacred Scripture is the word of God in that it is put in writing under the inspiration of the divine Spirit; the word of God, entrusted by Christ the Lord and the Holy Spirit to the Apostles, is transmitted in its entirety from sacred tradition to their successors, so that the latter, enlightened by the Spirit of truth, by their preaching faithfully preserve it, expose it and spread it" (n. 9).
- The existence of **a single action of the Holy Spirit makes Sacred Scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium of the Church something that can exist only in their entirety, something inseparable**: "It is clear, then, that Sacred Tradition, Sacred Scripture and the Magisterium of the Church, by the most wise disposition of God, are so connected and joined together that they cannot exist independently, and that all together, each in its own way, under the action of a single Holy Spirit, contribute effectively to the salvation of souls" (n. 10). This last reference, *sub actione unius Spiritus Sancti*, was added in the fourth and last edition of *Dei Verbum,* completing a sentence in which Scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium were subjects who, each in their own way, contributed to the salvation of souls.

##### 2.5.3 The Holy Spirit and the Twelve: Revelation and the Apostolic Era
Just as in Christ the major categories of the relationship between man and God revealed in the OT (covenant, spousal love, sonship, etc.) reach their fullness, so, *thanks to the action of the Spirit*, many of the biblical categories once associated with the Chosen People find fulfilment in the Church: in the Church that messianic epoch is fulfilled in which God will pour His Spirit on all flesh (cf. *Acts* 2:14-17); the Twelve Apostles are the new foundations on which, as for the tribes which originated from the 12 sons of Jacob, the new Israel will be built (cf. *Mt* 19:28); the community of the baptized, worshippers of the Father in Spirit and truth (cf. *Jn* 4:21-24), will replace with the offering of their own prayer and the sacrifice of their whole life the worship which the people of Israel gave to God in the temple of Jerusalem (cf. *Rom* 6:13; 8:26-27; 12:1; *1 Pet* 2:4-5.9).

However, in a different way from what happened for "the hour of the Son", "the time of the Spirit" and "the time of the Church" admit a sacramental development, an *already but not yet*, which will find its full accomplishment only in an eschatological perspective, not as the achievement of an entirely new novelty, but as the fullness of what has already been deserved/conquered once and for all by the Son. This perspective solves and overcomes the problem, to which we shall refer elsewhere in this volume (see chapter V, n. 7), that is, whether or not the original Christian message foresaw a new ecclesial foundation extraneous to Judaism: the Spirit is in any case the protagonist of a new foundation, that which takes place in the heart of those who, Jew or pagan, welcome the word and decide to enter into the logic and life of the Kingdom of God, whose law is precisely that of the Spirit.

It is around the mission of the Holy Spirit that the time of Revelation is *articulated* with the time of the Church. As we have already observed on several occasions, the Paschal Mystery of Christ pays tribute to the Revelation an aspect of fulfillment: no new revelation is to be awaited before the glorious return of Christ (cf. DV, 4).

To this accomplishment belongs in a special and unrepeatable way the Church of the apostolic era. In the economy of Revelation, the testimony of the Apostles is absolutely unique, both in terms of the election they received and in terms of their frequenting Jesus Christ, who passed on to them what he did not pass on to others (cf. *Acts* 1:3; 10:39-43). The Apostles participated, through their existence beside Christ, in the fullness of the Word made flesh. This is why it is customary to say that the content of the Revelation was "formally" concluded with the death of the last of the *apostles* (cf. Decr.) Not because only the twelve apostles, expressly chosen and sent by Jesus, could still add something of their own to the fulfillment of the divine word, but rather because they, with their lives, participated in the fullness of *Christ, they* were the foundation stones of a revelation delivered in an existential way, through attendance, example, testimony.

The action of the Spirit to guide us towards "the whole truth", although it will accompany the entire history of the Church, has already been present in a very special way in the lives of the Apostles, witnesses of the experience of Pentecost: "Many things I still have to tell you, but for the moment you are unable to bear the burden. When he comes, the Spirit of truth will guide you to all the truth, for he will not speak for himself, but will say all that he has heard and will announce to you the things to come. He will glorify me, for he will take what is mine and announce it to you." (*John* 16:12-14).

##### 2.5.4 The Spirit and the Church: Revelation and Ecclesial Tradition
The awareness of the uniqueness of apostolic witness and its importance for the whole economy of Revelation have progressively fostered, in the early Church, an awareness of the existence of a content of truth to be transmitted with absolute fidelity. The same writings from the NT are witnesses to this. In his letters to Timothy (cf. *1Tm* 6,20; *2Tm* 1,14), Saint Paul refers to a *deposit* (gr. παραθήκη) which must be preserved. Judas speaks in his letter (v. 3) of a faith that has been transmitted to believers once and for all (in the sense of *semel pro semper tradita*). The same idea is present in the *Second Thessalonians*: "Therefore, brethren, stand firm and keep the traditions you have learned both from our word and from our letter" (*2 Th 2*:15) and in the *First Corinthians*: "I praise you that in all things you remember me and keep the traditions as I have passed them on to you" (*1 Cor* 11:2). Among the Fathers, Saint Irenaeus will now speak of the existence of a *regula fidei*; Council II of Nicaea (787) implicitly taught that the Church is the guardian and not the author of the revealed deposit (cf. DH 602-603).

It should be noted immediately that the insistence of the apostles and the early Church on the objective-veritative content of Revelation does not impoverish the richness of its existential-salvific meaning. In the Epistles of the NT, in fact, the exhortation to preserve the deposit is inseparable from the moral exhortation to conduct a behaviour and a testimony of life worthy of the vocation received.

The Holy Spirit remains the main protagonist of the transmission of this content, both in its aspect of "deposit" and in that of "holiness of life", understood as fidelity to a practice. He is responsible for the opening of the Word to history, and thus he who presides over its transmission, its *traditio*, thus giving rise to ecclesial Tradition.

Thanks to the gift of the Holy Spirit, the mission of the Apostles is in continuity with the mission of the Son, sent into the world by the Father. It is again thanks to the gift of the Spirit that the mission of the Church can be in continuity with the mission of the Apostles. "The Churches received from the Apostles what the Apostles received from Christ and what Christ received from God.74

In the divine logic of this fidelity/transmission, the existence of a certain *tension* between the need to faithfully transmit a stable and immutable deposit over time and the inevitable complexity of history that weakens this transmission, including the free correspondence of the faithful, should not be overlooked. This tension can only be composed through the presence of the Holy Spirit and the action of his charisms. It is in fact always the same Spirit who guides to the knowledge of the whole truth, makes the disciples credible witnesses of the Word, assures the indefectibility of the Church's mission, guarantees the infallibility of her teaching with regard to salvation. The fact that progress in the understanding of Revelation and its diffusion in ever new historical and geographical contexts requires in the first place the gifts with which the Spirit enlightens the intellect and comforts the holiness of God's people and their pastors still calls into question the role of the Spirit, the source of sanctification and unity of the Church.

In summary, the manifold action of the Holy Spirit in the transmission of Revelation:

- *founds the possibility of* a transmission-tradition of the Word,
- guarantees its saving effectiveness,
- composes the tension between fidelity to the received word-truth and the opening of the word-announcement on the history and events of the world,
- gives the gifts necessary to transmit the Word with the holiness of life,
- and those necessary for the correct understanding of his written text, which must be authoritatively taught and proclaimed.

These are the theological reasons that indicate the Holy Spirit as the "great Protagonist" of the living and authentic transmission of Revelation by the Church.

As we shall see in chapter IV of this manual, the transmission of what the Apostles received directly from Jesus constitutes the great "apostolic tradition"; its transmission by the Church, over time, constitutes the "ecclesial tradition" (cf. CCC 83) or, simply, the "Tradition". The scope of this transmission is very broad:

What was transmitted by the apostles, then, includes everything that contributes to the holy conduct and the increase of faith of the people of God. Thus the Church, in her doctrine, in her life and in her worship, perpetuates and transmits to all generations all that she is and all that she believes" (DV, 8).

The Holy Spirit, with his mission, thus gives rise to "Tradition", *keeps it open*, thus leaving Revelation itself open, not according to its *constitutive* dimension, but according to its *declaratory* dimension, in its twofold aspect of proclamation and interpretation of the Word.
