### Chapter 4 *Traditio fidei*: the transmission of revelation in the Church {#tanzella-rivelazione.1.chapter-4-traditio-fidei}
The handing over of Revelation to the Church and the Church's task to make God's saving word continually present in time, pose the theme of the *transmission of Revelation*. The very nature of history, in particular the tension existing between the evolutionary and changeable aspect of it and the stable and accomplished aspect of the word to be preached/transmitted, raises the theme of the *authenticity of transmission,* thus introducing the question of the role and nature of a Magisterium. Moreover, both the transmission of the word and its authenticity must be seen in the light of the written content of the Word, that is, of *Sacred Scripture*.

A living example of how the Church and her pastors were from the beginning aware of the saving importance of this transmission and what it entailed, is offered by Paul's "farewell speech" to the elders of Ephesus before embarking for Jerusalem at Miletus:

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Acts* 20,17-38

In this evocative page of the *Acts*, it is worth mentioning: the decision to call "the elders of the Church" (τοὺς πρεσββυτέρους τῆς ἐκκλησίας: 20.17), to whom Paul reminds of their responsibility: "watch over yourselves and over the whole flock, in the midst of which the Holy Spirit has placed you as guardians (ἐπισκόπους) to be pastors of the Church of God (τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τοῦ Θεοῦ), which has been purchased with the blood of her Son (20:28)"; the inevitable presence of rapacious wolves, even from the newly evangelized community itself, who will not spare the flock, teaching perverse doctrines to attract disciples behind them; the power with which the Lord, the word of his grace and the Holy Spirit, make possible fidelity to the mission received to preach the Gospel with integrity (cf. 20,29-30).

#### 4.1 Revelation and Church: the Church called and generated by the Word
Revelation and the Church have the same end, namely the salvation of men. There are close and reciprocal implications between Revelation and the Church: **Revelation realizes, makes the Church possible, but *in a certain sense*, the Church makes Revelation possible**: she puts it in writing, incarnates it in life, transmits it to the world. However, the main of the two causal implications is that which sees **Revelation as the cause of the Church**. The relationship is somewhat analogous to that between the Eucharist and the Church: **the Church is built by the Eucharist, but the Church also makes the Eucharist.**1

The Church is in close relationship with Revelation through her special relationship with the Word: she is the community of those who have believed the Word, she is summoned by the Word, she transmits it, she guards it, she guarantees its authenticity: "Ecclesia verbo Dei generatur, alitur, nutritur, roboratur" (Saint Augustine).

From the beginning of her mission visible in the world on the day of Pentecost, **the Church is in fact indicated as the assembly (ἐκκλησία) of believers, of those who have responded to a calling-vocation (κλήσις) who have listened and accepted the word.** Christians are in fact the called (κλητοὶ) or the chosen (ἐκλετοὶ), terms derived from the verb convene (ἐκκαλέιν). Christians, the members of the Church, are those who are "called by Jesus Christ" (*Rom* 1:6), "called by God according to his plan" (*Rom* 8:28), the victors together with the Lamb Redeemer of the apocalyptic vision, "those who are called with him, the chosen ones, the faithful" (*Rev* 17:14).

##### The Church is born from the preaching of Jesus Christ and the Apostles
**It is Jesus himself who convokes the Church through his words, his preaching, his gestures, the miracles that confirm his word and make her welcome in the faith**. He is the good Shepherd who calls his sheep one by one, and the sheep of his flock listen and recognize his voice (cf. *Jn* 10:3-4.16). The mandate received from the Father is to give his disciples the words heard from the Father, words which they have accepted, and thus to sanctify them in truth (cf. *Jn* 17:6-19). The convocation of the Church by the word is represented especially by the invitation *to enter the Kingdom and to live its needs*.

The Lord Jesus, in fact, began his Church by preaching the Good News, that is, the coming of the Kingdom of God promised for centuries in the Scriptures. [...] The word of the Lord is compared precisely to the seed that is sown in a field (cf. *Mk* 4:14): those who hear it with faith and belong to Christ's little flock (cf. *Lk* 12:32) have welcomed the very kingdom of God; then the seed by its own virtue germinates and grows until the time of harvest (cf. *Mk* 4:26-29) (LG, 5).

**The origin of the Church therefore lies primarily in the preaching of Christ, in the formation of a discipleship which follows his teachings and which, after his ascension to heaven, will bind the preaching and acceptance of these teachings to the administration of baptism (cf. *Mt* 28:19-20). Baptism becomes the sacramental-confessional moment that gives form to the incorporation into his Church.**

The generation of the Church by the word becomes evident especially in the narration of the *Acts of the Apostles*: those who listen to their word, that is, apostolic preaching, join the Church. The Apostles summon and generate the Church through the saving power of the Word: the extension of the Church is explained by saying that "the word of God grew and spread" (cf. *Acts* 12:24; 13:48-49; 17:11; 19:20). In Pauline preaching, the entry of those who welcome the word into the community of believers is compared to a true spiritual generation: "You may have ten thousand teachers in Christ, but certainly not many fathers: it is I who generated you in Christ Jesus *through the Gospel*" (*1 Cor* 4:15; cf. *2 Cor* 3:3).

##### The word that summons and generates is primarily that of the Paschal Mystery which becomes present in the communion around the Eucharistic liturgy.
But this **preaching-convocation has its summit in the Eucharistic celebration**. What the apostles announce is **not only a doctrine** - the doctrine for entering the Kingdom - **but a fact**: **Jesus Christ died for our sins and rose again for our salvation**. This *fact*, already recognized in the core of the apostolic **kerygma**, is sacramentally re-presented in the Eucharistic banquet, memorial of Christ's passion, death and resurrection. The Eucharist will progressively take on the character of a public witness of faith, of a new and perennial proclamation to the world of the risen Christ ("we announce your death, Lord, we proclaim your resurrection in expectation of your coming"). The Eucharistic supper, which from the beginning is linked to the proclamation-representing of the word, is a convocation of the baptized and a sign of fraternal communion, that in the Body of Christ to which those who convert are called. The catechumens, also summoned by the word Eucharist, will listen to the reading and exposition of the Scriptures, but will have to wait for baptism before they can take part in the breaking of bread.

From preaching is thus born communion, from the *kerygma* the *koinonía*, or *communio*. The community of the convoked identifies itself by their fraternal communion. The word accepted and put into practice generates unity, thus allowing a common participation in Christ, in the same faith, in the same goods. The Eucharist cannot but be at the centre of the nascent Church because of this unity-participation-communion is its very heart, because of its direct reference to Christ: the table of the word and the table of bread are linked by the same liturgy.[2](about:blank) The Sunday meeting "on the Lord's Day", with all that it implied for the Jewish culture in which they initially moved, is intended to maintain an explicit connection "with the events that took place in Jerusalem" (cf. *Lk* 24:18-20), and will be the first community act of Christians with progressive social relevance.

Already theological reflection within the NT, especially with Paul, is aware that the true and deepest "word" that has summoned and generated the Church is the Cross, the Paschal Mystery of Jesus' death and resurrection. The Church has been "purchased with the blood of Christ" and is especially united around the proclamation of the Resurrection (cf. *Acts* 10:34-43; 20:28; *Eph* 2:14-22; 5:25-27; *Col* 1:18-20). God's new people are born of the word which God spoke with the death and resurrection of His Son (cf. *1 Pet* 2:4-10). In John too it is the Cross that attracts everything and everyone to himself (cf. *Jn* 12:32).

In summary, we can affirm with René Latourelle that "Church and revelation, Church and word are two realities that are inextricably linked and are mutually enlivened. **The Church is both the one that convokes and the one that is summoned. Born of the Word is at the service of the Word and sign of the Word. The Bride has received the word from the Bridegroom and could not forget it. He lives by this word while awaiting the return of the Bridegroom"**.3

##### The relationship between the Church and the faith of Israel
Christ's convocation-predication shows the features, especially in Matthew's Gospel, of a convocation-renewal of the people of God, the people of Israel, so that they may prepare themselves to receive salvation; salvation which, through this people and in continuity with the promises made to the Fathers, is addressed to all the Gentiles. This design of "collecting the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (*Mt* 10:6) is common to the way of presenting the preaching and action of Christ throughout the NT and also clearly marks the attitude of the early Church narrated in the *Acts of the Apostles*. The Apostles attend the temple in Jerusalem and teach there the doctrine of Jesus. Saint Paul in all his missionary journeys began by preaching in the synagogues and, having arrived prisoner in Rome, immediately gathered the Jewish community there. It will be from the worsening rejection of the Jews when the apostolic preaching will take on the character of the convocation of a New Israel.

The relationship between the preaching of Jesus and the apostles and the intention to renew faith in Israel in the first place suggested to some authors the hypothesis that there was no intentional will in Christ to found a Church as a religious society to *replace* Israel. While it is true that this foundation may not have taken on the character of a formal act and that the consciousness of the first Christians of forming a new community, distinct from the Israelite one, admits a certain historical gradualness (we will never read *ekklesía* in Luke's Gospel, but only in the *Acts*; in the NT the expression *Church of Christ is* very rare, but it speaks instead of the Church of *God*), the *novelty of* Jesus' message and how this is recognized by the group of his apostles as a precise legacy, are nevertheless unequivocal facts. Even within the Old Testament categories (Easter dinner, prayer in the temple, call to the conversion of Israel), Jesus calls and forms a group of disciples to be witnesses of how a *new covenant, a* universal openness to all peoples, of which he is now himself and no longer Israel, the main author and mediator, is being realized in him, with his redeeming sacrifice.

In chapter V of this volume we will deal more extensively with the theme of the origin and foundation of the Church, trying to examine on what biblical data and reason it is possible to support a foundational will of Jesus Christ, explicit or at least implicit, regarding the origin of a new community formed by those who believe in Him. It is, however, possible to anticipate a few observations.

It is necessary to admit the existence, in the will of Jesus Christ, of a precise mission entrusted by him to the Twelve. This presents itself as an "extension" of the mission of the Incarnate Word, which thus continues in the mission of the Church. It is through it that Christ, in union with the inner action of the Spirit, continues to call people of every generation to salvation. The mission of the Church is born of the missions of the Son and the Holy Spirit and is their invisible extension in the world. Christ gives his word to the apostles and his successors: "he who listens to you listens to me, he who despises you, despises me". (*Lk* 10:16). The Church cannot escape this task because "faith depends on preaching" (*Rom* 10:16; cf. the whole context: vv. 10:13-17).

If Revelation had consisted in a philosophical doctrine, in a kind of human wisdom to be transmitted in the same way as the Hellenistic or Stoic schools, then it would have required teachers and preachers. But Revelation consists instead in a salvific intervention of God in history, in a message delivered at specific times and places, reached its fullness through the living example of the person of Christ. It therefore requires above all witnesses - "you will be witnesses to me": *Acts* 1:8 - before teachers. The Church, therefore, makes the word present through preaching and witness. The latter will be the subject of a specific reflection in chapter V, dedicated to the credibility of Christianity.

#### 4.2 The Church is the guardian of Revelation and the subject of its transmission throughout history: the notion of Tradition and its relationship to Sacred Scripture.
##### 4.2.1 Introduction to the relationship between Revelation and history
In order to speak about the transmission of Revelation *into history*, some clarifications are needed on the relationship between *Revelation and history*. In fact, we are in the presence of a singular relationship: on the one hand, Revelation is *accomplished through history*, because God manifests Himself through His salvific intrusion into events and through events; on the other, Revelation is in some way *subject to history,* because it presents itself with the traits of a historical development, that is, as a *history of salvation*. The desire to clarify this relationship raises some important questions about questions central to the theology of Revelation: a) Does history reveal itself by the mere fact of being history? b) Is the content of Revelation completely (and inevitably) immersed in the flow of historical becoming?

##### Delicacy of a relationship and some unsatisfactory solutions
The first of the previous questions was answered in the affirmative by W. Pannenberg (n. 1928) in his work *The Revelation as History* (1961). For Pannenberg "history is the progressive revelation of the Absolute", a perspective that can, however, go so far as to postulate an identity between history and the history of salvation, interpreting Revelation according to an idealistic-historicist scheme: the truth will be expressed in its fullness only in the *eschaton*. On the second question he tried to offer an R solution. Bultmann (1884-1976), "neutralizing" in a certain sense the scope of the problem through the development of an "existential exegesis": such an exegesis would be capable of placing us directly in meta-historical contact with the realities signified by the word of God, beyond the limits and conditioning of the flow of time. Revelation, for Bultmann, is always a word spoken *in the present*, an "act" by which God reveals Himself to the recipient of that message, inviting him to accept its salvation.

The conception advanced by Pannenberg would at first sight have certain advantages: it is in accordance with the universal character of Revelation (history, like Revelation, is before the eyes of all) and is in conformity with the idea that with the *ad extra* missions of the Son and the Spirit God himself has entered history, especially with the assumption of human nature by the Incarnate Word, thus justifying the fact that there is no longer a merely profane history. At the same time, however, the suggested perspective shows some limitations. If understood in a Hegelian-historicist framework, identification revelation = history easily leads to a more radical identification of the type reality = history = revelation. In this way it should be stated that the "fullness of revelation" would only occur in the *escathon*. The true meaning of the events will then be known only at the end of time, when they will all contribute together to clarify definitively the content of the whole, that is, of divine Revelation in solidarity with the entire history of the world, a conception which would not, however, be alien to the risk of *historicizing* God himself.4

The most coherent dogmatic perspective, present in Scripture and then taken up by *Dei Verbum*, lies rather in affirming that *history reveals when it is united to the word*. This is the meaning of the conciliar affirmation that "the economy of Revelation includes intimately connected events and words" (n. 2). Without the word that announces historical events and reveals their revelatory and salvific meaning, history, in itself, would reveal nothing. This word-history connection is paradigmatically visible in the history of Israel: it is the prophetic ministry that makes the people of God understand the meaning of their liberation and exile, the gifts they receive from God and the punishments. Without this ministry, the history of Israel would be the history of one of the many migrations of the peoples of antiquity. In the miracles performed by Christ the same correspondence is noted: the word reveals the meaning of the miraculous event and the event confirms, with its actual happening, the authenticity of the word (cf. *Mt* 9:6-7).

As for the position of Bultmann, who conceives of a Revelation *beyond history*, it may have had the merit of re-evaluating the existential value and the perennial contemporaneity of the word of God, to the point of making it a rule of its exegesis. However, it must pay the price of weakening the historicity of Revelation, and thus of the works of God, thus detaching faith from all ties with reason and historical research. The vision proposed by him completely eliminates the objective dimension of the Word, absorbing it completely into a subjective dimension; and this is not only because the Revelation is received by a subject, or because the subject helps to put it into action, but because it is in a certain way "created" by the one who announces it and by the one who receives it. To know what Jesus of Nazareth *actually said and preached* would paradoxically no longer have any meaning for the salvation of mankind... The Bultman perspective therefore closely recalls the vision of Revelation proper to modernism, where the existential aspects seemed to completely conceal the objective and historical content.

##### The guarantees of a Revelation in history
In light of the content of Revelation, evaluated according to the *fidei analogy*, the following points should be kept in mind in the relationship between Revelation and history:

- the revelatory power of history depends on the interpretative and dynamic action of the Word;
- The historical and progressive nature of Revelation consists in its tendency to Christ as the fullness of Revelation and the center of history; after the Paschal event of the dead and risen Christ, the history of Revelation develops into a new economy, that of a present which, through the Spirit, reactualizes and deepens what has been revealed and given to us once and for all in Christ;
- The historical nature of Revelation also consists in its being the fruit of freedom: freedom of God who reveals and freedom of man who welcomes the divine word; without freedom there is no history; if the conception of history is that of an ineluctable and deterministic development of events (Hegelian historicism), no significant revelation can be associated with such a history, nor can such a history be the dimension through which Revelation can take place;
- the meaning of history lies outside of history itself: "history acquires meaning and meaning only in relation to a final judgment, which is precisely the closure and the end of history. The purpose of the messianic mystery is the mystery of participation in the intimate life of God himself, which is not history, but eternity".5

To face correctly the problem of how the historical nature of Revelation, certainly immersed in the flow of history and its laws, is compatible with the perennial validity of its truthful content, it is necessary not to lose sight of two more important facts. In the first place, the God-truth to whose word must remain firm and faithful along the course of history is the same God who directs history according to its plans of salvation; a consideration, this, which has a precise Christocentric counterpart, to the reminder that the Christ to whom the Truth belongs in a personal way, is also the Christ who is the centre of the cosmos and of history. Secondly, it should not be forgotten that God Himself ensures the faithfulness of the interpretation and transmission of His Word over time, through the special assistance of His Spirit and the gift of His charisms. "The Church, which is the Bride of Christ, possesses her word as a deposit which she continually meditates on and assimilates in the light of the Spirit. Without this divinely instituted Magisterium and without this special assistance of the Spirit, we would agree that it would be impossible to conceive a doctrine, even of divine origin, that would escape the fluctuations of history".6

The encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998) contains some interesting food for thought on the relationship between Revelation and history. The ambiguity of "historicism" as a philosophical system unsuitable for understanding the *truth of* Revelation (n. 87) is noted, but a positive summary of this relationship is also offered (n. 11-12).

##### c4.2.2 The notion of Tradition in the Constitution *Dei Verbum* and its relationship with Scripture
The first reflections of the Magisterium of the Church on the nature of Tradition were born, as we have seen, in the context of the Protestant crisis with the Council of Trent. The First Vatican Council did not dedicate specific declarations on Tradition, limiting itself to recalling the Tridentine doctrine about the fact that the Gospel of salvation is contained in written books and unwritten traditions (cf. DH 1501; DH 3006) and that it is not lawful to interpret sacred Scripture against the common feeling of the Fathers of the Church (cf. DH 1507; DH 3007). However, we also find the affirmation "that the doctrine of the faith that God has revealed has been entrusted to the Church as a divine deposit, so that she may faithfully and infallibly guard it", then specifying how "the sense that sacred dogmas must always be preserved is what Holy Mother Church has determined once and for all". (DH 3020). It will be the Second Vatican Council to deal with Tradition in a widespread and profound way.7

The theme of the transmission of Revelation is introduced in n. 7 of the Constitution *Dei Verbum*. **Four main elements appear:**

- **the existence of a Gospel of salvation as the *only source of* the message transmitted by the Church (cf. Council of Trent, DH 1501);**
- **the need for its transmission into history;**
- **the introduction of the notion of "Tradition" which, in its first meaning here, means "transmission";**
- **the existence of a precise necessity, that of safeguarding the purity and fidelity of the Gospel to be transmitted, which introduces the role of the apostolic and episcopal ministry in this proclamation and in this transmission.**

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Dei Verbum* No. 7

In the Latin etymology of the term *traditio* there are the concepts of transmitting and delivering (*tradere*). Examining together nos. 7-8 of *Dei Verbum* we can recognize five mutually integrating ways of accessing the notion of Tradition.[8](about:blank) It must be recognised as the "object" of the *tradere:*

1. what has been revealed by God, namely

1. the Gospel promised to the prophets and promulgated by Christ, as the source of every salutary truth and every moral rule,

1. all that was transmitted by the apostles and which contributes to the holy conduct and increase of the faith of the people of God, in doctrine, in life, in worship.

More specifically, they are "forms" of this *tradere*:

1. the mandate to transmit this Gospel, faithfully carried out by the apostles in oral preaching, with examples and institutions;

1. the apostolic preaching later put in writing in inspired books.

In essence, the Tradition has a ***specific content*:** the apostles transmitted both what they had received from the lips, from attendance and from the works of Christ, and what they had learned by the work of the Holy Spirit. About the *nature and the manner of* this transmission we mention: the oral preaching, the examples, the institutions, the announcement made in writing with the inspiration of the Holy Spirit.

In n. 8 an important "definition" of the *object of the transmission* is offered, while, in specifying it, the *Dei Verbum* wishes to make understood all its depth and breadth:

What was transmitted by the apostles, then, includes everything that contributes to the holy conduct and the increase of faith of the people of God. Thus the Church, in her doctrine, in her life and in her worship, perpetuates and transmits to all generations all that she is and all that she believes (n. 8).

What is to be passed on to all generations, therefore, has its *only source* in what Christ revealed in fullness and commanded the apostles to preach and teach with the assistance of the Spirit. Its object is the whole reality of Christian life. The subject of this transmission is then the Church herself, which transmits the Gospel in the broadest sense of the term, as word and as life.

Always from the examination of the nos. 7-8, it is deduced that Tradition manifests the typical characteristics of Revelation as a whole: the personalism of its Author and its recipients (from the Father, to the Son, to the Church, to all men); the Christological centrality (Christ gives fulfillment to what he received from the Father and in turn hands it over to the apostles); the gratuitousness of divine initiative (born of God's benign disposition); its historicity (it progresses and is transmitted in time with life and in life); and its direct finalisation to salvation, not without a certain eschatological tension (a salvific message is transmitted while waiting for the earthly pilgrimage to end).

The term *Tradition*, used in a ***broad sense***, indicates precisely this entire transmission, what the Church has received from Christ, what she believes and what she lives. If used in the ***strict sense***, it indicates the transmission of something which, although belonging to the Gospel, has not been explicitly written in the sacred texts.

Tradition presents itself to us as a **living**, dynamic reality, open to history: it expresses the capacity with which, in time, the Church Bride of the Word *contemplates the Word, assimilates* the Word and, as a consequence, *knows how to speak of it better and better to* the world. In the Tradition both the idea of fidelity and that of progress are realized in a joint way.[9 For the](about:blank) theological understanding of Tradition, the factors of its progress are of considerable importance, as they are expounded by *Dei Verbum:*

This Tradition, which originates from the Apostles, progresses (*profit*) in the Church under the assistance of the Holy Spirit: in fact, understanding, both of things and of the words transmitted, grows both through reflection and study by believers who meditate on them in their hearts, and through the profound understanding they feel (*experiuntur*) of spiritual things, as well as through the preaching of those who have received a certain charism of truth through episcopal succession (n. 8).

Alongside the factors of progress that are apparently *negative in* character, such as the necessary clarification of errors and heresies, theological disputes or the difficulties encountered by evangelization, there are others that contribute to this with a *positive* character. First of all, the action of the Spirit in the Church, through the gift of holiness and its role in fostering the experience and intelligence of spiritual things; an intelligence strengthened by meditation on the word, study and progress in theology. Finally, the Spirit is still the protagonist of the charism of infallibility, in its various manifestations.

Within Tradition, considered in its broadest sense as the transmission of Revelation, sacred Scripture is the principal document to be transmitted. Sacred books have in fact a unique and indispensable function to identify and preserve the word that enlightens and saves. As stated by *Dei Verbum*: "everything that inspired authors or hagiographers assert is to be considered affirmed by the Holy Spirit" (n. 11). The writings of the NT have come to meet from the beginning the need to establish normative texts to preserve and transmit the apostolic tradition. An emblematic case is the decision of the first "Council" of Jerusalem, as reported in the *Acts of the Apostles*. After the speeches of Peter and James, the College of Twelve decided to determine in writing which traditions of Jewish law the Gentiles should live, so that the Gentiles would not be required to have unnecessary prescriptions and the nascent Church would develop in accordance with the teachings received from the Lord (cf. *Acts* 15:22-35).

Scripture, therefore, favours the faithful transmission of the apostolic tradition, but at the same time is dependent on it. In fact, life and the unwritten word precede Scripture chronologically and it is to the apostolic tradition that we turned to know what the inspired books were. Moreover, the sacred books were recognized and venerated precisely because they were the instruments that transmitted that Tradition, and guarded it over time.

##### 4.2.3 Criteria and places of recognition of the Tradition
When one considers Tradition in its *strict* sense, that is, as the transmission of a word delivered by Revelation but not written in sacred books, the theme of its "recognition" arises. How to distinguish between what belongs to Tradition and what is foreign to it? In essence, criteria are needed to determine which contents of the gospel and the original apostolic preaching are not contained in Scripture and to recognize where these contents have been gathered and transmitted, in order to reach us. It should not be forgotten that the subject of Tradition, even when understood in this narrower sense, is the Church itself as a whole, not the individual believer; therefore, the criteria and places of recognition of Tradition can never lack an ecclesial reference: **an individual believer can be subject of Tradition only to the extent that he or she is united with the whole Church**.

##### In search of appropriate criteria
In one of his famous texts, Saint Vincent of Lerins pointed out three fundamental criteria for the recognition of Tradition: "Id teneamus quod *ubique,* quod *semper*, quod *ab omnibus* creditum est".[11 It is](about:blank) therefore a request that a specific content or teaching should manifest: **a) *universality*, b) *antiquity* and c) *unanimity of consent*.**

In times closer to us, also thanks to the contribution of theologians like J.H. Newman, we have tried to deepen this criteriaology and, if possible, to update it. There are obvious difficulties, such as the fact that the **"unanimous consensus" is sometimes the consensus of a few authoritative writers, who have consigned their reflections to history**; moreover, it is **very difficult to establish whether a certain doctrine has been taught in different geographical regions, such as to represent the whole of Christianity**. In the recognition of Tradition, contemporary theology emphasizes that the guiding criterion for judging whether a particular doctrine, teaching or praxis belongs to the Gospel must be sought in **its connection with the Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ and with the mandate of mutual love as the fundamental law of the Christian**. Nevertheless, the so-called "Vincentian canon" remains in fact, despite its simplicity, an instrument of inalienable value.

##### The fundamental places
There is sufficient consensus in theology when it comes to indicating the fundamental places where to look for the content of Tradition. They are basically:

1. the *Magisterium of the Church,*

1. the teaching of the *Fathers,*

1. the *Liturgy*, and finally

1. the *common feeling of the faithful*.

**The Magisterium of the Church is the first *locus* where Tradition takes shape and is transmitted in an authoritative way**. This is testified by the progressive importance that the Councils of Christian antiquity had from the earliest centuries and by the fact that, in their teachings, **the Bishops gathered in Council were concerned to rely on those who had preceded them.** The Bishop of Rome played a special role in this regard, both because questions to be settled were addressed to his See, as witnessed for example by Ignatius of Antioch and Irenaeus of Lyons, and because a papal legate was present at the Councils, as well as because he was promptly informed of the Council's decisions in order to forward them to the Bishop of Rome.

Also the Fathers of the Church have always been considered as privileged witnesses of Tradition, especially because of their proximity to the apostolic times, their defence of dogma and their relationship with Scripture. In addition to what the text of *Dei Verbum* n. 8 tells us, points of interest on this role of the Fathers are contained in a document of the Congregation for Catholic Education.[12](about:blank) Theology has seen special authority in the Fathers who verify the four *classical* notes: Orthodox doctrine, antiquity, holiness, and approval of the Church. The Council of Trent had in fact recommended not to propose interpretations of Sacred Scripture that would go *against unanimem consensum Patrum* (cf. DH 1507), an indication then proposed again by the First Vatican Council (cf. DH 3007).

A third place of primary importance for the recognition and transmission of Tradition is the Liturgy. Its role in this transmission is briefly expressed by the ***lex orandi lex credendi*** principle.[13](about:blank) The liturgy is not only the place of the lived mystery, but also the place of *lived dogma*: even if it is not the liturgy that creates the dogma, what is believed and received is configured according to liturgical practice. In turn, the liturgy must faithfully transmit the faith of the Church so as not to give rise to erroneous practices or teachings. Hence the extreme importance of everything that has to do with the Church's liturgy - not only, evidently, what refers to the celebration of the Eucharist and the administration of the sacraments, but also what concerns the artistic and cultural expressions that nourish the life of the Church, prayer and devotion of the faithful - and the legitimate responsibility with which the Magisterium must watch over all this so that the faithful are not accustomed to practices that are no longer in accordance with the Gospel transmitted by the Apostles.[14](about:blank)

The role of the *sensus fidei* as a place of understanding and transmission of the Church's Tradition will be discussed in a next section (see 3.4 *below*).

##### 4.2.4 Unity, distinction and interdependence between Scripture and Tradition
When speaking of Tradition, theology used the expression **"Traditio interpretativa" to indicate the tradition that transmits the content of Scripture** and its righteous interpretation, distinguishing it into "declaratory" if what it transmits is explicitly contained therein, and "inhaesive" if it is only implicitly contained therein. **The need for such an "interpretative" tradition manifests, as we already know, the *formal* insufficiency of sacred Scripture: Scripture alone does not transmit the entire word of Revelation, nor can it ensure its full intelligibility.**

However, theology wanted to ask itself whether it could also speak of a "constitutive Traditio", meaning by this expression the presence of a form of tradition that conveys something that is in no way present in the written text. The existence of such a constitutive tradition would then manifest the *material* insufficiency of Sacred Scripture. The question was essentially formulated by asking whether the Tradition of the Church was exclusively an interpretative tradition or not. The theology prior to the Second Vatican Council dealt with the subject in depth and tried to answer it, before *Dei Verbum* showed that this question did not possess all the relevance attributed to it. Examining in greater depth the relationship between Scripture and Tradition is, however, very useful because it helps to clarify the unity, distinction and interdependence that exist between them, helping to clarify their respective theological nature.[15](about:blank)

##### The biblical testimony
It is Scripture itself that offers us, within it, some insight into its relationship with Tradition.

**First the oral tradition preceded the fixation of the written text: Jesus himself wrote nothing, but taught with words and deeds, and the evangelists took care to put in writing** "all that Jesus did and taught in the beginning until the day he was taken up into heaven, after he had given dispositions to the apostles whom he had chosen through the Holy Spirit" (*Acts* 1:1).

**Secondly, the fixation of the written text has not replaced the oral transmission of the Gospel: this can be seen from the fact that the individual gospels, and the writings of the NT in general, have a *partial* character compared to the entire apostolic preaching**. Each book has a limited and precise purpose. Moreover, it is explicitly said that "**many other signs Jesus mad**e in the presence of his disciples, but they were not written in this book" (*Jn* 20:30). I**t should be made clear, however, that to say that each book written contains a partial aspect of apostolic preaching does not mean that the whole of Scripture, including the OT, does not in some way contain the implicit basis of all Revelation.**

**Thirdly, there is explicit mention of an oral word transmitted by the Apostles, which must be put into practice:** "keep the traditions you have learned both from our word and from our letter" (*2Ts* 2:15; identical concept in *2Tm* 1:13 and *1Tm* 1:16). Paul, in turn, had orally compared his gospel with that preached by the apostles in Jerusalem, "that he might not run or have run in vain" (*Gal* 2:2).

Finally, **Scripture mentions the need to interpret Scripture correctly.** This task cannot be avoided and must be entrusted to those who have authority to do so: "know first of all this, that no prophetic scripture should be subject to private explanation" (*2Pt* 1:20). Saint Peter speaks of the need to interpret Paul's letters correctly (cf. *2Pt* 3:16) and Paul himself speaks of some who misinterpret his writings (cf. *2Ts* 2:1-2).

##### The testimony of the Fathers of the Church
When, starting at the end of the second century, the idea of a Tradition *in the strict sense* (i.e. no longer as a generic transmission in the broad sense of the Gospel received) was gaining ground, the Fathers emphasized certain aspects that helped to understand its relationship with Sacred Scripture.

**They insist that the content of an unwritten tradition is *primarily concerned with the interpretation of Holy Scripture*.** Outside the Church, Scripture cannot be interpreted correctly; it is a public and manifest interpretation-tradition, in opposition to what the Gnostics maintain, about the existence of an occult tradition for initiates only. Consequently, the preservation of this upright tradition is associated primarily with the assistance of the Holy Spirit, who is present in the Church.

Moreover, from about the fourth century onwards, recourse to the writings of the Fathers of the Church of previous eras is seen as necessary to preserve the correct interpretation of Scripture. It is significant that in this authoritative recourse there was almost inseparable reference both to their episcopal ministry (for most of them) and to their competence in understanding and teaching the content of Scripture.

With regard to the theme of a possible *material insufficiency of* Scripture - the biblical datum and what was said earlier about the Fathers would only demonstrate its *formal* insufficiency - various Fathers state that not all of the apostolic preaching was written, but there is *a part* transmitted only orally. They consider, for example, received from the Apostolic Tradition and not contained in Scripture the worship and liturgy that refer to the celebration of the sacraments; some sacramental practices with doctrinal content, such as the baptism of children, the validity of baptism administered by heretics, etc.; some aspects concerning the prayer of the Church, such as the adoration of the Holy Spirit, worship of saints, prayers for the dead, worship of images. The Fathers do not deal with the possible theological problem associated with this relationship, nor do they bother to go into what level this content might or might not be present in Scripture, but they simply set out the facts.

Even medieval theology did not propose to formally address this subject. In Thomas Aquinas, however, there is a close correspondence between the content of faith and Scripture: "sola canonica Scriptura est regula fidei" (*In Iohan. Ev.*, XXI, 25, lect. 6), but the truths of the faith are contained "in Scripture correctly understood according to the doctrine of the Church" (S.Th. II-II, q. 5, a. 3, ad 2um). For such "righteousness", one turns to the Fathers as an *argument of authority*. Regarding the *material* sufficiency of Scripture, Thomas believes that in Scripture there is *all that is necessary for the salvation and structure of the Church*. The transmitted and unwritten things, if they touch the essence of some sacrament, will then concern sacraments not necessary for salvation (cf. *In IV Sent*., d. 23, q. 1, a. 1). The Angelic is also quite explicit in believing that "many things have been done or said by the Lord, which the evangelists did not write, but which the Church has received from the Tradition of the Apostles" (S.Th. III, q. 83, a. 4, ad 2um).

##### Council of Trent
Patristicism and theology, where they had sustained the sufficiency of Sacred Scripture, had done so only with regard to its *material* aspect, never with regard to its *formal* aspect. Beginning with the Protestant Reformation, the *formal* sufficiency of Scripture, the correct interpretation of which up to then had been recognized by ecclesial tradition, will be supported for the first time. From this moment on, the understanding of the Scriptures is declared to be the competence of individual believers, thanks to the individual and private assistance offered by the Spirit whenever one encounters the sacred text.

The *Sacrosancta* Decree drawn up at the IV session of the Council of Trent (8.4.1546) sets out the main points of Catholic doctrine in contrast to the position of the reformers:

The Holy Council of Trent, having always before its eyes the intention of preserving in the Church, by eliminating errors, the same purity of the *Gospel* which, after having been previously promised by the Prophets in the Holy Scriptures, was made known first by the mouth of Our Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, then by his apostles to whom he entrusted the mission of proclaiming it to every creature as the source of every healthy truth and every rule of morals; and considering that this truth and this moral rule *are contained in the written books and unwritten traditions that have come down to us*, or received by the apostles through the mouth of Christ, or transmitted as from hand to hand by the apostles to whom the Holy Spirit had dictated them; The Council, therefore, according to the example of the Orthodox Fathers, receives all books, both Old and New Testament, since God himself is the author of both, as well as traditions concerning both faith and customs, since they come from the same mouth of Christ or dictated by the Holy Spirit and preserved in the Catholic Church in continuous succession: the Council receives and worships them with the same respect and the same piety (*Decretum de libris sacris et traditionibus recipiendis*, DH 1501).

The Council of Trent reaffirms that the *source* of saving truths and morals (*fons omnis et salutaris veritatis et morum disciplinae*) is the Gospel understood in the broad sense, as apostolic preaching. We are faced with a living reality, not identifiable with the written texts. All this has come down to us thanks to the work of the Holy Spirit.

**It is stated that the *means by* which the Gospel is transmitted to us are Scripture and unwritten traditions, thus attesting to the clear *formal insufficiency of* Scripture alone to guarantee such transmission.** The purpose of the Council is not to clarify the *material* sufficiency of Scripture. It is not stated whether the whole Gospel is transmitted in sacred books or unwritten traditions indifferently (in parallel) or whether it starts in one way and starts in the other (complementarily).

A first draft of the decree, entrusted to Giovanni Maria Ciocchi Del Monte, future Pope Julius III, and confirmed by the pontifical legate Cervini, recited *partim... partim...*, since at the end it was the most intuitive thesis at the moment. The observations of some Council Fathers (Bonmucci, Nacchianti and Bertano) led to the writing in the final edition instead *et... et...*: although the reason for the change was not made explicit, it would seem, however, that the change can be understood in the sense that "all revealed truths are contained in Scripture, of which traditions represent a living and communal channel of interpretation".[16](about:blank)

From the whole decree emerges the importance of an objective anchorage to the apostolic tradition as a norm of truthfulness and therefore any subjective interpretation is excluded.

The rejection of the "principle of tradition" by Luther and his followers deserves some clarification today. The conception of Tradition at the time of the Reformation did not enjoy the same theological depth that contemporary times will witness, especially after the studies of a historical nature that became established in the second half of the 19th century and, above all, after the Magisterium of the Second Vatican Council. In referring to Tradition, Luther therefore had a necessarily reductive vision; he intended to refer mainly to what we would call today, in the plural, *traditions,* in which he saw an obstacle for the understanding and transmission of the authentic Christian message, because they were superstructures linked to institutional and juridical practices. The fact that the Catholic side appealed precisely to the principle of tradition to support a key theme of disagreement, that of the doctrine on indulgences, did not help to clarify the situation. In our day, the criterion of *sola Scriptura as* proclaimed by Luther is not reflected in Protestant theology, where the role of a historical-religious tradition in the formation, delivery and interpretation of Scripture throughout the centuries is now recognized as undeniable.[17](about:blank)

##### The conceptual renewal of 19th century theology
Although some theologians after Trent had had some uncertainties as to whether to speak of Scripture and Tradition as two different sources, **the idea that they were two notions that could not be considered independently, thus reducing the problem of the material sufficiency of the written text,** is becoming increasingly common in later times. However, it was with the theological renewal of the 19th century (Franzelin, Scheeben, Newman) and above all with the Tübingen School, especially J.A. Möhler, that **the close correspondence and interdependence between Scripture and Tradition became clearer**, also thanks to a renewed ecclesiological conception which, without denying the hierarchical-structural aspect, once again gave attention to the pneumatic-charismatic aspect. The work of Möhler, *The Unity of the Church* (1825), will be of fundamental importance in this regard.

Among the main ideas that are emerging is that Tradition is not only the *transmission of a deposit*, but a *deepening*, a *development of a* testimonial content that the Church transmits with her own life. Moreover, the Author who makes this living testimony possible is the same Spirit who assures the integrity of the deposit: to lead to the whole truth implies not only its faithful custody but also to deepen and advance in history all its capacity to summon and save.

##### The contribution of the Second Vatican Council
The theological discussion of the 20th century prior to the Second Vatican Council could be summed up in three different positions on how to interpret the relationship between Scripture and Tradition. The first position was called "the thesis of the material sufficiency of Scripture" (Geiselmann) and interpreted the passage of the Council of Trent from "partim... partim..." to "et... et..." as a demonstration of the fact that the role of Tradition would be only *explanatory-interpretative* and therefore there would not exist a *constitutive* Tradition for the truths of faith. The second thesis, called "of the two Sources" (Lennerz), affirmed a material distinction between Scripture and Tradition also with regard to the truths of faith: Scripture and Tradition would not contain all Revelation alone, but only a part of it (partim... partim...); there would then also exist a constitutive Tradition and not only an interpretative one. Finally, according to the "thesis of the relative sufficiency of Scripture" (Beumer), all revealed truths transmitted through Tradition would have their foundation in Scripture, although not all of them would be strictly deductible from it.

***Dei Verbum* seems to want to overcome these previous settings by speaking, in the first place, of Tradition and Scripture as *a single repository of Revelation***. If **there is a distinction between them it is because Scripture is *an inspired word of God,* while Tradition is *an uninspired* word, a human word that conveys the word of God**:

Holy Tradition and Holy Scripture are closely linked and communicating. Since both of them spring from the same divine source, they form in a certain way one and tend towards the same end.

[...] thus it follows that the Church draws her certainty from all things revealed not only from Scripture, and that consequently both must be accepted and venerated with equal pity and reverence (n. 9).

The question of the material sufficiency of Scripture will therefore not be deliberately "resolved" by *Dei Verbum*, since the document makes it clear that **Scripture and Tradition cannot be considered separately, but must be understood in a constant and mutual reference which will necessarily also involve the function of the Magisterium** (cf. n. 10).

The following points can be summarized very schematically on the Scripture-Tradition relationship:[18](about:blank) *Unity*. It depends on various factors such as:

- the common origin from God, because God inspires Scripture and God establishes in Christ, with the assistance of the Spirit, an apostolic tradition;
- the common goal, which is the salvation of men;
- the common content, the Gospel, that is, apostolic preaching.

*Distinction*. They are also distinct realities because:

- one is an inspired divine word; the other, both in its written and oral forms, is an uninspired word, though enriched by the guarantee of divine assistance through the function of the Magisterium;
- one has the structure of a fixed text, while the other has the structure of a living message, which makes explicit in history and with the laws of history what the deposit (i.e. Scripture and Tradition itself) contains.

*Interdependence*. However, they are closely interdependent:

- for how the repository was formed: Tradition, which interprets Scripture, also predates Scripture;
- for how the deposit is recognized as authentic: Tradition delivers the canon, but at the same time Scripture is the ultimate foundation for comparing the truthfulness of what is transmitted;
- for their function in the life of the Church: Tradition exists primordially to interpret the written word of God correctly (tradition aimed at Scripture), but the written word of God, without the lived life of the Church, would remain a dead letter (Scripture aimed at living tradition).

##### Relationship between Scripture and Tradition: some concluding considerations
Still wanting to question ourselves about the possible existence of a "constitutive tradition" in the strong sense, and therefore about the possible insufficiency of the material content of Sacred Scripture, one should say that, after all, we are not faced with a question of principle, but only with a question of historical research. However, in the light of what we have seen so far, **it would seem more logical to conclude that Tradition transmits *things that are not written but are "contained" in Scripture*.** That is, if there are truths of faith - proposed as such by the Church and believed by the People of God - which do not appear even implicitly contained in Scripture, it is very reasonable to believe that **they are equally "contained" in it, once we consider *Scripture as interpreted by Tradition.***[**19**](about:blank) And this, both by recalling that **the intrinsic link between Scripture and Tradition makes it possible to conceive Scripture interpreted by Tradition as a single and coherent object**, and by reflecting on the fact that recognizing the belonging of a specific content to the Gospel preached by the Church is not an operation entrusted only to hermeneutical, historical and exegetical instruments; the coherence and unity of the Gospel transmitted are in fact due to the living presence of that Spirit in whom the Scripture has been written and must be read, whose recognition cannot be subject to mere formal or scientific analysis.

The validity of baptism administered by heretics, for example, can in fact still be considered to be contained *in Scripture as interpreted by Tradition,* thinking in this case of the interpretation offered by Tradition concerning all the implications of the universal salvific will, as this is contained in Scripture. In any case, the fact remains that **theology can use the adjective "implicit" in a rather broad way, and it is therefore legitimate to consider the truths contained in Scripture as interpreted by Tradition as truths still "implicitly contained in Scripture".**

This perspective would be confirmed by the fact that the proclamation by the Magisterium of the Church of truths of faith not contained in Scripture or not directly deductible from it was often based on biblical passages from whose tenor such truths could certainly not be immediately deduced, but in spite of this an ecclesial reading was proposed, precisely in confirmation of dogma, that is, not a reading of Scripture alone (though exegetically equipped), but of Scripture interpreted by Tradition.[20](about:blank)

##### Relations between Sacred Scripture and Tradition in ecumenical dialogue
We have already observed that the principle of *sola Scriptura* originally advanced by Luther's Reformation has undergone a certain downsizing over time, especially in recent times. The Churches of the Reformation today substantially agree that alongside Scripture, the content of the Gospel is to be found "in the creeds, in the liturgical formulas of the sacraments and other forms of worship, and also in the preaching of the Word and in the theological expositions of the doctrine of the Church", so that "a mere reiteration of the words of Sacred Scripture would be a betrayal of the Gospel".[21](about:blank)

Also in the light of the notion of great Tradition and its difference with other traditions variously understood and lived throughout the history of the Church, there is today a substantial consensus between Catholics and Lutherans on the following points:

- the preeminence of sacred Scripture does not exclude the function of some form of teaching ministry, nor the presence of traditions that transmit and keep alive the faith of the Church;
- there are no traditions of apostolic origin historically verifiable that are not also contained, in some way, in Holy Scripture and, therefore,
- Although not all the individual doctrines believed are literally contained in Scripture, they can all be deduced from it in some way.

#### 4.3 Revelation and Magisterium: indefectibility of the Church and infallibility of the Magisterium
##### 4.3.1 The function of the Magisterium of the Church in relation to Revelation
The function of the Magisterium in relation to Revelation should be understood in the first place as a service to the revealed word with regard to the salvation of souls. It makes it possible to harmonize the tension existing between fidelity to the revealed deposit on the one hand, and the transmission of the word in a manner appropriate to the needs of every age on the other, bearing in mind, however, that the ultimate reason for the possibility of such harmony is due to the presence and action of the Holy Spirit in the Church, guarantor both of the truth of the deposit and of its faithful proclamation in history.

##### The Church's Magisterium between the Apostolic and Post-Apostolic Periods
No reflection that investigates the theological nature of the Magisterium can ignore the fact that the apostolic epoch constitutes a unique and unrepeatable moment in the economy of Revelation. While it is true that the Apostles transmitted their pastoral and magisterial task to the Bishops who succeeded them, it is also true that they were unable to pass on to anyone their peculiarity of being the last authors of the depositum fidei.

This exceptionality of the apostolic role, which for Catholic theology represents the foundation of the authority transmitted by the Apostles to the Bishops, is instead interpreted by some Protestant theological currents as a real discontinuity. In other words, there is a separation between the apostolic and post-Apostolic periods, between the Church of the Apostles and the Church of Bishops; according to some, this discontinuity is much greater than the continuity that the apostolic tradition also possesses. One of the main supporters of this thesis was the evangelical theologian Oscar Cullmann. Once the canon of Scripture had been established at the end of the fourth century, with the Synod of Hippo and the Council of Carthage, the principle of Tradition would, according to this author, have lost its raison d'être, that is, that of handing over to the Church the written Word of God. An even more general and acute criticism was formulated by Emil Brunner (Das Missverständnis der Kirche, 1951), who saw in the development of the post-Apostolic Church "a tragic misunderstanding", where the norm and law erroneously replaced the charism and action of the Spirit.

The Catholic position, on the other hand, while aware of the peculiarity of the apostolic era, emphasizes much more continuity than discontinuity, holding that the principle of Tradition is not concluded with the fixation of the canon. In fact, the fixation of the canon does not correspond to a precise historical moment, after which the Church considers the task of oral tradition concluded. The process of recognizing authentically inspired books, in order to separate them from those that were not, has been an effort that has accompanied the Church for a long time, and there are significant testimonies of this even in apostolic times. The Apostolic Church and the post-Apostolic Church are the Church itself, the Church of Christ.

In reality, the accentuation of discontinuity or continuity corresponds to two different ecclesiological conceptions. The first, of Protestant roots, tends to see the Church as the community of the saved and limits the effectiveness of the saving means to the Paschal event of Jesus Christ, of which only the Apostles were witnesses. Salvation can only come directly from God: there should not exist in the Church a proper sacramental-salvific economy. The second, the Catholic conception, sees the Church as "the universal sacrament of salvation", as "a sign and instrument of intimate union with God and of the unity of the whole human race" (LG, 1). She is an effective mediator of salvation because it is Christ who continues to save through her, in mysterious continuity with his earthly event.

##### Authority of Apostolic Succession
If the apostolic epoch constitutes an unrepeatable moment in the economy of Revelation, the Church has been perfectly aware of this uniqueness since its origins. This is why she recognizes in the word of the Apostles, that is, in the twelve men chosen by Jesus to accompany him in his ministry (cf. Mk 3:13-19; Acts 1:21), the character of a special authority. Already in the early Christian community, all "were assiduous in listening to the teaching of the apostles" (Acts 2:42). The basis of this authority is their having been witnesses to the resurrection of Christ (cf. Acts 2:32; Acts 1:21-22). The Apostles, in turn, are concerned to establish their successors, giving rise to what we call "apostolic succession".

In the hierarchical structure of the Church, whose origin is rooted in the apostolic mandate addressed by Jesus to the twelve and in the Petrine mandate, this apostolic succession is realized through the sacrament of the episcopal order. Not considering the possibility of a continuity in this authority, through a sacramental guarantee, would be tantamount to weakening our understanding of the universal will of salvation of Jesus Christ and would devalue the promises of Jesus linked to the mission of the Holy Spirit. The custody of the depositum fidei therefore needs a *traditio fidei*. This is how *Lumen gentium* explains it:

That divine mission, entrusted by Christ to the apostles, will last until the end of the world, since the Gospel they are to teach is for all time the source of all life for the Church. And for this reason the apostles, appointed as rulers in this society, took care to appoint successors. […] in order that the mission assigned to them might continue after their death, they passed on to their immediate cooperators, as it were, in the form of a testament, the duty of confirming and finishing the work begun by themselves (LG, 20).

The purpose of the institution of this succession is the living custody of the Gospel, once again understood as the unique source of Revelation to be transmitted:

The apostles then, so that the Gospel would always remain intact and alive in the Church, left the bishops as successors, entrusting them with their own place of magisterium (DV, 7).

The Bishops, in union with the Roman Pontiff as head of their College, are therefore the subject of the Magisterial function of the Church. This Magisterium presents itself with an authority of a charismatic-sacramental kind, in continuity with the authority of Christ himself. Dei Verbum speaks of this function by associating with it "a charism certain of truth" (charisma certum veritatis) (n. 8), transmitted to the successors of the apostles. The interpretation of the deposit of Revelation made by the Magisterium is therefore called "authentic interpretation", in the meaning of " trustworthy" and "authoritative". Linked to this guarantee is the charism of infallibility, of which we will speak later (see 3.2 below), whose gift aims to defend the purity of the salvific content of Revelation and which is exercised organically by the Church, the Magisterium and the entire People of God.

In relation to Revelation, the role of the Magisterium must provide two clarifications, one concerning the object of its teaching, the other concerning its relationship to the Word of God.

##### Object and forms of exercise of the Church's Magisterium
Usually the object of the Magisterium is indicated by the expression *patristica res fidei et morum* (things concerning faith and behaviour). In fact, however, since the function of the Magisterium is to transmit the word that leads to salvation, those truths whose affirmation is necessary to understand, expose or defend precisely that salvific content will also be part of its teaching. This can lead the Magisterium to teach:

1. *formally revealed* truths, which we call the "primary object" of the Magisterium of the Church, that is, those truths explicitly taught by Scripture, or which can derive directly from them (Trinitarian dogmas, Christological dogmas, fundamental doctrine on the Church and the sacraments, etc.);
2. truths *not formally revealed*, which constitute the "secondary object" of the Magisterium, or truths not explicitly taught by Scripture, but whose affirmation is necessary to understand and transmit the Gospel of salvation of Jesus Christ; these are, however, teachings ordinarily referable to Tradition understood in the broad sense and, therefore, never completely alien to Revelation.

It should not be excluded that, in some cases, exegetical and theological progress could recognize the presence, in Sacred Scripture, of certain specific contents, thus making them explicit and showing their belonging to the primary object of the Magisterium. It should also be pointed out that some teachings of a philosophical, anthropological, metaphysical nature also belong to the secondary object of the Magisterium; such as: the natural knowledge of God, the existence of personal freedom, the existence of a natural moral law, the immortality of the human soul, etc. These are contents that constitute a *revelatum per accidens*, or even contents that cannot be traced back to a revealed word. As we shall see, the fact that the Church makes it the object of teaching responds to the fact that, ignoring such content, it would not be possible to understand Revelation itself, nor would it be possible to preserve or faithfully expose it. To this secondary object, finally, can also belong teachings in the moral realm, such as the Church's social doctrine, the Church's teachings on education, the family, the meaning of human sexuality, etc.

There are basically two forms of exercise of the Church's Magisterium: the "ordinary" Magisterium (whether universal or not), and the "solemn" Magisterium, also called "extraordinary". Each of them has within itself two distinctions, thus giving rise to four forms:

- Ordinary Magisterium commonly understood
- Ordinary and universal teaching
- Magisterium of the Bishop of Rome exercised with *ex cathedra* declarations
- Magisterium of Ecumenical Councils

1. The "ordinary teaching commonly understood" consists in the teaching of the Bishops (and therefore also of the Roman Pontiff as Bishop of Rome), when they exercise the ordinary pastoral care of the flock entrusted to them; usually, the corresponding teachings are not proposed with a particular tenor of universality or irreformability: if they possess it, it is because these teachings share in truth that they already enjoy those characteristics.
2. The ordinary Magisterium is called "ordinary and universal" when the Bishops who lead the parts of the People of God entrusted to them, in union with the Bishop of Rome, head of the College of Bishops, although dispersed on earth, converge together on a particular teaching or doctrine to be proposed to the People of God; this harmony/convergence is also likely to be evaluated on a historical basis (diachronic perspective) and not only on the basis of the contemporaneity of the various interventions (synchronic perspective).
3. We speak of the solemn Magisterium of the Bishop of Rome, when the Roman Pontiff, for the good of the whole People of God, proposes specific teachings ex cathedra, that is, explicitly appealing to the Petrine mandate to watch over the unity of the Church.
4. We are in the presence of the solemn Magisterium of the Church when the Bishops in union with the Bishop of Rome, gathered in Ecumenical Council, proclaim a particular teaching or doctrine as the result of a collegial act.

The variety of documents and ways of proposing a doctrine can, in principle, be traced back to the four previous forms of exercise of the Magisterium. Let us see, on an illustrative level, some specific cases.

The *pastoral letter of a diocesan bishop* is an expression of his ordinary Magisterium, but it could also contain an exhortation to teachings which, in fact, are already the patrimony of the universal ordinary Magisterium.

Thus *the post-synodal exhortation or encyclical of a Roman Pontiff* is often an expression of universal ordinary Magisterium, because it brings together a common doctrine in the Church, recalls it and formalizes it. In other cases, the encyclical of the Bishop of Rome, as was the case with Paul VI's Humanae Vitae (1966), can address new and urgent issues for the good of the Church, formulating teachings to which he asks to obey, pending their eventual becoming ordinary and universal Magisterium.

Many *Councils of Christian antiquity*, even if not all the bishops then in existence participated in them, are equated by authority with that of the ecumenical Councils of modern and contemporary times, because of the authority and representativeness of those who took part in them and their proximity to the apostolic era.

The *regional Synods of antiquity*, on the other hand, have a less stringent authority if they dealt with issues concerning local churches and historical contingencies; while their authority is certainly greater if they discussed doctrines of interest to the universal Church, later confirmed and lived by the praxis of the People of God and the teachings of their Pastors.

The *Synods of Bishops of the contemporary era*, desired by the Second Vatican Council as a consultative and fraternal collegial body, are important places for the clarification of the faith, but their documents are not comparable to those of a Council Magisterium, both because of the consultative and non-decisive nature of the assembly and because they are not an expression of the entire College of Bishops.

*Bishops' Conferences, regional and national,* are generally entrusted with the task of regulating and specifying, at the pastoral, liturgical and canonical level, what has been established for the universal Church: theirs is not, strictly speaking, a doctrinal Magisterium and, if it is, it rests on the teachings already formulated by the Magisterium of the Church as a whole.

*The Dicasteries of the Roman Curia* are organs of the Holy See aimed at clarifying doctrinal, disciplinary, liturgical, canonical aspects, etc., addressed to individual faithful or to the whole People of God, as an expression of the Bishop of Rome's concern and vigilance for the universal Church and support for the Magisterium of the Church as a whole. Among these, the declarations of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith take on particular importance.

##### Ways of teaching proposition and language used
As for the way in which the teachings of the Magisterium are proposed to the people of God, these can be given by specifying that they are irreformable teachings and therefore to be considered definitive, or by not specifying this particularity. The people of God are bound to obey both ways of proposing them, but they are susceptible to different outcomes: the teachings of the second type may be reformed in time, while those of the first type are to be considered irreformable. When, in the forms of exercise of the ordinary Magisterium, the Roman Pontiff or individual pastors intend to propose some teaching to the People of God in a non-definitive way (if they intend to propose it in a definitive way they would generally have recourse to forms of exercise of the extraordinary Magisterium), the faithful must adhere to them with religious reverence for their will and intellect, since these are teachings whose purpose, while awaiting possible further study, is still to serve the good and salvation of souls. We shall see later that with regard to the forms of extraordinary or ordinary and universal exercise, the Magisterium of the Church enjoys a certain charism of infallibility. This charism, on the other hand, is not associated with the form of ordinary Magisterium commonly understood, but this does not exempt the faithful, as has just been mentioned, from observing the teachings proposed in the latter form of exercising.

Of great importance is the language used by the Magisterium and the lexical context of its formulations. In a particular way, we can find ourselves faced with a defining language with which the Magisterium formulates and defines; or even with expressions whose verbal tenor makes us understand that we are faced with irreformable and definitive teachings. Sometimes, the specification that a teaching is irreformable and definitive can reach the faithful only through the verbal tone of the language used, because it is not always possible to confer particular solemnity, through external acts, to important moral teachings, whereas it is almost always possible to do so in the case of the promulgation of dogmatic declarations. Consider, by way of example, the verbal tone of a passage of John Paul II in his encyclical Evangelium vitae (1995): "Therefore, with the authority that Christ conferred on Peter and his Successors, in communion with the Bishops of the Catholic Church, I confirm that the direct and voluntary killing of an innocent human being is always gravely immoral" (n. 57).

Normally, defining language is used in the acts of the Solemn Magisterium, because these are acts in which the content of the doctrine to be believed by the faithful is defined. Typical expressions of this are the "canons" of Councils of past ages. Not all the acts of a solemn Magisterium, however, are drafted in definitory language, as we can well see from the pastoral tone, but no less charged with doctrinal value, transmitted to us by the documents of the Second Vatican Council.

In summary, not all the teachings to be considered definitive are formally proposed by acts of solemn and extraordinary Magisterium, nor do they necessarily all have a defining language. Not a few definitive teachings are transmitted through forms of universal ordinary Magisterium, especially through pontifical documents which highlight, precisely, the universality of a specific doctrine, for example, following the celebration of Synods or the holding of extensive collegial consultations, ratifying its binding for the People of God. It is by these means, for example, that we know how to be definitive teachings such as the grave unlawfulness of contraception or priestly ordination reserved only to men.

##### Assent of the People of God to the corresponding Magisterial teachings
The document of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith *Inde ab ipsis primordis* (1998) reproposes the text of the *Professio fidei* requested of those who assume an office "to be exercised in the name of the Church". It sets out the ways in which the faithful must adhere to the various forms of exercise of the Magisterium, offering in this regard numerous references to theological reason. The main contents of the Professio fidei and the theological considerations underlying it are summarized in three progressive articles or paragraphs, which follow the statement of the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Symbol. Let us see them schematically.

The first paragraph states:

I also believe with firm faith all that is contained in the written or transmitted word of God and that the Church, both with solemn judgment and with ordinary and universal magisterium, proposes to believe as divinely revealed.

These are therefore forms of exercise which, as we shall see, enjoy the charism of infallibility; they correspond to the primary object of the Magisterium, that is, formally revealed teachings (even if they are not present in the Symbol). To such teachings the People of God must give an assent of theological faith: their refusal would therefore not be compatible with full communion in the Catholic Church and would therefore fall into the censorship of heresy. In this case the assent of the faithful is based on faith in the authority of the word of God correctly interpreted in the Church (doctrina de fide credenda).

The second paragraph states:

I also firmly accept and hold all and individual truths about the doctrine concerning the faith and customs proposed by the Church in a definitive manner.

These teachings are intended to indicate in general those truths which belong to the secondary object of the Magisterium, that is, truths which are not formally revealed but which are necessary for understanding Revelation. In this case the assent is based on faith in the assistance of the Holy Spirit to the ecclesial Magisterium (doctrina de fide tenenda): it is not a question of theological faith in the strict sense, but of faith in the Church as the subject of a specific mission and charisms of divine origin. The denial of these truths corresponds to a position of rejection of truths of Catholic doctrine and therefore also causes the loss of full communion with the Catholic Church.

In the third paragraph, at the end of the exposition of the Symbol of Faith it is affirmed:

I also adhere with religious assent of will and intellect to the teachings that the Roman Pontiff and the College of Bishops propose when exercising their authentic magisterium, although they do not intend to proclaim them by a definitive act.

These are teachings which must be followed by the People of God even if, as in the present case, they are not proposed with a form of solemn exercise of the Magisterium, nor are they definitively so. The argument against this third type of teaching can be qualified as erroneous; in the case of teachings of a prudential nature, the contrary position can be qualified as reckless, dangerous for the faith and, in any case, "cannot be taught safely".

We can observe that, in general, to the first paragraph belong teachings that we have recognized as belonging to the primary object of the Magisterium, while to the second and third paragraphs belong teachings of the secondary object of the Magisterium. Moreover, the first two paragraphs reasonably correspond to teachings promulgated in a definitive way (even if not always with definitory language), while the third paragraph corresponds to teachings proposed in a non-definitive way.

Finally, the above-mentioned document lists some examples. To the first paragraph correspond the articles of faith of the Creed, the Christological and Marian dogmas, the doctrine about the existence of original sin, the institution of the sacraments by Jesus Christ and their salvific efficacy, the infallibility of the Magisterium of the Roman Pontiff, the sinful nature of what directly opposes the prescriptions of the Decalogue, the real and substantial presence of Jesus Christ in the Eucharist. In other words, these are truths explicitly present in Revelation, or deducible from it on the basis of the teaching of the Church, and formulated in a definitory way by acts of the solemn Magisterium. These truths all concern the primary object of the Magisterium. With regard to the teachings corresponding to the second paragraph, the document mentions the doctrine on priestly ordination to be reserved only to men, the doctrine on the unlawfulness of euthanasia, prostitution and fornication, the legitimacy of the election of the Roman Pontiff, the canonizations of saints. These are teachings generally formulated in a definitive way (even if not definitory) by the Roman Pontiff and/or referable to the ordinary and universal Magisterium of Bishops. With regard to the exemplifications relating to the third paragraph, "one can indicate in general the teachings proposed by the authentic ordinary Magisterium, in a non-definitive way, which require a differentiated degree of adherence, according to the mind and will manifested, which is especially evident both from the nature of the documents and from the frequent re-proposal of the same doctrine, as well as from the tenor of verbal expression". This could include, for example, disciplinary or doctrinal provisions relating to authors, institutions, etc. issued for prudential reasons, or moral teachings with which the People of God are oriented on new questions, not yet well studied from the theological point of view.

Clearly there is always a link between the proposed teachings and the Church's mission to "correctly interpret and sanctify" Revelation. The unlawfulness of contraception, for example, is a teaching that must be affirmed for a correct understanding of the role of human love and the dignity of human life in God's plans; or, similarly, the reservation of priestly ordination to men alone becomes necessary for a correct understanding of the anthropological and sacramental symbolism linked to the priesthood of Christ; the canonization of saints, then, must be considered primarily as the authoritative proposal to God's people of life and of the example of models to follow, in order to better understand the evangelical virtues and holiness connected with the following of Christ, and secondly as the attestation of their eternal beatitude, which obviously follows from such holiness.

##### The Magisterium and the revealed Word
In relation to the function performed by the Magisterium of the Church with regard to the divine word, one last important clarification should be offered. The Magisterium does not replace Revelation (as rebuked not infrequently to Catholics by the theology of the Reformers), but its only function is to serve and expose it, as Dei Verbum explicitly recalls: "The Magisterium, however, is not above the word of God, but serves it, teaching only what has been transmitted, in that, by divine mandate and with the assistance of the Holy Spirit, it listens to it piously, holily guards it and faithfully exposes it, and from this one deposit of faith it draws all that it proposes to be believed as revealed by God" (n. 10). The expression *pie audit*, was deliberately introduced by the Fathers in the III scheme of the Dei Verbum, as a progressive refinement of actions of the Magisterium which were then: to guard and interpret (I scheme), to guard and expose (II), to listen, to guard and expose (III, IV). Lumen Gentium will also make it clear that both the Pope and the Bishops, in the exercise of their Magisterium, "do not receive a new public revelation as belonging to the divine deposit of faith" (n. 25).

The aspect of service to the Word is not a novelty of Vatican II, but is a doctrine in conformity with what had already been affirmed in Trent and Vatican I. In these two previous Councils, in fact, when speaking of the authority of the Magisterium with regard to the correct interpretation of Sacred Scripture, expressions were never used that could suggest an authority over Scripture, but rather expressions that concerned the authority of the Church over the individual faithful who, with their individual reading, were erroneously placed outside the Church. With such authority, individuals are not slavishly bound to an absolutist interpretation of the Magisterium, but to the saving power of the Word, whose action depends in no small measure on the fidelity of its transmission/interpretation. The Magisterium is not, therefore, an absolute norm of Scripture, but a norm regulated by it.

This "normalized service" of the Magisterium to Scripture is ultimately a new way of seeing the Magisterium of the Church as an integral part of Tradition, without being assimilated or identified with it, because the Magisterium is its authentic organ of interpretation. Apostolic succession is the *sacramental form of Tradition and its unifying presence* (J. Ratzinger).

##### 4.3.2 Infallibility and indefectibility of the Church
The understanding of the concept of infallibility rests on that of the indefectibility of the Church and draws its justification from it. At least three contents are associated with the notion of "indefectibility" (from the lat. non deficio, I never fail, but also I do not dissolve, I do not lose consistency):

- the idea of stability in identity;
- the idea of permanence in time;
- the idea of a precise eschatological role.

All these characteristics are predicated in reference to the one Church founded by Christ. The last of them, its persistence aimed at an eschatological fulfillment, shows that indefectibility is thus willed by God because of the purpose for which the Church was ordained. The indefectibility of the Church is therefore a perfection shared by a divine Subjects and does not mean absolute perfection.

The indefectible character of the Church is reflected in the NT and depends on the foundational will of Christ. One of his most explicit presentations is the one related to the Petrine confession: "And I say to you, You are Peter, and on this stone I will build my Church, and the gates of hell will not prevail against it" (Mt 16:18). Christ himself promises the Apostles to accompany their mission until the end of time (cf. Mt 28:18-20) and announces the sending of the Holy Spirit for ever, as a perennial witness and guarantee of truth (cf. Jn 14:17,26). In this same light one could read the parable of the wheat and the tares, in which the universality of a Kingdom destined to be present until the eschatological fulfillment of time (cf. Mt 13:24-30.36-43). Finally, the qualification of the sacramental economy entrusted by Christ to the Church as the economy of a new and eternal covenant, explicit in the institution of the Eucharist, refers naturally to the same concept of indefectibility.

The Fathers, Saint Augustine in a particular way, commented on the perennially stable character of the Church, especially in apologetic terms. "The Church will falter when her foundation falters. But how can Christ waver? As long as Christ does not waver, neither will the Church waver, for all eternity". Medieval theology did not use the concept of "infallibility", but accessed that of indefectibility through the category of *fidei firmitas*, linked to the "apostolicity" of doctrine. The Magisterium of the time did not know explicit formulations on the subject, even though the history of the Church and theological discussion lived through circumstances in which one wondered whether a Pope could contradict another Pope on some subject, or whether certain decisions, even those of a disciplinary nature, once taken, could no longer be modified.

The pre-Vatican II Magisterium pointed out the indefectibility of the Church primarily in its visible-institutional aspects, while the Second Vatican Council will do so in attention to its Christological foundation and its mysterious nature. Since this is a historical reality immersed in historical becoming, Lumen Gentium will make it clear that the indefectibility of the Church, her "never failing", is understandable only in the light of a particular divine assistance. An essential part of this indefectibility of the Church is her ability to guard Revelation in its integrity and purity: "In His immense goodness God disposed that Revelation, which He made for the salvation of all nations, should remain forever in its entirety" (DV, 7).

The concept of indefectibility therefore naturally introduces that of "infallibility". In relation to the teaching of the content of Revelation and its correct understanding, infallibility is therefore a manifestation of indefectibility on the level of knowledge, with precise consequences on the level of praxis, that is, indefectibility and infallibility in the preaching of the Word of salvation, as the very purpose of the Church's mission. The indefectibility of the Church in proposing and guaranteeing the means of salvation received from Christ is compatible with the weakness of its members, since it is the mission of the Church to be indefectible, while the individual Christians who compose it are neither infallible nor indefectible.

##### 4.3.3 Infallibility of the Church *in docendo
With reference to confession, teaching and interpretation of the authentic meaning of salvific truths, this indefectibility is presented as infallibility *in docendo* and *in credendo*: the Church cannot err in teaching what she proposes as necessary for salvation and the Church herself, as the People of God, cannot err when she unanimously believes and confesses what refers to that same salvation.

It is important to make some clarifications on the notion of infallibility, distinguishing it from other notions. “Infallibility" is something which *characterizes in the first place a subject* in his intention to profess something; it is a gift of the Spirit which enables him never to profess what is false as if it were true or vice versa, and *only secondly it characterizes the content* of what is professed. Infallible therefore means in the first instance "incapable of deceiving or deceiving oneself (*nec falli nec falliere posse*)", that is, never to say the false taking it as true. Infallibility is not the "decision of a truth" nor the "establishment *ex novo* of a truth", but the guarantee, present in an authoritative subject, that a certain teaching is recognized as belonging to Revelation or to a set of true knowledge that serves to correctly understand the Word of God. The notion of infallibility, moreover, should not be identified nor confused with that of "irreformability", nor with that of "definition", in the sense of "definition of a truth". The latter refer to the content of the proposed doctrine. Infallibility is also distinguished from "authority", because the teaching of the Magisterium is always authoritative, even in its forms of exercise which do not enjoy the charism of infallibility. Finally, "infallibility" does not mean "immutability": in fact, some teachings whose Magisterial subject, in proposing them, enjoys the charism of infallibility, do not admit - when this is not clearly excluded by explicit statements accompanying their promulgation - a certain dogmatic, hermeneutical and/or terminological development.

##### Manifestations of infallibility of the Church in docendo
Although the gift of the Spirit is bestowed upon all the people of God, without whom it would not be possible to "live according to the Spirit", and thus according to the fullness of the law revealed to us by Christ, this gift has a specific way of communicating itself through the sacrament of orders. Among the purposes of this sacrament, in its various degrees and in the various forms of exercise of the Magisterium connected with it, individual and collegial, is that of shepherding the People of God to lead them without error to eternal salvation. A task received in an essential way by the bishops, constituted in the fullness of this sacrament, and in a participatory way by the priests and deacons, also the subject of a *munus docendi*. There is therefore an infallibility proper to the Magisterium of the Church as Magisterium, expressly associated with its function of preaching and teaching. The infallibility of the Church shares in the eternal fidelity and truth of God: it is an infallibility shared and communicated (by God), limited to particular conditions.

A doctrine on the infallibility of the Magisterium of the Church has been systematized only in the last two Vatican Councils. The modalities of this Magisterial infallibility *in docendo* are summarized in the well-known n. 25 of the Constitution Lumen Gentium:

Although the individual Bishops do not enjoy the prerogative of infallibility, when however even scattered throughout the world, but preserving the bond of communion among themselves and with the Successor of Peter, in their authentic teaching on matters of faith and morals they agree on a doctrine to be considered as definitive, they infallibly propose the doctrine of Christ. And this is even more evident when, gathered together in the Ecumenical Council, they are doctors and judges of faith and morals for the whole Church; and their definitions must be adhered to in a submission of faith.

[...] The Roman Pontiff, head of the College of Bishops, benefits from this infallibility by virtue of his office when, as supreme pastor and doctor of all the faithful who confirm his brothers and sisters in the faith (cf. Lk 22:32), he proclaims by a definitive act a doctrine concerning faith or morals.

It is easy to recognize in this page the infallibility associated with three of the four forms of exercise of the Magisterium already encountered: the two forms of solemn Magisterium and the universal ordinary Magisterium. In the first case we are faced with the Magisterium of the Ecumenical Councils gathered around the Roman Pontiff or the Magisterium of the Roman Pontiff when he defines *ex cathedra*, by virtue of his office as head of the College of Bishops and pastor of the universal Church. In the second case, infallibility *in docendo* applies to the universal ordinary magisterium of the Bishops scattered throughout the world, in their task of shepherding the particular Church entrusted to them, but always as a successor to the College of Bishops, and therefore invested, as a whole, with pastoral concern for the universal Church.

##### Characteristics and importance of the universal ordinary Magisterium
It is important to reflect on the fact that the most usual and common manifestation of the Church's infallibility in teaching is in the form of the exercise of the universal ordinary Magisterium. For this reason the universal ordinary Magisterium, and not only the solemn Magisterium, must be listened to with diligent attention and obedience. This is an infallible mode of teaching which, unlike that exercised by Councils or by the declarations of the Roman Pontiff ex cathedra, does not usually possess a defining form, nor does it require recourse to expressions of a particular solemnity. As already noted, the Roman Pontiff can collect in an official and qualified way the teaching of the universal ordinary Magisterium in the form of documents, such as for example some encyclicals, post-synodal exhortations or other documents prepared by ecclesial bodies and countersigned by him, not infrequently the expression of consultations he had with broad representatives of the episcopate, thus confirming the universality and apostolicity of a particular teaching. Think, for example, of the teachings on the relationship between truth and conscience in the *Veritatis Splendor*, the unlawfulness of contraception in *Humanae Vitae*, the grave immorality of abortion in *Evangelium Vitae*, and the invalidity of priestly ordination conferred on women in the Apostolic Letter *Ordinatio Sacerdotalis*: although these are not, strictly speaking, pronouncements of the Pope ex cathedra, they express a universal teaching of the Pastors of the Church, whose subject therefore enjoys the charism of infallibility. With regard to the verification of the existence of the conditions for being able to speak of a universal ordinary Magisterium, it should be kept in mind that the unanimous consent of the Pastors is not necessarily to be understood in a synchronic way, but can also concern, in a diachronic way, the unanimity of their teaching throughout the various periods of history, as a guarantee of connection with apostolic doctrine; the evaluation that it is, precisely, an ordinary universal Magisterium could then require an analysis of a historical-dogmatic nature.

The ordinary Magisterium of the Pope and the Bishops, even when it is not presented with the explicit characteristics of universality and collegiality, that is, when it cannot be considered universal ordinary Magisterium, is a teaching equally given with authority. The authority of the Magisterium of the Church does not depend solely on the circumstances which make it infallible, nor should it be absorbed in infallibility. Lumen Gentium reminds us again: "When bishops teach in communion with the Roman Pontiff, they must be heard by all with veneration as witnesses of divine and Catholic truth; and the faithful must agree with the judgment given by their bishop in the name of Christ in matters of faith and morals, and adhere to it with religious reverence of the spirit" (n. 25).

##### On the Magisterium of the Roman Pontiff
The Apostolic Church has known and transmitted the specificity of the Petrine office. In the lists of the twelve apostles collected by the NT, Peter is always mentioned at the beginning in *Matthew* with the explicit adjective "first" (cf. *Mk* 3:16-19; *Mt* 10:2-4; *Lk* 6:14-16; *Acts* 1:13). In the narratives transmitted to us by Paul and Luke, the Risen *One* appears to him first (cf. *1 Cor* 15:5; *Lk* 24:34). Even if the solemn gospel passage of the "power of the keys" (cf. *Mt* 16:18) were considered - as some authors have said - to be an editorial text after the first apostolic preaching, it certainly testifies in favor of the privileged role that Peter occupied, both in Jerusalem and in relation to missionary expansion.

In the constitution *Pastor Aeternus*, Vatican Council I had thus defined the infallibility of the pontifical magisterium:

The Roman Pontiff, when he speaks *ex cathedra,* that is when, fulfilling the office of pastor and teacher of all Christians because of his supreme apostolic authority, he defines that a doctrine concerning faith and customs must be held by the whole Church, for the divine assistance promised to him in Blessed Peter, enjoys that infallibility which the divine Redeemer wished to endow his Church with in defining a doctrine concerning faith and customs; Therefore these definitions of the RomanPontiff are irreformable in themselves, not by the consent of the Church (*Romani Pontificis definitiones ex sese, non autem ex consensu Ecclesiae irreformabiles esse*) (DH 3074).

As will be noted, the expression referring to the definitions of the Roman Pontiff is the same that will later be collected verbatim by *Lumen Gentium*, n. 25:

The Roman Pontiff, head of the College of Bishops, benefits from this infallibility by virtue of his office when, as supreme pastor and doctor of all the faithful who confirm in the faith his brothers and sisters (cf. *Lk* 22:32), he proclaims by a definitive act a doctrine concerning faith or morals. Therefore his definitions are rightly said to be irreformable for themselves and not by virtue of the consent of the Church, because they are pronounced with the assistance of the Holy Spirit, promised to him in Blessed Peter, so that they do not require any approval from others nor admit any appeal to another judgment. In fact then the Roman Pontiff pronounces the sentence not as a private person (*non ut persona privata sententiam profert*), but as the supreme teacher of the universal Church, singularly distinguished by the charism of the infallibility of the Church itself, exposes or defends the doctrine of the Catholic faith.

The Second Vatican Council therefore follows the same line of definition as Vatican I. On the office of the Roman Pontiff in relation to the College of Bishops see the points of interest contained in the encyclical of John Paul II, *Ut unum sint* (25.5.1995)*.*

The statements of Vatican I, even before they were taken up a century later by Vatican II, were the subject of lively discussion, especially by the theology of the reformed tradition; not infrequently they were misrepresented, believing that Catholics placed the teachings of the Pope above the Gospel itself. It should not be forgotten that the theme and context of *Pastor Aeternus* is that of the infallibility "of the Church". At the proposal of some Council Fathers (Guidi, Ulhatorre, Martin) the title "The infallibility of the Roman Pontiff" was rejected, preferring that of "The infallible Magisterium of the Roman Pontiff". **What is infallible, because indefectible, is the Church, not the person of the Pope as such. T**he Roman Pontiff is infallible inasmuch as he is the subject of magisterial teaching and, therefore, in this aspect he is concrete and expresses in his person that salvific infallibility-indefinability of the Church itself. Moreover, it should be noted that the exercise of such infallibility, when seen as a "formally non-collegial" act, is a very infrequent form of magisterium, linked to teachings proposed *ex cathedra,* and therefore concerning a very limited number of cases.

In *Pastor Aeternus'* editorial staff, the phrase "*ex sese, non autem ex consensu Ecclesiae irreformabiles esse*" deserves a clarification. At the time of the First Vatican Council, the implicit addressee of this phrase was most probably Gallicanism which, in its attempt to establish the dogmatic assumptions of a French church independent of Rome, stated precisely that the Pope's judgment could not be considered definitive unless ratified by the consent of the Church. By the conciliar expression "*non ex consensu Ecclesiae*", however, it must not be understood that there is a magisterium of the Pope dispensed from being in tune - precisely in the exercise of his concern for the universal Church - with the needs, feelings and life itself of the Church. Such an interpretation would in itself be illogical and contradictory. It must instead be understood that the decisions of the Roman Pontiff, whose ultimate justification remains always the good and salvation of the People of God, in order to become operative are not subject to a juridical act of ratification, nor to a formal judgment and consensus, by the ecclesial community.[31](about:blank)

**The *true subject* of infallibility is, after all, always God, infallibility that even in Peter is shared, communicated and limited**. It is a special assistance, so that they do not deceive or deceive others about the teaching *in actu of* the Gospel. In seeking and deepening the truth, the Roman Pontiff, like all Bishops, must also make use of the usual means: study, information and dialogue, reflection, prayer, holiness of life. One can then understand an expression of St. Bulgakov, according to whom infallibility is for the Church, in a certain way, a *duty,* the duty to seek and live such a harmony with the Spirit, the fruit of holiness of life, from which the truth of the things taught and professed can flow. Although infallibility does not necessarily come from the sanctity of life, it must be sought with all strength so that its exercise does not become a "temptation of the Spirit".

##### 4.3.4 Extension of the infallibility of the Magisterium
The expression used by *Dei Verbum when speaking of the* extension of the object of the *Magisterium,* when, in reference to the Word of God, he recalls that "by divine mandate and with the assistance of the Holy Spirit, he hears it piously, holily keeps it and faithfully exposes it", is entirely analogous to the one used by *Lumen Gentium* when speaking of the extension of infallibility:

This infallibility, which the divine Redeemer wished to provide for his Church when she defines the doctrine of faith and morals, extends as much as the deposit of divine revelation, which must be scrupulously guarded and faithfully exposed (LG, n. 25).

If the mission of the Magisterium of the Church is "faithfully to guard and righteously expose" the content of Revelation with regard to the salvation of humanity, and the charism of infallibility being aimed at protecting the same good, that is, the salvation of all men, it is then very reasonable that the Magisterium should enjoy the charism of infallibility *over the whole object of its teaching*. That is, such infallibility is enjoyed both when the Magisterium teaches subjects that belong to its primary object and when it intends to deal, in an authoritative manner, with contents that concern its secondary object. If something is taught to lead people to salvation - and this is precisely the mission proper to the Magisterium, that is, the correct transmission of Revelation - such teaching enjoys the charism of truth assured by the Holy Spirit.

In agreement with this reasonable theological conclusion there is also a declaration of the CDF subsequent to the Second Vatican Council, which offers a more explicit hermeneutics of the cited passage of *Lumen gentium*, n. 25. In fact, we read in *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (1973): "According to Catholic doctrine, the infallibility of the Magisterium of the Church extends not only to the deposit of the faith, but also to all that is necessary for it to be properly preserved and exposed" (n. 3).

In fact, within the object proper to the Magisterium, that of expounding the salvific content of Revelation, the Church's tradition has never made any distinction as to which parts of it the teaching of the Magisterium enjoyed or did not enjoy the charism of infallibility. This charism has always been linked to the teaching *subject*, i.e. to the various forms or ways in which a magisterium is exercised, not to the type of things taught. The expression "for what refers to faith and customs (*fides et mores*)" should not be understood as something that cuts out or delimits infallibility to a part of the revealed deposit, but as a way of expressing what concerns the object proper to its teaching, because teaching in which salvation is involved.[33](about:blank) In reality, the expression "faith and customs" simultaneously indicates *both the object of the Magisterium* (i.e., everything that leads to salvation) and *the extent of its infallibility* and, therefore, does not indicate an internal separation from the object of the things taught. If we were to teach things on different subjects, we would not be facing the Magisterium of the Church, but the opinions of individual private authors, expressed on objects that have no relation to the salvation of mankind.

The object of the Magisterium is therefore rather large, more than one might at first glance think. It, in its primary and secondary object considered together, includes all that is necessary to "guard scrupulously and sanctionedly, and to expose faithfully" the content of the Gospel. Therefore, there are themes, truths or teachings which, although they do not belong to Revelation, perhaps not even implicitly, are nevertheless necessary to guard it and expose it as they should.

A classic example of such an *extension* concerns the teaching of the Magisterium on **natural moral law,** of which it speaks explicitly, referring to many documents preceding it, the encyclical *Humanae vitae.*[34](about:blank) A further reference to a *broad* view of the object of the Magisterium is found, for example, in the preface of the declaration *Gravissimus educationis of the* Second Vatican Council, when it speaks of the authority of the Magisterium of the Church regarding the teachings concerning the right of parents and the Church to devote themselves to the education of youth.

From a theological point of view, it is interesting to note that J.H. Newman had already pointed out how, once it is recognized that Revelation is susceptible of development and deepening, it is reasonable to think that the guarantee of infallibility can extend to everything that makes such a development possible or derives from it: "If, then, there are certain important truths, certain duties and certain observances, which naturally and legitimately descend from the originally professed doctrines, it is at all reasonable to include these results in the idea of Revelation itself and to consider them as parts of it. And if Revelation is not only true, but guaranteed as true, it is reasonable to judge a priori that even those truths will come to enjoy the privilege of infallibility".[35](about:blank)

##### 4.3.5 The *sensus fidei* of God's people and infallibility *in credendo
The Constitution *Lumen Gentium* speaks in No. 12 of the infallibility *in credendo* of the people of God:

The totality of the faithful who have received the anointing of the Holy Spirit (cf. *1 Jn* 2:20, 27) cannot err in believing, and manifests this property which is special to them through the supernatural sense of faith in the whole people, when from the bishops to the last lay faithful they express their universal consent in matters of faith and morals. In fact, because of that sense of faith, which is stirred and sustained by the Spirit of truth, the people of God, under the guidance of the sacred Magisterium, to which they faithfully conform, accept the word of mankind but, as it is in reality, the word of God (cf. *1 Th* 2:13), adheres indefectibly "to the faith once and for all transmitted to the saints" (*Judah* 3), with righteous judgment penetrates it deeper and more fully applies it in life.

The expression *sensus fidei* was known from the patristic and scholastic ages; the Church had mentioned it in various documents. In the proclamation of the dogmas of the Immaculate Conception (1854) and the Assumption of Mary (1950) reference is made to the common feeling of faith of the people of God. The origin of this infallibility *in credendo* is the Holy Spirit himself, guarantor of infallibility *in docendo*. The most direct theological reference is that of the doctrine of the gifts of the Holy Spirit, thanks to which the Christian faithful easily follow and recognize, with supernatural instinct, what refers to divine truth, virtue and good.

The term *consensus fidelium* is intended to indicate, in a certain sense, the collective result of the *sensus fidei, which is* also possessed in a personal way by each individual believer. Since it is a question of a sense of *faith,* it is clear that it will be more alive and active the more intense the participation of the faithful in the life of grace. Examples of this *consensus fidelium*, expression of an infallibility *in credendo*, the Eucharistic worship outside the Holy Mass, prayers of intercession with the saints, faith about the effectiveness of suffrages for the deceased faithful, veneration of relics and sacred images, aspects in some cases also supported by the explicit teaching of the *Magisterium,* after their rise and take root in the believing community.

It should be made clear that infallibility *in credendo* is not a type of participation of the faithful in the infallibility *in docendo* the Magisterium, because the latter is achieved through a sacrament - ordination in the episcopal degree - different from the sacrament of baptism. The two aspects are organically present in the one Mystical Body of Christ, but they are formally distinguishable. The mutual relationship between the teaching of the *Magisterium* and *consensus fidelium is* such that the latter, as the Council states, is realized "under the guidance of the sacred Magisterium, to which it faithfully conforms". In the declaration of the CDF *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (24.6.1973) it is further specified that "although the sacred *Magisterium* makes use of the contemplation, conduct and research of the faithful, its office is not reduced, however, to ratifying the consent already expressed by them; on the contrary, in the interpretation and explanation of the word of God written or transmitted, it can prevent and require such consent" (n. 2).

Both the three manifestations of infallibility *in docendo* and infallibility *in credendo* do not constitute different infallibility with which the Church would be endowed, but aspects of the *sole infallibility of* the mystical body of Christ, participations of the only absolute infallibility, that of God, the sum of Truth.

#### 4.4 Immutability and development of dogma
From the very beginning of the Church's evangelizing mission there has been a need to *fix in* some way certain contents of Revelation whose confession was considered of primary importance for salvation.[36](about:blank) Faith believed with the heart should also be able to be concretized in a public formulation: "with the heart one believes to obtain justice and with the mouth one makes the profession of faith to obtain salvation" (*Rom* 10:10).

##### 4.4.1 *Dogma* and its immutability
In its more general meaning, the term "dogma" (gr. δόγμα) means doctrine, teaching; in more stringent terms, decision, prescription. The NT uses the expression δόγματα several times, referring for example to the prescriptions of the Mosaic Law (cf. *Col* 2:20; *Eph* 2:15) or the decisions of the apostles and elders in Jerusalem (cf. *Acts* 16:4). The first authoritative example of "dogmatic fixation" is most probably the concise proclamation "Jesus is the Lord" which runs through the entire post-Easter proclamation and which we find in the verse preceding the Pauline recommendation just quoted: "For if with your mouth you proclaim: *Jesus is the Lord*, and with your heart you believe that God raised him from the dead, you will be saved. With the heart one believes to obtain justice, and with the mouth one makes the profession of faith to obtain salvation" (*Rom* 10:9-10). The formula "Jesus is the Lord" or its close equivalents appears in the Acts, in the Pauline letters and in the second closing of the Gospel of John (cf. *1 Cor* 12:3; *Acts 2:*36; *FIl* 2:11; *Acts* 10:36; *1 Cor* 8:6; cf. also *Jn* 21:7).

The first context in which the believing community encounters the need for such a fixation is that of preparation for the sacrament of baptism (catechesis) and its administration (baptismal professions of faith). Thus we see a *passage from kérygma to dogma*. Expression of this passage-fixation are the symbols of the faith written in the first centuries. They systematically organize and make explicit some of the confessions of faith already contained in the NT (cf. *Mt* 16:16; *Jn* 1:1-18; *1Cor* 15:3-5; *FIl* 2:6-11; *Col 1:*15-20; *1Tm* 3:16, etc.).

A *technical* use of the notion of "dogma" appears late in the theological vocabulary, when this term begins to indicate, in the ecclesiastical lexicon, the presence of defined and irreformable truths drawn from the content of divine Revelation. Chrismann in his *Regula Fidei Catholicae et collectio Dogmatum Credendorum* (1792), proposes a first definition: "Dogma fidei nihil aliud fit, quam doctrina, et veritas divinitus revelata, quae publico Ecclesia iudicio fide divina credenda ita proponitur, ut contraria ab Ecclesia tamquam heretica doctrina damnetur".

In the Constitution *Dei Filius* of the First Vatican Council we find for the first time an official reference to the content of dogma as "all that is contained in the Word of God, written or transmitted, and proposed by the Church, through a solemn judgment or through the ordinary universal Magisterium, as divinely revealed" (DH 3011; cf. in the light of DH 3020). It is significant to note, in the light of what has already been studied, that this statement of Vatican I contains all the elements necessary to recognize that a particular teaching (a "dogma", precisely) is proposed in an infallible way, that is, by a subject who enjoys this prerogative.

As can be seen from the primitive use of the Symbols of the faith, the Church has always had *dogmas*, even when it did not call them by this term. It is clear that the use of teachings or formulations with precise and well-established content is therefore linked to the mandate of Jesus Christ received by the Church with regard to the salvation of men; the use of dogmas is linked to Revelation before it is linked to the Church, and if it is linked to the latter it is linked to the Church because the Church depends in everything on the Word of God.

Because it seeks to express truths that belong to the mystery of God and his plans of salvation, dogma always presents itself as something *inexhaustible*. This often implies, as we shall see, the need to resort to an *analogue* language rather than a *single* language. Moreover, its peculiar relationship with the mystery of God implies that the interpretation of dogma never presents itself as a merely intellectual problem, but rather as a reality involving both theory and praxis: divine truths can be understood correctly to the extent that one disposes oneself to the gift of the Spirit and lives according to the Spirit.[37](about:blank)

##### Truth and story content
The question of the dogmatic nature of the content of faith (*fides quae*) is intertwined with the very question of truth and, above all, with that of the *relationship between truth and history*. Contemporary hermeneutic philosophy has rightly insisted that every knowledge, and therefore every approach to content considered true, takes place within a language, a tradition, a series of specifically cultural symbols and references. In the case of Jewish-Christian revelation, however, the relationship with history is not conflictual or dialectical. Revelation itself *is fulfilled in history and through history*. We know that the fullness of truth and the fulfillment of history flow into the person of Jesus Christ, the incarnate word and center of Revelation. Moreover, in relation to its recipient, the notion of Tradition leaves Revelation "open" on history.

As history progresses, recourse to hermeneutics for the interpretation of dogmatic formulas will, however, always remain necessary. It will be necessary to avoid two errors: that of a naive realism that pretends to place the interlocutor in front of a culturally aseptic truth, crystallized in formulations considered exhaustive once and for all; and that of a hermeneutic process that refers indefinitely beyond itself, or remains circularly closed in self-referential insights, incapable of making access to truthful and stable asserts, because incapable of referring to a content that transcends it.

*The dogma therefore has a truthful content*: it indicates and transmits a true knowledge of a definitive nature, although susceptible of clarification and deepening. To clarify that Christian dogmas possessed a truthful and objective content was mainly the anti-modernist Magisterium. Among the errors of the modernists was in fact that of an understanding of dogma in an immanentist key, considering it a mere explanation-fixation of personal religious experience or a binding prescription for its practical character, not for its cognitive one (cf. DH 3422, 3426). More recently, *Fides et ratio* (1998) recalled the need to employ in theology a philosophy always open "to a metaphysical instance", capable of moving from the phenomenon to the foundation, of being able to indicate the existence of a stable truth beyond the flow of history, the changing use of language, the appearance of phenomena (cf. nn. 82-83, 96-97).

##### Immutability of dogma
It is therefore legitimate to speak of an *immutability* of the dogmas proposed by the Church. This immutability concerns, as we shall see, the fact that the very meaning of dogmatic formulas always remains true and consistent, even when it is more clarified or better understood. In order to better understand what this immutability consists of, the following three texts should be examined.

The first is the one proposed by the CDF document, *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (24.6.1973):

The faithful must therefore shun the opinion that dogmatic formulas (or some category of them) cannot manifest the truth in a definite way, but only in its iridescent approximations [...] and that the same formulas, moreover, manifest only in an indefinite way the truth, which must be continually sought through these approximations. Those who thought so would not escape dogmatic relativism and would falsify the concept of the infallibility of the Church, relative to the truth to be taught and held in a determined way.

Such an opinion is in open contrast with the declarations of the First Vatican Council, which, while aware of the progress of the Church in the knowledge of revealed truth, nevertheless taught: "The sacred [...] dogmas must always be maintained the meaning declared once and for all by Holy Mother Church, and it is never permitted to depart from that meaning under the pretext and in the name of a more advanced intelligence". It also condemned the judgment that "the dogmas proposed by the Church must sometimes be given, on the basis of the progress of science, a meaning different from that which the Church has understood and intends". (n. 5).

The second text is the classical formulation from the *Commonitorium* of Saint Vincent of Lerins, adopted by Vatican Council I in its constitution *Dei Filius* (cf. DH 3020):

Let them also grow, therefore, and progress widely and intensely (*multum vehementerque proficiat*), for each as for all, for one man as for the whole Church, intelligence, science and wisdom, according to the rhythms proper to each generation and each time, but exclusively in their order, in the same belief, *in the same* sense and in the same thought (*in eodem dogmate, eodem sensu, eademque sententia*).[38](about:blank)

The third text is taken from an important speech with which John XXIII introduced the work of the Second Vatican Council and exhorted us to combine the concept of immutability with that of progress:

It is necessary that this certain and immutable doctrine, to which faithful respect is due, be explored and exposed in the way that our time demands. It is one thing, in fact, the deposit of faith, that is, the truths contained in our venerable doctrine, and another thing the manner of their enunciation, but always in the same sense and meaning.[39](about:blank)

From these quotations it seems quite clear that the key theme is the term "sense", a sort of "parameter" capable of measuring the stability and preservation over time of the truthful content of the dogma.

The immutability of dogmas, which is a property of the truthful content of the formulations, should not be confused with the notion of infallibility, which is instead the property of the subject who proclaims them, the Magisterium of the Church. As previously mentioned, it is not in fact correct to speak of "infallibility of dogmas", but rather of "infallibility of the Magisterium of the Church". Dogmas are not mysterious propositions in the face of which reason must set itself in a different way from other authoritative asserts. The dogma is subject to the conditions and developments of any assertion of truth: interpretation, deepening, development, contextual links, etc.. When we speak of the "irreformability" of a dogma, we mean the need for the stable and unequivocal preservation of its content of truth, without altering the meaning and significance it carries. In many cases this also requires the rigid immutability of certain key terms for the understanding and transmission of this content (the presence of three distinct Persons in one divine nature, two natures in the one person of the Incarnate Word, the existence of sin as a violation of God's law, etc.); at other times, the immutability and irreformability of dogma may admit a certain development in terminology.

When a certain dogma of faith is formulated, the terms, categories and concepts used by it are sufficient to express the truthful content that was intended to be expressed in that historical and ecclesial contingency. However, this does not exclude that, in that same epoch, those same truths could not be expressed in other words, nor that, as the Church's understanding of the Word of God progressed, those same truths could not be expressed in later epochs with new formulations. The renewal of the formulations, when it takes place, is not aimed at a contextual "updating" or "adjustment" of the contents of the faith, but rather at making that same Word explicit and even more comprehensible, since the new formulations must teach all the truth contained in the previous formulations.

##### 4.4.2 The dogmatic development of the Church
The existence of a dogmatic progress (*multum vehementerque proficiat...*) in the teaching of the ecclesiastical Magisterium can in the first instance be deduced from two facts:

1. the number of defined dogmas grows throughout the history of the Church;

1. the same dogmatic formulations are being enriched with new magisterial explanations.

We are therefore confronted with the theme of the *development of dogma*. The reality of the Church's dogmatic development can be easily accessed through the notion of Tradition, with which this development is intrinsically linked. The historical ways in which, for example, the Symbols of the faith have been progressively enriched in the first four centuries of the Christian era are an expression both of the development of Tradition (which has better deepened the meaning and implications of the truths believed) and of the dogmatic development carried out by the Magisterium of the great Councils, in a certain way within Tradition (which coined the dogmatic formulations, increasing them and progressively articulating them).

The set of dogmas can therefore increase, not because the Church adds something new to Revelation, of which she is the guardian and not the author, but because a series of truths already contained in Revelation are now infallibly proposed as revealed truths. It is as if the Church, having to promulgate and emphasize the saving message in the changed cultural and historical conditions, sees itself in the need to *enlighten and focus on* certain aspects of a panorama which it already possesses, in its repository, in a complete way.

Dogmatic formulations are then the object of development for the same historical and living nature of Tradition, of which the Magisterium - while distinguishing itself - is an integral part and authoritative interpretative instance, as a mysterious-sacramental form of Tradition itself. *The main development factors of the dogma are therefore the same as those of the Tradition*. In addition to the need to clarify errors and combat heresies - a historically rather decisive factor - the reasons for dogmatic progress are basically those contained in a passage already mentioned in *Dei Verbum:*

This Tradition, which originates from the Apostles, progresses (*profit*) in the Church under the assistance of the Holy Spirit: in fact, understanding, both of things and of the words transmitted, grows both through reflection and study by believers who meditate on them in their hearts, and through the profound understanding they feel (*experiuntur*) of spiritual things, as well as through the preaching of those who have received a charism certain of truth through episcopal succession. The Church, that is, over the centuries, tends unceasingly to the fullness of divine truth, until the words of God reach their fullness (n. 8).

The presence of the Spirit as the living and guiding presence of all these factors means that the dogmatic development is never only the result of a deductive-rational deepening, but is in some way caused by the activity of the Spirit and associated with the gifts which He unceasingly communicates to His Church. Not infrequently, for example, it is the lives of the saints that illuminate aspects of Revelation that have remained in darkness, and which then push the Magisterium to define them in an authoritative way.

In his work *The Development of Christian Doctrine* (1845), J.H. Newman lists seven criteria useful to distinguish, in the course of history, the true "development" of a doctrine from its corruption. Authentic doctrinal development has character:[40](about:blank)

- The permanence of a single *type*: i.e. the subject retains his identity, despite some external changes that may occur.
- Continuity in principles. Doctrine must always remain consistent with itself; there are guiding principles that never contradict each other and that allow the development of ideas throughout history.
- The power of assimilation. It is the ability to involve and incorporate what is different, remodeling, but always remaining themselves. The new, the history, are not seen as something that needs to be fought, but something in which one can move (today we would call this criterion "inculturation").
- More logical consistency. This is often discovered in retrospect, noting a better connection between the principles and the resulting fruits.
- The anticipation of future developments, especially in the form of intuitions and predictions, contained as early seeds in the present reality.
- A development that does not reject the past, but knows how to appreciate it and continually bring it back as a guarantee of true historical continuity.
- The presence of a perennial vigour and a continuous capacity for renewal that never fails.

The Church's dogmatic development does not concern only the pastoral aspect or that of evangelization: it is not dictated solely by the desire to make Christ's message more comprehensible to different peoples or at different times. Dogmatic development is an internal requirement of the faith, since it is a real *deepening in the truth*. The new dogmas enlighten each other and illuminate the previous ones, giving coherence and unity to the whole Christian mystery, helping to discover meanings previously unexpressed or even hidden. "A dogmatic assertion, therefore, is a statement that delves into the mystery: therefore it always needs to be further and progressively reinterpreted. Its immutable truth does not consist in the impossibility of rethinking it and perfecting it, but in the need for any further development to be normalized by its original meaning, which remains valid through all subsequent evolution".[41](about:blank)

##### 4.4.3 The relationship of dogmatic formulations with philosophy and language
In connection with the question of the immutability of dogma and its relationship with hermeneutics and history, the theme of the dependence of dogmatic formulations on a particular linguistic terminology used by a particular tradition of thought or used by a certain doctrinal or cultural context also arises.

##### Relationship with philosophy and discernment of the various philosophical systems
Since they are formulations that have recourse to human concepts and categories, the dogmas of the Church necessarily relate to philosophy, to the cultures of different peoples, to human language. It is an inevitable but at the same time fruitful relationship. When talking about the relationship between Revelation and philosophy, *Fides et ratio* seems to invoke the need for a philosophical reference grid, without which it would not be possible to understand how much Revelation intends to transmit to us. To a certain extent this grid is made up of what we might reasonably call *praeambula fidei*, philosophical truths, concepts and conclusions of reason that are permanently available and whose unambiguous knowledge is indispensable for understanding the Word of God.[42](about:blank)

*[Embedded Image]*
 Texts from *Fides et ratio* (1998), nos. 66-67

As a whole, this encyclical contains in several places a double, clear teaching:

1. recourse to philosophy, to some form of natural knowledge of God, to some fundamental truths concerning the world and man, is necessary for the intelligibility of Revelation (cf. also *Fides et ratio,* nos. 36, 83);

1. This recourse to philosophy implies, however, for those who proclaim the faith and elaborate theological discourse, an important discernment.

Although dogmatic formulations, inasmuch as they are expressed in human words, are immersed in history, it is worth noting the attempt made by the Magisterium of the Church to link these formulations as much as possible to a *philosophia perennis* which is placed on a meta-historical level, because it is linked to being, to its transcendental, to those immutable aspects of human nature, of its relations with things and with God. This attempt can be considered partly successful, as evidenced by the fact that for many centuries the definitions of the Magisterium have used concepts such as nature, person, substance, soul, virtue, sin, life, death, redemption, salvation, and others. These concepts are appropriate to indicate a stable truthful content, not infrequently coined in an original way, in relation to the needs of expressions of faith. On the "practicability" of such a hermeneutic-dogmatic program, beyond the conditioning of history and language, *Fides et ratio* is still explicitly expressed:

Within its mission of service to all God's people, theology is called to deepen the content of dogma in a manner consistent with the content of Revelation. It therefore has the task of making this content ever more intelligible, as *fides quaerens intellectum*, both by assuming and making its own those reflections which the human person - the image of God and creature capable of speaking of his Creator - has elaborated with a philosophical reason originated in the *margins of* biblical revelation, and by generating an authentic philosophical reflection within the categories of Revelation and faith. In its work of discernment, theology accepts those "seeds of the Word" contained in many philosophical elaborations that preceded Christianity, or even followed it, but at the same time rejects other philosophical systems as inadequate to understand and explain the Christian mystery, assisted in this task by the necessary orientations of the Magisterium. From the beginning of the Church's evangelizing mission, in fact, not all philosophical systems and not all categories of thought were considered adequate to transmit the Christian message. The Fathers of the Church and, before them, the authors of the books of the NT, used concepts that they considered capable of expressing the Christian mystery, but they discarded others because they did not ensure the same capacity, even if more accessible and widespread.

##### Relationship with language
Even in the light of the historical-dogmatic progress which both the understanding and the transmission of the Word of God are subject to, in order to best express the content of Revelation the Magisterium of the Church can sometimes point out the need to preserve certain specific words. It belongs to the centuries-old tradition of the Church the use, in this regard, of a lexicon taken from "dead" languages, that is no longer subject to change of interpretation, as Latin and classical Greek are. This does not exempt, even in this case, the use of correct hermeneutics, but the content of the term appears better protected from undue interpretation.

In the encyclical *Humani generis* (12.8.1950), Pius XII recalled that the legitimate claim to make dogma more comprehensible must not lead to the unjustified abandonment of terms that maintain an important link with the faith of the people of God (cf. DH 3881-3883).

Everyone knows that the expressions of these concepts, used both in schools and by the Magisterium of the Church, can be improved and perfected; it is also known that the Church has not always been constant in the use of those same words. It is also clear that the Church cannot be linked to any ephemeral philosophical system; but those expressions and terms, which were composed by general consensus over many centuries by Catholic doctors to arrive at some knowledge and understanding of dogma, undoubtedly do not rest on such a transient foundation. They rely instead on principles and notions deduced from a true knowledge of creation (DH 3883).

In that same encyclical, as it will do almost 50 years after *Fides et ratio*, the use of the metaphysics of being is supported and it is urged not to depart without reason from the method, doctrine and principles of Thomas Aquinas (cf. DH 3892-3894).

In a dogmatic formulation, form and content are distinguishable (i.e. one can look for more and more appropriate forms to convey the same content), but they are never completely independent (i.e. one must keep forms, which would also lose the content). The fact that the mystery of Christ surpasses all possibility of expression and that its perennial *novelty* is entrusted to the ineffable action of the Spirit, does not imply that the forms of expression are only external coverings, interchangeable at will because they are merely symbolic in nature. In the professions of faith, linguistic terminology can become a true *incarnation of the word and*, therefore, something where form and content, linguistic event and mystery meaning become inseparable.

With regard to the teachings with which, in dogmatic matters, the Magisterium instructs God's people, it must be kept in mind that a community of faith is also a community built on the unity of words, those which identify its own confession.[43](about:blank) Therefore, the Magisterium can sometimes exhort us to keep certain precise linguistic expressions, even if they are not explicitly present in the traditional *Symbols* of the faith. Think for example of the term "transubstantiation" and the term "soul". When speaking of the consecration of the body and blood of Christ, Paul VI urged not to replace the term "transubstantiation" with other words, such as transignification or transfinialization, because the latter did not fully indicate, if considered in isolation, the content of the Eucharistic dogma.[44](about:blank) A similar case, equally instructive, is that concerning a 1979 declaration of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith on the use of the term "soul".[45](about:blank)

This does not mean that there can be no dogmatic progress in understanding these or other truths of faith, but only that such progress must be able to preserve and fully express the content of what is already believed, without diminishing or altering it, and in addition it must bring new lights that improve the intelligence of faith.

##### 4.4.4 Interpretation of dogmatic formulas
From the historicity of dogmatic formulations derives, as we have seen, the need for their *interpretation*. Responsible for this interpretation are theology and the Magisterium, with different respective functions. The task of theology is to interpret and deepen in a scientific way the content of dogma and, in its historical-hermeneutical aspects, the teaching of the Magisterium as part of the living Tradition of the Church. In turn, however, the task of the Magisterium is to interpret the dogma in an *authentic*, that is, *authoritative* way.

##### Some hermeneutical criteria
A correct interpretation of the Church's teaching cannot disregard some important *hermeneutical criteria*:

- interpret every single truth of faith within and in the general context of the whole Revelation (*fidei analogy*);
- interpret the various formulations within the historical-doctrinal context in which they originated;
- interpret the formulations in the light of who they were addressed to and taking into account what doctrinal errors these statements were intended to clarify or correct.

Keeping in mind the *contextuality* of the dogmatic formulas does not mean, however, that they are (or were) valid only within the particular historical-doctrinal context in which they originated: it is a hermeneutical, not truthful contextuality. Their value, once the sense of the content conveyed by these formulations has been grasped, becomes extra-contextual.

From the historical point of view, the role of context has been particularly evident in the development of the doctrine on "consubstantiality" between Father and Son, from the Council of Antioch (year 264) to the Council of Nicaea (year 325). In much more recent times, an interesting example is that of the evolution of the concept of "religious freedom" from the Magisterium prior to the Second Vatican Council to the declaration *Dignitatis humanae* of Vatican II.[46](about:blank)

The criterion of the *fidei analogy* can in turn be further explained:[47](about:blank)

- Christological and Christocentric consistency of dogmatic formulations, because the Word made flesh, revealing and revealed, is the center of Revelation itself;
- apostolicity, because the content of the faith has been transmitted once and for all and it is necessary to continue to renew its memory by interpreting it in the light of its apostolic origins;
- Catholicity, as a requirement and criterion of universality, not only synchronic but also diachronic, about the content of what has always been believed, by all;
- presence in the *lex orandi* of the people of God, because the liturgy, especially the sacramental praxis with the Eucharistic sacrifice at its center, is the living and unifying theological place of the faith of the Church.

As regards a criterion of an *anthropological* nature, that is, interpreting a teaching of the Church in the light of its meaning for man, it must be said that it is certainly present within every hermeneutical process that does not lose sight of the ultimate recipient of the teaching and the proclamation of salvation. However, this criterion cannot be the determining one:

The "anthropological criterion" also plays an important role in interpretation today. This statement obviously does not mean that man himself, certain needs or interests, or even fashion trends, can be the measure of faith and interpretation of dogmas. This is already excluded by the fact that man is for himself an unresolved question, for which God alone is the integral answer. Only in Jesus Christ is the mystery of man clarified: in him, the New Man, God has fully revealed man to man, revealed to him his most sublime vocation. Man is therefore not the measure, but the point of reference for the interpretation of faith and also of dogmas. He is also the path of the Church in the explanation of its dogmas.[48](about:blank)

Finally, it helps to clarify the issues dealt with here, remembering that the Gospel mandate entrusted by Jesus Christ to his Church consists, in its essence, in the *transmission* (*betrayal*) of the truth, not in *adapting* the formulations to the cultural needs of the proclamation. *The priority of transmission over interpretation* is basically a request made by the same salvific purpose as the Christian proclamation.
