### Chapter 3 Faith, God's gift and man's response {#tanzella-rivelazione.1.chapter-3-faith-god-s-gi}
#### 3.1 Anthropological-theological reflection on faith
##### 3.1.1 A hermeneutical premise
An exposition of the theme of faith has always accompanied the reflection of Fundamental Theology, even when its program followed a different scheme from the contemporary one, as happened for example in the neo-scholastic approach, which came to offer a "theology of the analysis of the act of faith" (*analysis fidei*). In speaking of faith, today's fundamental theology, like the theology of the past, cannot ignore the different meanings that this term can assume. Both the necessary internal dynamics of the theological discourse and the frequent references to the faith today in the philosophical-cultural debate, especially those which have anthropology and religion as their object, oblige this term to continually clarify itself.

From the theological point of view faith is understood as "theological virtue"; this makes it the object of a dogmatic, but also of a moral one, normally entrusted to the treatises on virtues. Internally to the theological-fundamental discourse, it presents itself with a double dimension, expressed in various ways: that of the *profession of faith* - and therefore as an act of the subject who believes - and that of the *content of faith -* as a set of truths believed - thus being able to be described according to the two subjective and objective perspectives, in turn in a certain connection with the understanding of faith as *fides qua* (faith *through which one* believes) and as *fides quae* (*things* believed by faith). This double dimension is a reflection of the twofold aspect, personal-subjective and content-objective, of Revelation, with which, however, faith should not be identified. Moreover, precisely with regard to Revelation, the theological-fundamental discourse on faith must take note of the existence of a dynamic and complex relationship, according to which faith can be understood yes as a *response* to God who reveals Himself, but also, in other respects, as a *premise* without which Revelation itself could not be accepted by the subject.

When confronted with philosophical thought (the philosophy of religion in a special way) and with today's language and culture, it should not be forgotten that the term "faith" indicates realities that are also very different. In common language it is generically understood as an expression of religion (to which it is not infrequently assimilated as a synonym), or equivalent to meanings such as: relationship with God, content of Scripture, Church teachings, trust in something or someone, but also belief, ideal, adherence to improbable or otherwise unverifiable content, etc..

The notion of faith, so often juxtaposed with that of "reason" - and often "confronted" with it in a dialectical way - has suffered the setback of the many different understandings that the very notion of reason has known throughout history. Depending on whether the *ratio* in question was that of the Fathers, that of monastic theology or medieval theology, that represented by Cartesian reason, rationalism or idealism, or was that corresponding to a natural reason as understood by the Catholic Magisterium in the last decades of the 20th century, in dealing with the relationship between faith and reason *fides* has also seen its content and understanding change drastically and inevitably. These objective hermeneutical difficulties should not, however, discourage the theological-fundamental discourse, but rather make it more rigorous. In essence, a theology that wants to develop an accurate and credible discourse on God, which can also benefit catechesis and evangelization, especially when dealing with philosophy, science and different cultures, should try to take this into account in relation to its interlocutors and their linguistic-cultural context.

Finally, from the second half of the twentieth century, also because of the progressive overcoming of the distinction between revealed and undisclosed religions, the history and philosophy of religions speak of faith as an anthropological attitude that accompanies every religious phenomenology in a constant way. The encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998), while offering important clarifications on the specificity of faith, will not be afraid to present man as "one who lives by faith" (n. 31).

In more general terms, anthropology today signals "trust" as a vital attitude, part of the balanced development of a human personality. The latter aspect deserves to be seen more closely here, both because of its relationship with theological discourse and because it can foster an "anthropological" understanding of the dynamism between faith and reason.

##### 3.1.2 The exercise of belief as an anthropological condition of knowing and living
Theology has recently been able to make use of the contribution made by philosophical-anthropological reflection on the relationship between faith and trust. The Catholic understanding of this relationship - which distances itself from the idea of "faith of trust" elaborated by Luther and proposed by the Reformists - carefully avoids making the theological faith derive directly from trust, positively recognized as a founding anthropological attitude, but of this trust employs originality and existential value as a demonstration that faith does not belong to the irrational sphere, but can develop on the extension of a garment that characterizes the human being from the earliest stages of his life.[1](about:blank) Trust is recognized as a key element in the formation of a psychically healthy personality, and as a basic prerequisite for orienting its relationship with reality, including the physical order. This attitude is so essential that, even in situations of despair, the search for a horizon of trust on which to rely remains a vital hope, because "only on the ground of trust is life generally possible".2

How to correctly set the relationship between *basic trust* and faith and how to use its virtualities in a "theology of faith"?[3](about:blank) If it is true that philosophical anthropology and psychology highlight the importance of an attitude of trust so that human beings can open themselves to reality, understand it and internalize it correctly, on pain of self-sufficiency and the inability to compare the truth of their knowledge and their actions, it is equally true that psychology or philosophy alone cannot determine the ultimate meaning towards which such dynamism is directed. Simply recording the presence of this provision, or interpreting it in purely psychological or biological terms (not least its value for survival within a Darwinian scheme of understanding human history) is insufficient. Trust and reliance demand to consider what the objective reasons for reliance are: they refer to something else and to someone else. They are as if "oriented" by the truth and goodness of the object, of the person, of the source of meaning, to which one entrusts oneself and, in the final analysis, to which one believes.

This insufficiency of a trust *sic et simpliciter* legitimate and makes a real distinction between trust and faith plausible: faith is born on the ground of trust, but faith is more than trust, because it includes the *personalist term to trust*, which trust alone does not see, if not, precisely, from within the relationships it is able to inaugurate and which, lately, could even refer to the biological sphere alone. Faith does not end in the erotic satisfaction of a dialogical relationship, but is motivated by an agapic tension towards the goodness-truth of the object to which it is directed, in order to know it, which, it should not be forgotten, theological faith employs both natural knowledge and Revelation. Already in the exercise of a human faith, and even more clearly for theological faith, faith and basic trust are not identified, because the former gives access to a horizon of trust that surpasses what direct experience, reason and even nature itself would suggest.

As Graziano Lingua notes briefly, "recovering the anthropological assumptions of faith, theological knowledge imposes on philosophy, in order to become a credible interlocutor, to abandon the conviction that faith is a dogmatic attitude and that only philosophy is able to preserve criticality. And consequently that only a faith conceived as a paradoxical leap can impose itself as sensible, precisely because it excludes itself from any relationship with critical thinking. Internal reliance on the act of faith would at this point be something purely irrational, or worse still, a ploy to resolve the irreducible problems of existence with a "positivism of faith". If there is a distant conception in the relapse into the line of thought to which a recovery of the relationship between faith and basic trust belongs, this is precisely the interpretation of faith as an "unreasonable leap"".4

A positive evaluation of human trust and faith as a founding anthropological dimension, capable of directing the relationship between faith and reason, can also be found in some pages of *Fides et ratio* (1998).

##### 3.1.3 Different anthropological and theological perspectives associated with faith
The task of Fundamental Theology is certainly to begin its understanding of the nature of faith by turning to Sacred Scripture, both in so far as the Word of God can explicitly say about it and in so far as, starting from it, those anthropological transformations/characterizations that God, by revealing Himself to man, induces, originates and reveals in him. It is therefore useful, for the purposes of an ever better understanding of the Christian faith, to highlight certain anthropological values associated with the faith, examining the images and categories used by Scripture.

1. *Faith as a response to Revelation.* Presenting faith in these terms emphasizes the priority of Revelation, the divine initiative, the gratuitous and unavailable character of the Word of God. Seen from the perspective of the human being, the existence of faith then becomes a sort of "condition of possibility" of Revelation as a word addressed to man and an event that encounters in man its phenomenologically accomplished outcome.[5](about:blank) The ontological priority of Revelation as Word makes it clear that being a hearer of this Word represents a true existential condition for the human person.

Faith as a response still highlights the vocational character of every human life, called by God not only to existence, but also to listen to his Creator and respond to him. The existentially pregnant value of this dialogue between God and human beings - willed by God *ut eos ad societatem Secum invitet in eamque suscipiat,* according to the well-known expression of *Dei Verbum*, 2 - means that the outcome of losing or finding oneself, of living according to one's own creatural truth or of deluding oneself that it is sufficient for oneself, depends on faith. If faith is expressed in a complete manner as an affirmative response to the Word heard and made its own, it should not be forgotten that even the amazement, the wonder, the sincere attention and the holy fear that man can manifest when he simply listens to the Word, also belong to the beginning of an itinerary which, although still unfinished, already represents in a significant way a first form of response.

1. *Faith as the fruit of listening to the Word.* The important biblical expression *fides ex auditu* makes it explicit that, in the dynamic of faith, the Word heard has priority over the thought elaborated: "Faith therefore depends on preaching, and preaching in turn is carried out through the word of Christ" (*Rom* 10:17). Faith is in the first place the acceptance of realities and truths that come to man in a free way and, only later, the result of rational articulation, the fruit of critical confrontation, the solution to a problem that has been placed and recognized as significant. Human rational elaboration does not *generate* faith, but deepens it and in a certain way can precede it, it highlights its coherence and internal articulation, its harmony with reason.

1. *Faith is expressed in words and deeds and has a dual dimension of mystery and event.* The nature of Revelation as a mystery offered and an event occurred, as God's self-communication in favour of man through words and deeds, means that faith too must show a double articulation of words and deeds. It presents a dimension of *mystery*, because through it man expresses an attitude that goes beyond the human, to the point of overcoming, and apparently contradicting, prudence, reason or life itself. However, the expressions of faith are also an *event*, because it manifests itself with human gestures that mark history and leave a visible trace, as a testimony of events that respond to precise circumstances: such are the decisions that it moves and that mark history, the monuments that faith builds, the relationships and the communities that it creates.

1. *Faith as knowledge.* Its relationship to Revelation, to characterizing faith as a response to a *Word of truth* also highlights its role as *knowledge*. The theology of virtues has not hesitated to present it as a participation in the knowledge of God and Christian tradition has transmitted the value of certain knowledge, capable of giving rise to existentially determinant attitudes, first and foremost *martyría*.[6](about:blank) The cognitive dimension of faith will then determine the possibility of theology, understood with Anselm of Aosta as *fides quaerens intellectum* which sanctions its equivalence with an *intelligere*, giving rise to what we call theological gnoseology, that is, a theology as a science of faith.

1. *Faith includes trust.* Faith certainly generates "trust" and in a certain way comes from it (thus the Greek πιστεύω translates both "to believe" and "to have trust in"), but its Catholic understanding invites us to distinguish it clearly from the latter, contrary to the understanding that the Reformists, Luther in particular (trusting faith), had of it. If Catholic currents, such as the Franciscan perspective, for example, put faith more in relation to love than to knowledge, it was not to deny the value of knowledge, but to indicate that no knowledge would be possible without the role of love, a role that a *fides a caritate formed, after all*, foresees and necessarily expresses. It should be considered that at the time when the Council of Trent elaborated a response to the Lutheran conception of justification, the two sides attributed a different emphasis to the notion of faith: the Catholic position understood it first of all as adherence and assent to the truths contained in the Gospel, and the second as a global act of the person who, giving himself with trust to God, obtains salvation from him.
2. *Faith as an existential commitment.* As knowledge capable of generating a demanding work, faith itself is this *commitment* of the subject, expressed by his act of "believing". This is in fact expressed by the etymology of various languages. In the German and English languages, the act of faith (*der Glaube*) or belief (*glauben*, *to believe*) appear in relation to terms indicating assumption of responsibility, such as love or promise (*geloben, lieben, to love)*. The Latin language also suggests that one of the possible derivations of the verb to believe is *cor dare*. Well known, in the Hebrew language, the content of commitment and adherence of the subject associated with the verb believe and expressed by his personal conjugation *amen* (from *'amàn*: to be firm, to sustain, to be faithful; in particular his intensive *he'emîn,* which indicates in man a process of faithful recognition at the end of which one adheres to Jahvè with certainty and reliability). Faith is therefore presented as an act of the person, by which the subject responds and gives himself to a Word which is also personal, that of the divine Word. *Having faith* is such a radically decisive act for the subject that he himself, in his most profound and binding attitudes, can rightly be qualified as a *believer,* or even a *believer*.

1. *Faith as a religious and personal act.* What has just been observed makes one understand, more generally, the need to consider the act of believing always as a *religious* act. As obvious as this consideration may seem, it is opposed, for example, to those conceptions that wanted to arrive at the act of faith only through an exclusively objective and rational itinerary. Alongside the conclusions of reflected reason, the logic of self-giving and self-giving is necessary. The act of faith is not an act due only to the intellect, an act more or less founded, more or less cogent within a specific form of rationality, dialoguing with which the dynamics of "believing" can be fully exposed: as a religious act, the act of faith implies the prior recognition of a horizon of creatural understanding, an attitude of humility (or at least the awareness of one's own finiteness and dependence), the admission of one's own non-self-sufficiency, the readiness to encounter the mystery. Only by accepting these implications are possible intellectual itineraries that aim to present the reasonableness of faith.

1. *Faith as obedient conversion.* The word of Revelation is a call to *conversion*. To turn back to God, who calls and questions man, is always a *cum vertere,* a directing together, that is, to interrupt one's own solitary progress and to decide resolutely that the direction of the journey is determined by an "us", to be undertaken in common, and therefore implies a change of orientation. The human being turning to God who calls must possess in himself the willingness to a *turn*, to a change. Faith is therefore renewal, in full harmony with the dynamic and effective value of the word of God, sent not so much to inform as to *trans-form,* having in itself the capacity to create and recreate, especially when its acceptance implies the passage from the nothingness of sin to the new being of grace, as Thomas Aquinas illustrated with a meaningful expression.[7](about:blank) Faith therefore implies a praxis, and not only because it is a response expressed in words and deeds: because of its intimate dynamic, in fact, the word heard and received is not delimited by a simple *audio,* but must give rise to an *ob-audio*. That is, he demands to let himself be led, guided, accompanied, because to God who reveals himself "is due the *obedience of faith,* with which man abandons himself to him in one piece and freely gives him the full respect of intellect and will" (DV, 2).

1. *The ecclesial and public dimension of the faith.* There are various reasons for recognizing in faith a *communal* and *social* dimension. It is a response to an invitation to participate in intratrinitarian communion, of which the baptismal formulas offer an explicit testimony. The word received, which the subject has accepted in the faith, has been transmitted to him by an ecclesial community and can only be understood within a living tradition. The contents of the faith (*fides quae*) are recognized and made proper by the individual *faithful in* front of an assembly-ekklesía, because "no one can determine for himself if he is a believer".[8](about:blank) The public profession of these contents associates the new faithful with the community of believers, which has become *congregatio fidelium*. However, the *subjective* and *free* nature with which the act of faith enables a new knowledge possessed and faced individually (*fides qua*), means that the community dimension can never replace or absorb the personal dimension of faith.

1. *Faith and prayer.* If Revelation is God's condescending dialogue with men, in this dialogue man's faith is expressed as *prayer*. After all, this is the true and most appropriate response, that is, faith, with which human beings turn to God. And he does so both after knowing its interventions in the history of salvation, and when he experiences his own creaturality and finiteness, perceiving that he is already on the horizon of a response to a natural revelation, confronting the mystery of Being and opening himself to the recognition of his Creator. One can therefore speak, as the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1997) intended, of a "revelation of prayer" and "prayer as an event of the Covenant" (cf. CCC 2567).

#### 3.2 Faith according to Holy Scripture
##### 3.2.1 The faith of Israel
The semantic area within which the relationship of *believing* Israel with its God is described includes many verbs in Hebrew, the main ones being: *he'emîn* (to have faith), *'amaàn* (to be firm, to sustain, but also to be faithful), *batah* (to trust), *hasah* (to find refuge), *qawah* (to hope), *hakah* (to expect), *jahal* (to wait). Understood in a broad sense, the human condition of "believing" expresses all that the just man must live towards God. The "believing" sums up, in fact, all these attitudes and their consequences, to the point that its opposite, *not* believing in God, or apostasy and idolatry, are the sin par excellence, the anti-behaviour of Israel, which God blames and condemns.9

In the Old Testament the fundamental root for understanding faith and its rich phenomenology is *'mn*. It gives rise to the verbs *he'emîn* and *'amaàn* and to the nouns: *'emûnah* (fidelity generated by faith), *he'mîn* (stability, security deriving from support on someone), *'emûn* (faith as exclusive and trusting love). Of special interest is the relationship of this root with the verbal form *amen* (from *'amaàn*) - translatable as "in truth", "I believe", "I say yes" - which expresses a firm and solemn commitment, of public value, a sort of responsible assent comparable to an oath.

A very special attention should be paid to the verb ***he'emîn*, present in the Jewish Bible 51 times in all, which expresses the solemn act of believing in Yahweh as faithful and is used exclusively in reference to Him.** Although numerically less frequent if compared to the corresponding πιστεύω of the NT (where it appears almost 250 times), *he'emîn* we meet him in a group of steps of special relevance: heard the promise of a lineage as numerous as the stars of heaven, "Abraham *believed the Lord*, who credited it to him as justice" (*Gen* 15:6); Having landed safely on the shore of the Red Sea after Yahweh had protected their exodus, "Israel saw the mighty hand with which the Lord had acted against Egypt and the people feared the Lord and *believed in him* and in Moses his servant" (*Ex* 14:31); in the face of Jonah's preaching, "the citizens of Nineveh *believed in God and* banned fasting, clothed the sack, great and small" (*Gen* 3:5).

Always translated with the Greek verb πιστεύω (to believe) in the version of LXX, *he'emîn* appears as the result of a religious itinerary experienced by the believer, who comes to recognize in God a firm and totally reliable support, worthy of trust and abandonment, invoked almost always in circumstances of danger and threat, for the individual as well as for the people.[10](about:blank) The action of believing in God therefore causes a shift from fear and fear to trust and peace. **In accordance with the logic expressed by the Hebrew language, this verb basically means an *action*, a *process*, not a state, nor just a relational fact.**

**The faith of Israel is neither opinion nor mere hope, but adherence to a stable word and, therefore, a principle of *true* understanding of the real**.[11](about:blank) Isaiah's statement in this regard is emblematic: "If you do not believe, you will not stand firm" (*Is* 7:9; cf. also *Is* 28:16 and *2Cr* 20:20). The sense of step does not stop at the Gnoseological plan, but reaches the ontological one: the question at stake, for Israel, is that between being and not being, between subsisting as God's people or disappearing from the face of the earth. The emphasis placed by Christian theology on the gnoseological aspect of this verse from Isaiah was historically dictated by the choice of the LXX to translate this passage into Greek "if you do not have faith you will not understand" (the Volgate, on the other hand, already translated more literally "si non credideritis non subsistetis"), thus inducing later thought to grasp faith mainly in its cognitive dimension, an approach that will mature in the Augustinian perspective with *credo ut intelligas*. In reality, the two translations are less distant than they seem at first sight, because the understanding generated by faith is understanding that rests on God and receives stability from Him alone, beyond appearances.

The Old Testament faith has in **Abraham its clearest icon**, destined to be solemnly re-proposed also in the New Testament (cf. e.g. *Rom* 4:1-22) because it is able to **highlight the two main aspects of biblical faith,** that of **trust/abandonment** and that of **firm** persuasion. In Abraham, faith takes on the commitment to leave the past in God's hands, as witnessed by his call to leave the land of Ur of the Chaldeans, but also the future, as shown by his willingness to sacrifice his son Isaac, the fruit of the promise of universal descent and blessing. The use of the perfect "*believed the Lord*, who credited it to him as justice" (*Gen* 15:6) still highlights faith as a condition of life capable of determining the entire existence of Abraham and not the description of a simple isolated act. Starting with Abraham, faith will be irrevocably linked to the recognition of an absolute monotheism: Abraham's faith, the origin of Israel's faith, is faith in the one true God, the Most High God, the creator of heaven and earth (cf. *Gen* 14:17-20). The NT, with the gospel of John, will present faith in Jesus Christ as the fulfilment of Abraham's faith (cf. *Jn* 8:31-40), demonstrating that adherence to Jesus of Nazareth implies a radicality of dedication to God that is in continuity with the faith of the Patriarchs and Fathers (cf. also *Heb* 1:1-2).

Dominated by the operative faith of Moses, the religious experience of Exodus reveals that it is faith in God that generates the identity of Israel as a people, a people that in the *Amen* recognizes itself as the people *of God,* thus arriving at its own identity and freedom (cf. *Dt* 27:9-26). Even before specific conceptual formulations - which will not be lacking - the faith of Israel develops as a result of the "memory" of events realized by God and behaviors required of man. Stability of faith and stability of memory recall one another: to be faithful means to preserve the memory of God's Love (cf. *Ps* 136). In this way, synthetic narratives of salvation history play the role of "confessions of faith", of which Deuteronomy will present several examples (cf. *Dt* 4:1-2; 6:1-9; 26:5-9). The same function is carried out by the religious hymns of praise and thanksgiving (cf. *Ex* 15:1-21) and by prayers, of which the Psalms offer ample testimony.

Precisely because it is rooted in a lived experience, the act of believing maintains in the People of God a great breadth of content and attitudes. *Psalms* 78 and 106, recalling the prodigies accomplished by Yahweh in the history of his people, show clearly that the act of "believing" includes knowledge of the saving power of Yahweh revealed through his works, trust in him and his promises, obedience to his words, first and foremost the words of the covenant and the law: faith is adherence to the God of the Promise and the Covenant. On the contrary, "not believing" translates into not considering/recognizing the works of the Lord, rebelling against His commandments, forgetting His goodness, whispering, questioning, not obeying.[12](about:blank)

Among the many figures in the First Testament who embody the faith of Israel and live its full consequences, the prophets and martyrs stand out. In the prophet, not infrequently a witness to martyrdom, faith summarizes and expresses his holiness of life, the firmness of his preaching, the radicality of his attitudes. The radicality of faith finds in the *martyr* - that is, in the one who, "testifying to the faith", is put to death by others because of the consistency of his belief - his fulfilled expression. Exemplary in this regard are the episode of Elijah's dispute against the false prophets of Baal (cf. *1Kings* 18,20-40) and the story of the Maccabees' insurrection, with the stories that originate in it (cf. *1Mac* 2,29-38; *2Mac* 7,1-41). The prophet and the martyr see in faith, understood as fidelity to God and his teachings, the greatest good to be defended, and they do so beyond their own lives, to affirm the meaning that life possesses. If in the First Testament the faith of the saints and martyrs will only gradually become faith in the resurrection and in life beyond death, it is, from the beginning, faith in the living God, and therefore faith in the One who recognizes himself as the origin, fullness and guarantor of life (cf. *Dt* 5:26; *Jos* 3:10; *Ps* 84:3; *Is* 37:4; *Ez* 33:11; etc.).

Approaching the phenomenology and content of the faith of Israel one understands why faith cannot be understood reductively as a search for subjective security in the face of questions that rationality and empirical knowledge could not solve. The dialogical dimension, personal and existential, is constitutive of the whole experience of faith, an experience capable of generating a firmer knowledge than any demonstration, because it is based on a Word that creates and saves. This way of understanding faith, as a summary of the relationships that must bind man to God and a response to the initiative of God who reveals himself, is undoubtedly very singular within the religious panorama. If faith is a common anthropological dimension, the ways in which this dimension is expressed and developed in salvation history are extremely original. Greek thought does not know faith as a gift to the divinity; not even knowledge of the depths of reality is entrusted to listening to a word that reveals and asks to be accepted, because it is entrusted instead to the exercise of rational thought. The worship of sovereigns in charge of governing peoples does not presuppose faith, because the divine nature of the sovereign simply descends from his authority. The Gnostic and mysterious religions, and in general the forms of religion that turn their attention mainly to nature, privilege a relationship with the divinity based on contemplation and ecstasy, not on faith.[13](about:blank) The character of Israel's faith seems to depend closely on the *historical and personal* nature of the Revelation of its God: what generates this faith are events that are remembered, what expresses this faith is personal adherence to a source of meaning and truth. But the faith of Israel, even when protected - not without difficulty - from idolatry, will know over time two great temptations: the first is the possibility that the unity and identity of the people, based on the unity of faith in their God, will be corrupted in nationalistic ideals; the second is to place one's own security in an extrinsic obedience to the Law, understood in a formalistic way, rather than in one's personal relationship with God. It was against these drifts that Jesus of Nazareth directed his preaching, whose first recipients were the members of his people.

##### 3.2.2 Faith in Christ and His saving message according to the New Testament
The multiple aspects of faith are concentrated in the NT in the terminology πίστις (faith)/πιστεύω (I believe). The object of this faith is primarily Jesus Christ in his divine mission as sent by the Father. The "faith" requested by Jesus from his disciples and those who come into or will come into vital contact with him is rich in important characterizations.

##### The perspective of the synoptic gospels
In the contexts of preaching and mission, typical of the synoptics, faith indicates above all the acceptance of the contents and logic of the Kingdom of God and the recognition of the divine power of the Master. The **object of faith is the Gospel** (cf. *Mk* 1:15), **as much if preached by Jesus as if spread by the apostles after his resurrection** (cf. *Mk* 16:16-17). An example of faith is Mary, whose belief in the word of God is praised by Elizabeth (cf. *Lk* 1:45) and presented by Luke in opposition to Zechariah's disbelief. For the stability of Peter's faith, Jesus himself prays (cf. *Lk* 22:32). The context of Jesus' resurrection is the terrain where faith and disbelief clash: the Synoptics will always use negative verbal forms (they did not want to believe, they did not believe, they doubted, they were foolish and late in believing...: cf. *Mk* 16:11.13; *Mt* 28:17; *Lk* 24:11; 24:25; 24:41), while we will encounter positive forms of the verb to believe only in John (cf. *Jn* 20:8; for the summary testimony of the evangelist: *Jn* 20:31; Jesus' answers to the disbelief of the apostles, of Thomas in particular: *JV* 20.26-29).

**The term "faith" can also mean an attitude of trust**, that is, **"trusting Jesus", or even "trusting God**". Peter, invited to walk on the waters, deserves the reproach of a man of "little faith" (gr. ὀλιγόπιστε: *Mt* 14,31); as do the apostles frightened by the storm on the lake, despite the presence of Jesus in their midst (gr. ὀλιγόπιστοι: cf. *Mt* 8,26). There are still ὀλιγόπιστοι, men of little faith, those who worry about material things without putting their faith in God (cf. *Mt* 6:30; *Lk* 12:28), or the disciples themselves, because they are worried that they have not brought with them bread stocks (cf. *Mt* 16:8).

T**he synoptics speak of faith mainly in terms of *faith that Jesus can do something*, essentially in reference to healing miracles** (cf. *Mk* 9:23-24; *Mt 9:*27-30; Mt 15:28; *Mk* 5:36; *Mt* 8:5-10; *Mt 9:*2; *Mk* 2:5; *Lk* 5:20; *Mt 9:*22; *Lk* 17:19; etc.). Apart from the passage reported by Matthew on the exhortation not to scandalize the little ones who have faith in Him (cf. *Mt* 18:6), **the synoptics do not explicitly speak of a faith *in Jesus,*** unlike what John and Paul will do much more extensively. In his homeland Jesus performs few miracles because of their "unbelief" (gr. ἀπιστία, *Mt* 13,58; *Mk* 6,6). The disciples themselves are unable to drive out some demons because of their little faith (cf. *Mt* 17:20).

The synoptics also know a **dialogical dimension of faith, as faith in Jesus Christ as an adherence to his person.** In the proximity of the events of the Passion, in fact, to believe what Jesus said and taught means above all to adhere to Him, to be willing to follow Him, including the experience of the cross. Moreover, *love for the person of Jesus, as* witnessed by the synoptics (cf. *Mt* 10:37-39; *Lk* 7:47-50), is certainly an expression of a faith that has been directed recently to him and not only faith in his thaumaturgical abilities. In the synoptics faith is also associated with **prayer**, indicating the trust and security with which to turn to God (cf. *Mk* 11:22-24; *Mt* 17:20; *Lk* 17:5-6), typical of the filial condition (cf. *Mt* 7:7-11).

##### The Christocentric content of faith and belief according to the Fourth Gospel
The strongly Christocentric ways in which John's Gospel speaks of faith and belief are rich in numerous theological-fundamental resonances. Even if the noun "faith" appears only once (cf. *Jn* 14:1), the verb πιστεύω dominates the whole Johannineum. The latter appears 98 times in the *Gospel* and 9 times in the *First Letter*, compared to 34 times in the three synoptics as a whole.

**Faith, and therefore belief, are understood as adhering to the person of Jesus;** this is shown by the great frequency with which the expression **"to believe in" is proposed (πιστεύειν εἰς) referred to Him as an object** (more than 30 times: cf. e.g. *Jn* 2,11; 2,23; 3,15-18; 3,36; 9,35; 17,20; etc.). To convince us that we are faced here with an original conception of the use of the verb "to believe" it might suffice to consider that in classical Greek this construction appears only in 8% of cases, while John uses it 35% of the times he uses this verb and all NT uses it in 18% of cases. Jesus himself invites us to believe in him (cf. *Jn* 9:35; 14:1), to believe that he is the Messiah (cf. *Jn* 4:26; 11:25-27), making it clear that to believe in him is to believe in him who sent him (cf. *Jn* 12:44; cf. also *Jn* 11:42; 17:3.8.21). **Believing in God and believing in Jesus Christ his Son are on the same level** (cf. *Jn* 14:10). In John, **faith in Jesus Christ is also faith in *the name of Jesus*** (cf. *Jn* 1:12; 2:23; 3:18; cf. also *1Jn* 5:13) or faith in his divine sonship, in his divine condition.

Faith establishes between the believer and Christ an interpersonal relationship of profound and **mutual immanence**, expressed by the verb "to remain" (cf. *Jn* 6:56; 15:15; 17:20), according to an idea which John will propose again in all his *First Letter,* also calling into question the presence of the Spirit, the revelator of living in faith and truth. **The request to adhere to Jesus and his teachings** - an adherence that expresses at bottom the biblical content of the exercise of faith - is represented by the widespread presence of passages in which Jesus exhorts his disciples **to love him** (cf. *Jn* 14:15.21; 16:27; 21:15-17), as a correspondence to his love for them and an image of the love that binds the Father and the Son.

The strong personalist accent that John takes on the act of believing does not prevent his Gospel from clearly exposing the ***cognitive* aspect of faith: believing is also *knowing***. A brief analysis, originally proposed by J. Alfaro[14](about:blank) and slightly reworked by us, shows some salient passages where this correspondence appears explicitly, almost literally:

In the language of John, it would seem that **the verb believe precedes the verb know**, that is, we know why we believe, thus leading to the affirmation of a *credite ut cognoscatis* (*John* 10:38). "We have believed and known that you are the Holy One of God" (*Jn* 6:69); "they truly know that I came out of you and they have believed that you sent me" (*Jn* 17:8; cf. 8:31-32; 4:42; *1Jn* 4:16). As Alpharo pointed out, the main conclusion of this singular gnoseology is that in John's mind, believing in Christ and knowing Christ (and God through Christ) are in fact inseparable. However, in the relationship between the Son and the Father, even for John there is only a knowledge and never a belief.

Knowledge of Jesus Christ, whose Person claims to be the same Truth (cf. *Jn* 12:36; 14:6), is not limited to a personal-dialogical relationship, but also implies an understanding of events (cf. *Jn* 20:8), of Scripture (cf. *Jn* 2:22), of the meaning of gestures and words (cf. *Jn* 6:29). One could say that in John's Gospel, although the Christocentric-personalist dimension of faith is clearly privileged, the *fides quae* and *fides qua* still remain well harmonized: faith in Jesus is faith/fidelity to his words, which are the words of his Father.

Significantly, throughout the Fourth Gospel, the link between faith/truth and salvation: the fruit of the act of believing is the knowledge of God, but precisely in this consists eternal life (cf. *Jn* 6:40; 11:40; 20:31). Concepts that manifest a certain analogy with the "knowledge of faith", such as light, truth and word, are combined with concepts of soteriological value, such as life and salvation (cf. *Jn* 1:4; 6:68; 14:6).

For the purposes of a theology of credibility, the Johannine insistence that Christ admonishes with a decision to *believe in his works* as a suitable sign to inspire faith in him is of great interest (cf. *Jn* 2:11.23; 4:53; 6:30; 11:15; 14:11; 20:26-29). But faith is in constant struggle with incredulity, as emerges from the Johannine's continuous re-proposal of the contrast between light and darkness, between truth and lies (cf. *Jn* 1:5.10; 3:19-20; 9:39-41). As a whole, the whole of the Fourth Gospel is self-propelled as a solemn appeal to believe, in order to sustain the interlocutor in living contact with Christ, the source of this faith, and with his works: "Many other signs Jesus made in the presence of his disciples, but they have not been written in this book. These were written, that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that, believing, you may have life in his name" (*Jn* 20:30-31).

##### The Acts of the Apostles: the congregatio fidelium as an effect of belief
In reporting the preaching of the nascent Church, the *Acts of the Apostles* place faith in Jesus-Messiah and his Resurrection at the centre of the proclamation (*Acts* 4:4; 5:14; 10:43; 11:21; 13:39; 16:31; etc.). Faith in Christ the Messiah is not merely a feeling nourished by hope, but is presented as certain knowledge: "let the whole house of Israel know with certainty that God has made Lord and Christ the Jesus whom you have crucified" (*Acts* 2:36). Believing is the effect of preaching a Word heard and accepted, and immediately finds its public concretion in the administration of the sacrament of baptism (cf. *Acts* 2:41; 8:12; 15:7; 16:14-15). Thus, in the episode of the jailer evangelized by Paul and Silas, we find a synthetic summary of this dynamic: "Then he led them out and said: "Gentlemen, what must I do to be saved?". They replied, "Believe in the Lord Jesus and you and your family will be saved. And they proclaimed the word of the Lord to him and to all those in his house. He took them with him at that hour of the night, washed their sores and was immediately baptized with all his own; then he took them up into the house, set the table and was filled with joy with all his own for having believed in God" (*Acts* 16:30-34).

Becoming part of God's people through baptism is indicated as "adhering to the faith" (cf. *Acts* 4:32; 6:7; 13:48; 15:7), thus showing a use of the term πίστις which refers as much to the following of Christ as to the content of things believed. This is how the term "believers" (gr. πιστεύοντες; cf. *Acts* 2,44. Cf. also *1Pt* 1,8) and "faithful" (gr. πιστοὶ; cf. *Acts* 10,45; 16,15), used as a synonym for "saints" (cf. *Acts* 9,13; 9,32; 26,10), are coined, to indicate those who have accepted in faith the preaching of the Apostles. The saints, the faithful and believers are, in essence, "those who have believed" (cf. *Acts* 14:1; 15:5; 17:34; 18:27; etc.).

In apostolic preaching, faith in Jesus Christ is inseparable from conversion: the proclamation of the Risen One is at the same time a heartfelt call to conversion from the works of evil to the works of good, from the hardness of the heart to the logic of the Kingdom, from the expectation of the Messiah to faith in Jesus the Christ (cf. *Acts* 3:26; 5:31; 9:35; 11:18; 11:21; 14:15; 15:3; etc.). In *Acts*, belief is never limited to the mere intellectual acceptance of the kerygma announced, but implies a decision to change one's life involving the whole person, the resolution to place the event of Christ at the center of a renewed existence. In this sense, the adjective *believer* is never for Christians a simple socio-religious description, but always remains a highly meaningful qualification, sufficient to indicate a precise lifestyle and sufficient to identify a series of behaviors that Jews and pagans expect to see reproduced in them.

##### Understanding of faith in Paul
In the Pauline *corpus* the faith is understood in a horizon of grace, and therefore according to a personalist perspective that links existentially to the mystery of Jesus Christ. However, there is no lack of explicit reference to faith as a deposit of truth to be guarded and faithfully transmitted. Believing in Jesus Christ and confessing one's faith brings for the Apostle essentially three contents:

- to adhere in a vital way to the Son of God, taking as its effect the justification of the sinner;
- to recognize that in Christ God's promises to His people were fulfilled, thus reaffirming His faithfulness;
- to transmit with care a doctrine and a practice of life that characterize what is received from Christian preaching, that is, a *depositum fidei*.

In Paul, "faith" is manifested in the logic of grace, that of God's free gift and that which makes man capable of welcoming him. Faith would even seem to have little of a human nature, because it requires such radical reliance and such a new logic that it can be linked solely to God's gift, without any point of support in man's desire and expectations. In theological terms this excess is that of the grace of faith over the works of the law, a relationship which, as is well known, is mainly developed in the *Letter to the Romans* (cf. *cc.* 3-5). In the discussion of this report the term "faith" is often used in an absolute way, without any complement, as a pole of a federation/work opposition.

Expression-guide for the Pauline understanding of the Christian saving dynamic is the justification/salvation that comes "through faith in Christ Jesus" (rendered with both prepositions ἐν and εἰς), and which finds development in the letters to the Romans and the Galatians: "Knowing however that man is not justified for the works of the Law but only through faith in Jesus Christ, we too have believed in Christ Jesus to be justified for faith in Christ and not for the works of the Law; for for for the works of the Law no one will ever be justified". (*Gal* 2:16; see also *Gal* 3:26).

In Pauline theology we find clear examples of the use of the term "faith" both as an act/state of the believer (cf. *Rom* 12:3; *Rom* 14:1; *Eph* 4:13; *Col* 2:6-7; etc.) and as the content of the kerygma believed (cf. *Rm* 10,8; *Gal* 1,23; *Eph* 4,5; *1Tm* 4,1; etc.), legitimising from the beginning the double dimension of a *fides qua creditur* and a *fides quae creditur*, contrary to those who would like the latter to be extraneous to the Apostle's message. In particular, the *Pastoral Letters* are the Pauline place where the term "faith", probably because of the climate of exhortation to fidelity typical of these writings, comes to mean quite clearly also "the contents transmitted", whose careful custody is aimed at the good of the faithful (cf. *1Tm* 1,4; 6,12), thus giving rise to the concept of "deposit received" (cf. *2Tm* 4,7; 1,13-14).

Paul does not lack in Paul the classical understanding of faith and belief as a response to the Christian proclamation, especially to that of the redeeming death and resurrection of Jesus (cf. *Rom* 6:8; *1 Th* 4:14); indeed, faith itself finds in the resurrection its raison d'être, which, if it is denied, faith would be "in vain" (cf. *1 Cor* 15:17). Evident is the bond of faith with the word of preaching (cf. *Rom* 10:14-17), often reaffirmed with particular solemnity: "Then I proclaim to you, brethren, the Gospel which I have proclaimed to you and which you have received, in which you remain firm and from which you are saved, if you keep it as I have proclaimed it to you. Unless you believed in vain [...]. So both they and I preach thus, and so you have believed" (*1 Cor* 15:1-2.11). There is a precise correspondence between what is preached and what is believed (cf. *1Tm* 3,16; *1Cor* 3,5; *2Ts* 1,10). The meaning of faith as obedience is also clear (cf. *Rom* 1:5; 10:17; 16:26; *2 Cor* 10:5-6).

##### Other New Testament books
A very significant biblical page for the theology of faith is the solemn praise of the faith reported in chapter 11 of the *Letter to the Hebrews*. In it faith is presented as a global orientation of the life of the just, a source of fidelity, perseverance, a spirit of sacrifice and a sure sign of heroism in practically all the virtues. Faith - it is affirmed in the verses preceding the development of the chapter cited - informs the entire existence of the just man, because *iustus ex fide vivit* (*Heb* 10:38) according to an aphorism repeatedly proposed by Scripture (cf. *Ab* 2:4; *Rom* 1:17; *Gal* 3:11; cf. also *Gal 2:*20). The chapter retraces the history of Israel's salvation, marked by the faith of patriarchs and prophets, showing that faith consists in *having believed God's promises,* as a reason capable of motivating an entire life practice. The Author chooses to start this singular "history of faith" with a cosmological reference, "by faith, we know that the worlds were formed by the word of God, so that from the invisible the visible world originated" (*Heb* 11:3), as if to emphasize that faith in God the Creator offers an important theological basis from which that history can later unfold and be understood. At the beginning of the chapter we encounter a "definition" of faith destined to exert a broad influence in the history of theology, that is, faith as "the foundation of what is hoped for and what is not seen" (*Heb* 11:1). Although it admits different interpretations, this expression is basically intended to draw attention to the fact that through faith, by listening to the word of God, we can already know how much we can only hope for by not seeing.

Particular mention should be made of the doctrine of the *Letter of James* concerning the need to unite faith to works: faith without works is dead (cf. *Jas* 2:14-26). The doctrine of James, which concerns the works of charity, thus completes and clarifies that of Paul, who considered rather the works of the Law, of which the Apostle of the Gentiles pointed out the intrinsic insufficiency.

The *First Letter of Peter* presents, similarly to what happens in the Pastoral Epistles, a widespread understanding of faith in an objective sense, as a doctrine which leads to salvation and which must therefore be defended as a precious good: "[you] who are guarded by the power of God through faith, in view of the salvation which is about to be revealed in the last time" (*1 Pet 1*:5; cf. *1 Pet 1:*7.9). The object of faith is God, but also Christ, the cornerstone, according to a metaphor that recalls the stability of faith (*1 Pet* 2:6-8), reproposed in closing with reference to the fight against the evil one: "stand firm in the faith" (*1 Pet* 5:9). Faith reaches the historical Jesus Christ, even though you have not physically attended him: "you love him, even though you have not seen him and now, without seeing him, you believe in him". (*1Pt* 1.8).

##### 3.2.3 Forms of understanding faith in Sacred Scripture
As already examined in a previous section (see 1.3), in the biblical message on faith it is possible to find numerous anthropological and theological perspectives that favour the understanding of its dynamism, its contents and its effects. Let us now return to some of the previous perspectives, for a brief biblical in-depth examination.

##### Faith expresses man's obedient listening to God
Faith and obedience recall each other, each as the fruit of the other, also according to a reciprocal virtuous circularity. As both Greek and Latin etymology seems to indicate, obeying presupposes listening (ὑπακούω, from ὑπο-ἀκούω, I turn the ear, obbedisco; *oboedio*, from *ob-audio*). If listening is an intrinsic dimension of faith and prior to it when it is understood as a response to God who reveals himself, at the same time listening is realized within the horizon of the Covenant, possible thanks to man's decision to correspond with faith to God's initiative. The great importance given by all Scripture to human hearing depends on the fact that the Word is the fundamental way in which God reveals Himself and that man, created in the image of God, is constitutively capable of listening to it. Faith without obedience would turn out to be mere trust of a sentimental nature; obedience without faith would be incapable of orienting itself to the true God, because it would remain closed in its own selfishness or it would bind itself only to an idol, thus understanding itself as slavery, because true faith brings with it precisely freedom. Listening to the word of God, welcoming it and putting it into practice by obeying it, as shown by the great icon of the Annunciation (cf. *Lk* 1:38), are phases of a single itinerary, praised as a path and source of bliss (cf. *Lk* 11:28). Faith itself is obedience, that is, obedient faith, faith that listens and puts into practice, thus being able to speak of ob*edience of faith* (cf. *Rom* 16:26; *Rom* 1:5).

The link that faith has with listening, even before seeing, indicates that the obedience of faith welcomes as its own a truth that is narrated in a dialogical-personal way, rather than shown as evident or demonstrated in an objective way. It requires thinking and seeing through the mind and eyes of the "Other". As expressed by the psalmist, "light for my steps is your word, light on my path" (*Ps* 119:105; cf. *Ps* 18:29): in the exercise of faith it is the word that "makes one see", not vice versa. The obedience of faith requires silence, recollection, interiority, all those factors that favour listening. The image of the desert as a place of encounter with God and prayer, a place of trial and a place of faith (cf. *Hos* 2:16; *Dt* 8:2-6; *Rev* 12:14. Cf. also *Mt* 14:13; *Mk* 1:35; *Lk* 4:42).

##### Faith as inclusion in the horizon of knowledge and divine life
Both in the First Testament (cf. *Is* 43:10-12; *Wisdom* 3:9; *Ps* 77) and in the New (the Fourth Gospel in particular), the act of believing and the act of knowing are brought together. However, this convergence should not be interpreted in a reductive way, as if it were a matter of material knowledge that man draws from God and draws towards himself. In reality, through the exercise of faith, it is man who is captivated by the spiritual sphere of God's life, not only by penetrating what, contemplated by that sphere, he now knows, but also by reaching a new understanding of reality, now captured in his radical dependence on God. "If you do not believe, you will not have stability" (*Is* 7:9): the experience of faith is an experience of *stability, that is,* of a new ontological position of the subject before God, which also drags with it a new relationship with reality. In this sense, the experience of faith formed by love is an experience of grace, a participation in the way God knows and the way all things depend on God.

Therefore, those attempts to place the core of faith in an act of the intellect, or in an act of the will, are insufficient, considering them as human faculties which refer to (and in grace elevate) only *a part of* man's relationship with God. The sphere to which the life of faith enables us is not only knowledge that goes beyond what natural man could reasonably know, nor only will that chooses values that transcend it and to which we must rely. As Romano Guardini rightly pointed out, what presents itself to the conscience of the believer is not really a "truth" or a "value", but a reality.[15](about:blank) The faith that Jesus asks Peter and many other interlocutors who cross his path is the access to this space and this vision of things, which participates in God's vision: for this reason, Peter can walk on the waters as long as he keeps his gaze fixed on Christ and sees things through him; for this reason, those who ask him with faith for a miracle can experience almost spontaneously what nature is capable of doing when it reveals its original, ontological bond with the Creator. Faith is affirmed where reason leaves open a door through which it cannot enter alone. For this reason faith drags reason beyond itself, redeeming it from fear of its creatural limit and finiteness, opening up to it the horizon of Infinity and its Promise.[16](about:blank)

Faith is yes knowledge, but not purely intellectual knowledge, because it is an act guided by love and aimed at a personal relationship. It enables one to "think affectionately and love intelligently", according to an expression by Antonio Rosmini, proposing oneself as a "sapiential knowledge".[17](about:blank) As with the exercise of faith, the exercise of wisdom also consists in living according to the word of God by sharing in His knowledge of all things (cf. e.g. *Prv* 2:1-13; 3:1-20; *Wisdom* 8:1-4). As we have already mentioned when speaking of wisdom revelation in the First Testament, the search for Wisdom is itself a search for God, to possess Wisdom is to live according to God. For this reason, wisdom must be sought more than anything else and, by possessing it, one achieves the purpose of one's existence.

In Pauline theology we observe that faith in Christ is capable of generating *wisdom*, distinct from the wisdom of the world, because it is based on the Revelation of God, known through the gift of the Spirit and manifested in the scandal of the Cross (cf. *1 Cor* 1:18-25; 2:2.6-14). Yet, the "wisdom of the cross" does not imply a mere surrender of reason, but offers it access to a wider and deeper vision of things, reclaimed by the same fundamental questions that reason encounters and leaves unsolved. Reason finds "in the preaching of Christ crucified and risen the rock against which it can shipwreck, but beyond which it can flow into the boundless ocean of truth".[18](about:blank) Since Christ himself is the Wisdom of God (cf. *1 Cor* 1:24) and since he contains all the treasures of wisdom and science (cf. *Col* 2:3), to adhere to Christ in faith means to possess the νοῦς of Christ (*1 Cor* 2:16), to acquire His knowledge, that is, the knowledge of God. It is therefore legitimate to speak not only of a *sophia*, but also of a Christian *gnosis*. In contemporary terms, as discussed again by Romano Guardini, we can affirm that there is a *Catholic Weltanschauung,* an organic vision of the world, which becomes for the believer taking a stand before the world, a task, a unitary intuition. The gaze of the Catholic vision of the world is the gaze of Jesus Christ.[19](about:blank)

##### Faith as a continuous and constant path of conversion
The act of faith, the act of embracing faith within one's own existence, represents a precise condition of life, that of the *faithful*. This act is presented by Scripture as a radical act, capable of determining an essential turning point, and yet an act called to confirm itself and to reproduce itself constantly over time, thus becoming an *attitude*. Christian salvation does not come because it is the effect of "progress" - according to the meaning of this term handed down to us by Enlightenment and idealist thought - but because it gives rise to an event of conversion.[20](about:blank) As already mentioned, "conversion" implies the recognition of an otherness, the listening to a source of meaning that invites one to a personal act of faith/trust, and therefore capable of determining the direction of one's own journey: "this is why *metanoia* means the same thing as obedience and faith".[21](about:blank)

Illustrative in this regard is the close relationship between preaching and conversion: the word of God, addressed to man by his Prophets, by the Baptist or by Jesus himself, is answered with a conversion of life. Thus Jonah's preaching to the inhabitants of Nineveh: "and the citizens of Nineveh believed in God" (*Jn* 3:1); that of the Baptist, who appeared "to preach in the desert of Judea saying, 'Convert, for the kingdom of heaven is near'". (*Mt* 3:1-2); that of Jesus, who "went into Galilee preaching the gospel of God and saying, 'The time is fulfilled and the kingdom of God is at hand; repent and believe the gospel'". (*Mc* 1.14-15).

Only after this conversion can we speak of a *fruitfulness*, and therefore of a "journey of faith". Without the orientation of faith, any progress is merely a shift, but not a progress. Only in faith and conversion is progress *faithfulness*. In understanding faith as a *way* (cf. *2 Cor* 5:6-7) it is then recognized that truth can also manifest itself as a *way* (cf. *Jn* 14:6), thus showing that one never completely possesses it, because one is never completely converted. Indeed, one is possessed by truth to the extent that it is allowed to continually direct the progress of man, just as the magnet, permeated by the magnetic field in which it is immersed, can always maintain the same direction.

The experience of Exodus (literally, "path to the exit") - destined to leave a fundamental mark not only in the religious life of Israel, but also in the understanding of the Christian faith - can then be read as a continuous journey of faith and conversion. This is marked by events that mark God's relationship with Moses and the chosen people, according to an itinerary whose stages are represented by prodigious works and events of revelation on the one hand, and by afterthoughts and conversions on the other.

The biblical vision and Christian tradition includes the image of the Church as a people on the *road* (cf. LG 6:8,9), whose pilgrim members are on the way while they have not yet reached those who are already *at home*; an image founded in turn on the oldest metaphor of Christian life as a *way* (cf. *Acts* 18:26: "the way of God").[22](about:blank)

The life of Mary, mother of Jesus, is a shining example of the "journey of faith" (cf. LG, 58). Her total entrustment to God in the Annunciation "represents the culminating moment of Mary's faith in expectation of Christ, but it is also the starting point from which her entire "journey towards God", her entire journey of faith, begins. [...] And this "obedience of faith" on Mary's part throughout her journey will have surprising analogies with the faith of Abraham".[23](about:blank)

##### Faith as a passage from fear and fear to safety and joy
It is astonishing how many times the exhortation to have faith, both in the First and the New Testament, is associated with the encouragement to "do not be afraid". The act of believing realizes in the believer a passage from a situation of insecurity, threat and fear, to a situation that enjoys the comfort and consolation of God, to whom one entrusts oneself trusting, that is, believing, in his word: "do not fear, continue only to have faith" (*Mk* 5:36). Practically all prophetic preaching bears ample witness to this, especially around the experience of the second exile: "Fear not, for I am with you; go not astray, for I am your God. I make you strong and come to your aid and support you with the right hand of my justice" (*Is* 41:10; cf. *Is* 40:9; 41:14; *Jer* 23:4; 30:10; *Dn* 10:19; *Gl* 2:21; etc.). The idea of a faith that heals uncertainty and fear often also appears in the Gospels (cf. *Mt* 6,30; *Mt* 16,8; *Mt* 17,20; *Lk* 12,28); in particular, the encouragement not to be afraid/feeling followed by a warning to have faith (cf. *Mt 8,*26; *Mt* 14,27.31; *Mk* 5,36; *Lk* 5,10; *Lk* 8,50; *Jn* 6,19-20). We can also trace it in the original proclamation of the resurrection of Jesus (cf. *Mt* 28:5; *Mk* 16:6) and in the encounter of the twelve with the Risen One, described as the passage from fear to seeing a ghost to joy in recognizing Him who was dead alive (cf. *Lk* 24:37-41).

As in the Annunciation to Mary, mother of Jesus, the invitation to "fear not" is intended to dispose the interlocutor interiorly to listen to the Word with fruitfulness and to believe in it with a resolute spirit (cf. *Lk* 1:30; cf. also, for Joseph, *Mt* 1:20).

##### Faith as a set of confessed truths and a repository to cherish
The presentation of the biblical datum on the notion of faith here synthetically carried out has pointed out that the act of believing (gr. πιστεύω) not only indicates personal dedication, but also indicates the recognition, profession, custody and transmission of knowledge that express the content of things believed through formulations that are progressively structured as "teachings", i.e. *dogmas* (gr. δόγματα). It is an itinerary already present in the religious experience of Israel, which knows specific professions of faith with which to crystallize one's own creed (cf. *Dt* 6:20-24; 7:6-9; 26:5-9; *Gs* 24:2-13; *Ps* 78; 106; 135; 136), often introduced by the usual call to listen: "Listen to Israel: the Lord is our God, the Lord is one" (*Dt* 6:4). As in the First Testament, verbs such as "to proclaim", "to confess" or "to believe" in the New Testament will have as their object precise verbal content, namely "faith" (cf. *Gal* 1:23; *1Tm* 3:16; *1Pt* 1:21). According to the Pauline expression, "with the heart one believes to obtain justice and with the mouth one makes the profession of faith to obtain salvation" (*Rom* 10:10). The notions of *depositum fidei* and *regula fidei* are thus progressively affirmed: the economy of Revelation, as we shall see more extensively in the talk of Tradition (see chapter IV), necessarily includes the custody and transmission of a "deposit" (gr. παραθήκη: cf. *1Tm* 6.20; *2Tm* 1.12.14; cf. also *2Ts 2.*15; 3.6).

Precisely because articulated in professable and recognizable contents, the "creed" becomes "confession" and faith can have its *confessors*. This is an original aspect of Christianity (and Judaism before it), because unlike the religions neighbouring Israel and, subsequently, unlike the Greek-Roman pagan religion, believing in the God of Israel, in the God of Jesus Christ, implies making choices, implies giving an irrevocable value to words and things. Christianity could not accept to be a religion among the others in the *Pantheon* of the gods, peacefully tolerated but distorted, because this required a ductility and flexibility in the professed contents, nonchalantly subscribed to by many local religions, but to which Christianity was not available. Faith, in fact, "was passed on to believers once and for all". (*Gd* 1.3).

Faith and confessed truths become synonymous: turning away from the truth is qualified as a turning away from faith, or even a "shipwreck" (cf. *1Tm* 1:19). Faith is something that we share in common (cf. *Rom* 1:12; *Gal* 6:10; *2Pt* 1:1), it must be preserved to be presented before God (cf. *Rom* 14:22), it is a source of unity in knowledge (cf. *Eph* 4:13). The truth of the confession of faith becomes an object of pastoral concern: Paul exhorts the elders of the Church of Ephesus to preserve the transmitted teachings (cf. *Acts* 20:29-30; cf. the whole context vv. 17-38) and sends Timothy to the Thessalonians to "take news" about their faith and "confirm" it, to rejoice immediately afterwards in the knowledge that it was kept in its entirety (cf. *1 Th* 3:2-7).

The public and external dimension of the *confession of faith means* that this, even if formulated through propositions or articles, should not be seen as a mere enunciation, but remains, precisely, a "confession".[24](about:blank) He confesses to someone and in front of someone: that is, it is a public proclamation with the value of a testimony. As he manifests the specificity of the event and the mystery of Jesus Christ, the confessor at the same time shows its universal value, that of a creed proclaimed and announced to the people, not a private gnosis or ideology.

##### Faith is manifested and understood within a horizon of grace
All biblical theology of faith is pervaded by a *horizon of grace*. Not only in the sense of the gratuitousness with which Revelation and the gifts of God reach the one who believes, but also because the act of believing presents itself as an act accomplished *in grace* and *by the work of grace*: "Through him we have received the grace to be apostles, to arouse obedience to the faith in all nations, for the glory of his name" (*Rom* 1:5). Although the role of grace is especially evident, among others, in the Pauline understanding of faith, it is all the biblical aspects of faith, in one way or another, that manifest their connection with the divine Gift. They demand the obedience and conversion with which man reacts to listening to the Word, possible only through a gift of God who disposes him to listen and moves his heart, thus making him the recipient of an "inner revelation". This is required by the ontological elevation implied by faith, so that it makes the subject capable of accessing knowledge that belongs to the sphere of God and therefore goes beyond what is simply known by reason. The gift of grace is manifested in fidelity, perseverance of faith that keeps the adherence to God steadfast during the journey of life, despite temptations and discouragement. The *status of* the believer who adheres to Christ through faith and remains grafted onto the logic of his life is still an expression of grace.

The "theology of faith" must therefore include the *gift of grace*, as the founding and constitutive moment of the act of believing, as well as of the faithful persevering in all that this existentially and historically involves (cf. *Rom* 1:2; 4:16; *Eph* 2:8; *1Tm* 1:14). This justifies the request that the divine gift of grace be foreseen by every possible *analysis fidei*, if it indicates the more or less successful attempt to understand the canons of man's reasonable and free self-giving to God who reveals himself. Consequently, such an analysis of faith can never be exhausted in a rational description, but must remain willing to allow itself to be regulated by the transcendence of the mystery of God. The horizon of grace, finally, cannot be obscured even by a dialogical-personalist understanding of faith that reads it as an encounter/donation on a merely horizontal level, between human persons, thus underestimating the grace of conversion that such an encounter necessarily implies when it is to God that one goes to meet and give oneself to Him.

#### 3.3 Faith according to the Magisterium of the Church
As is the case for many other subjects covered by the ecclesial magisterium, the teachings on faith also require an examination of the historical-linguistic context contemporary to their formulation, as well as an examination of the positions of the interlocutors to whom these doctrinal clarifications were directed. This is particularly true in the case of faith, both for the different facets that its understanding has shown over the centuries, and for its intertwining with the development of the doctrine of grace and justification.

##### 3.3.1 Catholic understanding of the faith according to the Council of Trent
Prior to the Council of Trent (1545-1563) we find no organic interventions by the Magisterium of the Church that would seek to clarify the nature of the faith. Teachings on this subject can generally only be found indirectly and within broader discussions on the relationship between nature and grace, as for example in Synod II of Orange (529) and probably already in the pseudo-Celestine *Indiculus* (ca. 431). The Council of Trent takes up, and in part rediscovers, precisely the Augustinian doctrine collected in the 25 *canons of* the Synod II of Orange, convened by Bishop Caesar of Arles and then confirmed by Boniface II (530-532), concerning the relationship between nature and grace, and therefore in part also faith. In this Synod it is stated that both the inclination to believe and the *initium fidei, as* well as the subsequent growth of faith, are all a gift of grace and the fruit of the inspiration of the Holy Spirit; human nature alone, that is, without the support of grace, cannot cause faith (cf. DH 375-377, 398-399).

The Catholic understanding of the faith of the Council of Trent is essentially exposed in the Sixth Session (1547), dedicated to the doctrine on justification, drafted with the aim of clarifying, a few years after the spread of the Reformation, the distance between the position of Lutheran and Catholic theology. The main subject of the Tridentine document is therefore, in relation to the Protestant crisis, the justification, in particular its cause, not faith; we are therefore moving in a context that is that of the life of grace, indeed of the relationship between grace and nature, not that of man's response to Revelation. For this reason, faith can take on not always unequivocal meanings in the decree. However, there are still a number of points that remain well defined.

First of all, faith is received through listening (*ex auditu*); with it and with the help of divine grace, people "turn freely to God, believing true what has been divinely revealed and promised by Him" (DH 1526). *This faith* must therefore be distinguished from simple *trust* in divine forgiveness, as a vague religious sentiment: the faith that disposes one to justification is not merely trust in divine mercy (cf. DH 1533, 1562). Faith, in essence, is knowledge generated by listening and not feeling. But the certainty in listening to God is not matched by a similar certainty in listening to one's own conscience: here faith, understood subjectively, is not a sufficient criterion of objective knowledge about one's state of grace or eternal salvation (cf. DH 1563-1566).

Secondly, of faith, the Council sets out some aspects of its relationship with hope and charity: the three theological virtues are at the same time a single gift received in justification, which sanctions one's insertion in Christ (cf. DH 1530); at the same time, faith enjoys a special status, that of not being lost through the loss of divine grace (which leads instead to the loss of hope and charity), or of subsisting even without charity, unless grace is lost through an explicit sin against faith (cf. DH 1544, 1578).

Perhaps a contemporary sensibility might be surprised by such a doctrine, since one is inclined today to consider the faith as a global act of the whole man and would judge as insubstantial, or even hypocritical, a faith not supported by charity and works. As a partial clarification of the Tridentine position, it should be noted that today's understanding of the "globality" or "anthropological fullness" of the act of faith says nothing other than the desire to thematize, precisely, a *faith formed by charity,* the only one we can easily recognize as such, and that ecclesial tradition has never had difficulty in indicating the true situation of the believer's faith. The evaluation of "degrees of faith" is always insufficient and not infrequently reductive for man. In any case, it is only the judgment of God that can recognize in the sinner the presence of a faith which, manifesting itself in the desire for forgiveness and reconciliation, can be mysteriously united with the charity *of His grace,* since it is a desire moved by God himself. A further clarification derives from the fact that nothing prevents, in agreement with the Council's doctrine on mortal sin (cf. DH 1578), that any true mortal sin against any virtue can legitimately be considered, because of its intrinsic gravity, also as a sin against faith, thus making it easier to understand the loss, in the state of sin, of a *formed fides*.

On the other hand, that faith must be essentially united with charity and informed by it, is also evident from a third point reiterated by the Council, namely its insistence that only a faith that works through charity, capable of manifesting itself through works, should be considered a living element of justification (cf. DH 1531, 1535, 1538, 1548). The expression "to be justified by faith", Trent observes, must also be understood in the sense that faith is the beginning of salvation, not that it alone causes salvation (cf. DH 1532).

Almost five centuries later, the ecclesial community has greeted with satisfaction the drafting of a document, *Church and Justification* (1993), signed jointly by Catholics and Lutherans, in which, in an effort to understand the value and significance of the Church's mediating role, there is substantial agreement on the way of understanding the relationship between faith and works, thanks to a more pertinent exegesis of Pauline doctrine. This exegesis brings Lutheran and Catholic theology closer today, thanks to a better recognition of the substantial difference between the works of the law, blamed by Paul and assimilated by the first Lutheran critics to works in general, and industrious charity, whose role was always emphasized by the Catholic position and now better presented by the latter as a gift of grace.[25](about:blank)

##### 3.3.2 The Chapter *De fide* of the Constitution *Dei Filius of the* First Vatican Council
In the third chapter of the constitution *Dei Filius*, the First Vatican Council (1870) deals both with the nature and object of the faith (cf. DH 3008, 3010, 3011), and with the reasons for its credibility, in particular that linked to the holiness of the Church (cf. DH 3009, 3012-3014). Referring elsewhere to the discussion of credibility (see chapter V of this volume), here we recall what the Council Fathers specifically stated about faith:

*[Embedded Image]*
 *Dei Filius*, chapter 3, DH 3008.

As a whole, the text presents faith as a ***free* act**, ***moved by divine grace****,* but also ***dutiful***, linking the latter aspect, even before the outcome of a judgment of credibility, to the ***creaturely* condition of man,** that is, to the **total dependence of reason created by God, the reason for the obedient submission of intellect and will to divine Revelation.** Faith is implicitly linked to a *logos* and an *ethos*: believing involves reason and freedom, and it is an act that can be defended and sustained with appropriate demands for universality, ultimately anchored in the universality of revelation (chap. 2) and the general accessibility of a natural recognition of God (cf. chap. 1). In particular, we meet here a succinct and well researched definition of faith, qualified as a

supernatural virtue, for which under divine inspiration and with the help of grace, we believe the things revealed by God to be true, not because of the intrinsic truth of things perceived by the natural light of reason, but because of the authority of God himself who reveals them, who can neither deceive nor deceive (DH 3008).

Because of the clarity with which the "formal reason" of the faith is made explicit, also reaffirmed in a subsequent canon (cf. DH 3032), this formulation will be destined to have wide influence in all later theology and also in the catechesis of the Church, being reproposed in particular by neo-scholasticism, not without its own hermeneutics.

The text of the chapter *De fide* dwells again on the *nature* of faith, **denying that it is "a blind movement of the spirit" and affirming that it must always be considered "in itself a gift of God";** man, instead of cooperating in the grace with which he receives this gift, "could also freely resist it" (cf. DH 3010). With regard to the *object of* faith, the Council opts for a technical formulation, which will survive in different contexts to the present day: "**all that is contained in the word of God, written or handed down (*quae in verbo Dei scripto vel tradito continentur*), and which the Church proposes to believe as divinely revealed, both by a solemn judgment and in her ordinary and universal Magisterium, must be believed" (DH 3011).**

In the subsequent canons corresponding to this chapter of the Constitution it is stated that divine faith is **distinct from the natural knowledge of God** (cf. DH 3032) and **the free nature of faith is reaffirmed**, both by condemning the position of those who claim that the assent of faith is necessarily produced by the arguments of human reason (cf. DH 3035) and by arguing that it is not permissible to suspend the assent of faith pending a scientific demonstration of its credibility and truth (cf. DH 3036). In continuity with the doctrine on grace of the previous Magisterium, those who consider the grace of God to be necessary only for the living *faith* which works through charity are also condemned (cf. DH 3035).

The intention of the First Vatican Council to expose the notion of faith above all in relation to the correlative understanding of the notion of Revelation, as set forth by *Dei Filius* in the previous chapter (ch. 2), without even forgetting the place of the human being before God the Creator (ch. 1), who is in definition the very Subject of Revelation. Faith, as set out here, has as its object substantially the *beliefs,* in keeping with what the Council had just affirmed about Revelation, of which it highlights above all the written and transmitted contents, as well as the formulations with which the *Magisterium* proposes them throughout history to the faithful, thus emphasizing that Revelation offers us specific knowledge, recognized as supernatural *quoad modum*.

One can legitimately assume that the declarations of *Dei Filius* were not intended to offer a complete elaboration of the nature of faith: the perspective adopted by the Council Fathers wanted to respond in the first place to the errors of the time, rationalism and fideism, which would follow a few years after modernism. With regard to rationalism, which placed human reason (in continuity with the Enlightenment vision) as the only absolute, autonomous and independent source of knowledge, the Council wishes to reaffirm the precise cognitive character of the faith, after having recalled the creatural horizon - and therefore not lately independent - in which reason must also be understood; fideism will be answered instead by the doctrine on the reasonableness (credibility) of faith.

Historical criticism is now unanimous in stating that the position of Vatican I did not intend to constitute itself against modernity, but actually sought a dialogue with it,[26](about:blank) as well summarized by the incisive judgment of Heinrich Fries, according to whom "Vatican Council I is better than its reputation".[27](about:blank) This is demonstrated by the concern of the Fathers to develop a positive notion of reason, clarifying in a special chapter of the same Constitution the relationship between reason and faith (chapter 4). In a more general sense, this is also made clear by the tenor of the discussion in the classroom, which is rather respectful of the cognitive progress of the sciences and contrary to a direct clash with the demands of modernity, manifesting in this a courageous change of perspective compared to what the sharp (though historically necessary) statements of the *Syllabus errorum* (1864) prepared in Rome could suggest only a few years earlier.[28](about:blank) It must be ascribed to the majority currents of the subsequent neo-scholasticism the responsibility of having offered this assembly to reconcile an often anti-modern hermeneutics, sometimes going so far as to propose a specific rationalism in opposition to that inherited from the philosophy of the Enlightenment, emphasizing the objective-impersonal character of faith and linking it almost exclusively to the concept of authority.

This reductive hermeneutics, although dictated by objective concerns then present, is echoed in some passages of the *anti-modernist Oath* (1910), which will propose its own summary reading of the doctrine of Vatican I on the faith: "Surely I hold firm and sincerely confess that faith is not a blind religious sentiment that erupts from the darkness of the subconscious through the impulse of the heart and the inclination of the morally formed will, but a true assent of the intellect to a truth received from outside through listening for which, on the basis of the authority of God's supremely true authority, we believe that all things which have been said, attested and revealed by the personal God, our Creator and Lord, are true" (DH 3543).

##### 3.3.3 The clarification of the Church's Magisterium with regard to Fideism and Modernism
In addition to what was promulgated in Vatican I, two specific contingencies led the Catholic Magisterium, between the second half of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century, to intervene on the themes of faith and revelation: these are the clarifications made necessary with regard to fideism and traditionalism, and the Roman documents with regard to modernism, in particular the encyclical *Pascendi*. Although the purpose of these interventions is not to propose an organic teaching on the nature of faith, some elements of interest in our theme can be seen.

Fideism was born historically in the 19th century as a reaction, especially in French circles, to Enlightenment rationalism, whose idea of reason it rejected, and as an attempt to respond to forms of theological rationalism. The tendency to consider faith as the only access to knowledge of God and to the intelligence of the moral life constitutes a possible attitude in every religion: this happens when a revealed, or in any case supernatural, knowledge is exalted at the expense of reason, separating it from what the latter could know or understand on its own.[29](about:blank) In relation to the Christian faith, fideism presents itself as a difficult and delicate subject, not subject to easy simplification. It not only involves central topics such as natural knowledge of God's existence, recognition of natural moral law or the meaning of *praeambula fidei,* but also implies a specific understanding of the relationship between faith and reason, between grace and nature, whose uncertainties and complexity it actually embodies.

For a correct understanding of faith, the exact knowledge of what *fideism* means is necessary, both because the affirmation of an objective superiority of Revelation and faith over the forces of reason alone is certainly legitimate, and because the Christian faith is called to accept a revelation that becomes scandal and paradox, thus presenting itself with a logic that surpasses that of reason. "It is for these reasons," says P. Poupard - that some believers have suffered the fascination of fideism, others have been mistakenly considered as fideists, or finally fideism itself has been confused as an intrinsic element of faith. In reality these are misunderstandings that can only be overcome by making it clear that faith, going beyond reason, neither contradicts it nor intends to ignore it".[30](about:blank)

Although its roots go back to the theological climate of France just after the Revolution, the fideist current usually began with Philippe Olympe Gerbet (1798-1864), author of *Des doctrines philosophiques dans leurs rapports avec les fondements de la théologie* (1826), to be then mainly associated with the names of Louis Bautain (1796-1867), author of *La philosophie du christianisme (*1835) and Augustin Bonnetty (1798-1879), editor of the journal *Les Annales de la philosophie chrétienne* (1830-1879).

Bautain was asked to subscribe, first by the local authorities (1835-1840) and then by the Roman Curia (1844), to some formulations concerning the relationship between faith and reason (cf. DH 2751-2756, 2765-2769). From the thesis undersigned emerges the vision of a faith called to listen to the conclusions of reason and to benefit from them in order to fulfill its option: the natural knowledge of the existence of God, the reasons for credibility derived from prophecies and miracles, the historical evidence of Jesus' resurrection will be explicitly mentioned. If some of the statements to which one was asked to adhere might suggest that a foundation of faith *on reason was* being affirmed there - and the doctrinal climate of the moment might in some cases have favored this interpretation - the general idea that should be drawn from it today is only to record the Magisterium's concern that faith be understood in itself as something "founded". A foundation which certainly cannot be placed reductively on empirical rationality - as the historical teaching of the Church as a whole would well show - but which must nevertheless be rooted in a complete anthropology, respectful of the various components of the human, including rationality. The Catholic Magisterium essentially states that reason precedes faith and faith must lead us (cf. DH 2755, 2813).

Traditionalism, generally associated with the name of Augustin Bonnetty - although he was rather the exponent of "moderate traditionalism" - supported the thesis that a divine revelation was *absolutely* necessary (Thomas Aquinas had spoken of *relative moral* necessity) in order to know not only supernatural truths, but also those of a philosophical, metaphysical, moral or religious order. On the political and social level it followed that reason would not be able to establish an adequate social and governance system, something made possible only by a moral order revealed by God to men. Among the theses that the French thinker was asked to subscribe to (cf. DH 2811-2814) we read that, although faith is superior to reason, there can be no disagreement between them, because both derive from one and the same source of Truth. The rational, non-rationalist nature of theology, of which fideists and traditionalists denounced a supposed drift towards rationalism, is also reiterated.

These trends are also present today, judging by the fact that *Fides et ratio* (1998), a century and a half after the speeches mentioned earlier, is concerned to mention their incompatibility with the Catholic faith, pointing out some forms in which fideism, or a certain fideist *temptation,* continue to be operative even today in theology.[31](about:blank) Today, there are fideist *guidelines,* especially on the relationship with scientific reason. They sometimes express themselves through the idea that metaphysical reason, useful to science, is instead harmful to faith, or that relativism, necessary to strip all dogmatism, even atheistic and anti-religious dogmatism, must also be embraced by the Christian in the name of understanding the other. Frequently, in these orientations, the use of fallible epistemology and the so-called "weak thinking" considered suitable instruments to combat the anti-spiritualist and atheistic demands of modern scientific reason.

Thus set, the debate brings with it consequences. It ends by considering that metaphysics coincides with the understanding that some exponents of neo-scholasticism had of it (i.e. the metaphysics that these fideist orientations intend to overcome), thus fostering a *reductive* understanding of the "reason" of which the Magisterium speaks, when it defends the possibility of human thought to access the true and the good. To leave unresolved the question of relations with contemporary fideist orientations means to assume as a consequence the renunciation of any *praeambulum fidei* available to reason, denying the role that such preambles could have for the intelligibility of Revelation itself.

With regard to the modernist conception of faith, the encyclical *Pascendi* (8.9.1907) shows itself committed to affirm a basic thesis: the "foundation" of faith does not lie in subjective experience, nor in religious experience. The religious sentiment, which the Modernists put at the basis of their anthropology, cannot be identified with faith (cf. DH 3478). The encyclical rejects the idea of a dualism between faith and scientific reason, blaming the conception that "faith deals only with what science professes to be unknowable to itself" or, also, that "science is all in the reality of phenomena, where faith does not enter at all; on the contrary, it deals with the divine reality which is completely unknown to science" (DH 3485). Against these beliefs, the Encyclical reaffirms that the contents of faith are a source of knowledge that also intersects the sphere of reason, setting an example for the earthly life of Jesus Christ, while at the same time making it clear that this does not subordinate faith to science (cf. DH 3486).

A few months earlier, the decree *Lamentabili* (3.7.1907) had condemned some modernist propositions concerning the faith, including the statement that the dogmatic teachings of the Church are aimed at providing norms of practical action by not representing any norm of belief (cf. DH 3426), and that "the assent of faith is ultimately based on the conjecture of a set of *probabilities* (*in congerie probabilitatum*)". (DH 3425). This last observation could raise questions about the relationship between such probabilistic knowledge and "illative sense" as discussed by J.H. Newman,[32 with](about:blank) which the Oxford theologian indicated the ability to grasp the reasonableness of faith by means of a "series of convergent clues" each of which could not, on its own, bear the burden of proof. In reality, the decree seems here to condemn the role of *ultimate* foundation attributed to this probabilistic knowledge in the option of faith, even if it should not be excluded that this affirmation of the decree, like others, suffers from the still insufficient theological maturation of important notions which, precisely in the delicacy of the moment, were beginning to make their way, not least that of religious experience.

##### 3.3.4 The nature of faith according to the Constitution *Dei Verbum* and other documents of the Second Vatican Council
The initial scheme *De Fontibus Revelationis* presented to the Council Fathers in November 1962 did not propose to speak about the nature of Revelation, nor did it propose to speak about faith. The four successive editorial offices came to formulate, in No. 5 of the Constitution *Dei Verbum*, the most extensive paragraph that the Second Vatican Council had dedicated to the nature of faith:

To God who reveals is due the obedience of faith (cf. *Rom* 16:26; *Rom* 1:5; *2 Cor* 10:5-6), by which man surrenders everything to God freely, lending "the full obedience of intellect and will to God who reveals" [DH 3008] and voluntarily absenting himself from the revelation made by him. In order to lend this faith, it is necessary the grace of God who prevents and helps, and the inner help of the Holy Spirit, who moves the heart and turns it to God, opens the eyes of the mind, and gives "all gentleness in allowing and believing the truth" [DH 3010; 377]. In order that the intelligence of revelation may become ever more profound, the Holy Spirit himself continually perfects faith through his gifts.

In accordance with the personalist perspective adopted for Revelation (cf. DV, 2), faith is also **presented as a personal relationship in which the human being gives himself entirely to God**, according to a **global** and accomplished act, which involves intelligence, will and freedom, and which can qualify, biblically, *ob-auditus fidei*. In general, throughout the history of the writing of this text33 one can see a **progressive introduction of personalist concepts partially replacing objective and impersonal terms -** such as the variation of the *incipit*: *Deo revelanti praestanda* instead of *Revelationis divinae praestanda* present in the first scheme.34

Once the Revelation is known, the act of faith is judged "**dutiful**", not far from what *Dei Filius* already established. It is an act that admits **two sides, the giving of man to God and the gift of God to man**, the first characterized by obedient assent, the second by the condescension of self-revelation. The **primacy assumed by grace** in this dynamic and vital encounter is also declined in a personalistic way, through a double, significant reference to the role of the person of the Holy Spirit. It is He who moves intellect (eyes of the mind) and will (heart), allowing, thanks to His gifts, that progressive deepening of Revelation which, in turn, strengthens in a virtuous circle the option of a faith strengthened by grace.

In addition to talking about the dynamics of faith, the *interni auxilii of* the Holy Spirit, the editorial staff of ***Dei Verbum* do not place side by side the role of external aid, which here would have had a meaning similar to that of the classic reasons for credibility, nor is the existence of "preambles" for the faith mentioned**. Questioned on this point by some Council Fathers, the theological commission in charge of drafting the text will answer that these are not the aims of the text, pointing out that the preambles of the faith had already been dealt with by Vatican I.35

It should be noted, however, that although unlike Vatican I *Dei Verbum* 5 **it does not propose a *formal definition of faith,*** nevertheless this Council page explains, in an undeniably clear way and with a valuable vision of synthesis, *what faith is*.

The documents of Vatican II use the term faith, as expected, within a rather broad semantic field, where personal-existential and cognitive-objective aspects coexist. We list some of them, with a reference to the documents in which they appear.

**Faith is a repository from which the Church draws everything she proposes to the faithful to be believed** (cf. DV, 10); from the Apostles we have received the mandate to fight for the faith and to increase it by a holy life (DV, 8); tradition and canonical writings are the supreme rule of faith (cf. DV, 21); members of the People of God are called to profess the faith and to spread it (cf. LG, 11); thanks to the preaching of the word of salvation, faith is enkindled in the hearts of non-believers and is nourished in the hearts of believers (cf. PO, 4); the people of God who live a holy life possess a supernatural sense of faith, a *sensus fidei* (cf. LG 12; DV, 8) which allows them to orient themselves infallibly towards what must be believed; the Roman Pontiff and all the bishops are asked to maintain unity in faith (cf. LG, 18 and 23), and they, doctors and judges of faith and morals, preach to the people of God the faith to be believed (cf. LG, 25); the lay faithful are in a special way heralds of faith in the midst of the world and transmitters of faith in the family (cf. LG, 35); saints and martyrs have given witness to faith and charity (cf. LG, 50); it is in the light of faith that the great questions of man are illuminated (cf. GS, 11); the profane realities and the realities of faith originate from the same God (cf. GS, 36); the detachment between the faith that is professed and daily life must be counted among the most serious errors of our time (cf. GS, 43); etc.

Finally, in a large number of occurrences, representing the context of evangelization one of the most widespread in the Council documents, the term "faith" is simply used as a synonym for Gospel, Christian doctrine, preaching.

Elements of certain interest are contained in the declaration *Dignitatis humanae* where, in the context of religious freedom, the link between faith and freedom develops, because "religious freedom in society is in full correspondence with the freedom of the act of Christian faith" (n. 9). The obligation to spread the faith, therefore, must respect the freedom of the interlocutor, denied which faith could not even exist: "no one can be forced to embrace the faith against his will; in fact faith is by its very nature a voluntary act, since human beings [...] cannot adhere to God who reveals himself to them if the Father does not draw them out and if they do not lend God a reasonable and free reverence of faith" (n. 10; cf. n. 4).

Although they do not present further specific teachings on the nature of faith, the pages of *Gaudium et Spes* dedicated to the relationship between faith and culture (cf. GS, 57-59) should be mentioned in chapter 2 of Part II of the document, which is entirely entitled to the human and Christian sense of the promotion of culture and its relationship with the Church.

In more recent times, in the Magisterium following Vatican II, it should be remembered the direction taken in pointing out precise differences between theological faith and religious belief. Belief is recognized as a basic anthropological attitude, positive for the attainment of truth in knowledge; but Christian faith, theological faith in a Revelation accomplished in Christ, "goes beyond the stage of simple belief".36 However, philosophy must be credited with having emancipated and purified ancient traditions, making it possible to "arrive at a rational foundation for belief in the divinity".37 A more radical distinction between theological faith and belief, the latter proper to non-Christian religions, is strongly emphasized, probably because of the more disciplinary approach of the document, by the Instruction of the CDF, *Dominus Iesus* (2000): "If faith is the acceptance in the grace of revealed truth, which allows one to enter into the mystery, favoring its coherent intelligence, belief in other religions is that set of experience and thought, which constitute the human treasures of wisdom and religiosity, that man in his search for truth has conceived and put into practice in his reference to the Divine and the Absolute" (n. 7).

#### 3.4 Theological insight into faith
##### 3.4.1 The Christological value of faith
The specific role that the Incarnate Word plays as mediator and fullness of Revelation is a source of important theological determinations also with regard to welcoming Revelation and understanding it, and thus with regard to faith, as man's response to God who reveals himself. T**he NT has already shown us how the faith required by the preaching of Jesus and then the Apostles, is primarily faith *in Christ*, with the acts that must accompany it.** The personal-salvific character of Revelation, moreover, has already instructed us that the reception of Revelation is the reception of a person - the person of Jesus Christ -, and that the credibility of Revelation is ultimately based on the credibility of a person - that of Christ. All this means that not only does fundamental theology, understood as a whole, possess a clear *Christological concentration,* but also that the treatise on faith, in its specificity, admits a precise Christocentric perspective.

##### Jesus Christ in the place of the material, formal and final object of the theological faith
In general terms, according to the well-known Augustinian expression later taken up by Thomas Aquinas, the theological character of faith can be extended in the threefold consideration that, of faith, God is the material object, the formal object and the final cause, that is, faith is a ***Credere Deum, credere Deo, credere in Deum.***38 In other words, God is the object, the guarantor, and the end of faith. **God is the first and most radical truth believed (material object), but also the authority that gives reason for that belief (motive or formal object); moreover, he is also the end of it, since faith is presented as a personal gift to God and to God (final cause)**. These are three specific aspects of a single act, of which the second and the third - *credere Deo, credere in Deum* - express, respectively, the gnoseological and unitive aspect of the theological faith.

The NT, the IV Gospel in particular, presents a singular convergence between theological faith and faith in Christ, to the point that it can reproduce the previous threefold articulation of faith also in explicit reference to Jesus Christ. He, in fact, is not only proposed as the **object of faith** (he who in Himself reveals what one must believe), but also as the **formal motive** and **final cause** of faith itself (he through whom, and in whom, one believes). The Christian faith is therefore a ***credere Christum, credere Christo, credere in Christum****,* and the object-end-witness articulation presents itself, in Christ, fulfilled. It must not, however, erroneously think of a sort of "substitution" of the subject, Christ *in place of* God, because it is more accessible as a term of the believing act. One should speak, rather, of **a "concretion".** **The act of universal significance by which the believer entrusts himself to God and accepts what He has revealed *is made concrete* in an act of faith which has the person of Jesus as its object.** As Mouroux recalled, "our faith is Christological; and because Christological is Trinitarian: the two statements are not opposed, they are included. In Christ, and only in Christ, we come to the Trinity".39

 J. Mouroux: Faith, reality *in Christ*

The Greek text of the NT manifests the convergence between theological faith and Christian faith through the widespread use, in reference to the verb "believe" or the term "faith", of both expressions ἐν Χριστὗͺ and εἰς Χριστὸν (or with the corresponding pronouns). The first, with a meaning close to that which would assume the Latin *credere Christo* or *credere Christum*; the second, with a meaning similar to the Latin *in Christum*. In the first case Christ Jesus is presented as the material and formal place of our faith (cf. *Gal* 3:26; *Gal* 5:6; *Col 1:*4; *Eph* 1:15); in the second, as the final motive for the faith (cf. *Jn* 14:1; *Col* 2:5; *Gal* 2:16; *FIl 1:*29). However, it should be pointed out that, unlike Latin where *in* with the accusative it translates a clear final causality, in Greek the same syntactic form does not necessarily indicate finalism and personal-unitive tension, while maintaining a dynamic meaning. It should be remembered, in fact, that the articles of the Symbols of Faith, for example, use the preposition εἰς with the accusative also to express the belief in non-personal subjects, such as the forgiveness of sins, the resurrection of the flesh, etc..

In John's gospel the objective, final and formal specifications of the faith of Christ, as well as a good part of all the faith to which this gospel exhorts, converge towards *faith in the divine sonship of Jesus*. To believe in Christ or to believe in Christ means, for John, to believe *that Christ is the Son of God* (which we could relate to a *fides quae creditur*), while to believe in Him means to believe in Him *as the Son of God* (and therefore a *fides qua creditur*). "If I do not do the works of my Father, do not believe me; but if I do them, even if you do not want to believe me, believe the works [...]. Many went to him and said, "John did not make any sign, but everything John said about him was true. And in that place many believed in him (ἐπίστευσαν εἰς αὐτὸν)" (*John* 10:37-38.41-42. Cf. *Jn* 3,36; 4,21; 4,39; 5,38.46; 6,29-30.40; 8,30; 11,25-26; 14,1; 17,20).

In essence, the Christological dimension of faith is manifested in the NT in the fact that the believer, through the act of faith required of him, adheres to the person of Christ, recognizes him as the Son sent by the Father and accepts his words as true words of God.40 However, to present the person of Jesus as the authoritative foundation of faith would not yet be an original and characterizing element, **since there are truths that can be the object of faith and which can be believed because of their role as prophets** or mediators. **The novelty and originality lies instead in the fact that the person of Jesus is seen as the *end and purpose of faith*, of that faith which is due only to God**: "Let not your heart be troubled. Have faith in God and have faith in me too (εἰς τὸν Θεὸν καὶ εἰς ἐμε πιστεύετε)". (*John* 14.1).

The Christological dimension of the New Testament faith still has an important aspect: it mysteriously contains within itself the whole core of ***salvation***. Believing in Jesus Christ *is* man's salvation. This means, in the final analysis, that through faith in God-the-Son one can gain access to the saving fatherhood of God-the-Father, that is, one recognizes that there is a fatherhood which is a source not only of justice, but also of mercy and forgiveness. Jesus' insistence on having faith in him *as sent by the Father* wants to give birth in his disciples the awareness that, through him, they have access to the Father and that from the hands of this Father no one can ever abduct them (cf. *Jn* 10:28-30). **This faith in the divine sonship of Jesus is directed towards establishing in his disciples a filial relationship with God that will introduce them into the life of the Trinity:** "For all of you are children of God through faith in Christ Jesus" (*Gal* 3:26).

What in the soteriological order would present itself as a relationship between faith and filial grace, in the experience of Christ's disciples is initially presented as an invitation to recognize that in God there is a true paternity and this paternity is in its essence good, provident, salvific. M.J. Scheeben affirmed: "He manifested Himself to us not only as God, but also as Father, so that we might know how and why He can and wants to be our Father: and if He demands that we faithfully confess that He is the Father of His Only Begotten Son, it is because He wants to be recognized and confessed as our Father as well.41

##### Adherence to the mystery of Christ, the form of human response to divine revelation
Christ Jesus is not only the mediator of man's response to God, he is the one who makes it possible by revealing the content of what is to be believed and proposing himself as the end of a personal love towards which to turn: **he is also the *form of* man's response**, the Christian form of human faith.

In Christ, religion is no longer a "seeking God as if by temptation", but a *response of faith* to God who reveals himself: a response in which man speaks to God as to his Creator and Father; a response made possible by that unique Man who is at the same time the consubstantial Word to the Father, in which God speaks to every man and every man is made capable of responding to God. Even more, in this Man, the whole of creation responds to God.42

But what does it mean, in concrete terms, that Christ represents the form of the response of faith? One could begin by recalling that **the relationship between Christ and the Christian, a relationship of grace, implies a true *incorporation* in Him**, an ***in-existence*** that we find well witnessed by the constant call of all Pauline theology to think and act *in Christ Jesus.*43 In the second place - and this is the point we intend to develop here - to adhere to God in faith means **to allow oneself to be in-*formed*, in one's existential options, by categories which, precisely in order to be Christian, are those *of the person of Jesus Christ*.**

When man accesses an interpretative or moral sphere, he usually does so through general categories, and then embodies them in the historical-concrete conditions of his individual existence: the personal *determination of* these categories normally takes place, in a more or less successful way, in the person of the subject. In Christianity this is not the case: instead of **the general norm lies the historical person of Jesus and the subject must conform to a person, not an idea**. Faith then presents itself in the subject not only as an encounter with the truth of God revealed in Christ (according to a criterion already recognized as personalist), but as a true and proper **readiness to receive a new "personal form": the believer recognizes in Christ the form of his own truth**: "faith is a complex attitude proper to man who says to Jesus Christ: You are my truth; that is, You measure the truth of my way of being man".44

The fact that Christ Jesus, as vigorously stressed by Guardini, constitutes the essence of Christianity is not something that concerns a generic gnoseological category, an ideal that allows us to better understand what Christianity is, in the abstract. In reality, one is asked to recognize, precisely through the option/donation of faith, that **in Him there are reasons not for human existence generally understood, but those of my personal existence**. But on closer inspection this constitutes - pointed out Giovanni Moioli - a sort of paradox because of the apparent heteronomy that this option of faith involves, that of seeking one's own centre and one's own truth outside oneself, in Another.45 Yet, entrusting the ultimate rule of one's own truth and one's own work to a concrete person is not reductive for the believer/lover, but rather fontal. Only in this entrustment are the conditions for a relationship of love, precisely personal, necessary for the option of faith to assume its full meaning, that of a free act of love from person to person. Only in this way can one understand why Christianity is the religion of the commitment to one's own freedom and one's own life to the point of losing it, and then finding it again.46

 R. Guardini: Christ as the form of the response of the Christian's faith

In the "core" of the form of this answer we can recognize, essentially, a *filial form*. This is the form of obedience, of prayer, of freedom, with which we respond *in Christ* to God who reveals Himself.

Remembering that the economy of Christian Revelation is marked by the logic of a *universal concretum*, when we affirm that Christ Jesus is the form of our faith, it means that we admit the possibility of recognizing this same logic, that of a *personal universal concretum*,47 also in the determination and understanding of the *form of* the human response to God that is revealed.

A critique of the economy of the concrete universal, in relation to man's response to God who reveals himself in Christ, has been moved by certain currents of existentialist thought. The *universal* richness of the human spirit open to the Absolute, and of an Absolute infinitely transcendent with respect to our spirit, would prevent an encounter on the *concrete* terrain of limit and history, because they would allow it only in a tension never satisfied towards the infinite, towards a truth placed beyond all determination or certainty, which can exist only in a dimension of hope and constant openness never resolved.48 It is basically a re-proposal, in personal-existential terms, of what Lessing already objected in a historical-objective key.

To this criticism, however, one can reply that the phenomenology of the human spirit also admits a different reading. This, it is true, is certainly open to infinity, the protagonist of a self-transcendence that cannot be achieved, limited or concretted; but it is equally true that such openness in knowledge, love or freedom, is always oriented towards something accomplished: one passes from the infinite to the finite, from the universal to the particular. And this not as a loss, but as an "actualization" of that opening which, otherwise, would remain unexpressed or frustrated. It is not the replacement of a universal good by a particular good, but the updating of the orientation towards a universal good by means of finished acts recognised as conforming and in accordance with that good. This manifests the choice of a spouse, the response to a professional vocation, dedication to God in a precise ecclesial journey... The economy of a *universal concretum, by* which God offers his Word in history and the human person historically welcomes and responds to it in faith, adhering to Christ according to a specific *ubi* and *ibi,* is in fact fully adapted to the phenomenology of the human spirit. The concreteness and realism of this response do not distract the infinite openness of the human spirit, they do not deceive it, they do not humiliate it, but they lead it towards its only possible fulfillment. Ultimately, recognizing in Christ the form of the human response in faith shows the adherence to a concrete historical and categorical as an actualization of the transcendent dimension of freedom.

Christian life and the lives of the saints have shown the full effectiveness of this perspective when they attribute, for example, to concrete and limited acts a value of eternity (cf. *Mt* 10:42). This is certainly true in the life of grace, whose transcendent logic makes possible, *in Christ*, access to a meta-temporal dimension; but it also remains true in all the historical determinations which place man before God, as manifested also by the equally eternal effects of a refusal to God which is expressed by acts whose temporal value can only be finite and limited.

##### 3.4.2 The ecclesial value of faith
The triple articulation object-testimony-end that faith possesses when considered in its theological and christic dimensions, also admits an ecclesial application: ***credimus Ecclesiam*, because the Church is a mysterious reality, which originates from the salvific plan of the Father and is realized in history through the missions of the Son and the Holy Spirit (cf. LG, 2-4); *credimus Ecclesiae*, because we lend faith to the Church, when we accept a divine Word which she already hears, holily keeps and faithfully exposes (cf. DV, 10; LG, 25); and again *credimus in Ecclesiam,* because our adherence/incorporation to Christ through faith is adherence to his body, which is the Church, towards which we obey (cf. LG, 7). As we shall see, this triple articulation is enriched by a further specification, that of *credimus in Ecclesia*, which expresses both the communionality of the believing act and the necessary vital, hermeneutical and doctrinal context in which faith arises and is professed.**

It should be pointed out that the Symbols of the faith know both the expression "believe the Church" [“credere la Chiesa”] (cf. e.g. DH 10, 11, 26, 30, 150) and the expression "believe in the Church" [“credere nella Chiesa”], which is present above all in the Greek language editors (cf. e.g. DH 41, 42, 44, 46, 48, 150). The theological tradition has intended to read the second in the light of the first, wanting with this to emphasize that **the ultimate goal of believing adherence is always God the Trinity in Christ, and that adherence to the Church expresses the recognition of the mediation that the Church exercises**, as already the humanity of Christ. So understood Thomas Aquinas, observing that rather than "believe in the Church", the formula "I believe in the Holy Spirit who *sanctifies* the Church" should be preferred, suggesting to use, more simply, only the formula *credere sanctam Ecclesiam..*49 This was recalled more recently in the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* (1997).50

##### Modalities of the ecclesial value of faith in the life of believers
The ecclesial value of the faith lends itself to being declined according to several important dimensions. Let's see some of them.

- ***The faith is stirred up by a word spoken by the Church***. A first, fundamental ecclesial dimension of the faith is manifested in the fact that the subject directs his ***auditus fidei*** to the Church, because it is the Church that preaches the Word that she lives and preserves, even if it may be the individual apostle who proclaims it, since, precisely, it is the Church that sends it. The Church, summoned by the Word, summons in turn, stirring up the faith **through preaching** an**d receiving the response of faith from those who receive it.**
- ***The faith of the believer is professed in the Church***. Secondly, the believer hands over to the Church the response of his faith, as shown by the profession of faith required at the time of baptism and the incorporations to the Catholic Church of those from other non-Catholic denominations. Baptism constitutes the sacramental bond that visibly sanctions "believing all the same faith" and, therefore, by adhering to it, makes one adhere as members to the one Body of Christ. At the same time, the baptismal liturgy shows that the Church is *asked for faith*, in the sense that it recognizes in the Church the guarantee of her integrity and the truth of the goods connected with her: "What do you ask of the Church of God?" the minister of baptism asks the catechumenumen in the *Roman Ritual* - and his answer: "The faith"; "What does faith give you?", he continues - and the catechumen: "Eternal life.
- ***The believer proceeds with the Church***. Reference to the Church is then necessary throughout the *journey of faith*. With the Church the believer continually compares what he believes, so as not to risk running *extra viam* (cf. *Gal* 2:2). From it he receives the example of a Gospel lived which makes it possible to understand the word received and strengthens it, helping the subject to remain faithful to it. As Joseph Ratzinger observed, "no one can determine for himself, if he is a believer: to believe is a process of death and birth, an active passivity and a passive activity and therefore there is need for others: need for worship of this Church in which the liturgy of the cross and the resurrection of Jesus Christ is celebrated".51
- ***The believer believes with the Church*. The ecclesial dimension of faith still operates in that specific application of the faith which is the *intellectus fidei*, the task of theology.** Not only, evidently, in the sense that theology must be carried out by exercising faith, but in the deeper sense that the faith that identifies the epistemological status of theology as science is not the faith of the *theologian,* but the faith of the *Church* effectively active in the task of the theologian, the only one capable of providing him with elements of Catholic discernment and intellectual light.
- ***The believer feels with the Church*. Ecclesiality and unity of faith** are also manifested in that knowledge, not necessarily reflected or speculative, which proceeds from the spiritual life of the People of God: one can speak thus of a *sensus fidei*, which generates a *consensus fidelium* capable of indicating in an infallible way what belongs to the faith of the Church.
- *T****he believer is in communion with the Church*.** The ecclesial value of faith is finally revealed in presenting the **Church itself as *a single believing subject*:** "Confessio fidei traditur in Symbolo quasi ex persona totius Ecclesiae, quae per fidem unitur".52 As is well known, this unity/communion finds its verbal expression in the solemn plural "we believe" which inaugurated the professions of faith of the great Councils of antiquity: (Πιστεύομεν εἰς ένα Θεόν: DH 125, 150). "This faith," observed Irenaeus of Lyons, "the Church scattered throughout the world diligently guards, forming almost a single family: the same faith with one soul and one heart, the same preaching, teaching, tradition, as if it had only one mouth.53 Believing is always a shared act, a *Catholic* act *in itself*.54

Because of all this, similarly to what has been said about the person of Jesus Christ as a *form of* human faith, it can also be argued that the Church also represents the form of the believer's response of faith. A meaningful and not contradictory understanding of this parallel will be all the more accessible the more Christological the understanding of the Church itself becomes: this can be a form of the faith of believers because Christ Himself has imprinted *His form* in it, and He has done so in an organic and normative way, through the presence of His Spirit. The numerous Pauline passages confirm this perspective, where the two conceptions *in Ecclesia* and *in Christo* become almost interchangeable (cf. *Rom* 16:7.11; *1Cor* 1:30; *Gal 1:*22; *1Ts* 2:14, etc.).

##### Some consequences on the ecclesiological-fundamental level
One understands, then, why it would make no sense to set the ecclesial and personal dimensions of the faith in opposition to one another. The faith that the believer personally professes, he receives it from the Church, which has made possible its historical transmission and present understanding, in continuity with the founding event, through the principle of Tradition. The faith of the Church is manifested as the set of personal adhesions to Christ professed by a *congregatio fidelium*, whose subjects have undertaken a free and personal *sequela Christi*.

One also understands the increasingly urgent need today for the act of believing, while remaining personal, not to remain a private and subjective act or conviction, rejecting confrontation with the Church and listening to her word. Such a refusal would be all the more meaningless the more the subjective act and private conviction claim to involve, in intention or deed, the person of Christ or his religious message. A faith that closes in self-sufficient subjectivism has lost the form of charity because *it has lost the form of communion*. Only communion makes it possible to distinguish faith from belief or simple opinion in religious matters.

Alongside religious indifference and secularization, fundamental theology must face, in service to evangelization, the widespread practice of those who believe they can believe in the God of Jesus Christ without believing in the Church. Against this state of affairs, convincing arguments will have to be presented that highlight the intimate, painful contradiction of that practice. And this must be done without naivety, taking charge of the historical events of the Church, of the necessary purification of her memory; but also with the awareness of all the strength of the witness of her charity and of the vitality of the signs of transcendence present in her, to which she herself refers as a sign of Christ.

A similar judgement must be formulated with regard to those who, although externally they can declare a basic convergence on the contents of ecclesial belief, in their personal life and in their existential decisions do not manifest this real belonging. When faith ceases to inform the concrete choices of the believer's life, it can reasonably be considered that it ceases to be faith and becomes, precisely, belief, more or less well-founded opinion, faith without faith. The lack of commitment in the faith is equivalent to the same negation of the Christian message to which faith says to respond and adhere.55

The ecclesial manifestation of faith is therefore the *sentiment cum Ecclesia*. This expression should not be considered only in its legal-normative content, which is clearly present. Theologically it points to something deeper. *Sentire cum Ecclesia* - a condition of the true ecclesial faith - consists *specifically* in *sentire quod et in Christo Jesu*, that is, in possessing the same sentiments as Christ (cf. *FIl* 1:5).56

Just as the believer must let Jesus Christ become the form of his belief, stripping himself and his own categories so that Christ may be recognized as the norm of his own thinking and working, so the baptized, as ***faithful*, must let the *ecclesial criterion* inform every fold of his belief**, allowing the Church to discern the charisms which the individual receives or believes he has received, to judge his suitability for the service of God's people and the presence of specific vocational calls. As a member of the Church, the Christian is placed by the Church "in the form of Christ".57 The **protagonist of this "information" is, after all, always the Divine Person of the Holy Spirit, whose action as spiritual seal is now characterized by expressing in Himself the unity which He realizes in the Church as the soul of the Mystical Body and source of every charism.** Within such a perspective we can also understand the overcoming of any opposition between the hierarchical and charismatic dimension of the Church (cf. LG, 8), inasmuch as their unity and mutual interpenetration is required *by the unity of the form* which the Holy Spirit, through the Church, puts into action in every believer and which each one allows to be impressed in himself.

An ecclesial understanding of the faith that points to the Church as the existential bedrock from which faith is given and in which faith is received, and therefore the **Church as the context in which the word that arouses faith is transmitted and lived, postulates, indeed includes in itself, a *principle of Tradition***. It is in fact within this Tradition that one's belief must be continually compared and corroborated. The notion of Tradition thus descends naturally from the notions of Revelation and faith. "There is no belief without reference to the tradition in which both the subject and the content of his belief are placed. Without the dimension of tradition, belief would be submerged by subjectivism without any possibility of an objective referent. In fact, but also in law, the act of believing is placed in tradition".58 The ecclesial principle of Tradition expresses on the sacramental level of Revelation that essential anthropological condition of knowing and living which we have recognized previously operative in every manifestation of faith, even on the human level.

The Church, as the place that guards and transmits Revelation, necessarily also becomes the place where the language of faith is welcomed, understood and confessed. *Language and community recall each other and* this also applies to the dynamic of fellowship established in a community of faith. A community is also built by the language it uses and a language is fully understandable only within the community that generated it. This justifies why it is the Church's task to watch over the language of the faith, pointing out how much it favours and how much it can hinder its faithful transmission, thus making an appropriate discernment of the philosophical and linguistic systems invoked to express and communicate the faith. The delicate activity of the Magisterium is part of this dynamic, aimed at fostering the homogeneous development of dogma, while guaranteeing its immutability.
