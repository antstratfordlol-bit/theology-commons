### Chapter 1 The nature of fundamental theology: in the context of theological studies {#tanzella-rivelazione.1.chapter-1-the-nature-of}
#### 1.1 The peculiarity of theological studies and the existential situation of the believer
Man knows by listening to being: he listens to a word that comes from outside himself, from the reality of the world, and a word that resonates in his inner self, in the interiority of his own conscience. What the world tells us about itself remains open to ever deeper levels of knowledge, which lead us to question ourselves about the ultimate foundation of all things. Listening to our conscience also generates profound questions about the meaning and purpose of our life, questions that move, like the previous ones, towards the search for a Foundation, indeed towards the search for a Face. Religion, philosophy and science are the context in which this search takes place; each of them, according to its own sphere, offers the language and the categories that allow these questions to take shape. In their highest expression, religion, philosophy and scientific knowledge investigate the logos with which being meets us, thus generating the culture that characterizes what is properly human, manifesting its dignity.

To deny that being comes to us with a logos, that is, that nature and conscience have something to tell us, is always possible, but the price to pay is high: man must formulate an explicit option for nihilism, the absurd, nonsense. An option that does not satisfy him, indeed deeply upsets him, because he recognizes himself in constant listening to the real and is inclined, by nature, to question and question himself. Three seem to be, in this regard, the positions capable of characterizing the existential situation of the human being: he is *alone* and beyond the world there is no one; b) beyond the world there is something or someone in his Founding, but he is still alone because this something or someone has nothing to say to him; c) the human being is *not alone*, because in the Founding of the world and in front of him there is a personal Being who has something to say to him.1 Towards this last position men have directed themselves from the very beginning of their evolutionary journey, recognizing a transcendent dimension that presides over nature and its forces, that preserves the meaning of life and death, that justifies why they approve of good and reject evil.

The persuasion that in the Founding of the world and in front of one's own self there is a personal Being has sustained man's religious sense. He knows then that, besides listening to the real, he can also know by listening to the Word of God, that is, a Logos that reveals to him the meaning of the world and of things, the meaning of his own life. This Word, too, he reasonably questions, he strives to listen and understand, almost engaging with God in a *struggle to* which his religious and cultural traditions, his religious experience, his sacred books are witnesses. From this Word he is abducted, not renouncing the search for its intelligibility, he welcomes it in the obedience of faith, without omitting to evaluate its implications.

Theology is knowledge born of listening to this Word; a Word given to us first of all by creation, then becoming history in the religious experience of the people of Israel, and finally coming to us, "lately in these days" (*Heb* 1:2), in the Word made flesh (cf. *Jn 1:*14). Founded on Revelation, theology can thus operate a rereading of the other knowledge that man possesses about himself and the cosmos, exploring what connection exists between the Logos revealed in Christ and the logos that the religious sense, philosophy and the study of nature glimpse in the Founding of all reality and in the heart of our conscience. If human knowledge can ascend, from man and from the cosmos, towards God, according to an anthropological and cosmological itinerary, Revelation is a knowledge that descends from God towards man, but does not remain "in mid-air", because the Word is directed in a complete way into the heart of man, where it resounds, precisely, *revealing,* to be recognized by him and to be transformed by him.

Exploring the connection between the divine Logos and the human Logos is not an easy task. To propose a *re-reading* of the world and man from Revelation implies for theology the difficult task of dealing with the relationship between the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and the God of philosophers and scientists... In dealing with this relationship - which in fact accompanies all philosophical and theological thought - a good theology will try to avoid two errors: on the one hand, to believe that the human search for God (philosophy, religion and, in part, even scientific thought, in their common aim of the Absolute) has nothing to do with what God reveals about Himself; on the other hand, to believe that what God reveals is significant for us only to the extent that it meets our research and expectations. In the first case, one yields to fideism, today accustomed to multiple intellectual mutations, but all united by subscribing to a divine transcendence incapable of giving rise to a true *Lex incarnationis*. In the second case, it gives rise to an anthropological turning point that stifles the divine word by taking away its possibility of overtaking us to the point of scandalizing us.

Compared to what has been done in the course of philosophical studies, theological studies are therefore the repositories of an important *novelty*. We will never reflect sufficiently on the fact that theology is a peculiar discipline, unique, precisely because of the particularity of its "object", since it is a discourse (*lógos*) on God (*Théos)*. And God himself reveals himself to us with a Word that creates and transforms us, places us in being and elevates us to the life of grace, lets himself be circumscribed by us to the point of letting himself be crucified, but possesses us to the point of dragging us, transfigured, into a new creation. A Word that, with the expression of Saint Augustine, knows how to be "interior intimo meo et superior summo meo".2 Doing theology is not an easy task, because one cannot speak, study or teach about God without being aware of the transcendent and at the same time personal nature of this *object*, which of our study or teaching is actually the true *Subject*. But precisely this peculiarity becomes a source of precise implications that are worth making explicit:

1. The *transcendence* of the object of one's study makes one approach the revealed Word with respect, something that we should not primarily interpret and possess, because we are rather interpreted and possessed by it. Therefore, as intrinsic characteristics of every theological epistemology, dispositions of humility and constant openness to the mystery are necessary.

1. The *personal nature* of God implies that what concerns Him cannot be known to man without a personal relationship: theology, unlike other disciplines that can name the word *God* (history of religions, psychology of religion, philosophy, natural theology, literature, etc.), cannot deal with His theme except in a challenging and involving way. It is therefore necessary to exercise faith as a condition for welcoming and understanding Revelation. But God's personal nature also tells us that any knowledge of Him could only come about through encounter, dialogue, contemplation, trust, abandonment, prayer.

1. The existential dimension of the knowledge of God implies the existence of a peculiar relationship between the written and unwritten word. Listening has priority over the relationship with the text and the main criterion of truth will always be that of witness. The study and knowledge of the written word of God are enlightened by the existential encounter with the unwritten word of God, and strengthened by the testimony of those who transmit its content to us.

Again, and in summary, in the face of such a peculiar object/subject, one must conclude that to know one must live what one adheres to, and that to understand one must love. Faith and loving contemplation therefore belong to the epistemological status of theology, as a discipline; they are what characterize it compared to all the other possible ways of speaking about God. It is not surprising, then, that those who wish to study or do theology should wish, before God, to be holy. A school of theology must form saints. Theology, before it is done at the table and in the library, is done on one's knees.3

#### 1.2 Object, method and purpose of Fundamental Theology
In the documents of the Church the diction "Fundamental Theology" appears for the first time in relatively recent times, in the constitution *Deus Scientiarum Dominus* (1931) promulgated by Pius XI. The Second Vatican Council does not mention the title of this discipline. Instead, it is mentioned in a widespread and systematic way in the document of the Congregation for Catholic Education, *The Theological Formation of Future Priests* (22.2.1976), which dedicates nn. 107-113 to our discipline. It states, among other things: "All theological subjects suppose as the basis of their rational procedure fundamental theology, which has as its object of study the fact of Christian revelation and its transmission in the Church: these themes are at the center of every problem on the relationship between reason and faith. Fundamental theology will be studied as an introductory discipline to dogmatics and indeed as preparation, reflection and development of the act of faith (the "Creed" of the Symbol), in the context of the demands of reason and the relationship between faith, cultures and great religions" (nn. 107-108). The Constitution *Sapientia christiana* (29.4.1979), which reformed theological studies, presented it as an obligatory discipline, in the first year of theology following the philosophical two-year period, immediately after Sacred Scripture and before Dogmatic Theology, specifying that it should also address questions of ecumenism, non-Christian religions and atheism (cf. art. 51). More recently, *Fides et ratio (*1998) dedicates an entire paragraph to our discipline, stressing the link between philosophical and theological knowledge and the task of expounding Revelation and its credibility (cf. n. 67).

Beyond these clarifications, the contents of Fundamental Theology have always represented a specific field of theological teaching and a body of reflections on the understanding and transmission of Revelation, although this field, as we shall see, has been indicated in the course of history under different names.

The object, method and aims of Fundamental Theology characterize it according to some important specificities. It is:

- situated at the *foundation* and *frontier of* theological discourse;
- *called to expose the faith, through a theological discourse carried out in the context of other knowledge and in front of an interlocutor.*

The Fundamental lies in the *foundation* because its primary object, Revelation, is the starting point of every theological reflection. It lies in that foundation according to a different formality from what already happens in Sacred Scripture. Fundamental Theology, in fact, deals with Revelation *as a whole*: understanding, intelligibility, origin, written and oral transmission, authentic teaching, meaning for man, credibility, relationships with other knowledge, etc., etc. The way it looks at Scripture, therefore, is more general than the disciplines dealing with the origins of the written text and its exegesis. The Fundamental is also situated at the *frontier of* theological knowledge, because it must give reason for the credibility of Revelation and therefore, through it, the whole of theology relates to philosophical reason, anthropology, history and culture. It enters into relationship with almost all the human sciences and sometimes also with some natural sciences, thus avoiding that theology presents its discourse in a self-referential way, that is, protecting it from the risk of speaking only to itself or, worse, from the risk of fundamentalism. This implies the need to know how to "expose" the contents of the faith, that is, to illustrate them by answering the questions that others may ask: both from within the faith, to deepen it, and from those who do not yet believe, to ask the reasons.4

Although, as we shall see, Fundamental Theology also employs a dogmatic perspective in some cases, it differs from dogmatic theology proper, both in object and method. Hans Waldenfels has well expressed the specificity of the Fundamental by proposing the suggestive metaphor of the fundamental theologian as a scholar placed at the entrance of a house: "The scholar of fundamental theology can be compared to one who stands on the threshold of a house (*Türsituation*). He who stands on the threshold is, so to speak, inside and outside at the same time. He hears the arguments of those at the door and those in the house. But what he cares about is entering the house. On the one hand, he makes his own what people outside know and see - in the field of philosophy, the historical and social sciences - what they think of God, Jesus of Nazareth and the Church, of themselves, the world and the society in which they live. On the other hand, it is presented with knowledge that comes from within as an invitation to all those who are inside *and* outside. The salient point of Christian theology is then to present the door, of which we speak metaphorically, as the door of man's real salvation in relation to the one who, in the words of John's Gospel, said: "I am *the* door" (*Jn* 10:7.9)".5

##### 1.2.1 Object of Fundamental Theology
Revelation, the primary object of Fundamental Theology, has very precise characteristics: it is a *mystery* and at the same time an *event*. Revelation is a "mystery" because the decision to reveal oneself depends only on the freedom and gratuitous love of God, which belong to the mystery of His intimate and personal life. Therefore, both the ultimate reason for God's communication to man and the reason for his creative love and salvific condescension, as well as the source of Revelation, are a mystery. But Revelation is also an "event", because it is a matter of a fact, a series of facts, which break into human history, leaving a trace in the religious experience of peoples; it is a matter of events which have intersected, in a particular way, the history and culture of the people of Israel, events which have been and to a great extent still are before everyone's eyes. It would be enough to examine carefully the content of the Symbol of Nicea-Constantinople to see how history flows into it in the exposition of faith. In Revelation, *mystery and event are inseparable*: God gives man His mystery in the historicity and concreteness of the event; the logic, the meaning of the event are fully grasped only in the light of the mystery of God and His condescending love.

Revelation has a *recipient*, the human being called by God to build with him a history of salvation (Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses, David, the prophets, etc.), but also men who listen and will listen to the preaching of this salvific proclamation throughout history. The divine word is received by man in the context of his religious experience and conscience, it is evaluated by his philosophical and scientific reasoning; he questions himself about the significance and reasonableness of this Word. In this way, the necessary reference to the recipient extends the primary object of fundamental theology, Revelation, to include its *credibility*. The inseparability of these two elements - Revelation and its credibility - within the one object of Fundamental Theology is precisely what gives this discipline its specificity in the panorama of systematic theology. In a more profound way than is already the case in other treatises, Fundamental Theology deals not only with what we *believe*, but also with *why we believe it*. This credibility, as we shall see, is not to be understood only as a "defense" of the reasonableness and truth of the content of Revelation (Apologetica), but also as a qualification of the act of the believer who moves towards his intelle*ct, in order* to gain a greater intelligence of Revelation in the context of his own knowledge (*fides quaerens intellectum*), making it a morally sensible act.

Beyond the historical-theological discussion about the division and value to be attributed to the various "reasons" or "criteria" of credibility - miracles, prophecies, internal criteria of the subject, historical evidence, credibility of the Church, reliability of the person of Jesus Christ, etc., including their contemporary understanding as "signs of salvation" - contemporary Fundamental Theology prefers to present the credibility of Revelation not as an extrinsic or juxtaposed property to Revelation, but as an *internal* property to Revelation, something that is given to us together with it and relates to its own hermeneutics. By making an analogy with what happens in the communication/revelation of a personal being, this is equivalent to saying that the credibility of the one who reveals and reveals himself should not be sought/founded on elements external to him, for example relying on references offered by strangers, but starting from what the subject says about himself, how and why he says it. This way of presenting Revelation places fundamental theology in a position to propose the credibility of the Christian mystery (God's plan for man and the world and the truths it contains) by highlighting the unity and coherence of this plan (salvation history), showing the world the beauty and intelligibility of its form (*Gestalt*).

From the historical point of view, it should not be forgotten that the different emphasis placed on the extrinsic or intrinsic perspective of the credibility of Revelation also depended on a not always explicit difference between the "fact" of Revelation and its "content". In order to demonstrate the fact of Revelation (a divine revelation has indeed taken place and can reasonably be distinguished from other phenomena which are not such) we preferred to base ourselves on external reasons (prophecies, miracles): the intention of showing them, precisely, as objective and independent proofs, led us to consider them in an extrinsic way with respect to Revelation and what it transmitted, to the point of believing that we could understand them regardless of the content of the Word of God and its Christological foundation. With regard to the contents of Revelation, it was rightly believed that they did not admit an external demonstration, but their credibility had to be based on properties intrinsic to the Word of God (coherence, universality of the call, beauty, etc.), we would say today on the reliability and personality of the Revelator. The contemporary sensitivity to a divine Revelation that offers itself to us, inseparably, in words and works (cf. DV, n. 2), as a mystery and as an event, has led us today not to separate the fact of Revelation from its contents, thus diminishing interest in an extrinsic treatment of credibility.6

The reference to the recipient of the Revelation implies the entry into the scene of a third element: "faith". This too belongs to the object of fundamental theology because it expresses the response of the man who accepts Revelation as a mystery; a response aroused and sustained by the significance and credibility of the Word. The ways of understanding the association between faith and credibility have not been univocal throughout history. Classical Apologetics considered credibility a moment prior to faith, the act of which was seen as the result or point of arrival of a theological-rational analysis (*analytical* conception of the *actus fidei*). The theology of reformed tradition, especially with Karl Barth, saw in faith the dialectical overcoming of reason and history (*dialectical* conception of credibility). Giving oneself to God in faith and recognizing Revelation as credible are part of the same act (P. Rousselot), a credibility whose motives should be sought above all within the subject (M. Blondel), rather than outside (P. Hettinger). Contemporary Fundamental Catholic Theology seeks a synthesis capable of presenting a credibility of the contents of faith that can appeal to reason (J. Ratzinger), re-evaluate the importance of the human subject and its openings to Revelation (K. Rahner), but at the same time showing the excess of Revelation over our demands and expectations, as can be seen from its dimensions of scandal and paradox (R. Latourelle), while not neglecting the light that the credibility of Revelation acquires when it is presented to the believer with the characteristics of coherence and beauty (H.U. von Balthasar).

A fourth element in very close relationship with Revelation is the "Church". This is a dynamic relationship: on the one hand, the Church presents itself as the community of those who have accepted the Word of God in faith, that is, the summoned (gr. κλητοί, ἐκλετοί) from the Word; on the other hand, these live the Word and proclaim it, thus transmitting it into history. The Church finds herself, thanks to the mission of the Holy Spirit, on the *prolongation of Revelation*. The Church makes us grasp the existence, in Revelation, of the presence of a "Tradition", in the sense of *betraying,* handing over, transmitting. Without Revelation there is no Church, but also, although not in the same sense, without Church there is no Revelation. The Church makes the Word present in every age, allowing it to continue to question and convoke every man; but the Church also puts the Word in writing, interprets it with authority, distinguishes it from other contents that it does not recognize as conforming to the Gospel. The way of conceiving the relationship between the Church and Revelation has also undergone some changes throughout history. Today his study cannot ignore the themes of ecumenism and the relationship between Christianity and religions.

In the light of the more detailed examination of the elements that contribute to the formation of its object, Fundamental Theology will therefore involve the following themes, generating as many areas and, to a certain extent, specific treatises:

- Revelation and its supporting categories (*theology of Revelation*)
- the Church, generated and convoked by the Word, guardian and subject of the transmission of Revelation (*Fundamental Ecclesiology*)
- faith, as the response of the man who welcomes divine Revelation (*Theological-Fundamental Treatise on Faith*)
- the credibility of Revelation (*theology of credibility* and *Apologetics*).

In reality, other ways of dividing the object of Fundamental Theology are also possible, but each proposal will sooner or later converge in recognizing the Revelation-credibility axis as its specific element, then bringing into play, in the study of Revelation, the themes of the Church and faith.

The articulation present within the object of the Fundamental also allows a certain freedom of choice of the starting point for the whole treatment. It should be noted that there would be reasonable grounds to start the itinerary from any of the four elements highlighted above:

1. A discussion beginning with *Revelation* would present from the outset a biblical-dogmatic perspective; it would emphasize divine initiative, the gratuitousness of its message, its excess over any human expectation or demand.

1. Starting instead from *credibility*, one would follow an ascending path, of a more phenomenological and anthropological character, whose purpose would be to recognize Revelation as a meaningful, reasonable, attractive Word, in conformity with the aspirations of human nature.

1. To give a certain priority to *faith* would be tantamount to starting from an anthropological perspective, with the difference that, in this case, faith would now be seen as a prior condition for recognizing and welcoming Revelation; but this itinerary should take care to offer a link between man's openness to Revelation and theological faith proper.

1. A beginning of Fundamental Theology from the reality of the Church, finally, would underline that Revelation is always delivered to us in the flow of a tradition, within an ecclesial context that necessarily mediates its transmission and understanding, as well as the very formation of the written text. The canons of witness and listening would then play a central role, without which it would make no sense to approach Revelation as a written text.

Returning to the *subject* of Fundamental Theology, we must note that, in addition to the previous four themes, two more should come together. The first is "religion". It would fall within Fundamental Theology both as a *theological* (not philosophical) reflection on religion, especially in its aspects which present man as a being *naturaliter religiosus* and open to the revelation of God, and as the relationship between Christian revelation and the religions of the earth. Recently, this last thematic area is giving rise to an autonomous area of reflection, called "theology of religions", in which there are two sides, one typical of the theology of revelation (universality and uniqueness of Revelation in Christ), the other belonging to soteriology (universality and uniqueness of salvation in Christ), both to be placed, however, preferably at the end of the studies of dogmatic theology. The second theme is "theological epistemology", which in turn is divided into two parts. One, addressed to the outside world, concerns the discussion of how it is possible to understand a divine Logos, the critical evaluation of how to recognize the intelligibility of its content, and how this knowledge relates to other knowledge. The other, addressed internally, is concerned with examining what it means to "prove a thesis" in theology, what method to use, what sources and theological places to consider.

In fact, most of the treatises of "Introduction to Theology" today develop (or should develop) these aspects of theological epistemology, in an organic and propaedeutic relationship with fundamental Theology proper. Even the discussion of atheism, agnosticism and criticism of religion generally understood, as well as the important phenomenon of religious indifference, deserve a place within a theological-fundamental path, although there is no unanimous opinion, depending on the different methodological approaches, on where and how it should be discussed.

In the light of the previous thematic articulations, we are convinced that the *theological-fundamental path* corresponding to Cycle I of institutional *theological* studies should be represented, in a complete and organic way, by the three courses called "Introduction to the Christian Mystery" (or Introduction to the Mystery of Salvation), "Introduction to Theology" and "Fundamental Theology" proper, seen in adjacent continuity. Theological reflection on religion and criticism of religion (therefore a "theology *of religion*" but not a "theology of *religions*"), enter fully into the first of these courses, because it is intended as a junction between the philosophical and theological cycle, among whose aims there is also the evaluation of the specificity of Christian Revelation when considered against the background of philosophical thought. This is also the place to deal, in a preparatory way, with the significance of Christian Revelation (not yet its credibility) and the preambles of faith, that is, the significance of its salvific appeal for contemporary man. The course of Introduction to theology, as already noted, will host, always in continuity with what was developed by the Fundamental, the themes proper to a theological epistemology, especially in its *ad intra side* (theological sources and places), leaving again to the Introduction to the Christian mystery the task of dealing with the epistemological side *ad extra* (the relationship between human logos and Logos on God), because in greater connection with philosophical thought. In this way the theological-fundamental path of the Institutional Cycle finds its continuity and the various topics an adequate place for discussion.

##### 1.2.2 The method of Fundamental Theology: articulation between biblical-dogmatic moment and dialogical-apologetic moment
The current panorama of Fundamental Theology presents a certain variety of programs and settings.7 A good part of the authors from the German area continue to elaborate models that can be considered as developments of what was once the classical structure of the treatise, articulated as *religious demonstratio - christian demonstratio - catholic demonstratio,* although today we speak of *monstratio,* rather than *demonstratio,* since, as observed, it is not a question of rationally demonstrating faith through arguments provided by the outside (history, philosophy, anthropology, etc.), but of exposing, *showing* precisely, the content of Revelation and its salvific appeal. There are various proposals that have come down to us: an "apologetics of immanence" that leverages on the expectation of Revelation and privileges the motives of credibility within the subject (M. Blondel); a "transcendental anthropology" that emphasizes the *transcendental* openness of every man to Revelation, sometimes at the expense of its categorical content (K. Rahner); an exposition that privileges the dogmatic treatment of Revelation, strongly Christocentric, then used as an interpretative key to unravel the major knots of philosophical anthropology (R. Latourelle).

In Fundamental Theology two requirements coexist, which are not always easy to put together. On the one hand, there is the desire to offer a rational-anthropological foundation to man's openness/expectation towards a divine revelation, as well as a justification of the same conditions of possibility of such openness, with the corresponding desire to use arguments that can be used even by those who do not yet believe. On the other hand, there is the awareness that the offer of Revelation contains in itself its hermeneutical principle and the ultimate reason for its credibility, which cannot be reduced to the question of a search for meaning, and which therefore escapes a complete grasp of reason, since it is a Word which it intends to convert before convincing.

If we remember the double dimension of Revelation as a mystery and as an event, it is easy to recognize that this duplicity will give rise to two methodological perspectives. As a mystery, Revelation demands a *dogmatic perspective* for its study: study of Scripture, in the light of Tradition, under the guidance of the Magisterium, in the faith of the Church. The dogmatic perspective moves from faith and is directed towards the intelligence of faith. As an event, the study of Revelation must interact with other disciplines, such as history, philosophy, cultural anthropology, psychology, philosophy of religion, literary criticism, natural sciences, etc., each of which would claim its own methodology. With regard to them, Fundamental Theology is obliged to confront and clarify what those events through which God comes together consist: the origins of the cultural journey of *Homo sapiens, the* history and migrations of the people of Israel, the formation of their religious texts, the earthly history of Jesus of Nazareth, the diffusion of the proclamation of the Risen One, etc., etc. To do so, it must employ a *dialogical-apologetic perspective* and is therefore called to develop a *dimension of apology when it comes to* exposing faith in the context of critical knowledge.

The two methodological perspectives, biblical-dogmatic and apologetic-dialogical, should not be seen as an alternative, nor can they separate the object or fragment the method of Fundamental Theology. Instead, they must manifest a certain "circularity". Both a "fundamental" theology and an "apologetic" or "in context" theology speak of the same faith and the same content, one with a discourse addressed *intra*, the other with a discourse addressed mainly (but not exclusively) *extra*. However, there are not two different discourses, one addressed to believers and one to non-believers, but only two different *procedures.* Moreover, the apologetic-dialogical procedure is never carried out completely without faith, because we are always dealing with a theological discipline. It is a matter of understanding the reasons of faith, strengthening them in the believing conscience and knowing how to explain them to those who do not yet believe, moving from faith towards the universality of reason.

The two methodological perspectives just seen can then correspond, in fact, to *two different itineraries*, from God to man and from man to God. They too should not be seen as alternatives, but in a complementary way and carried out according to a progressive circularity, thus characterizing what some authors have called the "double movement" of Fundamental Theology.8 The major theological-fundamental proposals of the twentieth century have been, after all, as many researches of articulation between these two itineraries, reaching in some cases precise theological-pastoral programs. Thus, the theological-fundamental proposals of authors such as Karl Rahner and Hans Urs von Balthasar, but also those of the German schools of Max Seckler, Heinrich Fries or Hansjürgen Verweyen, the Roman school of René Latourelle and Rino Fisichella, and that of Salvador Pié-Ninot, should be recognized.9 According to the specific perspective of the respective authors, each of these proposals seeks a composition between apologetic discourse/momentum - more often called dialogical or dialogical-contextual moment - and dogmatic discourse/momentum, essentially through the search for a Christological articulation capable of giving reason for the two complementary itineraries.

Along the first itinerary, carried out precisely according to a dogmatic-theological methodological perspective, we begin with Revelation and the richness of the mystery of God, and then move towards man; the discussion on Revelation is then carried out based on the internal logic of the Word of God and the categories to understand it are drawn from Revelation itself and not from other sources, then moving towards the study of its transmission in history, that is, the study of its organic relationship with the Church and her Magisterium. The second itinerary, in accordance with its anthropological-dialogical methodological perspective, starts from the existential situation of the human being as a creature open to Revelation. It then begins more easily by speaking about the anthropological dimensions of faith and, again from the perspective of man, we move on to discuss the very possibility of a supernatural revelation, its knowability and intelligibility, the possibility of a discourse on God using human words and the existential relevance that all this entails for man.

Two paradigmatic examples of the two *mo(vi)ments highlighted* here are the proposals of H.U. von Balthasar (1905-1988) and K. Rahner (1904-1984).

For Balthasar, fundamental theology and dogmatic theology are inseparable and any apologetic reflection must move in a theological, not a philosophical one. Revelation flaunts itself with its beauty (*pulchrum*) and its coherent figure (*Gestalt*), beyond any interpretation, bringing with it its hermeneutical principle and credibility. Fundamental Theology thus becomes the discipline called to perceive, rather than explain, the form, beauty and Glory of Revelation, highlighting its appeal to man as a unitary form. Credibility is therefore also a descending motion, given in the person of Jesus Christ, which sums up in itself all Revelation and its beauty as a *universal concretum,* and proposes it with the hermeneutics of love, a nondeductible hermeneutics. Since the Revelation of God is expressed in the objectivity of a *Gestalt* that contains, as in a whole, also hermeneutics and credibility, then every ascending path to God, cosmological or anthropological, remains insufficient and in a certain way fails, because it is measured on the subject. These ascending paths do not bear any significant credibility for man because such can only be the *credibility of love*.

According to Rahner's anthropological approach, every man is constitutively open to the transcendent and is oriented towards Christian Revelation as *Hearer of the Word*. This is an anthropology within a fundamental theology. Philosophical anthropology must be understood as the metaphysics of a *potentia oboedientialis* to God's Revelation. Man's self-transcendence, his spiritual being in a certain infinite way, his openness to God, is precisely what characterizes him as a human being, even when he is not fully aware that the aim of this openness is God, or he perceives the dynamics of this movement only in an athematic, not categorical form. The Rahnerian approach is called transcendental, because he uses the a priori conditions of the believing subject as a starting point for the knowledge of the truths of faith. One's openness to God is a *transcendental* experience because it is an experience of *transcendence*. Any free decision regarding the acceptance or rejection of this transcendental phenomenology that man reads in himself is, after all, an attitude for or against God. Thus a correlation is founded between the infinite openness of the human being and the possible revelation of God; indeed, the human being expresses at bottom the real possibility of the latter. The historicity of Revelation ultimately depends on the historicity of the human being.

In general, in the last decades of the 20th century and at the beginning of our century the dialogical-anthropological itinerary was developed mainly in a pastoral rather than theological-systematic key, following the furrow opened by the various documents of the Second Vatican Council and moving on the basis of the numerous exhortations to a new evangelization formulated by the Magisterium of John Paul II. At the end of the day it is a road that has become available thanks to that theological elaboration which, already with de Lubac, had been able to present a humanism with its full accomplishment in Christ, reassuring the Church that she can speak of God even when she goes to meet man; or, to put it in the words of the *Redemptor hominis*, inviting her to tread without fear the path of man, because it is the path travelled by Christ himself.10

##### 1.2.3 Purpose of Fundamental Theology: *intellectus fidei* and diakonia of faith
If the primary object of the Fundamental is represented, as we have seen, by Revelation and its credibility, the aim of this discipline will be the exposition of this object in the context of critical knowledge, knowing how to develop its theological discourse towards a recipient in possession of precise knowledge in the historical, philosophical and scientific fields. This aim will be expressed through a task *ad intra*, addressed to believers, and one *ad extra*; the latter can be addressed to those who do not yet believe, in order to remove obstacles to the act of faith, or to those who already believe, in order to provide them with arguments to support the proclamation of the Gospel in a credible way.

The *ad intra* task is especially important, because it is directed to a subject, one who has already accepted the Word of God in faith, whose contextual knowledge is constantly increasing and whose experience of life proceeds with age and reason. In this sense, the dialogue between faith and reason which fundamental theology is called to serve does not (only) concern the comparison between belief and non-belief, but rather the harmonious development, in every believing conscience, between the contents of one's own faith and the knowledge progressively acquired. Fundamental Theology must then answer the question which has always accompanied, in a more or less explicit way, all Christian evangelization, that *of the believer who questions himself about his situation as a believer*. What is the meaning of the Revelation with which God questions me and of the faith with which I am invited to answer him? What is this meaning in the face of my knowledge of the world, of myself, of the history of other people? "Fundamental Theology is thus a systematic and scientific reflection from that spontaneous attitude which, in one way or another, arises in every believer. It is about the basic theological attitude: that of the faith that seeks to understand (*fides quaerens intellectum*) or, if you prefer, the intelligence of the believer who questions himself, becomes reflective, in an attempt to integrate what he knows with what he believes".11 It is therefore *a dialogue between reason and the faith of every Christian as a believer*, which will then be prolonged in the dialogue between the Christian faith and the reason of those who do not yet believe.

The tasks that Thomas Aquinas already assigned to reason (philosophy) with regard to revealed faith, as set out for example in the *Contra Gentiles,* certainly belong to the aims of fundamental theology.12 These are reduced to four:

1. *to defend rationally*, demonstratively or at least plausibly, the natural truths known by reason alone,

1. *pave the way for errors* that would claim to show the contents of Revelation as irrational,

1. *propose* grounds for credibility that lead to taking the authority of Scripture seriously, and finally

1. *support in a subsidiary* but not probative *way* the contents of faith, showing their reasonableness.

The first three tasks are ordered to a *praeparatio fidei*: philosophy can demonstrate certain preambles of faith, clarify the fallacy of arguments that would claim to contradict the truths of faith, and finally take care to attach reasons that show the divine origin of the *fact of* Revelation. A fourth task, which Saint Thomas advises us to employ with prudence, is to show the reasonableness of the revealed contents, by means of arguments of convenience, the recourse to analogies, heuristic considerations. This last task is not aimed at "demonstrating" the revealed contents (philosophy could not do that), but at illustrating that, in them, reason can still feel at home: they are contents that surpass it but do not contradict it.

The *intra* and *extra* purposes can still be summarized in this way in succession, in a schematic way:

- to show believers the solidity and consistency of things believed: they come from a Word delivered to us by witnesses worthy of faith, first of all Jesus Christ himself, who offers reasons to believe in Him;
- show believers the conformity of things believed with knowledge from other sources of knowledge, because the latter do not contradict or falsify them (faith/region, faith/science);
- remove, in those who still do not believe, the obstacles that may prevent them from making the act of faith, or from recognizing it as reasonably and morally sensible;
- to provide (or remember) those who do not yet believe with the knowledge that is preparatory to the understanding of the proclamation of faith.

Faced with today's drive for secularism and growing secularization, many Christians feel the difficulty of being able to explain the reasons for their faith in an intellectual context in which it is difficult to find a common language, a shared logos, that can reasonably qualify as "non-confessional. Not knowing how to do so, the majority of believers then resort to motivations of an exclusively subjective or experiential nature, when not simply emotional, and often take a path in the direction of fideism. This discomfort could be healed, at least in principle, precisely by the program of Thomas Aquinas, who re-evaluates the task of philosophy in being able to demonstrate certain natural truths, knowable by reason, truths that are in themselves "non confessional", but certainly useful in preparing the proclamation of the Gospel and in fostering its understanding. Such are, for example, the existence of God, the common dependence on a Creator, the existence of a natural moral law that obliges us in conscience to do good and avoid evil. Within its anthropological and contextual itinerary, the "apologetic moment" of Fundamental Theology should then lead it to exercise a real diakonia to the act of faith, directing the interlocutor towards that propaedeutic knowledge of the faith, now no longer shared, but still shared by a *recta ratio,* we will say today by a reason healed by its weaknesses.

##### 1.2.4 The Christological Concentration of Fundamental Theology
In the centre of Fundamental Theology there is Revelation as an object: its subject is the Triune God. The communication of God to man, the expression of his salvific condescension, is fully realised in Jesus Christ, the Word of God incarnate. The mystery of Christ, *Logos* become flesh (cf. *Jn* 1:14) is at the centre of every theological discourse and is therefore at the centre of fundamental theology. All the words of Revelation point to the Word made flesh, the only one capable of summing them all up and summarizing them all in themselves. All the events of salvific history point to the Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ, the Christian event par excellence, but also the Christian mystery capable of expressing and interpreting, through the *Lex Incarnationis*, all the relationships between God and man and, in a mysterious way, also those between God and the world. The Easter of Christ is the hermeneutic principle of the whole Revelation, but also the apex of its noetic content, of what it means and delivers us.

The Incarnate Word is the Revelation and the Revelator, the Promise and the fulfilment, the fullness of the contents that God wants to reveal to us and the mediation necessary to know them. It is of Him that the Scriptures speak, to the point that ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ, and He is the main object of *Traditio,* because what the Church transmits in history is His Paschal Mystery and what derives from it for the life of the world. It is thanks to him that creation belongs to an economy of revelation (cf. *Jn* 1:1-3), and it is again thanks to him that creation is the repository of a promise of which His glorious resurrection is eschatological first fruits. He is the only one capable of mediating at the beginning as well as at the end, in the Alpha and Omega (cf. *Rev* 1:8; 21:6; 22:13).

Jesus Christ is the witness par excellence, the one to whom all witnesses must conform, and he is the decisive sign given to the world so that the world may believe (cf. *Mt* 12:38-39.42; *Jn* 2:18-22), the only sign capable of drawing all things to Himself (cf. *Jn* 12:32). The reasons for believing in Christ, and believing him as the Son sent by the Father, are He Himself and, ultimately, should not be sought outside of Him. Prophecies point to him and his miracles speak of him. It is His person and His credibility that we trust. The Church and Christians are signs for the world, attracting people to the faith, to the extent that they know they are signs of *Christ*.

Our faith is directed to Jesus Christ. To believe in his words is to believe in him. He alone can be presented to men as the way, the truth and the life (cf. *Jn* 14:6). In the historicity (and concreteness) of his humanity the universal content of life, goodness and salvation are offered to the world. Seeing Him, one sees the Father.

The fact that the fullness and fulfilment of Revelation are given in Jesus Christ and that the logic of the Incarnation expresses in a supreme way God's self-communication to man, completely condition fundamental theology, not only its object, but also the way of approaching its content and understanding its method. All this makes it possible to speak of a "Christological concentration" of Fundamental Theology. Let us see some implications.

- Revelation is not only knowledge of an objective content, but also revelation of a *personal subject*; God's self-communication is not only a gift in which we are offered knowledge of the mystery of His life and His saving plan, but it is the gift of Himself, the gift of His life to the world.
- Revealer and Revelation recall each other, its author and its content are inseparable.
- The nature of mystery and event are inseparable, in Revelation, as they are between them, in Christ, the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word and the historical event of Jesus of Nazareth.
- The acceptance of Revelation is the acceptance of a personal being; faith is not only faith in a content, but above all faith in someone, faith in the person of Jesus Christ.
- The credibility of Revelation is, in fact, the credibility of a person, the person of Jesus Christ.
- The relationship between Christian Revelation and the religions of the earth is measured by the way in which Jesus Christ includes within himself every human aspiration to truth and to God.

The logic with which the Logos made flesh dominates, in fact, the whole economy of Revelation and its credibility, perhaps comparable to the majesty with which the mosaics of Christ the Pantocrator dominate the apses of the Byzantine basilicas, must not, however, make us forget the role of the Spirit, inseparable from that of the Son. The Trinitarian breath of Revelation tells us that God gives Himself to the world through the missions of the Son and the Holy Spirit, joint and articulated missions, such that the Spirit prolongs and makes present in history the Word delivered once and for all by the Son. As we will see in the next sections of this volume, the Holy Spirit is also the subject of an important protagonism, capable of highlighting essential aspects of Revelation, its reception and its transmission.

#### 1.3 Brief historical overview of the treatise on Fundamental Theology
The idea that it was necessary to develop a theological discourse with the dual purpose of strengthening the faith of believers and preparing those who did not yet believe for the faith, has accompanied the history of the Church from the beginning, thus constituting a historical antecedent of Fundamental Theology, here understood in its broadest sense. The elements that induced and historically conditioned the rise of an Apologetics first, and of a Fundamental Theology later, were many. These include the different situations of the Church in relation to the surrounding environment, the different needs encountered by evangelization, the presence of heresies and doctrinal errors, the progress of human knowledge, and the changing anthropological, cultural and social situations. Let us briefly examine some of the most important historical stages that have led to the configuration of the modern Treaty of Fundamental Theology.13

##### 1.3.1 Some ideas in the New Testament
There are *traces* in the NT that seem to refer to what we would call today a "Fundamental Theology". The Gospels are at bottom the most explicit example of this. We find in them expressions that indicate them as documents written with the purpose of consolidating the reasons of believers' faith and, more generally, to testify the reasonableness of adherence to the doctrine of Jesus Christ, Messiah and Son sent by the Father.

Since many people have been involved in drawing up an account of the events that have taken place between us, just as those who witnessed them from the beginning have transmitted them to us and became ministers of the word, so I too have decided to do careful research into every circumstance from the beginning and to write for you an orderly account of them, illustrious Theophilus, so that you may become aware of the solidity of the teachings you have received (*Lk* 1:1-4);

Many other signs Jesus made in the presence of his disciples, but they were not written in this book. These were written, because you believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and because, believing, you have life in his name (*Jn* 20:30-31).

The Gospels delivered a new and unprecedented announcement, which therefore needed to be supported by evidence. It is the proclamation that God, the God whom no one has ever seen and who has created all things, has made himself visible and accessible in Jesus of Nazareth, who is the Christ Messiah, because it is *he who has revealed him* (cf. *Jn* 1:1-18). To this saving proclamation, which generates and convokes a community of believers, that is, the Church, is asked to respond *with the adherence of faith*: faith in the divine sonship of Christ and in what God reveals through him. The *reasonableness of the assent of faith* is affirmed by Jesus himself with explicit reference to his works (cf. *Jn* 10:37-38); in general, it is to this logic that Jesus' miracles respond (cf. *Mk* 1:1; *Jn* 2:11; *Jn* 11:40-42). The Gospels are written *in the Church*, in close relation to the mandate received to transmit to the world the teachings of Jesus Christ (cf. *Mt* 28:18-20). From these brief considerations we can see that the Gospel message contains the four main elements of a theological-fundamental path: Revelation, credibility, faith and the role of the Church.

The announcement is quickly becoming structured in what we call the "apostolic kerygma", formed by four relevant segments: Jesus of Nazareth is the Christ - Jesus Christ is the Son of God - he died for our sins - he rose from the dead. To be preached is therefore first and foremost an event, although an event with salvific consequences of absolutely universal scope. The first proof of this event is therefore entrusted to a connection with the story of Jesus of Nazareth, of whose attendance the apostles declare themselves privileged witnesses. It is easy to realise that, in order for this announcement to be intelligible to its addressees, it was necessary to know the Scriptures of Israel (it is Christ), the recognition of a Creator God whose existence could also be available to the Gentiles (he is the Son of God), the perception of a natural moral law capable of instructing us about our condition as sinners before God (who died for our sins), the salvific novelty that a resurrection from the dead entailed, compared to the expectations and hopes of every human being (risen from the dead). With the apostolic kerygma the fulfilment of the Scriptures is announced, but also the fulfilment of a salvific plan whose breadth and depth only now becomes comprehensible in the light of the Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ.

The announcement, whose subject is Jesus of Nazareth, is an announcement of resurrection and to the resurrection it points. For this reason, the NT contains important speeches of the Apostles, whose structure consists in the exposition of the mystery of Christ, dead and risen, and at the same time in showing the listeners the reasonableness of accepting this faith, its relevance for the world, its consonance with the aspirations of man; the objective proofs of the prophecies, miracles and apparitions of the risen man are mentioned (cf. *Acts* 2:22-41; *Acts* 10:34-43; *Acts* 17:22-31; *2Pt* 1:16-19).

In an illustrative way the *First Letter of Peter*, in particular Chapter 1, seems to contain all the essential elements of what we might call a small treatise on Fundamental Theology *ante litteram*. The announcement of the Revelation made in Christ, the reasons for its credibility, the attention to the historical-existential situation of its addressees, the preambles of the faith, the testimony of those who have already accepted the faith, the reasons that strengthen the faith of those who believe by showing it to them ever more reasonable.

##### 1.3.2 The works of the Fathers
Although the books of the NT contain the elements proper to a theological-fundamental treatise - essentially the presentation of the Christian message combined with its credibility - they do not constitute an example of apologetic literature *strictu sensu*. They are an exposition of the faith before its defence. It will be in the works of the Fathers, especially those of the 2nd and 3rd centuries and before Constantine's Edict (313), where the exposition of the Christian message and its appeal to every man, will take the explicit form of an *Apologetics* (*apo-logos*, to speak before, in favor of), that is, a *defense of* such content before those who misinterpreted, averted or despised it.

In this period are born the two *Apologies* and the *Dialogue with* Justin's *Triphon* († 165), the *Apology* of Aristide (2nd century), the *Oratio ad Graecos (*170) by Taziano, the *Legatio pro christianis* (180) by Athenagoras, the *Letter to Diognetus* by an unknown author of the 2nd century, *Ad Autolicum* (185) by Theophilus of Antioch, the *Apologeticum* (197) by Tertullian, the *Octavius (*235) by Minucius Felix*,* the *Contra Celsum (*249) by Origen and *the Adversus Nationes (*297) by Arnobius, just to mention some of the best known.

The apologetic fathers and writers never limited themselves to contesting the accusations. They endeavoured to explain the substance and consistency of the things believed, and even more so the very life of the Christian communities, so that the pagans would be attracted to them. On closer inspection, the style of the *apologies* is not polemical (as instead in the writings addressed to heretics), nor does it have only a juridical or technical tenor, aimed at rejecting the accusations: the Fathers are also and always interested in the positive exposition of the faith, the conversion of the interlocutor, as testified in an emblematic way by the episode of the dialogue between Justin and his persecutor Rustico, transmitted to us by the devoted account of his *Acta martyrii*. It is therefore insufficient to read the term "apologia" as a mere *defense*, because it is reductive compared to what this kind of writings hosted from the beginning. Both ends, the refutation of the accusations made against Christians and the exposition of the faith in terms of evangelical proclamation, are present and interdependent. In the Gospels, the invitation to conversion was proposed in kerygmatic form and supported by arguments of credibility; in the writings of Justin and Athenagoras, Aristide and Theophilus, the kerygma takes an apologetic form, and conversion is proposed as a consequence of the truth of an apology.

The organicity of the patristic cultural operation can also be seen from the presence of works that expose the reasons of faith according to literary genres other than *Apologia* or *Legatio*. Theological-fundamental contents are also recognizable in the genre of *Dialogues* and *Expositions*, as well as in numerous works dedicated to the relationship between Revelation and philosophical thought or pagan culture. Such were the *Protretticus* of Clement of Alexandria († 216) followed by the *Pedagogue* and the *Stromatians*, the *Preparation for the Gospel and* the *Evangelical Demonstration of* Eusebius of Caesarea († 340), and the *Divinae institutiones* (310) of Lactantius. In this kind of writing, as even before in *Adversus haereses* by Irenaeus of Lyons († 200), we can already find the elaboration of an incipient but significant theology of Revelation. It is still in this sense that works such as the *De Incarnatione Verbi* (337) by Athanasius, the *Esameroni by* Basil of Caesarea and Ambrose of Milan, and the theology of history proposed by the great Augustinian synthesis of *De civitate Dei* (427) should be read.

The need to show the coherence and reasonableness of faith against the backdrop of history and philosophical thought - and not only in the face of individual accusations - is also evidenced by the presence of works of broad apologetic scope well beyond the Constantinian edict of tolerance. The apologetic thought of the Fathers is not only an apologia *de religione christiana*, but the progressive *maturation* of a thought capable of showing why Christianity fulfilled the requirements of a treatise on *true religion,* explaining to the Jews and the Greco-Roman world why a specific *oeconomia revelationis,* the Christian one.

The works of apologetic content of the patristic period, as a whole, entrust the exhibition and understanding of revelation to three great hermeneutical paintings. With regard to the pagan environment, the idea is insisted on that Christianity is linked to the revelation of God to Abraham, the one God creator of heaven and earth, to whom all righteous men can have access. With the Jewish interlocutors, on the other hand, the "proof of the prophecies" is emphasized: Christianity is the fulfillment of the Scriptures, it is true because the OT speaks of Christ. The *Acts of the Apostles* contain both argumentative structures: the Apostles speak in the synagogues to the Jews, showing that Jesus is the promised Messiah; but they also speak in the Agora, stating that the God they announce and who sent his Son into the world, raising him from the dead, is the same God who created the world and the human race. Finally, to everyone, pagans and Jews, the Fathers clarify, especially in an antignostic key, that Jesus Christ, and not others, is the *true revelator*. The incarnation of God made man is the full and definitive salvific mediation, the greatest that history can know or expect. He is the one who guarantees, with regard to the Jews, the continuity between the Old and the New Testament, and again, with regard to the Gentiles, the correspondence between creation and salvation. All the saving works of God can be read as works of Christ: everything from the Patriarchs to the prophets, and even before that the whole of creation, speaks of Him and points towards Him.

##### 1.3.3 Medieval and Renaissance forms of Apologetics
A long time has passed since the historical breakthrough of Christianity and the events concerning Jesus of Nazareth and his first disciples, a new theological-fundamental concern is beginning to emerge: that of showing the continuity of the Christian faith with its origins. In mediaeval times, the concept of *auctoritas* - Scripture, the magisterial definitions and the doctrine of the Fathers of the Church - as a proof of this continuity, against the possible assertion of errors, was made. In this way, if in the patristic era apologetic reflection had revolved around the *quaestio de vera religione,* privileging knowledge and synthetic arguments, in With the Jewish interlocutors, on the other hand, the "proof of the prophecies" is emphasized: Christianity is the fulfillment of the Scriptures, it is true because the OT speaks of Christ..

The first scholastic and then university context of medieval culture will thus push theology to elaborate important theological-fundamental forms: *auctoritas*, be it biblical or patristic, must now confront *ratio*. It deals with the *praeambula fidei*, the "proofs" of God's existence, the relationship between faith and reason, the knowability of the supernatural and its relationship with nature. The *fides* becomes increasingly aware of its obligation to *quaerere intellectum*. First Anselm of Aosta (1033-1109) and then mainly Thomas Aquinas (1224-1274) are the major interpreters of this requirement, which today we would also place in the field of Fundamental Theology. One of the best known works in this sense will be the *Summa contra Gentiles* (1269-1273) by Saint Thomas Aquinas.

However, a commonplace must be overcome, according to which mediaeval theology laid the foundations for a rational foundation of faith, thus devaluing its existential and personalist component. The program set out by Thomas Aquinas in *Summa contra Gentiles*, in fact, did not seek such a foundation, but was intended to "prepare for the faith", then supporting the faith of believers with arguments of convenience. Before him, Anselm of Aosta, in the *Monologium* (1076), proposed to show that there are *rationes* necessary and able to highlight the internal coherence, the non-contradictory nature and therefore the reasonableness of the truths of faith; for this program he had not employed rationalist criteria, but had rested on a reason recognized as the image of God, capable of truth and aware of moving towards the truth. William of Auxerre († 1231), probably the first author to introduce the notion of credibility, also moved in the same line: it is faith that shows "the reasons" for what is believed. The separation between faith and reason, prepared in the late mediaeval period, was only consumed later, first with Lutheran fideism and then with Cartesian rationalism.

Two specific interlocutors urge *ad extra* Christian preaching in the mediaeval period and at the beginning of the Renaissance: Judaism and Islam. The comparison with them, already present in Thomas, became more heated between the 14th and 16th centuries, giving rise to a controversial literature, as in Ramon Martì or, later, in Marsilio Ficino, but also to valuable examples of inter-religious dialogue, with Ramon Llull and then with Nicolò Cusano, author of an important operetta entitled *De pace fidei* (1453).

For diffusion and for its subsequent influence, we should also remember the apologetic work of Gerolamo Savonarola the *Triumphus Crucis* (1497). In the four books in which it is composed, the author proposes to show the truth of Christianity starting from the knowledge of natural reason about God (Book I), religion and moral life (Book II), and then moving on to the brief description of the articles of faith, of which he wishes to highlight the internal consistency, reasonableness and their convenience for the life of man (Book III), and finally moving on to the refutation of the adversaries of Christianity, whose theses are disproved through recourse to natural reason, mainly history and logic (Book IV). The work had 35 different editions and was used for about two centuries in the Schools of *Propaganda Fide* as a manual of apologetics for those who went to mission lands.

At the end of the 15th century all the elements that will give rise to the first two segments, the *religious demonstratio* and the *christian demonstratio*, of the classical tripartition that the theological-fundamental treatise will assume a century later are already outlined. The segment dedicated to the Church, the *Catholic demonstratio* - whose prolethetic elements are, moreover, already outlined in the Patristic - was born a few decades later, from a comparison with the Protestant Reformation and the new contents it brings with it, especially with regard to the way of understanding two crucial relationships, that between Scripture and tradition, and that between faith and reason.

Beginning with the Lutheran crisis (Wittemberg's thesis, 1517; Martin Luther's excommunication, 1521), the presentation of Revelation, faith and credibility, came up against important new problems. The question that Apologetics must now answer is no longer only "what is the true religion?", but also "what is the true Church of Christ?". Starting from the separation of the Protestants from the Catholic Church, a different conception of Revelation *in relation to its sources is emerging*, that is, a new way of conceiving the relations between Tradition, Scripture and the Magisterium, while the devaluation of reason with regard to the understanding of faith is causing evident consequences on the way of understanding the credibility of Revelation and the role to be attributed to the philosophical preambles of faith.

This state of affairs will favour two important reflections in the Catholic field: the progressive formation of the theological treatise *De Ecclesia* and the need to identify the true sources of theological knowledge, which will find its first systematic in the work of Melchior Cano, *De locis theologicis* (1563).

Finally, it should not be forgotten that progress in geographical knowledge, culminating in the discovery of the Americas (1492), leads to an initial reflection on the role of the Christian religion in the context of the religiosity of peoples, whose horizons are now considerably broadened. This is reflected in the works of Marsilio Ficino, *De christiana religione* (1474) and Luis Vives, *De veritate fidei christianae (*1543) and, among the reformers, that of Hugo Grozio, *De veritate religionis christianae* (1621).

##### 1.3.4 The structure of the Treaty in modern times
At the end of the 16th century the programme of Catholic Apologetics, which presents three adjacent treatises, was structured: *De Religione - De Vera Religione - De Ecclesia Christi*, also expressed in the form of three "demonstrations" organised in a propaedeutic order as *religious Demonstratio - demonstratio christiana - catholic demonstratio*. The scheme is well present in the work of Pierre Charron, *Des trois verités contre les Athées, Idolatres, Juifs, Mahumetans, Hérétiques et Schismatiques* (1593). The defence of the truth of the Church (*De vera Ecclesia*) is instead entrusted to the demonstration of three ways: the historical *way (the Church as the* legitimate expression of the Church historically founded by Christ), the *notarum way (*the Church as the only subject that fully possesses the notes of unity, holiness, catholicity and apostolic origin) and the *empirical way* (the Church as a moral miracle). In the 17th century, by now with the progressive differentiation of theological disciplines due to the abandonment of the *Summae* scheme, it will be possible to speak of an "Apologetic Theology", a discipline that includes together the *tria demonstrationes*, plus the treatise *de locis theologicis*.

In the 17th and 18th centuries, the exposition and defence of the faith had to face, above all, the criticism of the revealed religion prompted by the Enlightenment and the affirmation of the various forms of deism (J. Toland, Voltaire, Reimarus, M. Tindal), both committed to enhancing reason to the point of absolutizing it, either to use it against the contents of revealed religions, or to reduce religion within the rational sphere, or, finally, to transform reason itself into religion, as paradoxically happened in the farcical final results of Auguste Comte's positivism and revolutionary Enlightenment. We thus witness contradictory phenomena, such as the attempt of Enlightenment deism, in open contrast to atheism, to defend a rational religion and forms of natural morality; or the famous exhortation of intelligence to dare where it had never dared before, only to show itself sceptical and disenchanted, when necessary, in the face of the traditional aspirations kept alive by religions. The contents of the concepts of reason and religion are thus rapidly evolving, while the concept of nature is maturing a transition from a synonym of creation to a reality now stripped of any theological surplus value, thus paving the way towards the 19th century identification between naturalism and materialism. The declared program remains that of gathering consensus around an idea of religion which, in order to be truly universal and human, should be independent of any revelation. The revealed religions, according to the Enlightenment and the deists, find their origin in myth and superstition, then degenerating into ideologies responsible for the divisions between men: the reconquest of peace and tolerance would be possible only through rational access to a divinity recognized by the reason of all.

The defence of the possibility of a divine revelation, made against deism, as well as the defence of the Catholic religion against atheism, are both conceived, with few exceptions, as a sort of rational demonstration brought on the same ground as the adversaries, and therefore developed with logical, philosophical or historical instruments, shared also by non-believers. The progressive forgetfulness of what had only been put in brackets (personalist and existential aspects of faith, biblical foundation of the image of God, Christological perspective, etc.), make the apologetic method somewhat *extrinsic,* busy searching for proofs external to Revelation, mostly of an objective and impersonal character. In the middle of the seventeenth century, this time in opposition to scepticism and libertine indifferentism, it was the work of Blaise Pascal who insisted more on the personalist and existential nature of Christianity. His was a true and proper project of Apologetics of the Christian religion, which remained unfinished, then collected in the posthumous editions of his *Pensieri*.

In the eighteenth century until the early nineteenth century, the apologetic treatment was affected by the way of understanding the relationship between faith and reason, especially due to the growing influence of Immanuel Kant (1724-1804) and the subsequent rise of idealism. In Kant the reasons that can justify the reasonableness of an assent of faith or a natural knowledge of God are shifted to the realm of *practical reason* alone and denied in the realm of *pure reason*. Faith in a divine revelation is admissible only in the immanent experience of the subject.

With idealism and the currents of romanticism, reason takes on the characteristics of transcendence and makes them its own, wanting to justify everything, even religion, in its own system of thought. A "philosophy of revelation" is emerging, which ends by absorbing in itself the mysteries of Christianity (J.G. Fichte, F.W. Schelling, G.W. Hegel) or by proposing them as the most advanced form of human religious sentiment, expression of a universal sense of dependence on the Absolute (F. Schleirmacher). In its most advanced stage, idealism will lead to historicist elaborations that will attempt to "explain" the Christian religion in natural terms or, in any case, bring it back into an immanent framework. The absoluteness of Christianity is then understood as the point of arrival of the whole universal religious phenomenology, although the end to which the whole process actually aims is the historical affirmation of Reason or the Spirit.

##### 1.3.5 Developments in the 19th century
There is no doubt that the nineteenth century introduced themes and problems destined to mark in a decisive way the work of Fundamental Theology almost to the present day. Apologetic reflection is solicited not only in the philosophical sphere, but also when confronted with the sciences, both historical and natural. The life of the Church is at important junctures, first with Vatican Council I and then with the modernist crisis. The revaluation of the subject brought about by modernity pushes towards new syntheses between Christianity and philosophy (A. Rosmini, J.H. Newman, M. Blondel), but also brings with it dramatic conflicts (L. Feuerbach, F. Nietzsche).

History, hermeneutics and philology impose new canons of understanding of the biblical text, coming with the *Life of Jesus* (1835) by D.F. Strauss to formalise a criticism of the gospels and the historicity of Jesus of Nazareth already initiated by H.S. Reimarus with his *Apologia of those who worship God according to reason* (1768). Thus is prepared the approach that Adolf von Harnack will introduce into theological reflection at the end of the century with *The Essence of Christianity* (1900), according to which the gospels do not contain any record of the events that have occurred, since their only purpose is the proclamation of faith in a Jesus of which history can tell us very little.

The natural sciences drastically push in the same direction, also calling the biblical text into debate because of the immense widening of the temporal horizons of the age of the earth, the origins of mankind and life. The questions raised by Charles Darwin's (1809-1882) theory of biological evolution will enter the classrooms of the Holy Office and even the Council meetings of Vatican I: in these venues a non-belligerence will be chosen, thus remaining unanswered questions, also in order not to find at the moment a theology willing to reread their implications in a renewed dogmatic framework. It is still at this moment in history that the story linked to the name of Galileo Galilei14 is brought to light and taken as a paradigm of a perennial conflict between the results of the sciences and the Christian faith. What at the beginning of the seventeenth century had been understood as an unfortunate, albeit serious misunderstanding between the incipient scientific method and the exegetical-cultural framework, culpably dated, supported by the then more influential theological current, finds in the anti-Catholic climate of the second half of the nineteenth century, as required by the political contingencies of the time, a fertile ground to give rise to the idea of an inevitable break between faith and science.

In the search for the criteria of truth of Christianity, the German school, especially with Sebastian von Drey (1777-1853), shifts the emphasis to the coherence of Revelation as a whole and its historical character. Maurice Blondel (1861-1949) elaborated the method of immanence, in order to highlight the subject's expectations and his openness to salvific Revelation as a completion of his existential research (*L'Action*, 1893; *Letter on Apologetics*, 1896). John Henry Newman (1801-1890) developed with great apologetic vein the *historical path* in the defence of the doctrine of the Catholic Church (*The Development of Christian Doctrine*, 1845) and suggested an approach to the faith which, although based on the judgments of reason, cannot be derived from it (*Grammar of Assent*, 1870).

Significant, in the 19th century, were the interventions of the Catholic Magisterium on topics related to the object of Fundamental Theology. The Holy Office judged negatively the devaluation of reason against faith (L.E. Bautain, A. Bonnetty), condemning *fideism* and *traditionalism*. Pius IX intervened with the document *Qui pluribus* (1846) on the relationship between faith and reason and the reasons for credibility. The teachings of the First Vatican Council (1870) dedicated to Revelation and the relationship between faith and reason the constitution *Dei Filius,* to deal later, in *Pastor Aeternus*, with the Magisterium of the Roman Pontiff and his relations with the transmission of the revealed deposit. In particular, in the chapters *De Revelation - De Fide - De Fide et Ratione* of the Constitution *Dei Filius,* the exposition of the nature of Christian Revelation and the doctrine on faith are carried out against the background of precise clarifications of rationalism and fideism. What emerges is a necessarily more conceptualist vision of Revelation and faith, which Vatican II will complete (and in part overcome) with its more historical-salvific vision. In Pius X's encyclical *Pascendi* (1907) - by now at the beginning of the 20th century - the errors of modernism on the theme of Revelation, on the relationship between nature and grace, on the nature of faith, on the danger of overestimating the subjective aspects of faith and religious experience at the expense of the objectivity of the revealed deposit are reviewed.

The end of the nineteenth century and the beginning of the twentieth will see the light of a close confrontation - and in part reductive for theology - between neo-scholasticism and the new currents, including modernism, which seek to mediate between Christianity and the revaluation of the vital world of the subject. Neo-scholasticism will insist on the rational analysis of the act of faith and on *external* reasons for credibility, such as prophecies and miracles, in order to balance the emphasis given by other theological currents with *internal ones*. The recomposition of the fracture between faith and reason, but also the search for new syntheses, will not be favoured because of the existence of an exclusively "ancillary" understanding of the role of philosophy in relation to theology. It will be necessary to wait until the second half of the twentieth century to see the appearance of spousal, Mariological and Christological-incarnationist analogies (although there were precedents even in the nineteenth century, e.g. with M.J. Scheeben), which will gradually replace the ancillary model.

It can be instructive to summarize the most relevant ideas and events that, in this historical period, have conditioned the subsequent development of Fundamental Theology:

- the radicalisation of the separation between faith and reason, with an understanding of reason in Cartesian terms (the progressive affirmation of Kantian philosophy and the religious vision of the reformers);
- the rise of positive atheism (Feuerbach, Marx, Engels, Nietzsche);
- criticism of the gospels and the historicity of Jesus of Nazareth;
- the new perspectives brought by natural sciences and psychology (geology and biology; Darwin, Freud);
- the emergence of a supposed irremediable conflict between Christianity and the sciences (J.W. Draper, A.D. White);
- the proposal of an idealistic understanding (and defence) of Christianity (Kant, Schleiermacher);
- the emergence of a principle of immanence for the understanding of man and his history, and of a materialistic-evolutive principle for the understanding of nature (including the human being);
- the reconduction of religion to a subjective experience unhooked by normative references (modernism, prepared by idealism and romanticism);
- the emergence of a (not always justified) conflict between a subjective-personalist perspective and an objective-doctrinal perspective of Revelation and faith.

##### 1.3.6 The cultural context of the 20th century and the contemporary era
The 20th century has witnessed important changes that have gradually led to the form that the Treaty of Fundamental Theology has taken in contemporary times. The approach taken by neo-scholasticism, less prone to a biblical-theological exposition of Revelation and more concerned with providing the logical-philosophical reasons capable of attesting to its divine origin, has slowly faded away. The insistence on the cognitive aspects of faith and Revelation, on their doctrinal-objective dimension, has been completed by an appreciation of their personalist aspects. Once the radicalization of modernism had been exhausted and the conflicting vision between dogma and experience had been overcome, the notion of religious experience and the reference to the vital world of the subject were better integrated into the treatment of faith, also thanks to the positive impulses coming from philosophical personalism. The recovery of the Christocentrism of the Fathers of the Church and the deepening of biblical theology have then contributed to a more harmonious and certainly more *theological* presentation of Revelation, its saving offer and the signs that accompany it.

This process, which originated in the 1930s and then matured around the years of the Second Vatican Council, gave rise to what in the twentieth century was called the "passage" from Apologetics to Fundamental Theology.15 Even with the limitations that this expression brings with it - fundamental theology must in fact always include both a dimension of apology and a reflection on credibility - it is intended to indicate that it is the *theology* of Revelation, understood from a strongly Christocentric perspective (the Revelator and his personal credibility), that determines today the structure of every theological-fundamental itinerary. Just as the Fathers of the Second Vatican Council preferred to replace the positive exposition of the mystery of Christ and of the Church with a detailed treatment of the errors to be fought - initially present in the schemes prepared by the Roman Curia - so contemporary Fundamental Theology gives every defence of the faith the organic exposition of Revelation and seeks above all in it the ultimate reasons for its assent. As we shall see in the course of this manual, this new approach, centred on the *auditus fidei*, must not fail to deal also with the *auditus temporis,* giving rise to ways and contents that allow our discipline to exercise a true "diakonia to the faith", which has become necessary today because of the weakness of reason, the growing secularisation and a certain religious illiteracy of a good part of the People of God.

Precisely in order to encourage this *auditus temporis*, it is useful to turn our gaze to the second half of the 20th century and point out how it has given us an anthropologically and socially complex, but also promising, panorama that many authors are now used to presenting as the final outcome of modernity. In fact, from many quarters we can point out the entry into a new era, called "post-modernity".16 With regard to its relationship with religion, among the most characteristic elements would be religious indifference and detachment from institutional religions, a decomposed search for the sacred, but also the rebirth of a sort of cheerful polytheism and the assumption of divinity as an object of play and subjective consumption.

The proclamation of the Gospel therefore encounters, at the beginning of the twenty-first century, severe challenges which fundamental theology must somehow meet if it wishes to exercise its diakonia to the faith. We list, for illustrative purposes, some of the most important:

- the emergence of religious indifferentism sustained by ethical and philosophical relativism;
- the presence, especially among the more educated classes, of an atheistic existentialism with a nihilistic outlet;
- a society with a strong degree of secularisation and religious ignorance contracted after having already known a first evangelization;
- a widespread loss of metaphysical meaning, which makes it more difficult to grasp the existence of a creatural bond with God the Creator, the intrinsic contingency of matter, man's transcendence over nature;
- the difficulty in listening to one's conscience, in inner recollection, in deepening in general, so one is content with superficial and provisional knowledge;
- the rejection of a natural moral law, scepticism about the existence of a sharable logos in philosophical and religious matters;
- the presence, especially in countries of ancient Christian tradition, of a silent apostasy, with the relative rapid confluence of non-practising Catholics within a position that can be qualified as "non-believing Catholics" (Catholicism as a mere historical-cultural identity);
- the persistence of a certain scientism that proposes a materialistic interpretation of the world and man, regardless of God;
- the affirmation of a hedonist and superstitious materialism in vast social strata, even of good cultural level;
- a multi-religious and multi-ethnic social context mainly due to the globalisation of information and the migration of peoples;
- the rise of sects internal to Christianity, of pseudo-religious movements that are linked to oriental philosophies, of neo-gnostic forms of thought;
- the cultural and mediatic influence of a vision that associates religion and violence, including any religious experience in general.

However, the *opportunities* that the present century reserves for the preaching of the Christian faith should not be overlooked. Some of them could be qualified as "signs of the times" and therefore deserve to be pointed out:

- importance given to the value of freedom, human rights, equal opportunities (although it is difficult to grasp its transcendent foundation);
- appreciation for the authenticity of witnesses and the value of testimony in general;
- honesty and transparency, trust and altruism, are recognized as necessary elements for the *livability* of human society;
- importance of the role of women in society and the Church;
- sensitivity to nature's custody;
- sense of mystery and transcendence aroused by the discoveries of science and the broadening of our cognitive horizons;
- enormous development of the media, with important consequences in terms of human relations, education and documentation, social participation.

There is no need for the previous challenges and opportunities to be translated into new areas of a Fundamental Theology programme, but Fundamental Theology is called upon to reflect on them. Think, for example, of the importance of knowing well the personality and the socio-cultural context of the recipient to whom the proclamation is addressed, of the role that greater attention to nature (and to the revelation of God in it) could play in interreligious dialogue, of how to propose today the *signs of* witness and charity. In some cases, however, we are already witnessing a certain *theological elaboration* by authors engaged in the area of the Fundamental. This has given rise, for example, to a "theology of communication", to a more intense relationship with the anthropology of new religiosity and post-modern culture in general, to a growing reflection on the relationship between Christian faith and scientific thought.

Almost all the major theologians of the twentieth century have developed, in one way or another, their own apologetic perspective. That is, they did not limit themselves to the dogmatic exposition of the Christian mystery, but almost always tried to make it credible in the face of growing indifferentism and contemporary secularisation. Representatives in this sense have been Karl Adam, Romano Guardini, Karl Rahner, Paul Tillich, Hans Urs von Balthasar, Henri de Lubac, Jean Daniélou, Joseph Ratzinger; but also authors who were not theologians in the strict sense, such as: Paul Claudel, Georges Bernanos, Gabriel Marcel, Jacques Maritain, Joseph Pieper and various others.

Since the Second Vatican Council (1962-1965), the Magisterium of the Church has also felt the need to help the People of God to frame the contents of the faith within the precise historical, social and cultural context in which the faithful today move. This, after all, was the desire of the Fathers of the Council. Much of the documents that they prepared, even if not expressly dedicated to our subject matter, contain considerable points of interest for Fundamental Theology. Let us briefly recall some of them.

First of all, the important doctrine on Revelation set out in the dogmatic constitution *Dei Verbum* (18.11.1965) should be noted, especially with regard to its Christocentric and personalist perspective, and the teachings on the relationship between Scripture, Tradition and the Magisterium. The vision of the relationship between the Church and the contemporary world outlined in the pastoral constitution *Gaudium et spes was also* decisive, deserving of having developed an anthropology whose questions are asked in relation to the answers provided by the exposition of the mystery of Jesus Christ. Still of interest to fundamental theology are the decree *Ad Gentes* on the missionary activity of the Church, the declarations *Dignitatis Humanae, Unitatis Redintegratio* and *Nostra Aetate* which touch, respectively, on the theme of religious conscience and that of the relations of the Catholic faith with other Christian confessions and non-Christian religions.

In the post-conciliar period, the documents of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (24.6.1973) and *Donum Veritatis* (24.5.1990), the apostolic exhortations *Evangelii nuntiandi (*8.12.1975) and *Catechesis tradendae (*16.10.1979), the encyclicals of John Paul II *Redemptor hominis (*4.3.1979) and *Redemptoris Missio (*7.12.1990) should be mentioned with regard to our themes. The relations between Revelation, philosophical thought and religious sense were the subject, as is well known, of the encyclical *Fides et ratio* (14.9.1998). With regard to the new evangelization, the documents prepared around the Great Jubilee of the Year 2000, in particular *Novo millennio ineunte* (6.1.2001), should be remembered. The ordinary Magisterium of Benedict XVI (2005-2013) insisted on the situation of today's addressee in the face of the proclamation of the faith, and in various speeches he developed some of the challenges and opportunities summarised above.

In the encyclical *Lumen fidei* (2013), Pope Francis proposes a meditation on faith, within the Christian experience, although it does not deal with issues related to credibility, religious indifference or non-belief. Instead, the exhortation *Evangelii gaudium* (2013) deals more closely with the credible proclamation of the Gospel, developing two elements: the joy of the apostle, an unequivocal sign of his encounter with Christ, and concern for the poor and marginalised, a sign of the mercy and tenderness of Jesus Christ. This same document invites the theologian to elaborate an "original apologetics":

The announcement to culture also implies an announcement to professional, scientific and academic cultures. It is the encounter between faith, reason and the sciences, which aims to develop a new discourse on credibility, an original apologetics that helps to create the dispositions for the Gospel to be heard by all. When certain categories of reason and the sciences are accepted in the proclamation of the message, those same categories become instruments of evangelization; it is water transformed into wine. It is what, once assumed, is not only redeemed but becomes an instrument of the Spirit to enlighten and renew the world (n. 132).

The expression "signs of the times" is repeatedly recalled by Pope Francis, encouraging the evangelizer to recognize the opportunities presented to him and the thirst for God present in society today (cf. nn. 14, 51, 86, 108, etc.). One praises flexibility, reconciliation, creativity, the courage to explore new paths and propose new syntheses, attitudes necessary for the Church to abandon false certainties and to propose itself (but also to understand itself) as "outgoing Church": "I prefer a bumpy, wounded and dirty Church to have gone out into the streets, rather than a Church that is sick for the closure and comfort of clinging to its own certainties" (n. 49). Although the language of the document is essentially pastoral, the presence of important elements for theological work must be recognized: it offers cues not only to better understand the recipient of the Christian apostolate but also, and perhaps above all, to reflect theologically on the conditions of the apostle who evangelises, whether he is the baptised individual or the evangelising Church as a whole.

Finally, by way of documentation, it should be remembered that in recent decades the International Theological Commission has addressed topics of certain interest to Fundamental Theology. Among these, in chronological order, we point out: *The consciousness that Jesus had of himself and his mission* (1985), *Faith and inculturation (*1986), The *interpretation of dogmas (*1989), *Christianity and religions (*1996), *Theology today: perspectives, principles and criteria* (2002), *The sensus fidei in the life of the Church (*2014).
