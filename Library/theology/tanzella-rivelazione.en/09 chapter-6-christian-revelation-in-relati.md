### Chapter 6 Christian revelation in relation to religions {#tanzella-rivelazione.1.chapter-6-christian-reve}
Because of the easier and more numerous possibilities for relations between individuals and peoples, the society in which we live today presents itself as a multi-ethnic and multi-religious society. It is in this context that the theme of inter-religious dialogue is becoming increasingly important. This theme covers many areas and concerns, for example: theological aspects (the relationship between Christianity and non-Christian religions); anthropological and cultural-historical aspects (what is a religion); philosophical aspects (what is the truth and whether it is possible to reach a truth in matters of religion); sociological aspects (the state of the presence of one or more religions in a society, growing or diminishing, with their specific spiritual and cultural background); political aspects (the relationship between nation states and different ethnic groups and religions, also according to the different conceptions of the relationship between religion and state).

In a properly theological context, the relationship of Christianity with other religions is currently the subject of the "theology of religions". Although it is born within the theological-fundamental sphere, today it tends to constitute itself as its own discipline. It is the task of Fundamental Theology to give reason for the singularity of Christ as mediator and fullness of Revelation in the interreligious panorama, while the "theology of religions" should direct its attention to the meaning and value of non-Christian religions in relation to God's plans and, therefore, also within the history of salvation. However, it is not possible to disregard a prior philosophical reflection that clarifies the relationship between religion and philosophy (both in the cosmological and anthropological spheres), between religion and natural law, between religion and human dignity. The theology of religions essentially involves two dimensions, though communicating with each other: one belonging to the Theology of Revelation and one to soteriology.

We will develop our considerations - which will necessarily remain limited and of an introductory nature - mainly around the first of the two previous dimensions. We will briefly address the following issues:

1. a concise overview of the originality of Christian Revelation in the panorama of the phenomenologies of revelation relating to extra-Biblical philosophical-religious traditions;

1. the main contents of the CTI document, *Christianity and Religions* (1996) and some possible methodological developments;

1. the doctrinal points summarized in the declaration of the CDF, *Dominus Iesus* (2000) as essential elements of the Christian faith;

1. the relationship between Christianity and *religion*, especially on the subject of inculturation of the faith.

For an in-depth study of the theology of religions, in its various aspects, see the Bibliography offered at the end of this volume.1

#### 6.1 The uniqueness of the Jewish-Christian Revelation and its characteristic features in relation to other religious traditions
##### 6.1.1 Some important elements of specificity
Before considering the points of reference of a possible "theology of religions", it is appropriate to summarize some characteristic traits of the Jewish-Christian Revelation, already encountered in chapter I of this volume. Although the category of "revelation" is not exclusive, as already seen, of the Christian religion, presenting links with religious phenomenology in general, it should be noted that, in the panorama of religions, Christian revelation is proposed with specific and original characteristics. Those who prefer not to speak of the *superiority of* the Christian phenomenon have objective foundations to speak of its *uniqueness*. Even where there are, as is logical to expect, linguistic, cosmological or anthropological similarities between Christianity and other spiritual-religious revelations or traditions, these similarities show significant differences. It is the latter that characterize, precisely, the specificity of Christianity. Let us see some of these elements of originality, some of which have already been encountered in previous sections.2

##### Revelation is word, event, history: it takes place in history and through history.
In the historical unfolding of Israel's religious experience, unlike in many other religious traditions, the revelatory phenomena of the divine and ways of accessing God's will are less and less connected to the sacredness of the cosmos and nature to become above all *word* and *history*. Words and history are intimately connected, a word that becomes history and a history that reveals itself because it is guided by the word. The link with the objectivity of history, it is true, does not prevent the divine word from revealing itself also in the interiority of the subject who receives it; however, it must be considered that exterior revelation and interior revelation are two dimensions of a single revelation and a single Word, the one which, in fullness, enters into Christ in history. It will be above all historical events, guided by divine omnipotence, that manifest the action and will of the God of Israel, as witnessed by the development and memory of a *history of salvation* (famous summary in *Dt* 4:9-40).

Consequently, the *conception of history* must be adapted to accommodate such a logic of revelation. History has a beginning and is directed towards a term; history is the bearer of meaning; the God of Israel is the Lord of history.

In contrast to Eastern philosophy, Greek thought and the Greek mysteries that give no place to history or only do so in some cases, the Christian faith is essentially confronted with "events" that are "successes". Scripture retraces facts, presents people, describes institutions. In other words, the God of Christian revelation is not only the God of the cosmos but the God of interventions, of unexpected incursions into human history; he is a God who comes, who intervenes, who acts and who saves.3

Consequently, there is also an original relationship between revelation and the cosmos: the majority of extra-Biblical religious revelations were given in the sacredness of nature, whose cyclical phenomenology, closed in its eternal return, allowed only a notion of history immersed in natural cycles and devoid of innovative meaning. Those manifestations of cosmic sacredness that persist in the Jewish-Christian Revelation are purified and used within an original logic, which is that of a salvific history: divine action breaks into the closed cycle of natural religiosity and uses it for its own ends with the aim of periodically updating the salvific event. In this perspective is placed, for example, the sacredness of the Sabbath, which proposes the memory of God's creative work. History thus manifests a *sacramental* dimension: it is capable of effectively revealing and anticipating what is not yet in fullness and what, in order to be fullness, must transcend the contingency of history.4

##### Revelation has a dynamic ranging from promise to fulfillment
Precisely because of its link with a linear and progressive conception of history, Jewish-Christian Revelation presents itself with a dynamism that goes from promise to fulfilment, from expectation to encounter, from prophetic announcement to event. The dialogical dynamic that is made possible between the First and the New Testament is due to the historical innervature that sustains the manifestation of God to mankind. A history of revelation made in this way emphasizes the commitment of God's fidelity, but it also requires the commitment of man's freedom.

Although it is certain that TA, understood in the light of the Gospel, acquires a new meaning, it in turn gives the NT a density and temporal thickness that it could not have alone. One could not understand NT without the ever-present discourse of OT. Without the hermeneutical key of the OT, some passages of the NT, such as the Easter supper, the chalice of the blood of the new and eternal covenant, would still remain in the half-light. Walking together with the disciples of Emmaus, Christ inaugurated a new era of exegesis: he is in person the exegete of the OT of which he is the outcome and completion.5

The dynamism present between promise and fulfilment allows the divine Word to present itself with its own self-testimony, that of the credibility of a God who accomplishes what He promises.

##### In Revelation God reveals Himself and communicates Himself to man and, in revealing Himself, He also reveals the meaning of man and the world.
Revelation is first of all God's offering and self-communication, not a principle of understanding the cosmos, nor a self-understanding of man.

In biblical revelation the *mystery* does not represent the horizon of God's hiding from man and the limit of human knowledge, but rather the very rich ambit of God's self-communication and his turning to man: the mystery ceases to be something taken away from man, as is frequent in the divine-human dynamic of revelatory phenomena associated with most religions, to become something offered to him.6

The content of this offer is not a "gnosis", a global knowledge assumed as a system, or simply a rule of life, but the personal life of God. The God of Israel reveals to His people His intimate life to the point of revealing, in the New Testament revelation, the content of the Trinitarian mystery. What God offers man is not only the dictates of his will, but the invitation to enter into communion with him. The request to accept this word involves how logical an ultra-rational sphere is, but the faith that God asks man to enter this sphere is not mere trust abandonment. The faith of Israel is challenged and confirmed by God's interventions in history and by God's fidelity to his promises.

Revelation is not a word around man, but around God. Its purpose is not to explain the world or man, but to explain to man who God is, and as a consequence to re-read and understand the truth of the world and man in the light of the Word of God. If one can speak of *Gnosis* about man and the world, one can do so only as an effect of what God reveals around Himself.

Jewish-Christian Revelation, therefore, is not born within the closed horizon of human creaturality, nor is it intended to stimulate man so that, reflecting on himself, he seeks within the perimeter of his own being the ultimate reasons for his existential choices; it is presented above all as a manifestation of *things new to* man; things which, in imposing themselves historically on his personal conscience, challenge him. Through creation and then through salvation history, Revelation asks man to place himself before God and before things because only in this way can he understand himself.7 Revelation is not an anthropology disguised as theology, but a true *Theology*, a discourse about God. In it man cannot interpret God, but must *let himself be interpreted by Him*.8

##### Christian Revelation is the economy of the Incarnate Word: it reaches its fullness in the mystery of God made man.
The content of the offering and the measure of the condescension of Revelation touch their apex in the Incarnation of the Word. In Jesus Christ we have "the revelation of the *mystery which has been* silent for everlasting centuries" (*Rom* 16:25; cf. *Col* 1:25-27). All that God reveals and offers to man is fully expressed in the person of the Word made flesh. In God made man, the distance between the human attempt to know what belongs to the sphere of the divine and the eminence of the content of the mystery is radically and definitively filled. The Word of God is offered in human words. Christ is the *universal concretum*. The person of Jesus Christ proposes himself as the *content and form of Revelation*. Christ is both the revealing mystery and the revealed mystery, the mediator and the fullness of Revelation. He not only brings and reveals the salvation of God, but is in his person the norm and the content of this salvation.

The incarnation of the Word characterizes an entire economy of revelation because it ensures that the divine word can be delivered through human words. The true humanity of the Word assures that all the dimensions of human expression can be taken up by the divine word and used to reveal the mystery of God. It still belongs to this economy that what God wants to reveal is realized through a personalist logic, and therefore through a life practice, a filial condition, an existential situation.

##### Revelation is unheard of and superabundant: therefore, it is itself that must offer the hermeneutical principle of its own interpretation
Revelation offers much more than man seeks and, above all, with categories that often exceed human logic. It is the revelation of God's *jealousy* for his creatures, the offering of a covenant that places God and man on the same level, the divine commitment to repair man's sin in the first person, the scandal of the cross, the paradox of losing one's life in order to find it again, the logic of an ultra-worldly kingdom where to reign is to serve.

In order to communicate such an eminent content, Revelation must also provide the fundamental hermeneutical criterion to be interpreted: this criterion is *God's historical-salvific action,* moved by the sole freedom of love and to which man can correspond only in freedom and in the gift of self. God's revelatory action, therefore, cannot be predicted or commensurate with human categories, because what God reveals exceeds what natural thought seeks.

At the same time, reason, although outdated, is not humiliated. On the contrary, the task proper to reason is to ensure that knowledge/expectations of a metaphysical, cosmological, anthropological order, which allow us to recognize excess and scandal as such, to provide in essence that *dóxa* on the basis of which the paradox that surpasses it can be recognized as *para-dóxa.*9

Biblical Revelation is presented with the character of a *condescension* (gr. συγκατάβασις) of God towards man (cf. DV, 13), an authentic *gift of grace* which precedes any ascending religious initiative of seeking God, but also surpasses any descending offering known from any other non-Biblical revelation. Precisely because it was the God of Israel who wanted to reveal himself and make himself known to his people, Paul will be able to proclaim in Athens what other religions worship without knowing.

##### 6.1.2 Uniqueness and originality of Christ's mediation in an interreligious context
A second aspect that deserves to be considered as a premise for the relationship between Christianity and religions concerns the *singularity* of the mediation of Jesus Christ. The way in which this mediation is manifested places Christianity in a condition of absolute originality with respect to other religions.

In religious phenomenology in general the mediation between the human and the divine is destined to dissolve once the mediator has fulfilled his mission. The purpose of the proposed mediation is not to bind anyone, but rather to provide a depersonalized solution to the enigmas of man and his anxieties for salvation. The recipient of the divine message that benefits from the help provided by mediation is not called to conform to the mediator, but only, if necessary, to imitate him. He learns from the mediator a series of rules to be fulfilled, not excluding that of the annulment of one's own personality in an anonymous cosmic law, in order to achieve one's happiness through them. A classic example is the case of Buddhism:

Ultimately, he [Buddha] only says what basically everyone could say. He shows a path which, even without him, subsists with the value of a cosmic law. The person of the Buddha himself is not an essential part of what is properly religious; he is extinguished [...]. In truth, there is no persistent identity. The appearance of persistent reality, if man wants to attain liberation, must be removed layer by layer, while he, stretched out in aspiration, says to each of them: "This is not it" - and not to grasp at the end one last essential core, but to recognize that in principle nothing is there, and thus *dissolve.*10

In Christianity there are certainly mediations that are destined to disappear with time, as happens with the prophetic mission, or with the succession of the apostles' ministry. All these mediations, however, are aimed at the only mediation that remains, that of Christ, and all of them are aimed at perpetuating its relevance. Neither the prophet nor the apostle have anything of their own to proclaim: the Christian is not Paul or Apollo's, because only Christ was crucified for him and only in his name was he baptized (cf. *1 Cor* 1:11-13). Only Christ *is the* Way, Truth and Life (cf. *Jn* 14:6): he does not show the way, he does not lead to the truth, he does not give life, but *he is in himself* all this, as the source and cause of salvation. Christ's mediation reveals and saves by bonding to Himself is a mediation inseparable from the person of Jesus himself:

Since Christ is both the revealed mystery and the revealed mystery, the mediator and the fullness of revelation, it follows that he occupies in the Christian faith an absolutely unique position that distinguishes Christianity from all religions, including Judaism. Christianity is the only religion whose revelation is embodied in a person who presents himself as the living and absolute truth. Other religions have founders, but none of them (Buddha, Confucius, Zoroaster, Muhammad) has proposed itself as the object of the faith of its disciples. [...] Jesus makes gestures, proclaims a message, introduces into the world a quality of life and love never seen, never imagined, never lived and raises the problem of his real identity.11

It can therefore be affirmed that Christian Revelation, as we have already highlighted in chapter III of this volume when speaking of faith, is a *form* from which the *content* cannot be separated. Jesus Christ is constantly the category that determines the being, the acting and the theory of all that is Christian. With Romano Guardini's expression, the person of Jesus is "the essence of Christianity". One understands why the relationship between Christ and the Christian is not limited to an intellectual sphere, but implies a true *incorporation*. The Eucharist shows us paradigmatically in which relationship the believer must be in Christ: not *in front of* him, but *in* him.12 This *in-existence*, witnessed by the insistence of all Pauline theology to think and act *in Christo Iesu*, is realized through faith and the sacraments, lived as existential-mystical facts. The *sacrament* thus becomes the way in which the Christian belongs to the *mystery* of Christ (cf. CCC 1115).

#### 6.2 The CTI document, *Christianity and Religions* (1996)
The study of the relationship between Christianity and religions is immediately confronted with faith in the Incarnation of the Word, the affirmation of which is a determining factor in any possible subsequent theological development. The CTI document, *Christianity and Religions* (1996), which is still today, among the reflections promoted by bodies of the Holy See, the most organic document on the subject, immediately highlights *the central question to be resolved*, in all its radicality:

The greatest difficulty of Christianity has always focused on the "incarnation of God", which gives the person and action of Jesus Christ the characteristics of uniqueness and universality with regard to the salvation of humanity. But how can a particular and historical event have a universal claim? How can an interreligious dialogue be initiated, respecting all religions and without considering them as imperfect and inferior at the outset, if we recognize in Jesus Christ, and in him alone, the one and only Savior of humanity? Could one not conceive the person and the saving action of God from other mediators besides Jesus Christ?

[…]

The renunciation of this claim is considered essential if dialogue is to be fruitful: this is undoubtedly the most important point we have to deal with (nos. 18 and 93).

##### 6.2.1 The paradigm of exclusivist, inclusive and pluralist models: limits and possibilities
In order to set the problem in its more general aspects, much of the theological reflection, and with it the cited document, uses a tripartite reference, commonly referred to as the exclusivist model, inclusive model, and pluralist model.13 These three models would correspond to three perspectives: ecclesiocentric-cristocentric-theocentric. This is a useful conceptual division, but it has some limitations. The three perspectives, in fact, can be seen in a dialectical and self-exclusive way, therefore unable to grasp relations of a "transversal" nature. Moreover, if the first triad indicates a relationship between Christianity and other religions, the second indicates three different ways of reading within Christianity itself. Among the elements of each triad, therefore, it would not be possible to establish a two-way correspondence. For example, a model could not be simultaneously exclusivist and pluralist, while it could be both Christocentric and ecclesiocentric, according to the vision of the Church that assumes it.

The reflection of the document states at the beginning that an ecclesiocentric-exclusive model will not be developed, because it would set the relationship with other religions by denying the possibility of salvation and truth outside the Church. This would be tantamount to interpreting in a literal and reductive way the formulation *extra Ecclesiam nulla salus*, an expression that deserves some clarification.14 Its origin dates back to the first centuries and in the patristic sphere it indicated a warning addressed to those faithful who, after receiving baptism, strayed from Christian doctrine and practice, especially during periods of persecution. They were reminded that outside the Church in which they were born to the life of grace as Christians, there was no salvation for them. A reductive and absolute interpretation of this statement would not correspond to the dogmatic development of the Church-Salvation relationship, nor to a correct self-understanding of the mystery of the Church, People of God, Body of Christ, Temple of the Spirit, which certainly extends beyond its visible boundaries. It is enough to recall in this regard the doctrine of Vatican II collected by *Lumen Gentium* on the presence of salvation just beyond the visible boundaries of the Church:

The first of the three models indicated, called "ecclesiocentric-exclusive", is therefore excluded from the document; and this not so much because of a theological reasoning, but as an effect inherent in its own terminological premises: those of a reductive vision of the mystery of the Church.

The second model, called "Christocentric-Inclusivist", instead strives to understand non-Christian religions, leaving unchanged the Christological confession in the divinity of the Word incarnate as fullness and mediator of all Revelation, accomplished in the historical event of Jesus of Nazareth. The task of a theology that assumes such a model is to explain, after an operation of discernment, how other religions can be "included" in the mystery of Christ, that of his capitality on creation and redemption. This model, today generally shared by the great majority of Catholic theology, is not in itself a complete solution to the question previously formulated in all its radicality - how, that is, to maintain Christocentrism in an inter-religious context - but indicates a perspective, a path to follow, a methodological framework in which to seek an answer or suggest a line of reflection and deepening.

The third model, the "pluralist" one, would instead already represent, in a certain sense, a first response: the centrality of Jesus Christ should be abandoned in order to allow the dialogue between the various religions of the world to proceed in a theocentric perspective, on paths that are in part new. The event of Jesus of Nazareth could still be "normative", attributing to him no longer the exclusivity and fullness of revelatory and salvific mediation, but only a privileged place, that of the highest mediation among all those historically possible, or that of a subject with the closest relationship with God among all those unstable. But the event of Jesus could also be considered "non-regulatory", thus allowing the theology of religions to open itself to the possibility of revelations or mediations that are higher than that of Jesus of Nazareth, or radically different from it. These are perspectives that find correspondence in a part of evangelical theology (e.g. Ernst Troeltsch and Paul Tillich for normative pluralism, John Hick for non-regular pluralism).

The core of the pluralist proposal therefore lies in a sort of *Copernican revolution* in the religious sphere: every confession should cease to revolve around its founder-mediator - Christianity around Christ - and begin to revolve around the "sun" of a new theocentrism capable of integrating the demands of different religious traditions. It should be pointed out that this proposal is subject to a certain precomprehension of the theme of truth, as well as of the relationship between dialogue and the proclamation of the faith. In a gnoseology no longer linked to a metaphysical framework, as one hopes, affirmations of an irreformable character would no longer be possible even in the religious sphere; since no one is the repository of a truth of which one cannot be certain, the power of proclamation would fall and the most logical path would be to seek together a truth that no one yet possesses.

The theme of the uniqueness or singularity of Christ, the fullness of Revelation, in the context of the relationship between the different religions is therefore structured as a *problem of comparison between the inclusive and pluralist models*. Even with all the hermeneutical and anthropological contextualizations of the case, theology must operate this comparison always in the light of the biblical datum, on pain of losing the "theological" status of the discussion. The task of a theology of religions will then be to verify the conformity with the revealed datum for each of the suggested solutions and to show the possible inconsistency of some positions. We observe incidentally that if a theological discussion focused on the relationship between the two inclusive and pluralist models (i.e. leaving aside the ecclesiocentric model) can be considered more fruitful, it pays the price of having conditioned theological language to subscribe to the non-relevance of a reflection on the nature of the Church, renouncing to bring into play, or at least leaving more in the shadows, its relations with Christ and therefore with God.

##### 6.2.2 Theological inadequacy of the pluralist perspective
The pluralist-theocentric model advances a series of argumentative paths to defend its position; almost all of them operate a **process of "reduction" of the role of Jesus Christ in order to overcome the Christocentric position**, considered prejudicial for the purposes of interreligious dialogue. Let us briefly summarize some of these paths, together with some critical remarks.15

A first group of considerations concerns the hermeneutical field. The *universality* or *absoluteness* of Jesus Christ should be drastically reduced because they are dependent on the **emphasis of religious language (hyperbolic, apocalyptic, messianic, etc.) precisely of the Jewish culture in which the authors of the NT lived**; or because they are caused by an understandable s**trengthening of the announcement made by the first believing community in order to ensure a greater impact of the new saving content associated with their preaching**. The use of paradigms proper to Greek philosophy, first of all the one referring to the role of the Platonic and Neoplatonic Logos, would remain decisive for the amplification of Christological attributes.

A second group of arguments operates a series of *separations* between what could be preached about Jesus in a "historical-concrete" way and what belongs instead to a "ideal-universal" language. In fact, these dimensions - concrete and universal - would no longer exist simultaneously in the same subject: an erroneous composition of them is precisely what would have led to an incorrect Christocentric perspective, from which it would be necessary today to detach oneself in favour of a pluralist model. In this way a new separation between the Jesus of history and the Christ of faith, or between Jesus Christ and the Logos-Verbo of God acts: if the first pair of terms refers to a concrete, the second refers to a universal, which - this is the central point - one cannot see why it should manifest itself or recognize itself totally in that concrete. The cosmic Christ, like the Logos, could have other ways of revealing God or of revealing himself, of incarnating himself, of mediating, of saving, even different from the one historically given in Jesus of Nazareth, who, evidently, would not exhaust the scope of these prerogatives.

A variant of these "Christological reductions" would be to make a drastic separation between the Spirit of God and the Christ-Word incarnate. The first, ineffable, unpredictable, universal in its manifestations, would have a revelatory and salvific priority. Since it is the Spirit who has determined the incarnation of the Word and guided the mission of Jesus of Nazareth, in the same way He could guide and/or have already guided other important salvific manifestations of God. The interpretation is susceptible of a formulation in a historical key: in an eschatological perspective it would mark a passage from the economy of the Word, more historical-concrete, to that of the Spirit, decidedly more universal, unifying and totalizing.

Although some of the above arguments may have some points of support in individual biblical passages, taken as a whole the premises that are intended to justify the adoption of a pluralist position are quite far removed from the essential contents of the Christian faith. As pointed out in the CTI document cited above, we are faced with a historical, epistemological and theological simplification that drastically changes the understanding of Christianity and is incompatible with Revelation if read within Tradition and together with the Magisterium of the Church.16

The apostolic kerygma presents itself from the beginning as a unity of the universal salvific dimension of Christ and of the historical event of Jesus. We are not faced with an undue overlapping of two different concepts: "the object of faith is neither a Christ without Jesus, nor a Jesus without Christ; but precisely Jesus-the-Christ, that is, the historical Jesus, "constituted Christ" by God in his resurrection".17 While in language this announcement may be indebted to the narrative forms of Judaism or to certain elements of Greek culture, in its substance it presents itself as a novelty that upsets both Jewish messianic expectations and the canons of Greek thought. Twenty years after the resurrection of Jesus, Paul bears surprisingly explicit witness to this (cf. *1 Cor* 1:22-25; *Acts* 16:32-33). The apostles perceive the unprecedented weight of the word entrusted to them: if salvation could have been an expectation of their interlocutors, the way in which it has now been given is totally new. "The Gospel message which animates the Apostles of Christ appears as the fulfilment of what peoples, in a fragmentary and obscure way, already possess. That this fullness of light emerges as a historical event - Christ's death and resurrection - is precisely the difficulty and torment of preaching".18 The universality of redemption is not the result of a categorical association, but is proclaimed as a fact, the "fact" of Jesus' passion, death and resurrection.

A separation of Jesus Christ from the Word or Spirit would then lead to denying specific aspects of Trinitarian and Christological dogmas, solidly based on the biblical datum, emptying our understanding of the economy of salvation. If it is true that the uncreated Person of the Word exists *before* the historical event of Jesus of Nazareth, it is equally true that the person of Jesus *is* the person of the Word: the divine Word does not take possession of a body as a transitory thing, remaining open to multiple possible unions, but is *incarnated*. Other possible manifestations of the Word in history or in the space-time dimensions of creation cannot be detached from Jesus Christ, who is the *personal way in* which the Word entered history and creation. And it is in the precise humanity assumed by the Word, the humanity generated by the Virgin Mary, that Jesus Christ suffered and suffered for our redemption. The uniqueness of the Incarnation, against the possibility of multiple incarnations, also depends on the *radicality of* what the divine Trinitarian plan has disposed for the humanity of Jesus of Nazareth, that is, his sacrifice to the point of the outpouring of blood. The theme is certainly susceptible of deepening, but in the light of the revealed datum the Logos destined to incarnate and the Logos incarnate are the same reality: to deny it would lead to obscure the meaning of the personal being, not only in the created order, but within the Trinitarian life.

Even the Son and the Holy Spirit cannot be the protagonists of two parallel economies in the history of salvation. If the Spirit, like the Logos, precedes in their actuality the historical event of Jesus and, after all, creation itself, their action cannot be separated from that fullness of God's self-communication to man and creation which is, precisely, the Incarnation. The traits with which the mission of the Spirit is described by Scripture *in relation to the mission of the Son* are too strong to be misunderstood. The full outpouring of the Spirit comes after Easter, as the fruit of the Cross; Jesus Christ sends *his* Spirit with the Father. "The Holy Spirit directs all men towards Christ, the Anointed One; Christ, in turn, directs them towards the Father. No one goes to the Father except through Jesus, for he is the way (*Jn* 14:6); but it is the Holy Spirit who guides the disciples to the whole truth (*Jn* 16:12-13). The word "will guide" (ὁδηγήσει) includes the "way" (ὁδὸς) so the Spirit leads the way that is Jesus, who leads to the Father".19 Every pneumatic Christology cannot be separated from a Christology of the Incarnate Word.20

Finally, a philosophical critique must be made of the pluralist position, and on the same ground that it seeks to promote, that of ecumenical and interreligious dialogue. For the purposes of dialogue, this position considers it fundamental to relativize the demand for truth proper to every religion. Each party is asked to renounce a certain number of theologically acquired affirmations and to modify its own method in order to better accommodate the demands made by the other interlocutors. Here lies the sense of "courage" mentioned above, that of carrying out a real "Copernican revolution". In reality, it is precisely this relativization that compromises the dialogue. In fact, if in order to dialogue the claim of truth of each interlocutor is annulled, then the dialogue ceases, because no one has anything to say anymore.21 On the contrary, true dialogue must be conducted while preserving its own specificity and taking the truth of the other seriously. This certainly requires a greater speculative effort and greater righteousness of conscience before oneself, before others, and ultimately before that God whom one seeks to know and serve. Only with these provisions can a true interreligious dialogue be promoted, based on love of the truth and not on the rules of consensus or the marketplace.

##### 6.2.3 Understanding the relationship between Christianity and religions in the light of a Christocentric-inclusive theology
What, then, are the paths of inter-religious dialogue that should be explored within a Christocentric-inclusive model?

One of them is undoubtedly the "theology of the seeds of the Word". What participates in the truth and worship of the true God, even if outside the visible boundaries of the Church, belongs to *the Word*, to the mystery of His relationship with creation.

##### Theology of the seeds of the Word
The presence of the reflections or seeds of the divine Word in non-Christian religions, philosophy and culture, as sincere expressions of the search for truth, was brought to light from the origins of Christianity and boasts a good patristic tradition. The metaphor was originally applied to the relationship between philosophy and Christianity, but was later used naturally in the relationship with religions and cultures. Exposing his doctrine on the divine Word, Saint Justin directed himself in the second century to the Greco-Roman world, affirming that "what good has been said by all belongs to us Christians".22 Similarly, Clement of Alexandria considered philosophy a preparation for the Gospel, just as the OT was for the Jews a preparation for the coming of Christ. Irenaeus spoke of a Logos that accompanied the history of men by preparing for the Incarnation.

The Second Vatican Council made this perspective its own: in the Constitution *Lumen Gentium* it is affirmed that the Church recognizes that "outside her body there are many elements of sanctification and truth which, as gifts proper to the Church of Christ, lead to Catholic unity" (n. 8). The Council's declaration *Ad Gentes* exhorts those who work in mission lands to recognize these "germs of the Word" and a well-known passage of *Our Aetate* states that "the Catholic Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in these religions" (NA, 2).

*[Embedded Image]*
 Texts of *Ad Gentes*, n. 11 and *Nostra Aetate*, n. 2.

In the encyclical *Redemptoris missio*, John Paul II emphasizes the "Christocentric" character of these seeds of truth and goodness: "the gifts and spiritual riches which God has bestowed on every people cannot be separated from Jesus Christ, who is at the center of the divine plan of salvation" (n. 6). In fact, "how much the Spirit works in the hearts of men and women and in the history of peoples, in cultures and religions, assumes a role of evangelical preparation and cannot fail to refer to Christ, the Word made flesh through the action of the Spirit, in order to work him, the perfect man, the salvation of all and the universal recapitulation" (n. 29). On the historical-salvific level, this implies that every man who achieves salvation, whether he is saved, knows it or not, through the grace and merits of Christ. A "theology of the seeds of the Word" must therefore seek to make explicit their Christocentric character, as this appears in Revelation, not fearing to support it in a comparative theology of the history of religions.

Already in the Apostolic Exhortation *Evangelii Nuntiandi* (6.12.1975, n. 53), Paul VI had explicitly listed some of the positive values of non-Christian religions, while at the same time specifying that their "positivity" depends on the sincerity of the personal search for God. In addition to the value of containing, precisely, "seeds of the Word", Paul VI listed among the positive elements of the religions of humanity:

- expressing the life of the spirit of millions of people and embodying their search for God;
- the ability to educate to reflection and move to prayer;
- the fact that it constitutes an authentic preparation for the Gospel.

Equally clear, in this regard, are the guidelines of the Magisterium concerning the fact that the presence of the "seeds of the Word" in non-Christian religions does not diminish the responsibility for evangelization, nor does it impede the Church's missionary activity; an activity which finds its meaning precisely in the fact that the seeds are called to mature in fullness, and that this fullness is certainly not attainable independently of the word of Christ, since these, precisely, are seeds which *belong to him*.

The underlying optimism that permeates the Christian faith about the possibility of finding seeds of the Word in every search for the truth, which has in the authenticity of the religious sense its existentially binding expression, maintains a close relationship with what in chapter II of this volume we have called "Revelation through the creative Word". Precisely because the creative word and the prophetic word are pronounced by the same Word in the same Spirit, human nature always and everywhere maintains the capacity to access the truth about God even starting from the consideration of its own creaturality and the creaturality of the world. Therefore, the dialogue which Christians wish to establish with non-believers will always be marked by an awareness of this *latent metaphysics, within the* reach of every man who knows how to reflect on the world and on his own soul free from ideological conditioning. Anchored in the realism of being and the universality of its access, this dialogue can never take on the characteristics of intolerance and ideology.

##### Convergence between Christology and Anthropology
A second track along which to develop a Christocentric and inclusive model is that which highlights the "convergence between Christology and Anthropology". It is pursued by highlighting how the earthly event of Jesus Christ, crucified and risen, contains in itself a significant appeal for every man. The mystery of life and death, the suffering of the just innocent, the law of charity and the forgiveness of enemies, praise of the common Father and care for all his creatures, especially the weakest and most defenceless, are religious and anthropological elements which gather, in Christ, the aspirations of every true humanism, expressed or represented in the various religions. Regardless of differences in race, religion or culture, everyone can recognize in the history of Jesus Christ their own history, participating through work, love for others, suffering and death in "His mystery", that paschal mystery with which the Holy Spirit gives everyone the opportunity to come into contact.23

No human person can feel estranged from this proclamation, because Christianity declares that "through the Incarnation the Son of God has united himself in a certain way with every man".24 Christ, therefore, "includes" the whole of humanity in his mystery.25 He is not only *the way to go to the Father* (cf. *Jn* 14:5-6), but also *the way to go to every man.*26 Thanks to this fundamental correspondence between the divine plan for man and Jesus Christ, "the Church can look at every man with the eyes of Christ".27 and is aware that on the way to him it can encounter every people and every culture, because "whoever follows Christ, the perfect man, makes himself more of a man".28 Starting from the person and doctrine of Jesus of Nazareth, every man is then able to discover, as if against the light, what belongs to the dignity of the human person and what humbles him, what contributes to his fullness and what corrupts him.

The path of convergence between Christ and man, with the inclusion of every man in the logic of his salvific mystery, is connected with the "theology of the image of God in man", making explicit its Christological value. Also in this case it is a reflection of broad biblical-patristic tradition. If every man was created in Christ and every man is destined to conform to his image, it is reasonable to think that every healthy yearning for truth about man, for fullness or fulfilment, as present in the various religions, has an implicit link with the truth of Jesus Christ, in whose divine sonship we are all called to participate.

##### Inclusivism as a methodological program of "re-reading" the content of a religion
The Christocentric-inclusivist model, the only one reasonably practicable by a theology respectful of the revealed datum, contains within it an implicit methodological program: Christian theology should try to "read" other religious traditions *within* its own salvation history. The verb "to include" does not only mean to bring back within one's own boundary, nor, more simply, only to contain, as one contains an object that can be congealed until it is absorbed or transformed. Its deeper meaning goes beyond this. When we use the verb "to understand", we remember that what is *included* must also be intellectually and historically *understood*. This implies recognizing a role to everything that is included, without penalizing its identity: on the binomial "include-comprehend" Christocentric theology plays its decisive game.

Thus understood, the "criterion of inclusiveness", as a criterion and methodological path, would then become an indication of truthfulness: the religious tradition that has the capacity to include, that is, to understand in the sense of "reading better" the others, participates in the only truth in a greater way. Christianity, being the revealed religion that claims to be in precisely these conditions, must then take up the challenge of knowing how to make it explicit from the theological point of view. When we think about it, salvation history itself provides us with an emblematic example of how such a "criterion of inclusiveness" would act when it presents us with the reading of the First Testament in the light of the New, thus allowing us to *understand it* without penalizing it. The dynamic promise of fulfilment does not take anything away from the First Covenant, because if it were to lose its identity, the specificity of the New would inevitably be lost.

It is significant to note in this regard that a criterion of inclusiveness could act, precisely as an attempt to "understand", even moving from Judaism towards Christianity, giving rise to very interesting consequences. We find such an attempt in the episode narrated by Luke in chapter 5 of *Acts* after the arrest of Peter and the other apostles. Through the words of Gamaliel (cf. *Acts* 5:35-39), Judaism tries to "read" the movement that arose around Jesus of Nazareth within that phenomenon of messianism with its religious-political color, frequent starting from the Hellenic domination and then especially during the Roman domination. But Gamaliel's discourse, and with him the Jewish reading of Christianity, in order to find a foundation and an internal coherence within its own religious tradition, appeals to history and asks it to verify what is happening: "don't take care of these men and let them go. For if this plan or this work were of human origin, it would be destroyed; but if it comes from God, you will not succeed in destroying them". (*Acts* 5:38-39). Although this judgment is transmitted to us through Luke's narrative, it interprets a reasonable position of Judaism contemporary to Jesus. The relevant point for us is that when Judaism intended to employ a criterion of inclusiveness in relation to Christianity, it would have to reckon with history and thus, implicitly, open itself *to the possibility of the truth of* Christianity if it wanted to be fully consistent with itself.

Would a similar route to Islam be possible? While the relationship between Judaism and Christianity is absolutely unique in the panorama of inter-religious dialogue, it can be said that, although an inclusive reading of Islam by Christianity is not something immediate, there is certainly an attempt by Islam to read the Jewish-Christian tradition within a perspective that would have its fulfillment in Islam. In fact, the Islamic religion reaffirms the common origin from Abraham and attributes a particular role - of an intermediate and provisional nature with respect to Mohammed - to Jesus of Nazareth and also to his mother Mary. Now, it is this self-comprehension of Islam that should be conducted to the end, to the point of drawing consequences for its confrontation with history. To do so, Islam should clarify *from what historical sources* it deduces the hermeneutical ordinariness of Jesus of Nazareth to the prophet Muhammad. But to support this thesis it could no longer "include" Christianity, having to deny it instead, because a reading of Jesus Christ as a preparation of the Prophet would no longer be consistent with Christianity itself. For its part, Christianity would have to give reason for how Islam may have originated, deciding whether or not to continue reading it as a Christological *heresy* (with John Damascene or later with Thomas Aquinas), and what inclusive capacity such a reading would have. Theology would have here a task of its own, probably different from that of inter-religious dialogue at the political or socio-cultural level, because it would be called to clarify the internal coherence of the reciprocal statements and to discern what convergences, if any, exist regarding the image of God and his attributes.29

The inclusive model actually contains a deeper meaning than one might at first glance think. It reaffirms that the path to follow in theological research is certainly that of maintaining the uniqueness and universality of Christ's mediation, but the *ways* of following it are suggested, in history, by the Spirit. More generally, the relationship between Christianity and religions should be able to grasp ecclesiocentric, Christocentric or theocentric *Christian* perspectives as communicating and not independent. Precisely the dynamism of this articulated reference, from the Church to Christ in the Spirit to the Father, can help to better recognize the richness of the role of the Spirit, the only one capable of scrutinizing the depths of God and his secrets (cf. *1 Cor* 2:10-11), the only one who knows the paths of humanity, of all humanity, on the paths leading to Christ.

#### 6.3 The doctrinal reference points of the CDF document, *Dominus Iesus* (2000)
The Declaration of the CDF *Dominus Iesus* (6.8.2000) is not intended to develop a specific "theology of religions", but rather to emphasize certain statements of dogmatic and disciplinary content, relying on the corresponding authority of the various documents of the Catholic Magisterium which it mentions and recalls by placing them in the contemporary context. These are, first of all, the Christological declarations of the early centuries, the documents of the Second Vatican Council and the encyclical of John Paul II, *Redemptoris missio* (1990). For ecclesiological aspects, the encyclical *Ut unum sint* (1995) is also cited, and for philosophical aspects the encyclical *Fides et ratio* (1998). The CTI document, *Christianity and Religions* (1996), is not explicitly mentioned, but the main theological arguments developed there can be recognized in the text of *Dominus Iesus,* which are used here in support of the doctrinal statements set out. The tripartite division of the exclusivist, inclusive and pluralist models of interreligious dialogue is taken from that document as a scheme of the same exposition.

At the beginning the document presents in a concise but dense summary (cf. n. 4) what are the main truths of the Catholic faith which, in the contemporary climate of gnoseological and religious relativism, would today be considered as outdated. They are, schematically:

- the definitive and complete character of the revelation of Jesus Christ
- the theological nature of the Christian faith, when compared to the beliefs of other religions
- the inspired character of the books of Holy Scripture
- the personal unity between the eternal Word and Jesus of Nazareth
- the unity of the economy of the Incarnate Word and the Holy Spirit
- the uniqueness and salvific universality of the mystery of Jesus Christ
- the universal saving mediation of the Church
- the inseparability, though in distinction, between the Kingdom of God, the Kingdom of Christ and the Church
- the subsistence in the Catholic Church of the one Church of Jesus Christ.

In confirmation of these fixed points, which today have become the subject of discussion or open denial, the main documents of the Magisterium that declare them in an authoritative manner are recalled. Given the doctrinal nature (conceived also as a basis for possible disciplinary measures) of the document, the declaratory aspect prevails over the theological-argumentary one.

With regard to the *definitiveness of Revelation in Christ*, the document recalls that in Jesus Christ there is the fullness of God's revelation. This revelation is definitive and does not allow new economies to overcome it. This "definitiveness" is based on the divinity of the subject, the person of the Incarnate Word. It is due to this divinity that the Paschal Mystery represents the apex and fulfillment of Revelation and salvation.

Referring then to *Fides et ratio*, the document wishes to clarify *the specificity of the theological faith*, whose personalist dimension is lately based on *fides per Christum* and *in Christum*. The latter should be distinguished, according to the document, from religious experience or religious faith generally understood, for which the notion of "belief" is reserved: "If faith is the acceptance in the grace of revealed truth, "which allows one to enter into the mystery, favoring its coherent intelligence" (*Fides et ratio,* 13) belief in other religions is that set of experience and thought, which constitute the human treasures of wisdom and religiosity, that man in his search for truth has conceived and implemented in his reference to the Divine and the Absolute (cf. *ibid.* 30-31)" (n. 7). Against the background of the considerations developed by the CTI study, *Christianity and religions*, the declaration *Dominus Iesus* affirms the *inseparability between the saving economy of the Son*, the *Incarnate Word, and that of the Holy Spirit* (cf. nos. 9-12). Although the Holy Spirit has his own ways to lead people and individuals to salvation, ways which to a large extent we do not know and probably never will, what we certainly know is that these ways lead to Christ and not to other mediators, nor can they develop according to salvific economies other than those realized in the Paschal Mystery of Jesus Christ, dead and risen. "It is not compatible with the doctrine of the Church - it states - the theory that attributes a salvific activity to the Logos as such in his divinity, which would be exercised 'beyond' and 'beyond' the humanity of Christ, even after the Incarnation" (n. 10). Likewise, to those who propose the hypothesis of an economy of the Holy Spirit with a more universal character than that of the Word incarnate, crucified and risen, the declaration replies that "this affirmation is also contrary to the Catholic faith, which, instead, considers the salvific incarnation of the Word as a Trinitarian event" (n. 12).

In essence, the action of the Holy Spirit cannot be understood "outside" or "beside" that of Christ, but both belong to the one and triune saving action of God. The mission of the Spirit has the task of actualizing in history, extending it in time, the redemption accomplished by Jesus Christ, the Incarnate Word.

It is also necessary to affirm, continues the CDF document, *the uniqueness and singularity of Christ's mediation*. By virtue of his divine person, he cannot be considered a mediator among others (cf. nn. 13-15). If other possible mediations lead to the true God, then they participate in Christ's unique mediation, which in Him is given in fullness: "It must therefore be *firmly believed* as the truth of the Catholic faith that the universal salvific will of the One and Triune God is offered and fulfilled once and for all in the mystery of the Incarnation, death and resurrection of the Son of God" (n. 14).

In agreement with *Redemptoris missio*, n. 5 and *Lumen gentium,* n. 62, the role of other mediators or the existence of cooperation in the grace of Christ is not excluded, provided that they are mediations (whose action should be studied on a theological basis) that participate in the one mediation of Christ and only from the latter take meaning and content.

It still belongs to the Catholic faith to uphold *the inseparability between Christ and the Church, between the Kingdom of God and the Church founded by him*: "Just as the head and members of a living body are inseparable even though they do not identify themselves, Christ and the Church cannot be confused but neither can they be separated, and they constitute a single "total Christ"". (n. 16). This doctrine has a precise impact in the ecclesiological field, even before in the field of interreligious dialogue. Quoting *Mysterium Ecclesiae* (cf. n. 1), the document of the CDF states: "The faithful cannot, therefore, imagine the Church of Christ as the sum - differentiated and in some way unitary together - of the Churches and Ecclesial Communities; nor can they think that the Church of Christ today no longer exists in any place and that, therefore, it should only be the object of research by all the Churches and communities" (n. 16).

It follows that the universal and eschatological aspects attributed by Revelation to the Kingdom of God are already present in nuce in the Church. She is on earth the seed of the Kingdom, she possesses the means of salvation, the logic of universality and sign for the whole of humanity. If, on the one hand, it is stated that "the inseparable relationship between Church and Kingdom does not mean forgetting that the Kingdom of God, even if considered in its historical phase, does not identify with the Church in its visible and social reality" (n. 19), because working for the Kingdom means recognizing the divine dynamism that permeates human history and transforms it; on the other hand, it is reiterated that "it would be contrary to the Catholic faith to consider the Church as a way of salvation alongside those constituted by other religions, which would be complementary to the Church, indeed substantially equivalent to it, even if they converge with it towards the eschatological Kingdom of God" (n. 21).

Articulate is the exposition of the doctrine relating to *the necessity of the Church for salvation*.

First of all, the CDF document recalls that this need arises from the joint consideration of the uniqueness and universality of Christ's mediation, as well as the close relationship between Christ and the Church. The latter participates in the mystery of Jesus Christ**: its function is to apply its fruits, not to limit or preclude them**. For this reason, *Lumen Gentium* (cf. n. 16) had already recalled, not without biblical foundation and long theological tradition, that those who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ can attain the salvation prepared by the one true God.

Secondly, it is reaffirmed that the preaching and sanctification of the Church are necessary because the existence of the seeds of the Word even outside the visible boundaries of the Catholic Church, as already observed by *Evangelii nuntiandi* and *Redemptoris missio*, does not frustrate the Church's mission as desired by Jesus Christ (cf. n. 20). **The seeds of revelation and salvation which can exist beyond the visible boundaries of the Catholic Church, if they are seeds of *the Word*, then they belong to the Church and it is through her ministry that they must be led to full maturity**. **For the Church, to preach Christ is a duty and, for the people, to receive the proclamation of the Paschal Mystery is a right**. Every sincere search for the truth leads to salvation, but the Church must be aware that she has received in entrustment the truth given to her by Jesus Christ: "Those who obey the motion of the Spirit of truth are already on the path of salvation; but the Church, to whom this truth has been entrusted, must meet their desire by offering it to them" (No. 22; cf. also CCC 851).

#### 6.4 Philosophical and theological insights into the nature of religion in relation to the Christian event
The study of the relationship between Christianity and religions cannot be limited to a focus on the specificity of Christianity and a precise recording of what practices, theological or disciplinary, should be adopted in interreligious dialogue. Following what the Fathers of the Church did as a propaedeutic to evangelization, it is also necessary to deal with the gnoseological and foundational junctions, which need the rational contribution of *philosophy and the* existential contribution of *religion*. In essence, we need a philosophy of the "authenticity of the religious sense", which is at the same time a "philosophy of love for the truth". This must be followed by a "theology *of religion*", capable of leading to a reflection on the *holiness of life*. This last term, "holiness", is neither rhetorical nor superfluous, because the dialogue between Christianity and religions, as well as the dialogue between Catholicism and other Christian confessions, can only become fruitful by sharing the love of truth and the search for holiness. In other words, it can be conducted only in docility to the Spirit.

##### 6.4.1 Opening up to a universal philosophy in interreligious dialogue
Just as we say, with foundation, that every human being is *naturaliter religiosus,* so we can say that every human being is also open, *naturaliter*, in search of the truth. Culture, every human culture, expresses at bottom the path of this search, because it is structured, throughout history, around questions of a religious nature. Any reflection on religion or on the dialogue between religions cannot fail to register, therefore, a certain *circularity* between religion, culture and philosophy. By the term "religion" we do not mean here the historical concretions of the different religious traditions, some of which were able, in certain periods, to come into conflict with philosophical research, as happened in the case of Greek polytheism; instead, we mean first of all religious experience in its most authentic meaning, that capable of converging with the best instances of philosophical thought which, through speculative reflection, seeks the answer to the great questions of existence, which, ultimately, are questions of a religious nature too.

##### Religion and philosophy as instances of universality
Philosophy and religion are both instances of universality: they seek the reason of the whole, the ultimate and founding causes, according to rational or existential itineraries, each with its own anthropological context, but both open, in principle, to mutual convergence.30 In every religious tradition, the great philosophical-religious questions about the origin and the whole, the meaning of the world and the purpose of life, must remain open to the search for a philosophical truth which respects the canons of rationality and universality. In this regard, the Magisterium of the Church has not been afraid to speak of a certain "purifying action" that philosophical analysis carries out on religion.31 The openness to a philosophical body of a universal character can then be used as an important clarifying element in assessing the "truth" of a religion, at least in so far as the latter proposes specific knowledge about the world and man.

The same openness to philosophy, and to a certain extent also to the sciences, can then also operate within the framework of an interreligious dialogue. It is not a question of putting a religion under suspicion or radically subjecting it to the scrutiny of critical knowledge, but of asking it to enucleate what universal knowledge it presupposes or accepts, in order to begin a dialogue on the basis of contents which certainly participate, if they are authentic, in the unity of truth. Once the impossibility of a "pluralist" model has been recorded, a theology of religions would thus have the possibility of flanking the possible *re-reading* of other religions within a Christocentric inclusive model with the use of a philosophical criterion, using the existence of a circularity between religion/culture and philosophy.

Without renouncing the contribution of a certain and shared scientific knowledge, a theology of religions could then ask the different religious traditions what philosophical, *i.e. universal,* truths they contain, without fear of indicating when inconsistencies, contradictions or denials of acquired knowledge arise. The *philosophical* and *rational* nature of the contents sought requires that they maintain a necessary connection with reality, while allowing this to happen through interpretations that are not always homogeneous and equivalent, but which can ultimately be traced back to the experience of common human nature in which one participates and to the empirical experience of a world before everyone's eyes. Different forms of rationality, possible within different peoples and cultures, will in fact never be totally incommensurable, because they require the consent of men who participate in the same experience and are immersed in the same reality.

Because of the circularity between philosophy and religion, asking the great religions of the earth to enucleate that set of universal philosophical truths present within them means, in essence, asking them to know how to show a clear connection with natural religiosity, that is, with the authentic religious sense. In fact, being every human being *naturaliter philosophus et naturaliter religiosus*, a revealed religion not only cannot contradict what the natural religious sense would expect, but must satisfy it. An example of the journey, both metaphysical and anthropological, briefly mentioned here, can be found in Newman's *Grammar of Assent.* In the concluding pages that refer to assent in religious matters,32 The Oxford theologian uses the notion of "natural religion" with the meaning of "righteous philosophy", in order to show that a revealed religion must be able to account for both. Conscience is the heart of religiosity and is also the centre of the encounter with truth. For Newman, as for the Fathers of the Church and Thomas Aquinas, both love of truth and religious meaning are preparation for Revelation.33

Although possessing evidently meta-historical dimensions, every religion expresses itself through modes subject to history and is directed to men who live in history. As we well know, the sifting of history has made Christianity the object of critical analysis by the natural sciences and the humanities, subjecting it to assessments of rationalism, enlightenment and positive atheism, or allowing its documentary sources to be analyzed by literary criticism, psychology and history. It is not a question of demanding that all the religions of the earth be subjected to the same scrutiny that the history of Western thought has reserved for Christianity, in circumstances that often had no constructive intentionality; it is instead a question of observing that every religion could, perhaps should, have similar opportunities to be able to make explicit, in critical dialogue with philosophy and the sciences, the universal reasons that support its belief. Turned in constructive terms, such a comparative analysis of religions would not have the character of a dialectical comparison, but rather that of laying the foundations of an authentic dialogue, because it would help each of them to purify themselves and to move towards a truth recognizable in the world and in history, as an indispensable part of the truth of their God. An interreligious dialogue which would a priori prescind from this bridge towards the universality of reason could certainly travel the path of social cooperation or even that of political opportunity, but it would not be along the path which leads to the one true God.34

##### The opening of Christianity to an instance of truth in the philosophical sphere
Since its inception Christianity, to put it in some way, has consciously offered its side to the critical scrutiny of reason, precisely because its demand for universality has always been professed also on the objective, cosmic level. As is well known, in the encounter with Greek-Roman philosophy the appeal to reason was decisive for the beginning of evangelization. In *De civitate Dei*, following the well-known tripartition of Terence Varro, Augustine subscribed to the option of Christianity in favor of *naturalis theology,* and not civil or mythical theology.35 If Christians came into conflict with the Roman empire, it was because they did not agree to place "one more god" in the Pantheon, but had the audacity to speak of Christianity as the *true* religion, that is, a religion with a universal instance.36

Placed fully within the realm of the Jewish perspective and its monotheism, Christianity is not afraid to introduce the categories of true and false into the discourse on religion. If before then the essential categories of religion remained the pure and the impure, the sacred and the profane, now it is vigorously affirmed that there are false gods before the one true God.37 To the ambiguity of religious syncretism, which overcame the problem of truth because it was inessential to its worship, Christianity opposed the idea of an analogous and participatory truth, expressed through its doctrine of the "seeds of the Word", which originally intended to demonstrate the openness of Christianity to a philosophy in search of truth and the openness of philosophy to the answers now offered by Christianity.38

In addition to the appeal to reason made to the Greco-Roman world during the first evangelization, Christianity manifested other important elements of convergence with a search for truth in the philosophical sphere, thus showing itself open to an interreligious dialogue carried out on the watermark of philosophical thought.

First of all, we must remember, once again, the convergence present in the Jewish-Christian Revelation between the creative word and the prophetic word, which we have repeatedly highlighted. The God of Israel reveals himself as the God of everything and everyone, the Creator of heaven and earth, the subject of a special bond with history. The correspondence between the Word of God and the truth of things ensures that there is a viable path, that of natural knowledge of God, which moves from faith towards the universality of reason. The role of the wisdom tradition within biblical revelation should be emphasized once again here, because it shows that the Word of God can also be understood by *listening to things*. It is a listening that requires recollection and reflection: indeed, the divine word that is heard *proceeds from such reflection*. Although the sage, unlike the prophet, is not given a word by the authority of God who reveals himself, he is required to engage in meditation and research in order to find what God means to mankind.

In the famous chapter 13 of the *Book of Wisdom*, the text collected by the Christian canon will not be afraid to affirm, in an anti-idolatrous context, that from creatures, by analogy, we can arrive at the knowledge of the Creator, that is, at "He who is", using here the same Greek expression with which, in the LXX version, Jahve, the name of God proper to mosaic revelation, was translated.39 The revelation of God in Wisdom belongs to the logic of the relationship between faith and reason and thus provides, in a natural way, an important link between anthropology and cosmology, uniting them in the same instance of universality.

A further aspect that highlights the convergence between Christianity and openness to a search for philosophical truth undertaken from the real, that is, within the cosmos, can be highlighted by recalling the original reference to *cosmic* attributes of God. Faith in "God the Father Almighty, Creator of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible" inaugurates, with slight differences, practically all the symbols of faith, starting from the most ancient baptismal formulas. The Christian prayer par excellence also opens with a similar reference of cosmic breath, directing the invocation of the prayer to the Creator of all things, calling him "Our Father *who art in heaven* (gr. ὁ ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς)". It would not be an exaggeration to say that this "natural contextualization" of the God of Jesus Christ also lies at the heart of the apostolic kerygma and *places* the Christian message *cosmologically.*

The requirements of universality and openness to philosophical thought which concern the revelation of the God of Israel, retain all their validity even when referred to the Christian *Logos.* One of the basic themes of *Fides et ratio* was to reaffirm precisely the *universality* of Christian truth, which is open to comparison with the universality of being and reason. From the point of view of the practice of interreligious dialogue, we observe finally that the Christian presents himself as a person naturally open to such dialogue, because he knows that the God in whom he believes is also the end of an ascending path that *every man is able to follow*, both starting from the intimate voice of his own conscience and going back up from nature created to the Creator.

##### 6.4.2 Christianity and cultures: the Incarnation as a model of inculturation of the faith
The relationship between Christianity and religions must also pass through a final delicate junction: the divine word, whose historical revelation was handed over to the Jewish people, was embodied in a specific culture and then fixed/transmitted with the contribution of the Greco-Roman cultural context. Now it is this *incarnated* Word that wants and must relate, for the purposes of evangelization, to other peoples and cultures. Tackling the theme of the relationship between Revelation and culture thus becomes inevitable: the Word of God is entrusted to a people that possesses precise linguistic, intellectual and cultural canons; in its formative process it enters into relationship with other cultures (Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Assyrian-Babylonian, Greek, etc.); yet, when Christian revelation is announced, the word transmitted is often addressed to a different culture, that of the interlocutor to whom it is directed.40

##### Culture, religion, Christianity
A first way of approaching the topic is to clarify, in general, the relationship between Christianity and culture/religion. Is Christianity just a religion? Does the linguistic, anthropological, etc. context that made Revelation possible constitute, within this, a precise culture?

The consideration, made earlier, that the religious phenomenon in some way represents the "heart" of every culture contributes to providing an answer to the previous question. In fact, the culture of a people is structured in history around the existential questions that face the enigma of man and the cosmos and seek answers to it in the mystery of the sacred and transcendence, in the mystery of God:

Beyond all the differences that distinguish individuals and peoples, there is a fundamental commonality, since different cultures are in fact only different ways of dealing with the question of the meaning of personal existence. And precisely here we can identify a source of respect which is due to every culture and every nation: any culture is an effort to reflect on the mystery of the world and of man in particular: it is a way of giving expression to the transcendent dimension of human life. The heart of every culture is its approach to the greatest of mysteries: the mystery of God.41

Every authentic culture remains constitutively open to transcendence and is willing, in principle, to confront a content of a religious nature, and therefore, possibly, also to accept it as an answer to its own questions. When this openness ceases to exist, the authentic questioning of man and the world has then probably been removed, because it has been replaced by some form of *ideology,* or suffocated by forms of *idolatry*. The dialogue between Christianity and human culture or cultures therefore moves in the wake of the *authentically religious value of* each culture.

There is, however, a difference between the religiosity proper to each authentically cultural experience or tradition and Christian Revelation, that is, a difference between religion and Christianity. The difference lies in what we might call "excess of condescension" or "principle of superabundance", proper to the content of Revelation. Thanks to the overabundance of the divine offer compared to the horizon of the questions posed by man, the Christian religion cannot be considered, like other religions, a *totally cultural* fact: it is not *one religion among others*. If religion is an *ascending* motion of man to God, Christian Revelation breaks into history as a *condescending* motion of God towards man.42 The "figure" of this difference is represented, precisely, by the event of the Incarnation.43 This does not mean that other religious phenomenologies cannot include a certain manifestation of God, a "coming to meet" the subject: it is merely stated that, in the historical-salvific dynamic of the Jewish-Christian tradition, such condescension presents an unprecedented content and intensity, which place this religious tradition in a position of absolute singularity, as we have seen at the beginning of this chapter, in the panorama of religions. Christianity is not only a search for God, but above all a revelation and self-communication of God himself in the Word made flesh, according to a logic that is excessive and in a certain way paradoxical, and therefore totally given.

It is precisely because of this peculiarity that Christianity can recognize and value the instances of truth contained in all other religions. Precisely because it does not identify with a completely cultural fact, Christianity finds itself in the condition of being able to unite itself to one culture, to all cultures. It recognizes them as expressions of those demands for meaning by which man seeks to ascend to God, and of that veneration of the sacred by which man recognizes that God is giving something to man. In expressing the fullness and superabundance of the gift that God makes of Himself in Christ, the Christian religion makes those demands its own and takes on, overcoming and interpreting them, the logic of those gifts. In this way, the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word constitutes *the basis and the condition of possibility of* every inculturation of faith.

##### Incarnate Word and proclamation of the Gospel: the Incarnation of the Word, model of inculturation of faith
In the process of formation of Sacred Scripture, the mystery of the Incarnation is presented by the Magisterium of the Church as the most significant analogy for understanding the relationship between the human form of biblical expression and the divine content it conveys. In n. 13 of the Conciliar Constitution *Dei Verbum* we find this statement:

In Sacred Scripture, while God's truth and holiness remain always intact, the admirable condescension of eternal Wisdom is manifested, "that we may learn the ineffable goodness of God and how much he, solicitous and provident in regard to our nature, has contemplated his speech. For the words of God, expressed in human tongues, became similar to the language of men, as the Word of the eternal Father, having taken on the weaknesses of human nature, became similar to men.

Taking up the concept of "condescension" proposed by John Chrysostom and reconnecting with an idea already present in the *Divine afflicting Spiritu* (Pius XII, 1943), *Dei Verbum* thus establishes an analogy between sacred Scripture, considered as a whole, and the mystery of hypostatic union, between Verbum *scriptum* and *Verbum incarnatum.*44 Putting the word of God in writing is already, in some way, "a first step towards the Incarnation of the Word of God".45 "In revelation *God appropriates the human word and expresses himself in it*; in the Incarnation *God personally appropriates the human being and expresses himself in it*: the Incarnation definitively establishes the truth that God has spoken to men".46

This analogy also exists at the moment of considering the relationship between Revelation as the *proclamation of faith* and the particular culture that welcomes the Gospel message. That is, it is necessary to "incarnate faith in a culture", using an expression frequently used in the teachings of John Paul II: "the incarnation of the Word was also a cultural incarnation".47 Becoming a man, by the divine Word, has involved "descending" in a certain place, at a certain time, in a certain historical-cultural context, taking upon oneself all its particularities. Without "culture", in the most pregnant sense of the term, there would have been no preparation for the Incarnation, nor would there have been the Incarnation itself. The link with a particular culture, the Jewish culture, does not constitute a limit, but represents *the way in which* the Word participates in the history of the world and joins the whole of humanity. It is through his humanity as a Jewish son of Mary that Jesus of Nazareth was able to unite himself in a certain way to every man. Just as Jewish culture has been able to "actualize" the humanity of the Word, so every culture can and must be made capable of encountering the word of the Gospel and welcoming it.

The reference to the Incarnation, with regard to the problems posed by inculturation, is also useful for the purposes of what, in chapter IV of this volume, we have called the "historicity of dogmatic formulas". Cultures, "similarly comparable to the humanity of Christ in what they have good, can play a positive role of mediation for the expression and diffusion of the Christian faith".48 Just as Christ assumed humanity in everything equal to ours except sin, so cultures must, in order to express the Word, be purified from those elements introduced by error, ignorance, or sin which obscure their ability to reveal human dignity and a religiousness open to transcendence. Ignoring this reality would turn into naïve optimism, causing culture to fall into a state of dangerous closure with respect to the true religion, to the transcendence and holiness of the divine mystery, and therefore with respect to faith.

At the same time, as has already been pointed out, Christianity *transcends* every specific culture, just as the divine person of the Word transcends the created humanity to which he wanted to bind himself. The eternal Word enters the world but is not of the world. Human language is suitable for expressing the divine mystery but cannot exhaust it. This is also true from an existential perspective: every Christian lives in his culture and his culture, but with his faith he judges it, surpasses it, elevates it.49

Just as the person of the Word attracts his human nature into the sphere of eternity and divine life, so cultures which open themselves to faith are inserted into a new logic, that of the life of the Spirit: "inculturation is *the incarnation of the Gospel* in indigenous cultures and at the same time the introduction of them into the life of the Church".50 The Spirit of Christ must be able to reach every manifestation of that particular culture, with that fullness with which his humanity responds in every part to the personal action of the Word.

The position of the Incarnation of the Word in the relationship between faith and culture recalls in a certain way the exemplariness of the Incarnation of the Word with regard to the relationship between grace and nature, or that between faith and reason. Culture, like reason and nature, retain their autonomy in their encounter with the Christian faith, yet at the same time they recognize in it their meaning, the fulfilment of their τέλος. Their autonomy is as real as Christ's humanity is perfect, but their ordering to "incarnate" the faith is also real, as is the divine will to be united forever with man: *non horruisti Virginis uterum*, recites the Church in the *Te Deum*.
