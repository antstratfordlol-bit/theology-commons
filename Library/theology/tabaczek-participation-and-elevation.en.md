# Participation and Elevation: Clarifying the Metaphysics of Instrumental Causality in Aquinas

Mariusz Tabaczek

# Abstract

Thomas Aquinas’s account of instrumental causality is central to his understanding of divine action, yet his terminology often appears fluid. This paper investigates the metaphysical coherence of Aquinas’s theory, addressing how created agents produce effects transcending their natural powers. After analyzing principal and instrumental agency and the mechanism of elevation via _virtus fluens_, we propose a threefold typology of instrumentality: _Universal Metaphysical_, _Natural and Artistic_, and _Supernatural_. Engaging the Scotistic critique of “dual agency,” we defend Aquinas’s elevation model through the analogy of being, demonstrating its unique explanatory power in maintaining the ontological distinction between Creator and craftsman.

# Keywords

Thomas Aquinas; instrumental causality; secondary causality; divine action; elevation; _Virtus fluens_ (Flowing power as the effect of elevation); bestowal of _Esse_; dual agency; _universal metaphysical instrumentality_; _natural and artistic instrumentality_; _supernatural instrumentality_

# Introduction

The divine goodness … has imparted to creatures the dignity of causing. — _Summa Theologiae_ I, 22, 3, co.

The distinction between God as the primary cause and creatures as secondary causes is a cornerstone of Thomistic philosophical theology. It safeguards both the transcendence of divine agency and the genuine causal efficacy of created agents. However, alongside this familiar vertical hierarchy, Aquinas frequently employs a second, overlapping distinction: that between a principal agent and an instrumental agent. While the former distinction (primary/secondary) describes the general dependence of created being on Divine Being, the latter (principal/instrumental) is often reserved for more specific modes of operation, such as the sacraments, prophecy, or the motion of bodily members by the soul.

Yet, a close reading of the relevant texts in the _Opera Omnia_ reveals that these two schemas are not always kept in hermetic isolation. Aquinas sometimes speaks of all created agents as instruments of God, raising difficult questions about the metaphysical status of natural causality. Furthermore, how do we account for Aquinas’s shifting terminology regarding the causal contribution of the instrument, its “elevation,” and the unity of the action produced, given that the precise mechanics of instrumental action—specifically how a creature can perform its own proper action while simultaneously performing a higher instrumental action—remain a source of significant philosophical tension?

# 1. Thomistic Notion of Secondary and Instrumental Causation

One of the most significant aspects of the Thomistic model of _concursus divinus naturalis_ is a twofold distinction between the primary and principal causation of God and the secondary and instrumental causation of creatures. All four categories of causes mentioned here (primary/secondary and principal/instrumental) are predicated of God and creatures analogically. Specifically, the vertical relationship between divine and creaturely agency is understood by analogy to the way these distinctions apply to created agents acting through other created agents—a relationship we will support with textual evidence and further metaphysical analysis in the following sections.

## 1.1. Definitions

Michael Dodds serves as a prominent example of a contemporary Thomist who offers a clear-cut distinction between secondary and instrumental causes.[^c01-1] In his work _Unlocking Divine Action_, he first defines the latter, stating that “[i]n instrumental causality, the effect always exceeds the capacity of the instrumental cause acting alone.”[^c01-2] He provides two examples: a student using a pencil to write, and Aquinas’s classic illustration of a saw cutting wood according to a specific pattern.

Dodds then introduces the distinction between primary and secondary causality: “It is also possible for one efficient cause to work through another to produce an effect that does not exceed but is precisely proportionate to the capacity of the other. This is called ‘secondary causality.’ Here, the first agent is called the ‘primary cause,’ and the second agent the ‘secondary cause’.”[^c01-3] Dodds offers two illustrative examples of secondary causation, following the traditional Thomistic line. The first is the Aristotelian conviction —frequently cited by Aquinas—that “man is begotten by man and by the sun as well” (_Phys._ II, 2 [194b 13]). Here, the sun acts as the universal principal cause, while the organism’s generative power serves as the secondary cause.[^c01-4] The second is the more

Dodds finds the categories of secondary and instrumental causation crucial for a proper understanding of divine providence—specifically the execution of that providence through divine governance.[^c01-5] Indeed, both distinctions—between the primary and principal agency of God and the secondary and instrumental agency of creatures—become the kernel of his reaction and response to all major models of divine action discussed in the context of the contemporary dialogue between religion and science.[^c01-6] For Dodds, these distinctions are vital because they protect the Thomistic model from the opposing extremes of occasionalism and deism. By affirming that God works through created causes rather than instead of them, the model ensures that creatures possess real, intrinsic causal agency grounded in their own active powers. This framework allows for a “middle way” that strikes a proper balance between God’s transcendence and His immanence: God is not a remote “watchmaker” (deism), nor does He strip creatures of their causal efficacy by centralizing all agency in the Divine Being (occasionalism). Rather, He is the primary source of the very being and efficacy that secondary and instrumental causes exercise in their own right.

## 1.2. Complications

Crucial to this analysis is Dodds’s observation that “Aquinas _clearly distinguishes_ a cause (the secondary cause) that, under the influence of another cause, produces an effect proportionate to its nature, from a cause (the instrumental cause) that, under the influence of another, produces an effect that exceeds its nature.”[^c01-7] At the same time, Dodds adds: “His terminology … is somewhat fluid. Sometimes, for instance, he refers to the instrumental cause as a particular kind of secondary cause. In this sense, every cause that acts under the influence of another is a secondary cause. But the one that produces an effect exceeding its natural capacity is an ‘instrumental secondary cause [ _causa secunda instrumentalis_ ]’ (_ST_ 1, 45, 5, co.).”[^c01-8]

One might argue that Dodds’s suggestion—that secondary causation serves as the broader, fundamental category—finds strong support in Aquinas’s treatment of the execution of divine providence through divine governance. When discussing governance, Thomas refers to created agents predominantly as secondary causes rather than instruments. For Aquinas, God’s immediate provision—as exercised through the order of governance—“does not exclude the action of secondary causes; which are the executors of His order” (_ST_ I, 22, 3, ad 2), nor does the primacy of the First Agent render the operation of secondary agents “superfluous” (_ST_ I, 105, 5, ad 1).[^c01-9]

Crucially, unlike the strict definition of an instrument, these secondary causes are not elevated beyond their natural capacities to produce an effect exceeding their power. Rather, God “wishes to act by means of nature in order to preserve the order in things” (_De pot._ 3, 7, ad 16). Consequently, because they exercise causation proper to their own nature, they remain, as finite beings, liable to defect. Aquinas notes that “the effect of a first cause may be hindered by a defect of a secondary cause; as the effect of the motive power may be hindered by the weakness of the limb” (_ST_ I, 19, 6, ad 3).

As Wippel observes, “At times … Thomas uses ‘instrument’ in a broader way, to signify any second or caused cause, whether or not it has within itself a principle for its own action or motion.”[^c01-11] On this reading, the distinction between the two becomes blurred because every created (secondary) cause, insofar as it acts only by the power of the First Cause (as moved mover), can be viewed under the aspect of an instrument.

An example often cited in this context is Aquinas’s distinction of the four ways in which God causes creaturely agency in _De pot._ 3, 7, co. While some scholars argue that secondary causation encompasses the entirety of these four ways, the fourth mode —which further specifies the third, regarding God’s application of created agents to their operation—explicitly defines God as “serving as principal cause of that of which the created agent is an instrumental cause.”[^c01-12] This suggests that, at the moment of causal influx, the secondary cause functions as an instrument. As James Albertson contends, “the relation of First and second cause [which he reserves to the description of the agency of God and of created causes as bestowing _esse_ on other creatures alone] is but an application in a special order of the more general relation of principal and instrumental cause.”[^c01-13] Within this framework, all secondary causes are essentially instrumental when considered in their actual operation under the divine motion.

Those who see instrumental causality as a broader category co-extensive with secondary causality tend to distinguish between “general” and “proper” instrumental causality. Under this framework, while some created causes are proper instrumental causes—in the sense of being moved movers (see _SCG_ II, 21, no. 4; _ST_ III, 63, 5, ad 2) whose effects exceed the instrument’s power—all created causes are general instrumental causes, insofar as they are moved movers that produce effects within their native capacities while remaining dependent on a Higher Mover. In this context, Dodds’s definition of instrumentality must be seen as focusing on proper instrumental causality, where the instrument is specifically elevated to achieve an effect beyond its natural reach. In such a schema, general instrumentality appears functionally equivalent to secondary causality, insofar as both describe a moved mover acting within its own natural limits.

This raises the question of whether “secondary cause” is merely a placeholder for ontological dependence in causation, while “instrumental cause” provides the actual metaphysical structure of the causal action. As we shall see (in Section 3.1), this terminological fluidity may stem from Aquinas’s effort to synthesize the abstract language of “primary and secondary” causes—borrowed from the Neoplatonic tradition of the _Liber de causis_ to explain the hierarchical ordering of power—with the more concrete, technical framework of “principal and instrumental” agency (explaining how the thing acts under a mover). ## 1.3. Instrumentality and the Metaphysics of Esse

It is precisely at the level of being that Aquinas’s strategy of extending the category of instrumental causality to all created agents—while seemingly confusing—is justified once we realize its grounding in his metaphysics of _esse_. As established in the previous section, if we consider every creaturely action as instrumental relative to God’s principal causality, we must determine what exactly is being “elevated.” In this “universal” sense, elevation signifies that the creature is moved by God to participate in a causal reach that its finite essence does not possess by nature. Specifically, while a creature can naturally move another from potency to act (the proper domain of secondary causality), it cannot, by its own power, bestow _esse_ as such (_per se_).

Therefore, when a secondary cause acts, it functions as a “universal metaphysical instrument” because it mediates an intentionality and an effect—the actualization of being—that belongs properly to God alone. While God alone creates _ex nihilo_ without mediation, in the order of governance He communicates _esse_ through the operations of creatures. Dodds explains this in an encyclopedia entry on divine action, co-authored with Thomas Ch. O’Brien:

In a way, every creaturely effect exceeds the capacity of the creature because the effect has existence (even the fleeting existence of the duck quack), and God alone is the proper cause of existence. Efficient causality is always the active communication of existence to an effect. Because _esse_ is the proper effect of God, every other AGENT in causing must participate in the influence of divine causality. In this sense, the creature is an instrumental cause and God is the principal cause.[^c01-14]

A similar point regarding the interpretation of Aquinas is made by other Thomists.[^c01-15] Rudi te Velde—discussing the latter two of the four ways in which God causes created agents to act (in _De pot._ 3, 7, co.)—makes the following comment:

God can be understood to be the cause of any other thing’s action, as he actualizes the power of a thing to perform its own action. The point is not that the instrumental cause, by the power of the higher cause, is brought to perform an action which is foreign to it; it is brought to perform its own action. Besides this aspect of the “_applicatio_,” however, Thomas also mentions the immanence by participation of the power of the higher cause in the instrumental cause. In a certain sense the instrument may be called the cause of the effect which is proper to the principal cause, not by its own power, but inasmuch as it _participates_ in the power of the principal cause. So the instrument is not only “applied” to its own activity but even raised to a level of an effect that exceeds its own power …[^c01-16]

Furthermore, Kopf’s definition seems to suggest that an instrumental application is necessary for a secondary cause to be what it is _in actu_. As Kopf himself notes, if one does not accept the Wippel/te Velde thesis of universal instrumentality, the definition should instead state that a secondary cause is a power that can (but does not have to) be instrumentally used by God. To avoid any potential confusion regarding the nature of this ontological communication, it is important to specify here that the _esse_ communicated through instrumental causes is not the Divine Being (_Esse Subsistens_) itself, but _esse_ as the first and most formal effect of God in the creature—what is often called _esse commune_. This created being is not identical with God’s essence but is related to it through the analogy of being (_analogia entis_). While the creature truly (instrumentally) participates in the divine power to bestow being, it does so in a manner proportionate to its status as a finite effect, not as a source of existence in its own right.

## 2.1. Elevation of Instruments

In order to produce an effect that exceeds its natural powers—as seen in the mode of _Proper Instrumentality_ —an instrumental cause must be elevated by a principal agent.[^c01-27] As Hugon notes, “an instrumental cause receives a temporary influence from the agent, which elevates it and applies it.”[^c01-28] In this sense, once “elevated and ennobled,” the instrument is able to operate with its principal cause “together at the same level, so to speak.”[^c01-29] Albertson draws attention to Aquinas’s assertion concerning two stages in the reception of instrumental power: “The power of acting instrumentally is given to an instrument in two ways. In one way inchoatively [ _quasi inchoative_ ], as it were, when it is established as belonging to the species ‘instrument’ … In another way, it is given completely, when it is actually moved by a principal agent, as when a carpenter uses a saw” (_In IV Sent_. 1, 1, 4, sol. 2, ad 2). The latter stage constitutes the actual elevation of the instrument.[^c01-30]

Albertson further emphasizes that the classification of an agent as an instrumental cause does not hinge primarily on whether it must first be moved into act in order to exercise its proper activity (like a saw that must be moved by a carpenter). Such preliminary motion may or may not be required and is therefore not decisive for understanding instrumentality as such.[^c01-31] Rather, what fundamentally characterizes an instrumental cause is that, while exercising its own proper operation under the influence of a higher agent, it is elevated so as to bring about an effect that exceeds its intrinsic power, whether in terms of operative capacity or orientation toward a determinate end. Referring to Aquinas’s standard examples of instruments, Albertson observes:

As natural agents, that is, as beings with a proper activity (not in the sense of products of generation), the fire and the saw are quite different, for the saw must be moved to its proper activity while the fire requires no motion. But as instruments they are very much the same; for in the exercise of their proper activity they produce a result which cannot be accounted for by only their causality—an effect whose formality is proportioned to a higher or principal cause.[^c01-32]

In introducing the category of elevation, Hugon explicitly rejects the view—associated with Suárez—that what is elevated in the instrument is its obediential potency.[^c01-33] Obediential potency, he argues, is a purely passive capacity innate in creatures by virtue of which they can receive the omnipotent action of God. By contrast, instrumental causality is not innate but communicated to the instrument beyond and above its natural powers.[^c01-34] Hugon likewise dismisses the interpretation according to which the instrument receives merely an assisting power, since assisting efficient causes remain external to the agents they support and therefore do not modify their intrinsic mode of agency.

## 2.2. Transitory Character of Instrumental Causality

Thomists emphasize the transitory nature of the instrumental powers inherent in created things.[^c01-37] Hugon describes these powers as transitory qualities (_qualitates fluentes_) and as active and transitory motions (_motiones actuosae et transeuntes_). This account allows even spiritual powers to be received in material substances, not as permanent dispositions, but as transitory activities communicated by the principal agent.[^c01-38]

Frost articulates this same concept through what she terms the “borrowed power thesis.” She contends that the instrument receives from the principal cause a transitory power that enables it to act toward the second action. She quotes _In IV Sent._ 19, 1, 2, sol. 1: “[T]he principal agent is established in the fact that he has a certain form that can be transferred to another.” Furthermore, in reference to _In IV Sent._ 1, 1, 4, sol. 3, she explains that “the instrument does not have the power of the principal cause in it in the same way that the power is in the principal cause itself. The instrument only has the power which it receives from the principal as long as it is being moved by the principal.”[^c01-39]

Moreover, as she further elaborates:

Even while the instrument is being used by the principal, the power which the instrument gains from the principal has a fleeting, transitory, and imperfect mode of existence in the instrument. The power is not a stable quality of the instrument, rooted in one of its own forms.[^c01-40]

It is important to note, however, that the “transitory” nature of the instrumental power is primarily a modal distinction (_de iure_) rather than a strictly temporal one (_de facto_). While in artistic or supernatural agency the exercise of the instrumental power is typically sporadic, in cases where a creature instrumentally communicates _esse_, the elevation may be practically constant. This constancy occurs in two ways: (1) in the production of a new being, as long as the agent possesses the active power to bestow _esse_ on a new entity; (2) in the preservation of being, where a creaturely cause acts in analogy to God’s continual _conservatio_ to keep another being in existence (e.g. the ongoing biological and constitutive dependence of offspring on parental care or an environment). In both instances, the power remains “transitory” in the metaphysical sense (_fluens_) because it is never possessed as a stable, independent property of the creature’s own nature, but depends entirely on the ongoing motion of the First Cause.

## 2.3. Causal Contribution of the Instrument

Aquinas thinks that in order for an instrument to make effective use of the influence it receives from a superior agent, it must already possess its own proper power and exercise a prior activity that disposes and prepares it for the operation of the principal cause:

[T]he secondary instrumental cause does not participate the action of the superior cause, except inasmuch as by something proper to itself it acts dispositively to the effect of the principal agent. If therefore it effects nothing, according to what is proper to itself, it is used to no purpose; nor would there be any need of certain instruments for certain actions. (_ST_ I, 45, 5, co.)[^c01-41]

While Aquinas frequently describes the instrument as acting “dispositively” to prepare matter for a higher form—as seen in the classic example of a saw cutting wood—scholars have noted a significant development toward what might be called a “perfective” model of instrumentality. In his more mature works, Aquinas clarifies that an instrument does not merely prepare the way but can reach the final effect itself through the power communicated by the principal agent. As he notes in _ST_ III, 56, 1, ad 2: “it is appropriate to say that an instrument truly causes the effect intended by the principal cause,” implying that the instrument’s contribution is not merely a preliminary stage but a direct participation in the final production of the effect.[^c01-42]

This shift toward a more robust participation in bringing the final effect is corroborated by Hugon, who asserts that the instrument does not receive the power of the higher agent as a passive subject or as a merely neutral medium. Rather, it sustains and transmits that power as a genuinely efficacious and active principle. For this reason, the instrument must contribute its own real activity. Were it to contribute nothing, it would function merely as an occasion or a conduit, rather than as a true efficient cause.[^c01-43]

Albertson discusses the importance of this aspect of instrumental causation, noting that “if there is one point emphasized by St. Thomas in his writings on instrumental causality, it is that the instrument must contribute a proper activity of its own in order to act as instrument.”[^c01-44] He argues that further evidence of this can be seen in how Aquinas views the _virtus instrumentalis_ (the power an instrument possesses due to the influence of the principal agent) as being received by the instrument in a twofold way (as already mentioned in Section 2.1): first, when the instrument receives its kindspecific form, and second, when it is moved by the principal agent—the latter being impossible without the former.[^c01-45]

Hugon further observes that God Himself adheres to this principle, even though, as omnipotent, He could act in the universe without any created concurrence. Nevertheless, when God freely chooses to operate through instruments, those instruments cannot be reduced to a merely extrinsic medium or a passive occasion for divine action. Rather, they must function as true causes endowed with genuine causal efficacy.[^c01-46]

## 2.4. Twofold Agency of Instruments

Aquinas emphasizes that “[a]n instrument has a twofold action; one is instrumental, in respect of which it works not by its own power but by the power of the principal agent: the other is its proper action, which belongs to it in respect of its proper form” (_ST_ III, 62, 1, ad 2).[^c01-48] Frost acknowledges this fact as the “dual action thesis” and demonstrates its intrinsic connection to the “moved mover thesis.” She notes that each instrument achieves an outcome exceeding its inherent powers only in so far as it is moved by the principal cause. Referring to _De ver_. 26, 1, ad 8, she states:

In Aquinas’s view, the motion which the principal causes in the instrument is what enables it to work toward the end which surpasses its native power. … By moving the instrument, the principal transfers a power to it.[^c01-49]

Nevertheless, while the proper action of an instrument must not be neglected, Aquinas emphasizes that “an instrument acts not by reason of its own form, but by the power of the one who moves it” (_ST_ III, 64, 5, co.).[^c01-50] This leads him to assert, in turn, that “[a]n instrument is related to an action more like that by which it is done than like that which does it; for the principal agent acts by means of the instrument” (_De ver._ 27, 4, ad 8, referenced in Section 1.1, note 2). For this reason, Aquinas further upholds that “the power of the higher cause will be more immediate to the effect than the power of the lower cause, since the power of the lower cause is not joined to its effect save by the power of the higher cause” (_De pot_. 3, 7, co.).[^c01-51]

Therefore, as Frost notes, it is accurate to say that “the final effect resembles the principal cause more than the instrument.”[^c01-52] Albertson adds that the effect of instrumental causation is “superior to the native power of the instrument but proportioned to the proper or native power of the higher cause.”[^c01-53] This aspect of instrumentality becomes clearer once it is understood that the power in which the instrument participates is present within it as an imperfect act (_actus imperfectus_). Rooted in the nature of the principal cause, such power abides in the instrument as an incomplete reality—a progression or an _intentio_ proportioned to the motion that it causes.[^c01-54] Accordingly, Albertson notes:

[I]t is not necessary for the instrument to extend its causality all the way to the final perfection achieved by the principal cause. It sufficiently accomplishes its work as instrument if it attains some effect beyond what is due to its nature.[^c01-55]

## 2.5. Causal Unity in Instrumental Causation

While both the principal and the instrumental causes are active, one can speak of the causal unity of their combined agency—that is, a single effect (in its twofold dimension) that terminates the causality of both the principal cause and the instrument, albeit in distinct ways. Albertson asserts that they constitute “one complete causal principle” and speaks about “two formalities” under one “single activity.”[^c01-58] He refers to Aquinas’s observation that “the principal agent and the instrument are, in a way, one cause, since one acts through the other” (_ST_ I-II, 14, 3, ad 4). Furthermore, “the moved shares in the operation of the mover, and the mover makes use of the operation of the moved, and, consequently, each acts in communion with the other” (_ST_ III, 19, 1, co.).[^c01-59]

Hugon concurs, emphasizing that “instrumental activity cooperates in the _whole_ of the effect of the superior agent. The action of the instrument and that of the principal cause are not separate; there is only one passion and one term of the activity.”[^c01-60] This aligns with Aquinas’s teaching on primary and secondary causation in _ST_ I, 105, 5, ad 2, where he states that “One action does not proceed from two agents of the same order. But nothing hinders the same action from proceeding from a primary and a secondary agent.”[^c01-61]

The same idea finds another expression in Aquinas’s account of the instrumental agency of creatures with respect to God:

(_SCG_ III, 70, no. 8)

## 2.6. Finality of Instruments

Albertson devotes particular attention to the teleological dimensions of instrumental causality, contending that “we should expect final causality to be the dominant note in the consideration of instruments.”[^c01-64] Indeed, Aquinas clarifies this priority when he states:

[S]ometimes the material definition includes matter and form and efficient cause, but the formal definition includes the end … And this happens particularly with instruments, for with them almost the entire account of their species is taken from the end. (_In IV Sent._ 3, 1, sol. 1)

Following this observation, Albertson concludes that “[t]he instrument is constituted instrument and a determinate instrument by the end of the activity in which it is engaged. If the end of an action is such that this particular efficient cause active in the operation could of itself neither intend nor effect that end, then that cause is instrumental.”[^c01-65]

In other words, an agent qualifies as an instrument precisely insofar as the end toward which it acts exceeds both its intrinsic intentionality and its causal power, thereby requiring the direction and efficacy of a higher cause. For this reason, Albertson argues that the analysis of instrumental causality must begin with final causality, since the end—as the “cause of causes”—possesses ontological priority over efficient causation in determining the nature of instrumental action. Moreover, the notion of the _movens motum_ receives its full meaning within this teleological framework. The instrument operates beyond its own natural capacity precisely because it is being ordered toward an end that exceeds its species. This implies that it acts through the power of a superior agent present in it—not as a fully actualized natural power, but rather in a partial, dependent, and dynamically communicated mode—a participation in the principal agent’s own orientation toward the final effect.[^c01-66]

## 3.1. The Question of Terminological Clarity (1): General Usage

First, we can observe a certain lack of terminological clarity in Aquinas’s corpus. While the subsequent Thomistic tradition consistently juxtaposes the categories of primary and secondary causality or principal and instrumental causality, Aquinas himself often varies these pairings. On some occasions, he contrasts an instrumental cause with a “proper” or “first” agent (_proprium_ or _primum agens_), or even with a primary cause (_causa primaria_) (see, e.g. _In II Sent._ 35, 1, 4, co.; _SCG_ II, 21, no. 5). On other occasions, he pairs a secondary agent (_agens secundarium_) with the principal agent (_agens principale_) (see, e.g. _ST_ III, 71, 4, co.).

At the same time, it is possible to trace several instances where Aquinas mentions both distinctions (primary/secondary and principal/instrumental) in one place. To give an example, in _De ver_. 10, 6, ad 7, he says that “the phantasms act as instrumental and secondary agents. The agent intellect acts as the principal and first agent” (see also _SCG_ II, 89, no. 14; _SCG_ III, 148, no. 3; _De pot_. 3, 7, co.). Yet he does not seem to explain clearly the difference between those categories of causal agency in those specific passages. Indeed, as mentioned in Section 1.2, in numerous contexts Aquinas simultaneously employs the categories of secondary and instrumental causes, treating them as functionally co-extensive (see, e.g. _SCG_ III, 67, no. 5). He also speaks specifically about “secondary instrumental cause(s)” (_causa secunda instrumentalis_), effectively merging the two distinctions into a single operational description (see, e.g. _In IV Sent._ 1, 1, 1, sol. 4, co.; _In IV Sent._ 27, 1, 2, sol. 1, ad 1; _ST_ I, 45, 5, co.; _ST_ II-II, 17, 4, co.; _ST_ III, 56, 1, ad 2; _ST_ III, 71, 4, co.; _In De an._ II, lect. 8, § 331).

In fact, data drawn from the _Index Thomisticus_ (_via_ https://aquinas.cc/) shows that Aquinas uses the category of “instrument(al)” more than twice as often as the category of “secondary” (1,128 references to “instrument(al)” versus 501 references to “secondary”). Moreover, while virtually all of his references to instruments and instrumentality appear in a causal context, Aquinas frequently uses the adjective “secondary” as a qualifying term outside that context.

In relation to agency/causation, he speaks of a secondary: aim, agent, act (agency/ task), act of understanding, causality, cause, effect, end (as distinguished from a principal end), governor, mover, or production. Going beyond the strict context of causal explanation, we find him speaking of a secondary: aspect, authority, being, deity, component (of a body), consideration (meaning), creature, dignity, element (of the law), form (accidental), good, happiness, importance, intention, matter (meaning a principle or thing to do), meaning of a term (signification), merit (reward), object, object of sight, ordination (to the good), origin, part (of a virtue— _pars secundaria_), passion, perfection, place, point (in an analysis), potency, precept (in law), principle, punishment, quality, reason, relation, sense (qualified meaning), substance, thing, truth, understanding, use, virtue (contrasted with principal virtue), way (manner/mode), or willed thing.

Collectively, this evidence suggests that while “secondary” functions as a broad, flexible qualifier in Aquinas’s vocabulary, “instrumental” serves as his precise technical

## 3.2. The Question of Terminological Clarity (2): The Bestowal of Esse

A second layer of ambiguity arises when we consider the specific effect of _esse_. While Thomists universally agree that God alone is the Principal Cause of being, a significant debate exists regarding creaturely participation in its bestowal. The approach adopted here—following the “universal” reading—posits that when creatures bestow _esse_ upon other created beings, they act as instrumental causes under the Principal Causality of God. Aquinas applies this distinction in _SCG_ II, 21, no. 5., stating that “[t]he existence of every substance other than God is caused … [No] substance, then, could possibly be the cause of existence otherwise than as instrumental and as acting by virtue of another agent.” He makes a similar point in _De pot_. 3, 4, co.:

[T]o give being as such must be the effect of the first cause alone by its own proper power, and whatever other cause gives being does this insofar as in it is the power and activity of the first cause, and not by its own proper power. This is just as an instrument performs an instrumental action not by the power of its own proper nature, but by the power of its mover.[^c01-68]

However, it is worth noting that in these instances, instrumental causes of _esse_ are juxtaposed with God as “another agent” (_alterius_) or “mover” (_movens_), rather than explicitly as the “Principal Cause.” Conversely, in other contexts, Aquinas applies the distinction between secondary causes and the primary causality of God to this same issue. In _SCG_ III, 66, no. 5, he writes that “the perfection of the power of the secondary agent is due to the primary agent. … Therefore, the act of being is what secondary agents produce through the power of the primary agent.” Similarly, in _De pot_. 5, 1, obj. 7, Aquinas asserts:

Lower generating causes … cause the existence of the things they generate—not, however, as principal and first causes, but as secondary causes. Now, the first cause of existence, which is God, does not give the things he produces the beginning of existence except through the mediating secondary causes mentioned.

This variance in terminology leaves us with an open question: does Aquinas intend a strict metaphysical difference between being a “secondary cause” of existence and an “instrumental cause” of existence, or are these terms interchangeable in this specific context?

## 3.3. All Created Agents as Instrumental Causes

The ambiguity regarding _esse_ leads to the broader assertion that all created causality is arguably instrumental. At several points, Aquinas maintains that all natural created agents are instruments with respect to God. In _SCG_ III, 100, no. 3, he asserts that “since God is the primary agent … all things that come after Him are like instruments for Him.” Similarly, in _SCG_ II, 89, no. 14, we read: “every active power of nature is compared to God as an instrument to the primary and principal agent” (see also _SCG_ I, 13, no. 15).

On other occasions he characterizes the entirety of nature (or the irrational part of it) as an instrument of God: “God is the first agent in all natural activities, whatever nature does, it brings about as the instrumental agent cooperating with the first agent, which is God” (_In IV Sent._ 5, 1, 2, co.). Again, “it is not incompatible with nature that the natural movement be from God as the First Mover, inasmuch as nature is an instrument of God moving it” (_ST_ I-II, 6, 1, ad 3). Once again, Aquinas says: “For the entire irrational nature is in comparison to God as an instrument to the principal agent” (_ST_ I-II, 1, 2, co.).

Most importantly, in _De pot._ 3, 7, co., where Aquinas explains the four ways in which God can be regarded as the cause of the agency of creatures—as already discussed in Section 1—he invokes the same rhetoric and defines the fourth of those ways in reference to the category of instrumental causation. He applies it to all created beings saying that “one thing causes the action of another as a principal agent causes the action of its instrument; and in this way too we must say that God causes every action of a natural thing” (_De pot._ 3, 7, co.) He further elucidates the depth of this influence by adding: “God is the cause of every action inasmuch as every agent is an instrument of the divine power operating. … [T]he power of the higher cause will be more immediate to the effect than the power of the lower cause, since the power of the lower cause is not joined to its effect save by the power of the higher cause” (_De pot._ 3, 7, co.).[^c01-69]

What remains crucial is that in none of those passages does Aquinas seem to explain in greater detail the precise manner in which all created beings are instruments with respect to God. In what way do they bring about more than they can by nature? To what extent are they a particular subset of secondary causes (presuming this is a broader category)? As mentioned in Section 1, it seems justified for him to conclude that all creatures are instruments with respect to _esse_ that they bestow in any of their agency. However, this is not clearly expressed in the passages cited above. Nevertheless, substantive evidence for this position is found in _SCG_ II, 21, no. 5; _SCG_ III, 66, no. 5; and—most importantly—in _De pot._ 3, 4, co. As demonstrated in the previous section, the passage from this article explicitly links the bestowal of _esse_ to the power of the First Mover, offering a clear foundation for this view.

This framework, however, introduces a certain level of confusion that needs to be resolved. On the metaphysical level, all created efficient agents are instruments of God’s principal agency in their bestowing _esse_ upon other creatures that they either bring into existence or affect with their action. In this sense they do bring about an effect that is beyond their natural capacities, since God and only God is the ultimate source of all created _esse_. At the same time (as noted in Section 2.4, note 53), in the ordinary course of nature, the particular kinds of _esse_ that they bring about (i.e. this or that being, as distinguished from being as such) are proportionate to their nature, such that their causation does not violate the principle of proportionate causation, which

## 3.4. Particular Categories of Instrumental Causes

To resolve the confusion regarding the universal instrumentality of nature, we must distinguish between two modes in which God works through created instruments: the first (lower) order of natural causation and the second (higher) order of supernatural operation.

Within the first order, we find _Universal Metaphysical Instrumentality_ (_General Instrumentality_). In this mode, the _esse_ brought about is proportionate to the created agent’s natural or artistic dispositions—for example, an animal begetting an offspring or a carpenter fashioning a wooden chair. Taking the former as a paradigmatic instance, in biological generation, the parents act as principal agents regarding the specific form of the offspring, but they are instruments of God in the production of the offspring’s _esse_. In this sense, they are “instruments” precisely because the bestowal of existence is an effect that exceeds their natural power and belongs properly to the First Cause. While the mode of the effect is natural, the act of being is a participated divine effect that requires the creature to act by virtue of the divine power.

However, within this same order of nature, we find _Natural and Artistic Instrumentality_ **(** _Proper Instrumentality_ **),** where an instrument is elevated to produce an effect that exceeds its own capacity. In other words, the specific mode of _esse_ such instruments bring about is disproportionate with respect to their natural form. In this mode, we must distinguish between (1) natural instruments, which possess an intrinsic principle of motion—comprising active animate (1a) and inanimate (1b) agents—as well as (1c) passive natural instruments. These should be further differentiated from artistic instrumentality, which makes use of artifacts (2). An example of (1a) is semen, the instrument of the father which, upon joining an ovum, gives rise to a child. While it has an inclination toward a biological end, it is moved by the generative power of the principal agent to a specific effect of a higher order. An Aristotelian example of (1b) is the elemental heat (fire) used by the soul to digest. Even a passive natural instrument (1c)—such as a chemical compound used by a chemist in technological production—has a “proper” power that inclines it toward a specific agency that produces, under proper conditions, a natural effect that exceeds its own isolated capacity. In contrast, artistic instruments, i.e. artifacts (2) lack an intrinsic principle toward the specific effect of the art. An ax, for example, has a natural principle to fall downward due to gravity, but it possesses no internal principle to “carve” a table. That specific direction is entirely extrinsic, residing in the mind and hand of the principal agent. See Table 1.

In these cases, created causes are instruments not merely because they participate in the general bestowal of _esse_, but because they participate in bringing about a specific effect that is disproportionate to their own essence in a radical sense **.** In the case of the ax and the bench, the effect exceeds the nature of the instrument (metal) but remains proportionate to the nature of the created principal agent (the human art). Consequently, our analysis yields a threefold typology of instrumentality in Aquinas, which resolves the apparent terminological inconsistencies:

1. _Universal Metaphysical Instrumentality_ (_General Instrumentality_): Every created agent acts as an instrument of God in the bestowal of _esse_, since existence as such is an effect proportionate only to the Divine nature. This reflects the ontological truth that every moved mover is an instrument of the First Mover, even when the effect is entirely proportionate to the creature’s nature.[^c01-71]

2. _Natural and Artistic Instrumentality_ (_Proper Instrumentality_): An instrument is elevated to produce an effect that exceeds its own isolated capacity. In other words, the specific mode of _esse_ produced is disproportionate to the instrument’s natural form. This category distinguishes between natural instruments (active animate and inanimate, as well as passive) which possess an intrinsic principle of motion, and artistic instruments (artifacts) which receive their specific direction entirely from the extrinsic power of the principal agent. While the former cooperate _via_ an internal aptitude, the latter lack an intrinsic principle toward the effect of the art, receiving their specific direction entirely from the extrinsic power residing in the mind and operation of the principal agent. In all such cases, the resulting form remains proportionate to the integrated natural power or artistic skill of the principal agent or the causal matrix involved.

3. _Supernatural Instrumentality_: God elevates a created agent to produce an effect—such as grace, miracles, or the communication of Revealed Truth—that unequivocally exceeds the capacity of the entire order of created nature. In this mode, the instrument is elevated to mediate a trans-natural effect that remains disproportionate not only to the instrument’s own essence but to any natural causal power, being proportionate solely to the Divine Nature of the Principal Agent.[^c01-72]

Distinguishing these levels allows us to affirm the universal dependence of all causes on God without collapsing the specific distinction between the order of nature and the supernatural order of grace and direct divine operation **.** This distinction is vital for understanding divine action in the created universe, particularly when addressing complex issues such as evolutionary transitions, where the interplay between creaturely disposition and divine concurrence is paramount.[^c01-73]

## 3.6. Proper Disposition and Proportionality of Instruments

However, the structural classification of instruments—whether they are united to or separate from the agent—describes only their ontological status and does not exhaust the metaphysical requirements for their operation. Beyond the mere mode of their application, Aquinas insists on two specific operative conditions: the instrument must be properly disposed and it must be proportionate to the principal agent. As he states, “however perfect be the principal agent employing an instrument, it will produce an imperfect effect, if the instrument be not well disposed also” (_ST_ I-II, 58, 3, ad 2).[^c01-75] Again, he explicitly asserts that “the instrument should be proportionate to the agent” (_SCG_ IV, 74, no. 3).[^c01-76]

While Aquinas does not offer a systematic definition of these terms in a single locus, his broader usage suggests that proper disposition refers to the intrinsic structural, qualitative, and operative conditions that enable an entity to receive and transmit the influence of the first or principal cause. Proportionality, in turn, refers to the functional aptitude or ordered correspondence between the instrument and the principal agent relative to the specific effect to be produced. This explains why even a perfect principal agent produces an imperfect effect if the instrument is poorly disposed (see _ST_ I-II, 65, 3, ad 1). In other words, for Aquinas, an instrument must be properly disposed and proportionate precisely because—as mentioned in Section 2.3—it makes a real contribution to the action. The instrument receives the motion of the principal agent according to its own mode, thereby limiting or specifying the final effect through its own form. This point leads us to question the precise nature of that contribution.

## 3.7. The Causal Efficacy of the Instrument

The necessity of proper disposition and proportionality leads to the question of attribution: does the instrument truly cause the effect, or is it merely a passive conduit? In his early commentary on the _Sentences_, Aquinas seems to minimize the instrument’s role, stating that “an action is not attributed to an instrument, but to a principal agent, by whose power the instruments are applied to their work inasmuch as they are moved by it” (_In IV Sent._ 1, 1, 1, sol. 4, co.). Again, he asserts that “an action is not attributed properly to an instrument, but to the principal agent” (_In IV Sent._ 5, 2, 2, sol. 2, ad 1).

However, later in his career, his language shifts toward a more robust affirmation of the instrument’s causal reality. In _ST_ III, 56, 1, ad 2, we find him saying that “it is appropriate to say that an instrument truly causes the effect intended by the principal cause: since it [the principal agent] works through this instrument, such instrument causes the effect.”

This development should not be seen as a contradiction, but as a refinement and precision. The early texts emphasize the source of the power, ensuring that the effect (which exceeds the instrument’s nature) is not falsely attributed to the instrument as if it were the principal agent. The later texts, however, clarify that insofar as the instrument is moved, it is not merely an occasion—a passive condition that provides the opportunity for the principal agent to act—but a true efficient cause. The instrument modifies the action of the principal agent—limiting it or specifying it—precisely through its own form (as mentioned in Section 3.6). Therefore, the effect is “truly caused” by the instrument, but only _qua_ instrument.

## 3.8. Elevation and Causal Contribution of the Instrument Revisited

But this affirmation of genuine efficacy leads to an immediate tension. If the instrument causes the effect _via_ its own limited form (as seen in the previous section), how does it account for the perfection that transcends that form? To explain how the instrument acts as a true cause of an effect beyond its nature, we must return to the concept of “elevation” (discussed in Section 2.1). A legitimate metaphysical question arises: what is this elevation? Hugon speaks of “a temporary influence from the agent,”[^c01-77] but what is the ontological status of this influence?

Solère, arguing from a critical perspective, warns against treating this elevation as “magical”—as if a new accidental form were suddenly superimposed as a stable, permanent addition to the substance of the instrument.[^c01-78] Moreover, if elevation were a permanent quality, it would transform the nature of the instrument, which is impossible. Instead, for Aquinas (as mentioned in Section 2.1), this elevation must be understood as an _intentio_ —that is, a directed power—or _virtus instrumentalis_. It is a “flowing quality” (_qualitas fluens_), an active and transitory motion (_motio actuosa et transiens_) received from the principal agent, rather than inherent property (like heat in fire). As Aquinas explains, “the instrumental power has a being that passes from one thing into another, and is incomplete; just as motion is an imperfect act passing from agent to patient” (_ST_ I, 62, 4, co.).

From a Thomistic perspective, this distinction attempts to resolve the difficulty concerning the causal contribution. The instrument contributes two things: (1) its proper operation stemming from its natural form (e.g. the sharpness of the saw, the heat of the fire, or a pencil’s capacity to leave graphite on a surface), which performs a dispositive role by preparing the matter; and (2) the instrumental power, which it possesses transiently while being moved by the principal agent. This dual contribution is the hallmark of _Proper Instrumentality_, where the instrument’s own active power—its natural disposition to act in a certain way—is not bypassed, but rather assumed and elevated by the _virtus fluens_ to achieve the higher effect. As analyzed in Sections 2.3 and 2.4, this interaction underpins the distinction between the instrument’s dispositive role (preparing the matter according to its own mode) and its perfective role (reaching the final form that exceeds its isolated capacity).

Solère identifies the same tension inherent in this distinction, noting that Aquinas sometimes wavers on whether the instrument only prepares the matter or actually introduces the final form.[^c01-79] For instance, in _SCG_ II, 89, no. 15, Aquinas suggests that natural heat only prepares the body for the soul. Yet, he later affirms that accidental forms can act instrumentally to produce substantial effects: “An accidental form acts by virtue of the substantial form, as if it were its instrument; in this way, Aristotle says in book II of _On the Soul_ that the heat of fire is the instrument of the nutritive power. Therefore, there is nothing impossible in the action of an accidental form terminating in a substantial form” (_De pot._ 3, 8, ad 13).

This transition from a purely dispositive model—where the instrument is limited to preparing the matter—to a perfective model—where the instrument participates in the induction of the final form itself—marks a significant development in his thought. The full systematic implications of this shift, particularly how it safeguards the metaphysical unity of the causal act against Scotistic critiques of “dual agency,” will be resolved in Section 3.9.

Solère seems to suggest that even if we accept Thomistic definition of “flowing power,” a significant metaphysical tension remains regarding the mechanics of this operation. As he points out, Aquinas’s model implies that the instrument performs two simultaneous actions: its own proper action (call it _ϕ_, e.g. the saw cutting) and the instrumental action (call it χ, e.g. the producing of the bench form). According to Aquinas, action χ does not replace action _ϕ_; they are performed simultaneously. The subordinated cause executes its instrumental function (χ) by carrying out its own operation (_ϕ_).[^c01-80]

While this preserves the reality of the instrument’s nature, it introduces a complex “multi-tasking” requirement for the creature. Duns Scotus, as Solère notes, radically simplifies this by arguing that each cause has only one type of action—the one it is capable of by nature.[^c01-81] For Scotus, the instrument does not need to perform a “super-added” action. It simply acts, and the principal agent utilizes that action within a larger scheme.

This leaves us with an open question. The Thomistic posit of a _virtus fluens_ successfully avoids the absurdity of a permanent form (the “magic” objection), but does it fall prey to the objection of complexity? Is the “transitory power” a necessary metaphysical reality required to explain the unity of the causal operation (see Section 2.5), or is it—as Scotus suggests—an unnecessary multiplication of entities? While Aquinas’s account offers a profound vision of the instrument participating in the power of a principal cause—whether created or Divine—the Scotistic critique challenges us to consider whether this participation requires a distinct ontological status for the instrument’s motion (to account for the “dual effect” described by Silva in Section 1.3) or merely a teleological and operative subordination of the instrument’s own power to that of the principal agent.[^c01-82]

## 3.9. Causal Unity Revisited

The Scotistic critique brings us back to the question concerning the unity of the causal operation. Does the principal agent perform one action and the instrument another, or is there a single action? Aquinas appears to give conflicting answers. As previously noted in the context of the _virtus fluens_ (see Section 2.5), in _ST_ I, 105, 5, ad 2, he insists on unity: “one action does not proceed from two agents of the same order. But nothing prevents the same action from proceeding from a primary and a secondary agent.” Similarly, in _ST_ I-II, 14, 3, ad 4, he states, “the principal agent and the instrument are, in a way, one cause, since one acts through the other.”[^c01-83] However, in _Q. de An._ 2, Aquinas seems to maintain numerical distinction:

Even though, of two different substances, one can be the cause of the operation of the other, as the principal agent is for an instrument, nevertheless the action of the principal agent is not numerically identical with the action of the instrument, since the action of the principal agent is to move the instrument, while the action of the instrument is to be moved by the principal agent and to move something else.

Solère suggests that a way of resolving this apparent contradiction is to interpret the _De Anima_ passage as referring to the relations involved in the causal chain, or perhaps to instrumental causality in a very broad sense—such as when one billiard ball strikes another. Therefore, the very act of disposing the matter toward this specific form rather than another is already the effect of the _virtus fluens_. ## 3.10. Dual Agency Revisited

The logical consequence of the debate regarding causal unity brings us back to the most significant metaphysical objection: the philosophical difficulty inherent in the notion of “dual agency.” If we accept the strong Thomistic claim that the principal agent is the immediate cause of the entire action, and that the instrument is also a true cause, do we not end up with a superfluity? While Section 3.9 questioned how the actions are unified, this section questions why the instrumental agent is necessary at all if the principal is sufficient in its power to produce the effect.

This objection touches upon the broader problem of secondary causation. Although, as discussed in Section 3.1, the precise taxonomic relationship between secondary and instrumental causes in Aquinas is fluid, the metaphysical charge of redundancy applies to both, yet with specific intensity regarding instruments. Solère notes that the Scotistic critique often targets the Thomistic claim of “joint action” by reducing the instrument to a mere secondary cause that acts according to its own nature.[^c01-88] To resolve this, we must distinguish between the distinct orders of causation and the modes of necessity at play.

The first issue concerns the ontological “plane” of action. When speaking of transcendental causality (God _vis-a-vis_ creatures), God is the Primary and Principal Cause who possesses infinite active power. Because God and the creature operate on vertically distinct planes of causation—where God acts as the transcendent ground of the action’s very being (_esse_) while the creature acts as the immediate, finite agent—their agency is non-competitive. In this transcendental context, the instrument is not an “addition” to God’s power but a participation in it. Consequently, the claim that an effect is “100% from God and 100% from the creature” is non-competitive.

However, a different dynamic emerges in immanent (creaturely) causality, where the principal and instrumental causes reside on the same horizontal plane of finite being, such as a human writing with a pencil. This nuance exposes the precise point of the Scotistic critique. Recalling the objection raised in Section 3.8 regarding the complexity of _virtus fluens_, we can now see how Scotus resolves the “dual agency” dilemma without recourse to elevation. As Solère explains, Scotus avoids the problem by reclassifying these instances into categories that act strictly by their own nature. For inert tools like a pencil, Scotus posits “borrowed instrumentality”: since the tool has no natural active power to write, it does not need to be “elevated”; its causality is simply the motion of the agent passing through it.[^c01-89] Conversely, for agents that do possess active power (like heat or a hand), Scotus views them either as dispositive causes acting fully by their own nature to prepare matter, or as organic parts acting within a whole. In neither case is there a need for a single action constituted by two agents _via_ a mysterious “flowing power.” Rather, the unity of the act is merely operative or teleological, rather than ontologically shared through elevation.[^c01-90]

The second issue involves the nature of necessity. Unlike God, the created principal agent is not sufficient to produce the effect alone; one cannot write on paper without the hand and the pencil. Thus, in the immanent order, the instrument is characterized by an absolute necessity. Conversely, in the transcendental order, God’s use of instruments involves only suppositional necessity: while God requires no medium and is intrinsically sufficient to produce any effect independently, once He has freely ordained a natural order of causes, the instrument becomes necessary only on the supposition of

The lingering critical question is whether instrumental causality can be applied to both divine and created agents without equivocation. The Thomistic response lies in the analogy of being (_analogia entis_). Far from being a conceptual compromise, the use of analogy provides a unique explanatory power: it acknowledges that while the relationship is not identical (univocal) nor totally different (equivocal), it is truly proportional. Within this proportional framework, the concepts of elevation and _virtus fluens_ serve as a necessary metaphysical defense of the instrument’s genuine participation. Rather than being “magical” or redundant additions, they provide the ontological grounding for the instrument to reach an effect that—while exceeding its isolated nature—remains natively its own through its union with the principal cause. By acknowledging that the perfect unity of operation in the transcendental order is the primary analogate, which is only imperfectly mirrored in the immanent order from which our concepts are derived, one preserves the structural insight of instrumentality as a coherent metaphysical principle without collapsing the infinite qualitative distance between the Creator and the craftsman.

# Conclusion

The investigation into Aquinas’s account of instrumental causality reveals a system that is complex but ultimately coherent. At its foundation, this coherence depends on the distinction between the order of agents and the mode of their operation. While the “primary/secondary” distinction identifies the vertical hierarchy of being—where the secondary cause is totally dependent on the primary for its existence—the “principal/instrumental” distinction explains the horizontal unity of their action. In this framework, the instrument is not a separate agent acting alongside the principal, but a participant in the principal’s own power, functioning as a single, composite principle of operation. We began by observing the apparent fluidity in Aquinas’s terminology—specifically the overlapping usage of “secondary” and “instrumental” causes—which has often led to confusion regarding the precise nature of divine action. However, as we have argued, this ambiguity is not a sign of incoherence but of a subtle metaphysical distinction required by the analogy of being.

By mapping Aquinas’s thought across the _Opera Omnia_, we have proposed a threefold typology that resolves these tensions: (1) _Universal Metaphysical Instrumentality_ (_General Instrumentality_), where every creature serves as an instrument of God in the bestowal of _esse_; (2) _Natural and Artistic Instrumentality_ (_Proper Instrumentality_), governing the specific, intra-cosmic effects of particular agents; and (3) _Supernatural Instrumentality_, reserved for miracles, the sacraments, and the order of grace. This typology allows us to affirm that while all creatures are instruments of God in the order of being, they function according to distinct modes of operation in the order of nature and grace. The coherence of this system rests on the “twofold agency” of the instrument, which performs its proper natural action while being simultaneously elevated by a “flowing power” (_virtus fluens_) to produce an effect exceeding its native capacity. This mechanism ensures that the instrument is never a passive occasion but a genuine

participant in the causal event, whether it is a fire producing heat (natural), a saw cutting wood according to a specific design (artistic), or a sacrament conferring grace (supernatural).

Furthermore, our engagement with the critical objections raised by the Scotistic tradition has clarified the metaphysical stakes of the debate. While the notion of “dual agency” presents genuine philosophical difficulties—particularly when applied to immanent, creaturely causes—we have argued that the Thomistic insistence on _virtus fluens_ remains necessary to preserve the unity of the causal order. The Scotistic alternative, which reduces instrumentality to mere “borrowed” or “dispositive” causality, risks fragmenting the action and severing the intimate link between the Creator and the operation of the creature.

While this paper has restricted itself to the metaphysical anatomy of instrumentality, this distinction provides the necessary architectural framework for understanding complex scientific phenomena, such as evolutionary transitions, where natural disposition and divine elevation must coexist without competition.

In the final analysis, the Thomistic doctrine of instrumentality is a testament to the generosity of the Creator. Unlike human artisans who employ tools to overcome their limitations, God employs created instruments solely to communicate his perfection. By elevating secondary causes to the status of true instruments, God does not diminish His own glory but reveals it, imparting to creatures the “dignity of causing” (_dignitas causalitatis_) and allowing them to be true collaborators in the unfolding of His providential Government of the universe.
