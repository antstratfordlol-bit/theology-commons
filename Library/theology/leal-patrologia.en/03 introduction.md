### INTRODUCTION {#leal-patrologia.introduction-to-patristi.introduction}
The *Fathers of the Church* are those Christian writers who possess the following characteristics: antiquity (1st-8th centuries), orthodoxy of doctrine, holiness of life. The term *ecclesiastical writers* is applied instead to those who lack some of the last two characteristics, but who are witnesses of faith and *Tradition* in those first centuries in which dogma is fixed and theology is born. For this reason it is customary to call all of them *Fathers of the Church* indiscriminately, especially when speaking in the plural (this is what *the Instruction on the Study of the Fathers of the Church* does). This avoids the possible confusion between Tertullian and Pelagius, Origen and Arius, Theodore of Mopsuestia and Eutyches: they are all *ecclesiastical writers*, but not all are witnesses of faith and *Tradition*.

The main interest in knowing the Fathers of the Church resides in the fact that they are witnesses of *Tradition*. The dogmatic constitution *Dei Verbum* states: "The affirmations of the holy Fathers attest to the life-giving presence of this Tradition, whose riches are transfused in the practice and life of the Church that believes and prays. And then he adds: "It is clear, then, that sacred Tradition, sacred Scripture and the Magisterium of the Church, by the most wise disposition of God, are so connected and joined together that none of these realities exist without the others, and all together, each in its own way, under the action of a single Holy Spirit, contribute effectively to the salvation of souls". Or, as Vincent of Lérins wrote in the year 434 in his *Commonitorium*: "Precisely in the Catholic Church, in fact, we must with every care adhere to what has been believed everywhere, always and by everyone; this is truly and properly Catholic.” The statement can be taken as a patristic definition of *Tradition*.

*Patrology* is the study of the life, works and doctrine of the Fathers of the Church. The term *Patristic* is usually used to indicate the philosophical and theological thought of these authors, but many times it is also used with the same meaning as Patrology. The first Christian writings we possess form the New Testament. Those written immediately after, or not included in the canon, are precisely the first patristic writings.

Patrology can be divided into two parts, different periods and different groups of writings. The year 325 marks the division between the two parts: pre-Nicene and post-Nicene patrology. The first period, until the year 180, in which the first writings in Latin appear, includes the *Apostolic Fathers* (until the middle of the second century), so called because of their closeness to the Apostles, who wrote in Greek and addressed a Christian public, with familiar tones to build it; and the *Greek Apologists* (in the central fifty years of the second century) who wrote *apologies* (i.e. defenses) of the doctrine or customs of Christians, directed particularly to pagan public opinion. Towards the middle of the second century other kinds of writings, such as the *anti heretical literature* (Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Tertullian), born from the need to defend the faith against heterodox opinions, generally Gnostic. In this period we also find the *apocryphal literature* of the New Testament and the *acts of the martyrs*, sometimes simple transcriptions of the minutes of a judicial trial to the martyrs. Then, even before the Council of Nicaea (325), in Alexandria appeared masters of great level who inaugurated a school of thought, the so-called *school of Alexandria*, with Clement of Alexandria and Origin († 253). In Rome, on the other hand, we find Minucius Felix, Hippolytus and Novatian, who around 253 separated from the Church. In Africa, Tertullian and Cyprian († 258) are active. After the Council of Nicaea (the second part of Patrology) we can distinguish three periods marked, respectively, by this Council, by that of Chalcedon (451) and by the end of the patristic era (VI-VIII centuries). For greater clarity, you can consult the chronology that we have attached at the end of this manual, on pages 367, for the first part, and 366, for the second. The logic of chronology is that of the clock: you can read from above and right down and then left upwards.

Which Bible did the Fathers of the Church know? Except for the few who knew Hebrew, the Old Testament is the one translated into Greek (also mentioned in the New Testament), especially the so-called LXX version, translated in Alexandria by the Jews of the Diaspora. An ancient tradition tells of seventy translators who, while working separately, produced the same text in seventy copies. Instead, for the New Testament, as we have mentioned, the canon was still in the formation phase and, therefore, there could not be unanimity. Eusebius of Caesarea, in the fourth century, wrote in his *Storia Ecclesiastica* 25,1-7: "It is well at this point to summarise the New Testament writings examined so far. In first place must be put the divine scriptures of the four Gospels, followed by the Acts of the Apostles, 2. Then come the Letters of Paul, followed by the letter transmitted as the First of John and the First of Peter. These are followed, if it seems right, by the Revelation of John, on which we will report the different opinions at the appropriate time. 3. These are the authentic writings [ομολογουμενοις (homologouménois)]. Among those subject to controversy [αντιλεγομενων (antilegoménon), but known to most, are handed down the letter attributed to James, that to Judas, the second of Peter, and the so-called second and third of John, whether they are to be attributed to the evangelist or to one of his namesakes. 4. Among the non-testamentary writings [νοθοις (nothois)] are the Acts of Paul, the so-called Pastor, the Apocalypse of Peter, the so-called Letter of Barnabas, the so-called Didache of the Apostles, and also, as I said, the Apocalypse of John, if it seems the case: some, as I said, deny its authenticity, while others include it among the Apostle's authentic writings. 5. Now among the latter, some have included the Gospel according to the Jews, which is especially pleasing to the Jews who have accepted Christ. 6. All of these would be among the controversial texts (αντιλεγομενων), which had to be listed to distinguish authentic works, true and accepted by all on the basis of ecclesiastical tradition (τας τε χατα την εχχλησιαστιχην παραδοσιν αηθεις χαι απλαστους χαι ανομολογημενας γραφας) by those that are not only not testamentary (αντιλεγομενας), but also of discussed authenticity, and yet known to most ecclesiastical writers, to be able to distinguish the authentic from those written by heretics under the name of the apostles: the Gospels of Peter, Thomas, Matthias and some others besides these, the Acts of Andrew, John and the other apostles. None of the ecclesiastical authors who succeeded one another in time have considered them worthy of mention in their works: 7. Not only the character in which these writings are composed, far different from the apostolic one, but also the thought and doctrine exposed in them, far from true orthodoxy, make it clear that they were composed by heretics. Therefore they should not be counted even among the non-testamentary works (νοθοις), but rejected as completely senseless and unholy". And Irenaeus said that the gospels were four like the cardinal points: as the church, which is Catholic because it is found everywhere, so the gospels are four because they are those admitted by the whole Church.

Around the year 175 we find a very particular writing called *Diatessaron*, that is a harmony of the four gospels probably written in Syriac by Tazianus. Scholars think that at that time there were already translations in this language and that the harmony had previous editions already used by Justin, therefore composed around the year 140. Latin witnesses of these harmonies are preserved and perhaps we should date the first Latin translations of the gospels to the same date, although the most direct testimony is found in the Acts of the Scillitan martyrs around 180.

How did the Fathers of the Church read the Bible? A first statement, shared by all, is that the first meaning of a text of Scripture is its *literal sense*. Literal exegesis rests fundamentally on the study of language, habits and historical circumstances; its purpose is to understand the precise meaning of words and expressions that Scripture uses, and it needs no theorization. But it is not the only way to read Scripture, since the most attentive readers have always discovered a second sense beyond the first. It is the so-called allegorical exegesis, which must be specified in different types. As a general rule it can be said that the Old Testament is read in the light of the New. The New Testament, for example, often "interprets" some of the events and expressions of the Old Testament in reference to itself. This exegesis has been used by Jesus himself (the bronze snake is the figure of Jesus' death), by the writings of the New Testament (Adam and Eve are the figure of Christ and the Church) and by the oldest Fathers of the Church, as we will see later.

This exegesis is called *typological*: the connection between people, events, places and institutions of the Old Testament with the New Testament in which a connection is established according to which the former does not only mean itself, but also the other, and instead the latter includes or realizes the former. In this sense, Adam, Moses, Abraham [...] are the figure or *type* of Christ; Eve of Mary; the serpent raised up in the desert of the crucifixion [...] The second element, that of the New Testament, is called *antitype*. The typological interpretation will open the way to the allegorical one, from which it is often not easy to distinguish it, because they are different with respect to the content, but not with respect to the hermeneutical procedure (a reading made at a higher level than that of the letter).

*Allegorical exegesis*, instead, has its precedents in Greek culture. On the one hand, it is a characteristic of language that it is possible to enclose senses of different libel in the same literary expression. Moreover, in the face of the contrast between pagan mythological tales, a method of interpretation had long since become widespread, according to which the stories of Homer and Hesiod had no historical meaning, but represented the virtues and values as stories and genealogies. There are two differences between this interpretation made by the pagans, on the one hand, and by the Jew Philon and the Christians, on the other: first, the material on which they worked, since the Greeks interpreted texts created only by men, the Jews and the Christians inspired texts; second, the way in which the two levels are superimposed: for the Greeks the allegorical sense cancels the literal one, for Jews and Christians both levels coexist.

Now, in the Christian sphere, especially with the school of Alexandria, the allegorical sense is preferred, to the point of believing that every passage of Scripture has this sense. Some sketches of allegorical exegesis taken to the extreme can be found in the numerological exegesis used by some Fathers of the Church prior to the school of Alexandria: the letter of Ps. Barnabas (IX.8) interprets the circumcision of the three hundred and eighteen men (cf. Gen. 14,14; 17,23-27) saying that eighteen is indicated with *iota* = ten and *eta* = eight (initials of Jesus) and the cross is depicted in *tau* which also means three hundred, indicating Jesus in the first two letters and the cross in the other.

The search for allegorical meaning, which is important to understand Scripture in all its depth, is difficult and requires a special intellectual and, above all, supernatural sensitivity; it is very much exposed to subjectivism, which is not the case with the search for merely literal and historical meaning, which is in any case prior and necessary. Hence the reaction of some, their resistance to allegorical exegesis and their desire to bind themselves to a literal exegesis, although not necessarily shallow; or the relatively frequent attitude of those who use allegorical interpretation to draw moral or ascetic consequences from sacred texts, for edifying purposes. This type of interpretation applied to the Bible originated in Alexandria at the hands of the Jew Philon and continued in Christian authors. The systematic commentaries on Scripture and the sub-genres of allegorical exegesis begin: spiritual, moral, tropological, etc., and the Bible is then used as a starting point.

It will be our task, through the reading of the proposed texts, to analyze the exegesis that every Father of the Church makes. Not in all the texts presented here will we find exegesis in the proper sense. But the simple quotation of biblical texts is a very important fact for us, since it gives us news of what texts were considered inspired and shows us the great consideration in which the connection between the Old Testament and the New was held.
