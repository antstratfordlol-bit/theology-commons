### ISIDORE OF SEVILLE (560-636) {#leal-patrologia.introduction-to-patristi.isidore-of-seville-560-6}
Isidore received a classical and Christian education from his brother Leandro, whom he succeeded as bishop of Seville. His most famous work is the *Etymologies*, a name that comes from one of the books into which it is divided, an encyclopedic compendium of the knowledge of his time, in which it deals with mining up to theology and which is based on the works of the best known classical writers of that time; this work had an immediate success, its diffusion was rapid and its authority was maintained for centuries. The three *Books of Sentences* are a manual of dogmatic, spiritual and moral theology composed essentially of texts by St. Augustine and St. Gregory; it was the first Book of Judgments, ordered in a manner similar to the Middle Ages, that of Peter Lombard. For his work he has been called the last philologist of Antiquity and the founder of the Middle Ages.

Isidore prefers allegorical exegesis, but he always keeps it under control, since - he affirms - not all the writings of the law and the prophets are covered by mysteries, but those that have this sense are in relation with the other texts. In some passages he will also employ numerology. As sources for his treatment, he points to Origen, Victorinus, Ambrose, Jerome, Augustine, Fulgentius and John Cassian. With Isidore we enter the encyclopedic realm of the Middle Ages.

We offer a text on the importance of reading Scripture.

#### The Sentences III, 7-9
VII. Prayer

7.1. This is the remedy for one who is violently agitated by the temptations of vices: as many times as he is attacked by any vice, so many times let him dedicate himself to prayer, because frequent prayer stifles the assault of vices.

2. We must turn our soul to prayer and supplication with perseverance, until we manage to overcome, with an inflexible resolve, the shameless flatteries of carnal desires, which with their clamor molest our senses, and we must insist until, with our constancy, we obtain victory; for lazy prayers do not succeed in obtaining, even from man, what they want.

3. When one prays, he calls the Holy Spirit to himself; but, as soon as he arrives, the temptations of demons, which introduce themselves into the minds of men, but cannot bear his presence, immediately flee away. 4. Prayer belongs to the heart, not the lips; for God does not heed the words of him who beseeches him, but looks at the heart of him who prays. And if the heart prays in silence and the voice is silent, even if it escapes men, it cannot escape God, who is present to the conscience. It is better to pray with the heart in silence, without the voice being heard, than with words alone without the mind being concentrated on them. 5. One must never pray without tears; for the memory of sins generates sadness; while we pray, we recall our shortcomings and then we realize more clearly that we are guilty. Therefore, when we are in the presence of God, we must groan and weep, remembering how serious are the evils we have committed and how merciless are the torments of hell we fear.

6. The disposition of spirit, such as it appears in prayer, must be maintained even after it; for prayer is of no use if one commits again what one must still ask forgiveness for. He undoubtedly achieves the desired fruit of prayer, he who does not repeat, by sinning, what he asks, by praying, to be washed away. 7. Our spirit is heavenly and contemplates God well in prayer, when it is not held back by any earthly worry or error. In its nature it is connected with good, when it goes out of it, it remains upset. 8. Prayer is pure when, when it is to be recited, the worries of the world do not intervene to prevent it; instead, that soul which, during prayer, is occupied with the thoughts of the world, is far from God. Therefore, we truly pray when we do not draw thoughts from elsewhere; but very few are those who have prayers of this kind, and, even admitting that some have them, it is still difficult that they always have them. 9. A mind that, before prayer, remaining extraneous to God, occupies itself with illicit thoughts, when it arrives at it, is immediately invested with the imaginations it had just followed before, which block access to prayer, so that the free spirit does not rise to the desire for heaven. 10. First of all, therefore, it is necessary to purify the soul and separate it from the thoughts of temporal things, so that the conscience, with all the pure acuteness of its aspiration, may direct itself to God in truth and sincerity. Indeed, we believe that we can truly obtain the gifts of God when, at the moment of prayer, we are present in the gratuitousness of feeling.

11. There are many ways in which concentration in prayer is interrupted, when the vanities of the world introduce themselves, through negligence, into the soul of anyone who attends to prayer. The devil introduces the thoughts of worldly worries into human minds with greater intensity precisely when he sees someone praying. 12. There are two ways that prevent prayer from enabling anyone to obtain what he asks, and that is if one continues to commit evil or if one does not forgive debts to those who are lacking towards him. When one has cleared away this double evil from himself, he can immediately dedicate himself, without worries, to praying with urgency and freely raising his spirit to those goods he desires to obtain with prayer. 13. Whoever receives a wrong, let him not cease to pray for those who do it to him; moreover, according to the Lord's thought, he sins who does not pray for his enemies (Mt. 5:44). 14. Just as no medicine benefits a wound, if the iron still remains inside it, so the prayer of him who still keeps the grudge in his mind or hatred in his heart is of no use. 15. The affection towards God of him who prays must be so great as not to doubt the result of prayer; and then we pray in vain if we do not have the confidence of hope. Everyone, therefore, let him ask, as the Apostle says, without having any hesitation in faith; for he who hesitates is like a wave of the sea driven by the wind and dissolved (James 1:6).

16. The distrust of obtaining what is asked for in prayer arises when the conscience feels that it is still wandering in the realm of affection for sin; for he who is still lazy in observing the commandments of God and takes pleasure in remembering sin cannot have secure confidence in his prayer. 17. He who turns away from the precepts of God does not deserve to receive what he asks for in prayer; he does not obtain the good he asks from him whose law he does not obey; if we do what God commands, we undoubtedly obtain what we ask; for, as it is written: «He who turns away his ear from hearing the Law, his prayer will be detestable» (Prov. 28:9) 18. God inevitably strongly supports these two relationships, where action is supported by prayer and prayer by action. Hence Jeremiah says: "Let us lift up our hearts and hands to God" (Lam. 3:41). He lifts up his heart and hands to God who lifts up prayer with works; for whoever prays but does not work lifts up his heart but does not lift up his hands; whoever works but does not pray lifts up his hands but does not lift up his heart. But since it is necessary to work and pray, it is rightly said concerning both: Let us lift up our hearts and hands to God, to avoid being blamed for our hearts if we neglect the commandments, as we try to achieve our salvation either with prayer alone or with activity alone. 19. After we have done a good deed, let us pray with tears, so that the humility of prayer may obtain the merit of the action. 20. He who lifts up his open hands to God and, while praying, boastfully displays his actions, like the Pharisee who prayed with boasting in the Temple and wanted to praise himself rather than God for his righteous deeds (cf. Lk. 18:11-12), commits a sin.

21. The prayer of some turns into sin, as is written of Judas the traitor (Ps. 109 [108]:7; cf. Acts 1:20). For he who prays boastfully, craving human praise, not only does his prayer not erase sin, but it turns into sin itself. This happens to Jews and heretics, who, though they seem to fast and pray, their prayer does not benefit them as a merit of purification, but rather turns into sin. 22. Sometimes the prayer that the elect make in their afflictions is delayed (in its answer), so that the wickedness of the wicked may increase [as they persecute the good]. But when the righteous are answered in due time, it happens for the salvation of those who mistreat them, so that, while they are helped with a temporary remedy, the eyes of the wicked may open to conversion. Therefore, the fire of the three young men remained cool, so that Nebuchadnezzar recognized the true God (Dan. 3:50). As the prophet also says in the Psalms: "Deliver me because of my enemies" (Ps. 69 [68]:19). 23. The reason why the prayers of some are answered with considerable delay is that, while they are delayed, they are stimulated more energetically, receiving a greater mass of rewards; we have the example of winter frosts and the delay of the harvests, in which the seeds sown germinate later, the more abundantly they grow into harvest. 24. Whenever, when we pray, we are not quickly answered, let us look at our behavior, to attribute this delay to divine justice and our own fault. 25. Sometimes the fact that, although we pray perseveringly, we are not promptly answered, falls within the number of our advantages, not among our misfortunes. Often God does not answer many as they wish, to answer them for their salvation.

26. Many, when they pray, are not answered, because God provides for them better things than they ask, as usually happens to children, who pray to God not to be beaten at school. But they are not granted the result of what they ask, because such a request cannot be answered if one wants to achieve progress. It is no different with some chosen ones; they implore God regarding certain advantages or adversities of this life, but divine Providence does not care at all about what they desire in this world, since it promises them better things for eternity. 27. Prayer is poured out more appropriately in private settings and achieves its purpose more when it is expressed with only God as witness. 28. It is instead characteristic of hypocrites, when they pray, to show themselves to the public eye; what they get is not to please God, but to gain fame from men. 29. Men are not answered by God because of a flood of words, as if they were trying to bend him with verbal exuberance; the varied address of the one who prays does not gain his favor, but the pure and sincere tension of prayer. 30a. It is good to always pray with the heart and it is also good to glorify God by making the voice resound in spiritual hymns. 30b. It is nothing to sing with the voice alone without the concentration of the heart; but, as the Apostle says, (one must live in the Spirit) "singing in our hearts" (Eph. 5:19), that is, singing not only with the voice but also with the heart. Therefore, Paul also says elsewhere: "I will pray with the spirit, but I will also pray with the understanding" (1 Cor. 14:15).

31. As prayers direct us, so the willing application to the Psalms brings us enjoyment. The use of psalm singing brings us the profit of consoling sorrowful souls, making temperaments more amiable, recreating the dissatisfied, awakening the indolent, inviting sinners to cry tears of repentance. Indeed, however hard the hearts of men may be, immersed in the senses, as soon as the sweetness of a psalm resounds, it bends their soul to live piety effectively. 32. Although it should move the Christian not the modulation of the voice, but only the divine words that are uttered in the psalms, yet, I don't know how, from the modulation of the song a greater compunction of the heart is born. Indeed, there are many who, moved by the sweetness of the song, weep bitterly over their sins and are more inclined to tears in those passages where the sweetness of the psalmist resounds more pleasantly. 33. Prayer is poured out only in the present life as a remedy for sins, but the singing of psalms represents the eternal praise of God, which will extend throughout eternal glory, as it is written: "Blessed are those who dwell in your house, they will praise you forever and ever" (Psalm 84 [83], 5). Whoever undertakes the task of translating this activity into practice, and does so with fidelity and concentration of spirit, associates in some way with the angels.

VIII. Reading

1. Prayers purify us, readings instruct us; both are good when they can be done; when both cannot be done, it is better to pray than to read. 2. He who wants to be always with God must pray often and also read often; indeed, when we pray, we speak to God, when we read instead, it is God who speaks to us. 3. All progress comes from reading and meditation; indeed, what we do not know we learn from reading and what we have learned we preserve with meditations. 4. The reading of Sacred Scripture brings us a double gift, both in that it instructs the intellectual capacity of the mind, and in that it abstracts man from the vanities of the world and leads him to the love of God. Indeed, often, stimulated by His word, we are turned away from the desire for worldly life and, inflamed with the love of wisdom, the vain hope of our mortal condition seems so worthless, the more, by reading, eternal hope is illuminated. 5. There is a double problem for those who devote themselves to reading: first, how to understand the Scriptures; second, how to expound them fruitfully and nobly. Indeed, everyone will first come to understand what he reads, subsequently he will acquire the ability to expound well what he says.

6. A bold reader will be more fully inclined to put into practice what he reads than to know it. Indeed, not knowing what you desire to know involves less pain than not putting into practice what you have known. Indeed, just as, by reading, we desire to know, so, knowing what is right, we must put into practice what we have learned. 7. The law of God involves both a reward and a punishment for those who read it; a reward for those who, living well, observe it, a punishment instead for those who, living badly, despise it. 8. All those who, in their practice of life, depart from the precepts of God, every time they have had the opportunity to read or hear these same precepts of God, perceive themselves reproached in their innermost being and experience disturbance, because they feel reminded of what they do not do and, on the testimony of their conscience, they feel an accusation within themselves. Hence the plea that the prophet David expresses, saying: "Then I shall not be ashamed, when I have respect unto all thy commandments" (Psalm 119 [118], 6). Indeed, whoever experiences great confusion when, both reading and hearing them, looks to those commandments of God that, living, he despises. Since with the meditation of the commandments he has learned to know them, he feels reproached in his conscience for not having put into practice in his actions what he has learned about God's commands.

IX. Assiduity in Reading

1. No one can know the meaning of Sacred Scripture, if he is not familiar with reading it, as it is written: "Love her and she will exalt you; she will glorify you when you have embraced her" (Proverbs 4:8). 2. The greater the assiduity one has with the Sacred Scriptures, the richer the content of the intelligence he will draw from them, just as the earth, which is cultivated more diligently, yields more abundant fruit. 3. The more a man ascends to any art, the more the art itself descends to him, as is written in the Law: "Moses went up to the mountain, and the Lord came down" (Exodus 19:3, 20). 4. As for the collected occupation of the spirit, it is certain that only he who has recalled his soul from the management of earthly tasks and has applied himself to the Sacred Scriptures with a solicitous familiarity will succeed in penetrating acutely into the secrets of divine precepts. Indeed, just as a blind man and one who both the blind and the sighted can certainly walk, but not with equal ease, as the blind, going towards a place he does not see, is exposed to bumping into things, while the sighted avoids obstacles and recognizes the place he must go. Likewise, he who is clouded by the fog of earthly preoccupations, if he tries to deeply scrutinize divine mysteries, cannot do it, because, in the haze of his anxieties, he cannot see. Only he who abstracts himself from the external burdens of the world and is deeply rooted, entirely, in the meditation of the Scriptures can achieve this goal. 5. Some possess the sharpness of understanding, but neglect to dedicate themselves diligently to reading and despise, leaving aside, what they could have known by reading. Others, however, have a love of knowledge, but are hindered by the slowness of their intelligence; they nevertheless, through assiduous reading, come to understand what individuals endowed with genius, due to their laziness, have not known.

6. An individual who is rather slow of intellect, however, strengthens himself, if not by a natural process, at least by assiduous reading; indeed, although there is dullness of intuition, frequent reading increases intelligence. 7. Just as he who is slow to understand, however, in the name of his diligent application, receives the reward of his praiseworthy zeal, so he who neglects the sharpness of intelligence that God has given him, is liable to condemnation, because he despises the gift he has received and abandons it idle through laziness. 8. Some, by God's disposition, receive the gift of knowledge that they neglect, so that they may be more severely punished for what has been entrusted to them. Those who are intellectually slower find with difficulty what they desire to know, so that, in relation to the great effort they have faced, they receive, in recompense, a very great reward.
