### AUGUSTINE (354-430) {#leal-patrologia.introduction-to-patristi.augustine-354-430}
Saint Augustine is, according to many, the greatest of the Fathers and one of the most brilliant minds of humanity. His influence on later authors and the fact that studies on him multiply immeasurably are confirmation of this. Saint Augustine has a literary production that, due to its volume, can only be compared to that of Origen, but, unlike what happened with the works of the latter, only a few of Augustine's writings have been lost. Of the 93 titles (232 books) from which he himself quotes in his Retractions written three years before his death, only about ten have not reached us. Augustine's style makes it impossible to forget his ancient dedication to rhetoric: his language abounds in ideas and parables, often difficult to translate, but he always responds with great sincerity to what he intends to communicate. Nor does he disdain to use an almost vulgar language when he deems it appropriate for the audience.

The Augustinian sources for knowing the life of this Father of the Church are the following:

1. The Confessions, an autobiographical work, certainly his most popular work through the centuries, written shortly after being elected bishop, begun in 397 (after the death of Ambrose) and finished around 400, and which has an extraordinary value, not only to follow the spiritual evolution of Saint Augustine and to know him intimately, but also as an ancient testimony of many aspects of human psychology, the reactions of man towards himself, towards others and towards God.

2. The Retractions, written at the end of his life (427), represent a judgment, with corrections, on his previous works and a description of the reasons that led him to write them, a fundamental work for knowing the soul and the motives that inspired the writings of Saint Augustine.

3. Letters

4. Most important is also the Life of Saint Augustine by Possidius, written between 431 and 439, of exceptional historical value.

The life of Saint Augustine can be divided into several periods.

I. From birth to conversion (354-386)

Augustine was born on November 13, 354, in Tagaste (Numidia). He studied in Tagaste, Madaura, and Carthage. He knew both Latin and Greek culture, but not the Punic language. He became a Christian at 33, from Monica, his mother, but he did not follow her advice immediately. At 17 he read Cicero's Hortensius (106-43 BC), a lost work, but which was an exhortation to philosophy, through which he began his return to God. Shortly after, having left the Scripture, he was struck by its rough style, dictated by a master of rhetoric. In short, he began to teach grammar and rhetoric, first in Tagaste (374), then in Carthage (375-383), and in Rome (384), and finally, in Milan (autumn 384-estate 386). During this period (around 380) he wrote his first works, De Pulchro et Apto (lost).\*\*

In that era was the epoch of the Manichean doctrine, which offered a unique solution to the problem of evil, dividing reality into two opposing principles, light and darkness (good and evil), which coexist in man although they should separate to be powerful. This separation, according to the Manicheans, can be achieved by repressing three seals: the mouth (which dictates words and impure slanders), the hands (which do manual labor, especially the cultivation of the fields and the use of animals), and the senses (which vetoes carnal and matrimonial passions, and impedes the light from being torn away from matter).

Augustine was not firmly convinced at the bottom of Manichaeism, and through his study he came to realize the inconsistency of the Manichean religion, especially through a dialogue with the Manichean bishop Faustus, who made him fall into skepticism, since he avoided the prediction of Ambrose, who offered him the key to interpreting the Old Testament and to come to the conviction that the authority on which faith is founded is the Scripture, read as a letter.

II. From conversion to the episcopate (386-396)

In October 385 he went to Cassiciaco (Perhaps Oderna in Como, in Brianza) to prepare for baptism. He managed to resolve all his career and marriage problems. The reading of Platonic texts helped him resolve the problems of materialism and evil. First, he treated the problem of evil: interpreting evil as male, coming from the will of the male; not as coming from God, neither directly nor indirectly, in that male is a deficiency of being and therefore not a necessary cause.

In November, he wrote several philosophical treatises. Some main points of Augustine's philosophy stand out, especially two. The first is that the interiority of man is the same as the reality of the world, in that by studying the soul, one understands more than anything else what is outside of man. The second is the notion of participation: all beings have something in common that leads them to participate in a Supreme Being, unique, which is God. Secondly, faith is necessary for intellectual activity (crede ut intelligas), but faith is sought with intelligence, because faith affirms anything incredible. These two expressions summarize Augustine's thought regarding the relation between faith and reason.

In March of the following year, in Milan, he initiated catechumenate and baptism from Ambrose on April 25, during the vigil of Easter. After baptism he decided to return to Africa for life in the service of God. He left Milan and Ostia, Monica, his mother, became ill unexpectedly and died. Augustine decided to return to Rome, interested in a monastic life and continuing his studies. Some of this period is treated in some of his works. He stayed in Rome until August 388; then left for Africa and settled in Tagaste, where he put his program of life into practice. He wrote several other works, especially against the Manicheans, such as De Genesi contra Manichaeos (388-389). In this epoch is born his son Adeodatus (between 389 and 391).

In 391 he arrived in Hippo to found a monastery, but unexpectedly the bishops conferred on him the priestly ordination. These are the periods of his first sermons. From August 28 to 29, 392, he had a dispute in Hippo with the Manichean Fortunatus. He then began to write, translating Latin commentaries on the Bible, and composing Enarrationes in Psalmos.

(Most of) the 32 Psalms were finished in 392, but the work was interrupted until 420 and finished by Partem Donati.

17 January 395 Theodore and Evodius were nominated imperial archdeacons of Onorio (Occidente). This same year, or the following (395-396) he received the episcopal consecration, being sent for any task of Valerio of Ippona, and in 397 he became a bishop. Having left the monastery of the laity, he founded a monastery of clerics in the house of the bishop.

3. From the episcopate to polemical activity (396-410)

His episcopal activity was intense: he was interrupted only by trials and audiences to judge cases, he took care of the poor, the sick and the orphans, the formation of the clergy, the organization of monasteries, he made long journeys to be present at African councils, he intervened without restraint against the Manicheans, the Donatists, the Pelagians, the Arians, the pagans.\*\*

Donatism, from the name of one of its first founders, Donato, at the beginning was a schism, but then became a tendency to convert heretics. In essence, those who had maintained a correct behavior during the persecution of Diocletian refused to accept as pastors those who, during the persecution, had vacillated in the faith for some time. This led to the creation of a hierarchy of its own, to end up duplicating the number of bishops. The only ones who, under imperial authority, repeatedly decided in favor of the Catholic hierarchy. But the Donatist bishops did not respect the imperial decisions, until finally, under Constantine, they were forced to a violent repression. Donatism did not have influence outside Africa, but it was still alive two hundred years after Saint Augustine, and then it was swept away with the Muslim invasions, initiated by the Vandals and continued with the Muslims.

Augustine (395-396) had to debate with Proculus, Donatist bishop of Ippona and with other Donatists. His teachings on the Church were particularly luminous. The Church of the Donatists did not want to be the true Church, because in it there was no unity, sanctity, apostolicity or catholicity, outside of which there is no salvation. But if in their bosom we are good, the Church is good. He responds to the Donatists and to the pagans in general, Augustine insists on the validity of the sacraments in general, even if administered by unworthy ministers, because their effectiveness comes from Christ and not from man. It belongs to this first phase of his Christian doctrine (completed in 426), which we could call an introduction to the Sacred Scripture, as it treated pagan needs that could lead to studying the Bible, on how to interpret and use the Bible in preaching, and at the same time proposes a scheme of Christian education that uses the culture of the pagans. As for his interpretation, it is interesting to observe that he attributes to the literal sense only exegetical letters and in his operative works, in preaching he prefers the allegorical method and the mystical sense.

There are also of this period other works against the Manicheans and the Confessions (397-400). In 399 he began De Trinitate. The exposition of Saint Augustine on the Trinity is clearer and more profound than that of the preceding Fathers. He understands the principle of exploring the interiority of man to understand his relationship with God, and explains that the Trinity has three faculties: memory, intelligence and will. For this, the Son proceeds from the Father by way of intelligence, just as he had already said Tertullian, and the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son by way of will or love. The public debate with Felix the Manichean was held on January 7-12, 404.

4. Polemical activity against Pelagians (410-433)

On August 24, 410, Alaric sacked Rome and Pelagius passed through Ippona. Augustine was the soul of the council of 411 against the Catholics and Donatists and the main architect of the solution of the controversy against Pelagians. At the end of the year, he received news of the diffusion of the Pelagian doctrines and the condemnation of Celestius in a process to which Augustine had not participated.

The controversy against grace developed only through three paths and specialists, without mobilizing the people in one or the other sense. In a schematic way it could be said that Pelagius sustains that man can do good and avoid evil with his own strength; and that the sin of Adam is not transmitted to his descendants, but for them it was simply an evil example. In Africa, Pelagius clashed with the opposition of Saint Augustine, whose motives of the controversy developed explanations that were becoming increasingly harsh towards the Pelagian doctrine. These explanations were essentially the appeal of Adam for us in a state of original justice, of goodness, so that Adam sinned for himself and his descendants with original sin: all humans are therefore corrupt, since that sin remained in Adam and became transmitted to the human mass. This corruption is transmitted by generation. The original sin removes a separation from God, to which the only remedy is baptism. The human necessity of divine help is needed to perform good works that are humanly impossible.

A work particularly noted is The City of God, begun in 413 and finished in 426. It is, in part, an apology, where the classical theme that Christians are the cause of all evils, in this case of the ruin of the Roman Empire, is refuted with abundant data and arguments. Furthermore, it offers a vision of universal history, certainly as it knows, designing a drama that does not lack sense: its thread follows the development of the struggle between the city of God and the earthly city, from faith to incredulity, from good to bad, which are part of human nature or which God has abandoned. Those who are part of one or the other city are mixed, both in society and, definitively separated, at the final judgment.

In the last period of the life of Augustine, there is a predominance of works against the Pelagians. From 413-415 we have De natura et gratia. In the year 416 Augustine participated in several meetings (September-October), in which Pelagius and Celestius were condemned. On January 27, 417, Innocent I condemned Pelagius and Celestius. On March 18, the following year, Zosimus, bishop of Rome, reconsidered the case of Pelagius, announcing that if he sinned he had appealed to the Holy See. Augustine then wrote a letter from Africa and Rome to the Pelagians, and in 418 Pelagius and Celestius were excommunicated and expelled from Rome. In that same year he wrote the Enchiridion (Handbook) to Zosimus, which solemnly condemned Pelagianism.

Augustine continued to clarify various aspects of polemics. From 426-427 he wrote *De gratia et libero arbitrio* and 428 *De natura et gratia contra Pelagium*. On August 28, 430, at the probable time of the assault on Hippo by the Vandals, he died. Subsequently, the Catholic cathedrals, whose bodies were transported first to Sardinia and then to Pavia. The works had a wide diffusion and popularity in the following centuries. The thought of Saint Augustine influenced in a profound and extensive way philosophical and theological conceptions, law and political and social life. Augustine is one of the great architects of Europe, through his influence in medieval culture and in the following ones.

From a literary point of view, Augustine rarely overlaps the two levels of reading of the ancient Testament, but in cases where he does, he gives an interesting novelty. Indeed, he adopts a typology in two areas: historical and actualizing, in a way that, for example, the figure of the Hebrews (Jacob and Esau), the two peoples [...] are acquiring a second value within the life of the Church, representing two classes of Christians of different spiritual tenor and vitality but of one ardor. His interpretation of the Psalms is Christological, full of rubrics. The only interpretation that is truly interesting to Augustine is that spiritual, presented without any allegory. This does not mean that he does not imply allegory, but he prefers, in this case, the etymological and symbolic approach.

We present four texts of Augustine. The first, taken from *De doctrina Christiana*, to which Augustine refers to the interpretation of Tyconius, a theoretical treatise on the interpretation of Scripture, and speaks of examples. His purpose is to clarify and define the most difficult interpretations. The methodological criterion is then the discovery of the most significant meaning, gradually guiding the reader from the literal to the more difficult spiritual reading. The second text, one of the few comments on Genesis, is an example of how one should interpret some passages of the Old Testament. From Discourse 330/A, we receive an example of moral interpretation. To Psalm 140, Augustine gives us another example of how to interpret certain biblical passages.

#### On Christian Doctrine II,8.12-16,23
*The books accepted by the Church*

8.12. But let us now go back to consider the third step mentioned, for it is about it that I have set myself to speak and reason as the Lord shall grant me wisdom. The most skillful interpreter of the sacred writings, then, will be he who in the first place has read them all and retained them in his [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm), if not yet with full understanding, still with such [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) as reading gives — those of them, at least, that are called *canonical*. For he will read the others with greater safety when built up in the belief of the [truth](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15073a.htm), so that they will not take first possession of a weak [mind](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10321a.htm), nor, cheating it with dangerous falsehoods and delusions, fill it with prejudices adverse to a sound understanding. Now, in regard to the canonical Scriptures, he must follow the judgment of the greater number of Catholic churches; and among these, of course, a high place must be given to such as have been thought worthy to be the seat of an apostle and to receive epistles.

Accordingly, among the canonical Scriptures he will judge according to the following standard: to prefer those that are received by all the Catholic churches to those which some do not receive. Among those, again, which are not received by all, he will prefer such as having the sanction of the greater number and those of greater authority, to such as are held by the smaller number and those of less authority. If, however, he finds that some books are held by the greater number of churches, and others by the churches of greater authority (though this is not a very likely thing to happen), I think that in such a case the authority on the two sides is to be looked upon as equal. (…)

9.14. In all these books those who [fear](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06021a.htm) God and are of a meek and [pious](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12748a.htm) disposition seek the [will](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15624a.htm) of [God](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06608a.htm). And in pursuing this search the first rule to be observed is, as I said, to [know](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) these books, if not yet with the understanding, still to read them so as to commit them to memory, or at least so as not to remain wholly [ignorant](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07648a.htm) of them.

Next, those matters that are plainly laid down in them, whether rules of life or rules of [faith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05752c.htm), are to be searched into more carefully and more diligently; and the more of these a man discovers, the more capacious does his understanding become. For among the things that are plainly laid down in Scripture are to be found all matters that concern [faith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05752c.htm) and the manner of life — to wit, hope and [love](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09397a.htm), of which I have spoken in the previous book.

After this, when we have made ourselves to a certain extent familiar with the language of Scripture, we may proceed to open up and investigate the obscure passages, and in doing so draw examples from the plainer expressions to throw light upon the more obscure, and use the evidence of passages about which there is no [doubt](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05141a.htm) to remove all hesitation in regard to the doubtful passages. And in this matter memory counts for a great deal; but if the memory be defective, no rules can supply the want.

10.15. Now there are two causes which prevent what is written from being understood: its being veiled either under unknown, or under ambiguous signs. Signs are either proper or figurative. They are called proper when they are used to point out the objects they were designed to point out, as we say *bos* when we mean an ox, because all [men](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09580c.htm) who with us use the Latin tongue call it by this name. Signs are figurative when the things themselves which we indicate by the proper names are used to signify something else, as we say *bos*, and understand by that syllable the ox, which is ordinarily called by that name; but then further by that ox understand a preacher of the gospel, as Scripture signifies, according to the apostle's explanation, when it says: You shall not muzzle the ox that treads out the grain.

11.16. The great remedy for [ignorance](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07648a.htm) of proper signs is [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of languages. And men who speak the Latin tongue, of whom are those I have undertaken to instruct, need two other languages for the [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of Scripture, Hebrew and Greek, that they may have recourse to the original texts if the endless diversity of the Latin translators throws them into [doubt](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05141a.htm). Although, indeed, we often find Hebrew words untranslated in the books as for example, [Amen](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01407b.htm), Halleluia, Racha, [Hosanna](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07472b.htm), and others of the same kind. Some of these, although they could have been translated, have been preserved in their original form on account of the more sacred authority that attaches to it, as for example, [Amen](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01407b.htm) and Halleluia. Some of them, again, are said to be untranslatable into another tongue, of which the other two I have mentioned are examples. For in some languages there are words that cannot be translated into the idiom of another language. And this happens chiefly in the case of interjections, which are words that express rather an emotion of the mind than any part of a thought we have in our mind. And the two given above are said to be of this kind, Racha expressing the cry of an [angry](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01489a.htm) man, [Hosanna](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07472b.htm) that of a joyful man.

But the [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of these languages is necessary, not for the sake of a few words like these which it is very easy to mark and to ask about, but, as has been said, on account of the diversities among translators. For the translations of the [Scriptures](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/index.html) from Hebrew into Greek can be counted, but the Latin translators are out of all number. For in the early days of the [faith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05752c.htm) every man who happened to get his hands upon a Greek manuscript, and who thought he had any [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm), were it ever so little, of the two languages, ventured upon the work of translation.

12.17. And this circumstance would assist rather than hinder the understanding of Scripture, if only readers were not careless. For the examination of a number of texts has often thrown light upon some of the more obscure passages; for example, in that passage of the [prophet](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12477a.htm) Isaiah, one translator reads: And do not despise the domestics of your seed; another reads: And do not despise your own flesh. Each of these in turn confirms the other. For the one is explained by the other; because flesh may be taken in its literal sense, so that a man may understand that he is admonished not to despise his own body; and the domestics of your seed may be understood figuratively of [Christians](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/03712a.htm), because they are spiritually born of the same seed as ourselves, namely, the Word.

When now the meaning of the two translators is compared, a more likely sense of the words suggests itself, viz., that the command is not to despise our kinsmen, because when one brings the expression domestics of your seed into relation with flesh, kinsmen most naturally occur to one's mind. Whence, I think, that expression of the apostle, when he says, If by any means I may provoke to emulation them which are my flesh, and might save some of them; [Romans 11:14](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/rom011.htm#verse14) that is, that through emulation of those who had [believed](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm), some of them might [believe](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm) too. And he calls the [Jews](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08399a.htm) his flesh, on account of the relationship of blood.

Again, that passage from the same [prophet](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/12477a.htm) Isaiah: If you will not [believe](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm), you shall not understand, another has translated: If you will not [believe](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm), you shall not abide. Now which of these is the literal translation cannot be ascertained without reference to the text in the original tongue. And yet to those who read with [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm), a great [truth](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15073a.htm) is to be found in each. For it is difficult for interpreters to differ so widely as not to touch at some point. Accordingly here, as understanding consists in sight, and is abiding, but [faith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05752c.htm) feeds us as babes, upon milk, in the cradles of temporal things (for now we walk by [faith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05752c.htm), not by sight); [2 Corinthians 5:7](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/2co005.htm#verse7) as, moreover, unless we walk by [faith](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05752c.htm), we shall not attain to sight, which does not pass away, but abides, our understanding being purified by holding to the [truth](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15073a.htm) — for these reasons one says, If you will not [believe](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm), you shall not understand; but the other, If you will not [believe](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm), you shall not abide.

18. And very often a translator, to whom the meaning is not well known, is deceived by an ambiguity in the original language, and puts upon the passage a construction that is wholly alien to the sense of the writer. As for example, some texts read: Their feet are *sharp* to shed blood; [Romans 3:15](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/rom003.htm#verse15) for the word ὁζύς among the Greeks means both *sharp* and *swift*. And so he saw the [true](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15073a.htm) meaning who translated: Their feet are swift to shed blood. The other, taking the wrong sense of an ambiguous word, fell into [error](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05525a.htm).

Now translations such as this are not obscure, but false; and there is a wide difference between the two things. For we must learn not to interpret, but to correct texts of this sort. For the same reason it is, that because the Greek word μόσχος means a calf, some have not understood that μοσχεύματα [Wisdom 4:3](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/wis004.htm#verse3) are shoots of trees, and have translated the word calves; and this [error](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05525a.htm) has crept into so many texts, that you can hardly find it written in any other way. And yet the meaning is very clear; for it is made evident by the words that follow. For the plantings of an adulterer will not take deep root, is a more suitable form of expression than the calves; because these walk upon the ground with their feet, and are not fixed in the earth by roots. In this passage, indeed, the rest of the context also justifies this translation.

13.19. But since we do not clearly see what the actual thought is which the several translators endeavor to express, each according to his own ability and judgment, unless we examine it in the language which they translate; and since the translator, if he be not a very learned man, often departs from the meaning of his author, we must either endeavor to get a [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of those languages from which the [Scriptures](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/index.html) are translated into Latin, or we must get hold of the translations of those who keep rather close to the letter of the original, not because these are sufficient, but because we may use them to correct the freedom or the [error](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05525a.htm) of others, who in their translations have chosen to follow the sense quite as much as the words. For not only single words, but often whole phrases are translated, which could not be translated at all into the Latin idiom by any one who wished to hold by the usage of the ancients who spoke Latin. And though these sometimes do not interfere with the understanding of the passage, yet they are offensive to those who feel greater delight in things when even the signs of those things are kept in their own purity.

For what is called a solecism is nothing else than the putting of words together according to a different rule from that which those of our predecessors who spoke with any authority followed. For whether we say *inter homines* (among [men](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/09580c.htm)) or *inter hominibus*, is of no consequence to a man who only wishes to [know](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) the facts. And in the same way, what is a *barbarism* but the pronouncing of a word in a different way from that in which those who spoke Latin before us pronounced it? For whether the word *ignoscere* (to pardon) should be pronounced with the third syllable long or short, is not a matter of much concern to the man who is beseeching [God](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06608a.htm), in any way at all that he can get the words out, to pardon his [sins](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14004b.htm). What then is purity of speech, except the preserving of the custom of language established by the authority of former speakers?

20. And men are easily offended in a matter of this kind, just in proportion as they are weak; and they are weak just in proportion as they wish to seem learned, not in the [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of things which tend to edification, but in that of signs, by which it is hard not to be puffed up, [1 Corinthians 8:1](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/1co008.htm#verse1) seeing that the [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of things even would often set up our neck, if it were not held down by the yoke of our Master. For how does it prevent our understanding it to have the following passage thus expressed: *Quæ est terra in quo isti insidunt super eam, si bona est an nequam; et quæ sunt civitates, in quibus ipsi inhabitant in ipsis?* And I am more disposed to think that this is simply the idiom of another language than that any deeper meaning is intended. Again, that phrase, which we cannot now take away from the lips of the people who sing it: *Super ipsum autem floriet sanctificatio mea*, surely takes away nothing from the meaning. Yet a more learned man would prefer that this should be corrected, and that we should say, not *floriet*, but *florebit*. Nor does anything stand in the way of the correction being made, except the usage of the singers.

Mistakes of this kind, then, if a man does not choose to avoid them altogether, it is easy to treat with indifference, as not interfering with a right understanding. But take, on the other hand, the saying of the apostle: *Quod stultum est Dei, sapientius est hominibus, et quod infirmum est Dei, fortius est hominibus*. If any one should retain in this passage the Greek idiom, and say, *Quod stultum est Dei, sapientius est hominum et quod infirmum est Dei fortius est hominum*, a quick and careful reader would indeed by an effort attain to the [true](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/15073a.htm) meaning, but still a man of slower intelligence either would not understand it at all, or would put an utterly false construction upon it. For not only is such a form of speech faulty in the Latin tongue, but it is ambiguous too, as if the meaning might be, that the folly of men or the weakness of men is wiser or stronger than that of [God](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06608a.htm). But indeed even the expression *sapientius est hominibus* (stronger than men) is not free from ambiguity, even though it is free from solecism. For whether *hominibus* is put as the plural of the dative or as the plural of the ablative, does not appear, unless by reference to the meaning. It would be better then to say, *sapientius est quam homines*, and *fortius est quam homines*.

14.21. About ambiguous signs, however, I shall speak afterwards. I am treating at present of unknown signs, of which, as far as the words are concerned, there are two kinds. For either a word or an idiom, of which the reader is [ignorant](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07648a.htm), brings him to a stop. Now if these belong to foreign tongues, we must either make inquiry about them from men who speak those tongues, or if we have leisure we must learn the tongues ourselves, or we must consult and compare several translators. If, however, there are words or idioms in our own tongue that we are unacquainted with, we gradually come to [know](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) them through being accustomed to read or to hear them. There is nothing that it is better to commit to memory than those kinds of words and phrases whose meaning we do not [know](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm), so that where we happen to meet either with a more learned man of whom we can inquire, or with a passage that shows, either by the preceding or succeeding context, or by both, the force and significance of the phrase we are [ignorant](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07648a.htm) of, we can easily by the help of our memory turn our attention to the matter and learn all about it. So great, however, is the force of custom, even in regard to learning, that those who have been in a sort of way nurtured and brought up on the study of [Holy Scripture](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/index.html), are surprised at other forms of speech, and think them less pure Latin than those which they have learned from Scripture, but which are not to be found in Latin authors.

In this matter, too, the great number of the translators proves a very great assistance, if they are examined and discussed with a careful comparison of their texts. Only all positive [error](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05525a.htm) must be removed. For those who are anxious to [know](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm), the [Scriptures](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/index.html) ought in the first place to use their skill in the correction of the texts, so that the uncorrected ones should give way to the corrected, at least when they are copies of the same translation.

15.22. Now among translations themselves the Italian (*Itala*) is to be preferred to the others, for it keeps closer to the words without prejudice to clearness of expression. And to correct the Latin we must use the Greek versions, among which the authority of the [Septuagint](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/13722a.htm) is pre-eminent as far as the [Old Testament](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14526a.htm) is concerned; for it is reported through all the more learned churches that the seventy translators enjoyed so much of the presence and power of the [Holy Spirit](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07409a.htm) in their work of translation, that among that number of men there was but one voice. And if, as is reported, and as many not unworthy of confidence assert, they were separated during the work of translation, each man being in a cell by himself, and yet nothing was found in the manuscript of any one of them that was not found in the same words and in the same order of words in all the rest, who dares put anything in comparison with an authority like this, not to speak of preferring anything to it? And even if they conferred together with the result that a unanimous agreement sprang out of the common labor and judgment of them all; even so, it would not be right or becoming for any one man, whatever his experience, to aspire to correct the unanimous opinion of many venerable and learned men.

Wherefore, even if anything is found in the original Hebrew in a different form from that in which these men have expressed it, I think we must give way to the dispensation of Providence which used these men to bring it about, that books which the Jewish race were unwilling, either from religious scruple or from jealousy, to make [known](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) to other nations, were, with the assistance of the power of King Ptolemy, made [known](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) so long beforehand to the nations which in the future were to [believe](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/02408b.htm) in the Lord. And thus it is possible that they translated in such a way as the [Holy Spirit](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07409a.htm), who worked in them and had given them all one voice, thought most suitable for the [Gentiles](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/06422a.htm). But nevertheless, as I said above, a comparison of those translators also who have kept most closely to the words, is often not without value as a help to the clearing up of the meaning.

The Latin texts, therefore, of the [Old Testament](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14526a.htm) are, as I was about to say, to be corrected if necessary by the authority of the Greeks, and especially by that of those who, though they were seventy in number, are said to have translated as with one voice. As to the books of the [New Testament](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/14530a.htm), again, if any perplexity arises from the diversities of the Latin texts, we must of course yield to the Greek, especially those that are found in the churches of greater learning and research.

16.23. In the case of figurative signs, again, if [ignorance](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/07648a.htm) of any of them should chance to bring the reader to a stand-still, their meaning is to be traced partly by the [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of languages, partly by the [knowledge](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/08673a.htm) of things. The pool of Siloam, for example, where the man whose eyes our Lord had anointed with clay made out of spittle was commanded to wash, has a figurative significance, and undoubtedly conveys a secret sense; but yet if the [evangelist](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05645a.htm) had not interpreted that name, [John 9:7](https://www.newadvent.org/bible/joh009.htm#verse7) a meaning so important would lie unnoticed. And we cannot [doubt](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/05141a.htm) that, in the same way, many Hebrew names which have not been interpreted by the writers of those books, would, if any one could interpret them, be of great value and service in solving the enigmas of Scripture. And a number of men skilled in that language have conferred no small benefit on posterity by explaining all these words without reference to their place in Scripture, and telling us what [Adam](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01129a.htm) means, what Eve, what [Abraham](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01051a.htm), what [Moses](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/10596a.htm), and also the names of places, what Jerusalem signifies, or Sion, or Sinai, or Lebanon, or Jordan, and whatever other names in that language we are not acquainted with. And when these names have been investigated and explained, many figurative expressions in Scripture become clear.

#### On Genesis Against the Manicheans 17.27-22.34
*God and the parts of the body*

17.27. “And God said, ‘Let us make man to our image and likeness, and let him have power over the fish of the sea and the birds of heaven, and all cattle and wild beasts, and all the earth and all reptiles that creep upon the earth,’ ” and the rest, up to the evening and the morning by which the sixth day is completed. It is this question above all that the Manicheans raise with their endless chatter, and they taunt us for believing that man was made to the image and likeness of God. They look at the shape of our body and ask so infelicitously whether God has a nose and teeth and a beard and also inner organs and the other things we need. However, it is ridiculous, even wicked, to believe that there are such things in God, and so they deny that man was made to the image and likeness of God. We answer that the Scriptures generally mention these members in presenting God to an audience of the little ones, and this is true, not only of the books of the Old Testament, but also of the New Testament. For the New Testament mentions God’s eyes and ears and lips and feet, and the gospel proclaims that the Son is seated at the right hand of God the Father. The Lord himself says, “Do not swear by heaven, for it is the throne of God, nor by the earth, for it is his footstool.” Likewise he says that he was casting out demons by the finger of God. All who understand the Scriptures spiritually have learned to understand by those terms, not bodily members, but spiritual powers, as they do in the case of helmets and shields and swords and many other things. Hence, we should first point out to these heretics the impudence with which they attack such words of the Old Testament, since they see these things used in the New Testament as well. Or perhaps they do not see them since they are blinded in their disputes.

28. Let them know, nonetheless, that the spiritual believers in the Catholic teaching do not believe that God is limited by a bodily shape. When man is said to have been made to the image of God, these words refer to the interior man, where reason and intellect reside. From these man also has power over the fish of the sea and the birds of heaven and all cattle and wild animals and all the earth and all reptiles which creep upon the earth. For when God said, “Let us make man to our image and likeness,” he immediately added, “And let him have power over the fish of the sea and the birds of heaven,” and the rest, so that we might understand that man is said to have been made to the image of God, not on account of his body, but on account of that power by which he surpasses the cattle. For all the other animals are subject to man, not by reason of the body, but by reason of the intellect which we have and they do not have. Even our body has been made so that it reveals that we are better than the beasts and, for that reason, like God. For the bodies of all the animals which live either in the waters or on the earth, or which fly in the air, are turned toward the earth and are not erect as is the body of man. This signifies that our mind ought to be raised up toward those things above it, that is, to eternal spiritual things. It is especially by reason of the mind that we understand that man was made to the image and likeness of God, as even the erect form of the body testifies.

18.29. At times they also ask, “In what sense did man receive power over the fish of the sea and the birds of heaven and all the cattle and wild animals? For we see that men are killed by many wild animals and that many birds harm us when we want to avoid them or to capture them, though we often cannot. In what sense then did we receive power over these?” On this point they should first be told that they make a big mistake when they consider man after sin, when he has been condemned to the mortality of this life and has lost that perfection by which he was made to the image of God. But even man’s state of condemnation involves such power that he rules many animals. For though he can be killed by many wild animals on account of the fragility of his body, he can be tamed by none, although he tames very many and nearly all of them. If, then, this state of man’s condemnation involves such power, what ought we to think of that reign of his, which is promised to him by the word of God, once he has been renewed and set free?

19.30. There follows the words, “He made them male and female, and God blessed them, saying, ‘Increase and multiply, and generate and fill the earth.’” Here one is completely right to ask in what sense we should understand the union of male and female before sin, as well as the blessing that said, “Increase and multiply, and generate and fill the earth.” Should we understand it carnally or spiritually? For we are permitted to understand it spiritually and to believe that it was changed into carnal fecundity after sin. For there was first the chaste union of male and female, of the former to rule, of the latter to obey, and there was the spiritual offspring of intelligible and immortal joys filling the earth, that is, giving life to the body and ruling it. That is, man so held [the body] subject that he experienced from it no opposition or trouble. We should believe that it was this way, since they were not yet children of this world before they sinned. For the children of this world generate and are generated, as the Lord says, when he shows that we should contemn this carnal generation in comparison with the future life which is promised us.

20.31. Then God said to them, “Have power over the fish of the sea and the birds of the heaven and all reptiles that creep upon the earth.” Apart from that interpretation according to which it is clear that man is master of all these animals by reason, we can still understand this verse spiritually in the sense that we should hold in subjection all the affections and emotions of our soul, which are like those animals, and have mastery over them by temperance and modesty. For if we do not rule these emotions, they burst forth and turn into the foulest habits, carrying us off with all sorts of destructive pleasures and making us like every kind of beast. But when we rule them and hold them in subjection, they become completely tame and live in harmony with us. For the emotions of our mind are part of our nature. They are even nourished along with us by knowledge of the finest rational and moral principles of eternal life, as if by the grain and the fruit-trees and the green plants. Man’s life is happy and peaceful when all his emotions are in accord with reason and the truth. Then they are called joys and holy, chaste and fair loves. But if they are not in accord [with reason] and are managed with negligence, they tear the mind apart and dissipate it, making life most miserable. Then they are called perturbations and lusts and evil desires. We have been commanded to crucify them in ourselves with all the energy we can until death is swallowed up in victory. For the Apostle says, “Those who belong to Jesus Christ have crucified their flesh with its perturbations and desires.” We should also be warned not to understand these matters carnally from the fact that in Genesis the green plants and fruit-bearing trees were given to every kind of animal and to all the birds and to all the reptiles as food. Yet we see that lions, hawks, kites, and eagles feed only on meat and the killing of other animals. I believe this is also true of some serpents which live in sandy desert areas where there are neither trees nor grass.

21.32. Certainly we should not carelessly pass over the words of Scripture that say, “And God saw that all the things which he had made were very good.” For when dealing with individual things, it only says, “God saw that it is good,” but in speaking of all things, it was not enough to say “good” without adding “very” as well. For if prudent men consider the single works of God, they find that, individually in their own species, they have praiseworthy measures, numbers, and orders. How much more [then will this be true of] all of them together, that is, of the universe which is filled with these individual things gathered into unity? For every beauty that is composed of parts is much more praiseworthy in the whole than in a part. In the case of the human body, if we praise the eyes alone, or the nose, or the cheeks, or the head, or the hands, or the feet, and we praise the remaining beautiful parts individually and by themselves, how much more should we praise the whole body to which all the members, which individually are beautiful, contribute their beauty? If a beautiful hand, which we praise even by itself in the body, is separated from the body and loses its attractiveness, the other members also are ugly without it. The force and power of integrity and unity are so great that many good things are pleasing only when they come together and form a universe (*universum*). A universe gets its name from unity. If the Manichaeans would consider this, they would praise God, the author and creator of the universe. They would integrate into the beauty of the universe what they find displeasing in some part on account of the condition of our mortality, and they would see how God has made all things not only good, but very good. If in an ornate and polished speech we consider the individual syllables, or even the individual letters, which pass away as soon as they sound, we do not find in them what is pleasing and praiseworthy. For the whole speech is beautiful, not from the individual syllables or letters, but from all its parts.

22.33. Now let us also look at the verse that they mock with greater impudence than ignorance. Scripture says that, after completing heaven and earth and all the things he made, God rested from all his works on the seventh day and blessed the seventh day and made it holy by reason of his resting from his works. They say, “What need did God have for rest? Was he perhaps tired and worn out by the works of the six days?” They also add the testimony of the Lord, where he says, “My father works up to now,” and by this they deceive many of the uneducated, whom they try to convince that the New Testament contradicts the Old Testament. The [Jews] to whom the Lord said, “My father works up to now,” understood God's rest carnally, and in their carnal observance of the Sabbath they did not see what the meaning of that day foreshadowed. The [Manicheans], although with a different intention, equally fail to understand the mystery of the Sabbath. The former fail to understand the Sabbath because of their carnal observance, the latter because of their carnal accusation. Let each cross over to Christ so that the veil may be removed, as the Apostle says. For the veil is removed when the cloak of likeness and allegory is removed and the truth is stripped bare so that it can be seen.

34. We should first notice and learn the rule for this expression in many passages of the divine Scriptures. Scripture says that God rested from all his works which he made very good. What else does this signify but our rest, the rest, which he will give us from all our works, if we too have done good works? According to this figure of speech the Apostle also says, “We do not know what to pray for as we ought, but the Spirit himself intercedes on our behalf with ineffable groans.” The Holy Spirit, who intercedes with God on behalf of the saints, does not groan as if he were in need and experiencing distress. Rather he moves us to pray when we groan, and thus he is said to do what we do when he moves us. In the same way Scripture says, “The Lord your God tests you in order to know whether you love him.” He permits us to be tested, not in order that he, for whom nothing lies hidden, might know, but in order that he might make us know the extent of our progress in love for him. According to this same mode of speech our Lord also says that he does not know the day or the hour of the end of the world. What can there be that he does not know? He was concealing it from the disciples for their benefit, and he said that he did not know it, because he was causing them not to know by concealing it. According to this same figure he said that the Father alone knew that day, because it was the Father who made it known to the Son. This figure easily resolves many questions in the divine Scriptures for those who are familiar with this mode of expression. Our ordinary language also abounds with such expressions. For example, we call a day happy, because it makes us happy, the cold numbing, because it makes us numb, a ditch blind, because we do not see it, and a tongue polished, because it produces polished speech. Finally, we speak of a time of rest from all troubles, when we live in it at rest from them all. Scripture said that God too rested from all the works which he made very good, because we will rest in him from all our works, if we have done good works. For even our good works should be attributed to him, who calls, who commands, who shows the way of truth, who invites us to will, and supplies the power to fulfill what he commands.

#### Discourse 350/A, 1-4
*Charity can be read throughout the Scriptures. Charity renews man.*

1. We know, dearly beloved, that every day your hearts receive the healthy food offered by the word of God and the exhortations of the divine readings. However, because of the desire for love that enkindles our hearts in exchange, I must say something to you, my dear ones. And what else could I speak to you about if not charity? If someone wants to talk about it, he doesn't have to choose in the Bible some particular passage to be read; the subject is found, in Scripture, at the opening of each page. The Lord himself testifies to this, and we are informed of it from the Gospel, because when he was asked what the most important precepts of the law were, he answered: You will love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul, with all your mind 1; and: You will love your neighbor as yourself 2. And so that you would not go looking for anything else in the holy pages, he added: On these two precepts is based the whole Law and the Prophets 3. Charity renews man. Just as greed makes man old, so charity makes him new. That is why man, in the conflicts of his greed, says groaning: I have grown old in the midst of all of my enemies 4. May charity be the prerogative of the new man, the Lord Himself affirms with these words: I give you a new commandment: that you love one another 5. If, therefore, the Law and the Prophets are based on charity and the Old Testament is indicated in the Law and the Prophets, how much more the Gospel is explicitly called the New Testament! It is only about charity, since the Lord said that his command was but this: to love one another. And *new* He called this commandment. He came to renew us. He made us new men; He promised us a new and moreover eternal inheritance.

*Old Testament and charity.*

2. If by chance you are wondering why the Law is called Old Testament even though it is based on charity, when instead charity renews man and befits the new man, the reason is this. It is announced as the Old Testament because it is an earthly promise and the Lord there promises an earthly kingdom to those who worship it. But even then there were those who loved God without aiming at reward and purified his heart with the pure, poignant desire for him. These people, beyond the veils of the ancient promises, came to the intuition of the future New Testament and understood that all that was prescribed or promised in the Old Testament according to the Old Man was a foreshadowing of the New Testament; promises that the Lord would fulfill in the last times. The Apostle says it explicitly: These things happened to them in the figure and were written as a warning to us who are in the last age of history 6. The New Testament was foretold in an arcane way: it was foretold in those ancient figures. But as the time of the New Testament came, it began to be announced openly; they began to explain, to clarify those symbols: that is, how to recognize the new where the promises of the ancient were to be found. Moses was the herald of the Old Testament: he announced the Old, but sensed the New; he announced the Old to a people that was material, but he, spiritual, meant the New. The Apostles then were heralds and ministers of the New Testament, but not in the sense that beforehand there was not what would later manifest through them. Charity therefore in the Old, charity in the New, but there charity more hidden, fear more evident; here charity more evident, less fear. The more charity grows, in fact, the more the fear diminishes. As charity grows, the soul becomes more secure; when this state of security is at its highest, the fear disappears. The Apostle John also says this: Perfect charity drives out fear.

*Commentary on Psalm 36. The luckiest villains of the good in the world. Christ the hidden root of love.*

3. We have also taken the opportunity of this Psalm to speak to you, my good brothers, about charity because, as I have told you, whatever page inspired by God is read, [from it] we receive no other exhortation than that of charity. See if the divine words tend to do anything other than awaken love; see if they produce anything other than that we light up, that we become inflamed; that we consume desire, that we moan and sigh until we have reached the goal. The men who toil on earth and who struggle in the midst of very serious trials, often observe, with their mortal hearts and inadequate perspective, that here in time the wicked are usually more powerful than the others and are proud of their transitory happiness. It is a frequent temptation, for the servants of God, to think of this thought: as if those who worship God do so without reason, since they see themselves deprived of those goods of which the wicked abound. Since men are so formed, the Holy Spirit foreseeing this temptation changes the direction of our love in order to prevent us from seeing in ungodly and wicked men a model to imitate and the more we see them happy in their earthly perspective, in the love of those goods whose abundance makes them exalt. Therefore he admonishes: Do not envy the wicked - it is the beginning of the Psalm - do not envy those who commit iniquities, for they wither like the grass and wither like the green of the meadow. Perhaps the grass never blooms? Yes, but the time of its flowering is short because it withers immediately. And for it to bloom, fresh air is needed. The coming of our Lord Jesus Christ will be like the warm season of the year; this time of ours is the cold season, but let us beware of letting our charity cool down in the cold weather of the year. Our glory has not yet appeared: on the surface, it is cold. Let there be warmth in the root. So the trees that appear bare in winter, in summer they whisper and are beautiful and luxuriant. All the luxuriance you see in the branches in the summer was there during the winter? Yes, but hidden in the root. Our glory, which is promised to us, is not there yet. Give time to summer and it will come. It is not yet its time: now it is hidden. It is more accurate to say "it does not appear," rather than "it is not there. The Apostle affirms explicitly: You are as dead. It was as if he were speaking to trees in winter. But to make you understand that if the surface appeared dead, there was life inside it, he immediately added: And your life is hidden with Christ in God 10. It seems that we live on this earth, but reflect where our root is planted. The root of our love is with Christ, it is in God; there is the magnificence of our glory, but now it is not yet visible.

*Earthly optics celestial optics in the evaluation of life.*

4. How do you follow the Apostle's discourse? When Christ, your life, is manifested, then your glory will also be seen together with his 11. Now is the time of suffering, then of joy; now of desire, then of fulfillment; the object of our present desire is not here. But it is not for this reason that we cease to desire; instead, let us try to be constant in desire, because He who has promised does not deceive. Our recommendation, brothers, is not to not cool down; it is not to let yourselves be cooled down either. Men who love the world mock the servants of God in this way: "Here are the things that we possess and enjoy. And where is your happiness? You have no real goods that can be seen, you have only things to believe in. They do not believe in things that cannot be verified. You, on the other hand, are happy precisely because you have believed. You will be even more so when you see. If you are now afflicted by the impossibility of showing the object of your love, this suffering of yours will benefit salvation and also eternal glory. On the other hand, it is not that they show us something that is worth much. Their happiness appears today, ours will come. More precisely, we can say that they do not have it, neither present nor future, because they love a false present happiness and do not arrive at the true happiness, the future. If instead they neglect the false present happiness, if they underestimate their possessions, they will find what to do with them, they will know what to do with them. Let them listen to the advice of the blessed Apostle, the precepts for the rich that he prescribes to Timothy. He says: "The rich of this world are commanded not to mount in pride, not to put their hopes in precarious riches, but in that God who gives us everything with abundance so that we may enjoy it. He recommends that they be rich in good works, that they be generous, that they share their possessions with others, thus treasuring for themselves a good capital for the future, to conquer true life 12. If, then, my brothers, the Apostle sought to distract from the earthly perspective and address to the heavenly perspective those who seemed happy in the present time, if he did not want them to be confined to the pleasure of the present things, but hoped for the future, if he gave such warnings to those who possessed goods, how much more must he aim at the future with his heart the one who has decided not to possess anything on this earth! That is to say, not to have anything superfluous, nothing that is clumsy, that is burdensome, that constrains and prevents. The admonition: As people who have nothing and possess everything, they are faithfully realized even today in the servants of God. Let there be nothing that you call "yours" and everything will be yours. If you attach yourself to even one part, you lose everything. Let poverty be enough for you in so far as wealth would be enough for you.

#### On Psalm 140,1-4
*The Scriptures are full of mysteries.*

1. Brothers, when a moment ago you read the letter of the Apostle, you heard from his mouth what is also our warning and our pressing request. 1. Deign to consider these words as if they were my own, and remember that the Scriptures contain profound mysteries which are hidden so that they do not lose their value. They must be investigated so that the spirit may be continually trained [in the search], and in the end they are revealed to be of food [to the seeker]. The psalm that we have now sung in many of its expressions presents itself with more or less remarkable darkness. But when in the course of the exposition, with the help of the Lord, the meaning of what you had uttered begins to come to light, you will realize for yourself that these are things already known and that, if you repeat them several times, this tends to prevent, by varying the phraseology, a possible nausea for the truth.

*Scriptural doctrine is summed up in charity.*

2. For there may be, O brethren, among the precepts which you will never be given to hear and know, one which is broader and more effective for the salvation than the one which enjoins: You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your mind 2: Will you love your neighbor as yourself 3? Do not believe that these are small precepts. He says in fact: On these two precepts depends all the Law and the Prophets 4. Therefore, every healthy thought that is conceived with the mind or expressed with the mouth, every directive that is drawn from the divine books has no other end than charity. But this is not charity in the qualunquistic sense, such as, for example, that which exists among criminals. As if caught in the same net, they feel solidarity in their perverse conscience, and they say they love each other and never want to be separated from each other. Through the exchange of ideas, they fraternize; when one is absent, they miss him, while they rejoice when he is present. A love of this kind is hellish: it contains visco that plunges into the abyss, not wings that lift to heaven. So what must charity [genuine] be like so that it can be distinguished and separated from other so-called charity? True charity, that which is proper to Christians, has been described by Paul; although it is a reality which, in order to be divine, is infinite, he circumscribes it within its limits, so it is easy to distinguish it from its counterfeits. He says: The end of the precept is charity 5. He could stop here, as in fact one stops in other steps where his speech is addressed to people, let's say, advanced in science. Fullness of the law - he said - is charity 6, without explaining what charity he wanted to talk about. He did not speak of it on that occasion because he had spoken of it elsewhere. It is neither possible nor obligatory to repeat everything always and everywhere. So here. He says: The fullness of the law is charity. Perhaps you would have liked to ask him: But what charity? or how do you want this charity to be? Listen to the answer in that other passage: The end of the precept is charity [which proceeds] from a pure heart 7. You immediately realize if the charity that exists among murderers proceeds from a pure heart. Pure heart in charity is when you love man in the order of God. In fact, even love for yourself must be such that it does not betray the norm: You will love your neighbor as yourself 8. If the love you have for yourself is bad, it is also useless, and the same goes for your neighbor: if you love him in this way, what profit do you bring to him? But when do you have a bad love for yourself? The Scripture indicates it to you, and it doesn't flatter anyone. It convinces you that sometimes not only do you not love yourself, but you even hate yourself. In fact, it says: He who loves iniquity hates his soul 9. If therefore you love iniquity, do you believe that you love yourself? You are wrong. So it belongs to others. If with your love you bring it to evil, this love of yours is a trap for the one you love. So charity proceeds from a pure heart when it is according to God and comes from a righteous conscience and genuine faith 10. Such charity, outlined by the Apostle in these terms, has two precepts: love of God and love of neighbor. In the whole of Scripture seek no other [precept], and let no one come to order you [except charity]. In the dark passages of Scripture charity is hidden, in the clear passages charity becomes evident to you. If it were ever manifest to you, it could not feed you; if it were ever hidden, it would not tempt you to peer. Now it is this charity that from the bottom of the pure heart cries out with the words of the psalm: it cries out from the bottom of the heart of those who resemble our prayer. I will tell you who this is in a word. He is Christ.

*The words of the head Christ are also the words of the members.*

3. You will hear words that it will not seem convenient for you to refer to our Lord Jesus Christ. Someone, on the contrary, limited in understanding, will think that I have said too lightly that the person of Christ is to be seen in this psalm. For we know of our Lord Jesus Christ that he is the lamb without blemish, that in him - and in him alone - there is no sin, that he alone with absolute truth could affirm: Behold comes the prince of this world, and in me he will find nothing, that is, no guilt, no crime. He alone paid compensation for things he had not stolen 12, he alone innocently shed his own blood. For he was the only Son of God who took our flesh, not to belittle himself but to enrich us. How then can words like the following be conveniently applied to such a person: Place, O Lord, a custody at my mouth and a door, that of continence, around my lips, so as not to bend my heart to evil words and find excuses for sins 13? For it is very clear that their meaning is this: O Lord, guard my mouth with a door, with the door of your commandments, that my heart may not turn to evil words. What evil words? Those with which one wants to excuse one's sins. That it may not happen," he says, "that I prefer to excuse my sins rather than accuse them. Words like these do not agree with the person of our Lord Jesus Christ. What sin did he commit, of which he should have been guilty, not to defend himself, but to acknowledge himself guilty? Ours are these words, even if it is certainly Christ who tells them. But if they are our words, how can Christ pronounce them? What on earth? Where has the charity of which I spoke to you now gone? Do you not remember how for charity we are one in Christ? It is charity which, starting from our heart, cries out to Christ; it is charity which, starting from Christ, cries out to us. In what sense does charity, starting from us, cry out to Christ? And it will happen that whoever has invoked the name of the Lord will be saved 14. In what sense does charity itself, starting from Christ, cry out for us? Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me? 15. And the Apostle: You are the body of Christ and [his] members 16. Therefore, if he is the head and we are the body, the only man who speaks: the head speaks or the members speak, it is always the only Christ who speaks. And more properly, it is the task of the head to speak in place of the members. Observe what we ordinarily do. Notice in the first place that among all our members no one is endowed with speech other than the head, and notice again how the head speaks on behalf of all the other members. In a narrow room here one steps on your foot. "You step on me," says the head. One hurt your hand. "You hurt me," says the head. No one has touched your head, but through your head speaks the unity of your body. The tongue, which is located in your head, has taken the parts of all the limbs, and speaks on behalf of all of them. Thus we must listen to Christ when he speaks: each one must be able to recognize in him his own voice, as one who keeps himself in the body of Christ. It may happen sometimes that he utters words in which none of us discover our own person, but which belong exclusively to the head. However, he never detaches himself from our words but raises them up by identifying them with his own; and then it never happens that from his words he does not return to ours. And he himself, speaking of the same thing, said in the Gospel: Well, they are not two but one flesh 18. What I am saying to you is nothing new - you have always listened to them! - But when the occasion arises, it is necessary to remember them: first of all, because the Scriptures which we are presenting here are so intertwined that they are repeated often and on many things, and then because it is a matter [always] useful to you. In fact, the affairs of this world produce those thorns that try to stifle the seed, so it is a vital need to let ourselves be reminded very often by the Lord of what the world would like at all costs to make us forget.

*The invoking cry of the Church will last until the end of the world.*

4. [v 1.] Lord, I have cried out to you: listen to me. These are words that we can all say: words that I do not say but the total Christ. It is more appropriate, however, to consider them pronounced in the name of the body, because even when [the Head] was here [on earth] he prayed united with the flesh. He prayed to the Father in the name of his body, and it happened that while he was praying, drops of blood dripped down from his whole body. So it is written in the Gospel: Jesus prayed with intense prayer and sweated blood 19. What is this shedding of blood from the whole body if not the sufferings sustained by martyrs throughout the Church? Lord, I cried out to you: listen to me. Pay attention to the voice of my supplication while I cry to you. Saying: I have cried out to you, you thought the matter of crying was over. You shouted, it's true, but even now you don't believe you are safe. If the tribulation had ended, the shouting would have ended too; but if the tribulation of the Church and the body of Christ will last until the end of time, say not only: I shouted to you, hear me; but also: Pay attention to the voice of my supplication as I cry out to you.
