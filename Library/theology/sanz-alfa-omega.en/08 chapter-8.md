## Chapter 8 Consummated Eschatology: Heaven and Hell {#sanz-alfa-omega.8}

The King will say to those at his right hand: 'Come, O blessed of my Father, inherit the kingdom prepared for you from the foundation of the world' [...]. Then he will say to those at his left hand: 'Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels' [...]. And they will go away: these into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life (Matt 25:34, 41, 46).
The judgment will be definitive, followed by the separation between the righteous and the damned, described as the light that shines and the fire that burns (cf. Matt 13:41–43). The sentence will not be subject to revision, because in the resurrected state, the human person will no longer be capable either of meriting or of repenting842.
'Come [...] depart from me'. The Christological dimension of the ultimate outcomes implies that there will be only two possibilities: to remain close to Christ or to turn away from him forever. 'The branch must choose between one thing and another: either the vine or the fire (*aut vitis aut ignis*)'843.
The Church confesses faith in 'eternal life' (DS 30), in 'the kingdom that will have no end [...] in the life of the world to come' (DS 150). In the Apostles' Creed as in the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, the confession of faith reflects hope in God, and thus there is no mention of condemnation, which is not the direct object of hope. Condemnation appears, however, in other creeds: 'Those who have done good will go into eternal life; those who have done evil, into eternal fire' (DS 76).
In this chapter we shall examine the two eschatological outcomes: heaven and hell. Certain manuals expound these themes under individual eschatology, because they concern the lot of individuals after judgment, and are distinguished from general eschatology because the final states are differentiated. We prefer the designation *consummated eschatology*, because there exists a communion in heaven—it is therefore not an individual phenomenon—and because we are describing the definitive eschatological consummation844.

### 8.1. Heaven: Eternal Life in the Glory of Christ

Those who die in God's grace and friendship and are perfectly purified live forever with Christ [...]. This communion of life and love with the Holy Trinity, with the Virgin Mary, the angels, and all the blessed is called 'heaven'. Heaven is the ultimate end and fulfilment of the deepest human longings, the state of supreme, definitive happiness (CCC 1023–1024).
'Heaven' is one of several expressions in Christian tradition used to refer to the final state. Others emphasise the cosmic-material dimension: paradise (cf. Luke 23:43; Rev 2:7), banquet (cf. Isa 25:6; Rev 19:9, 18), rest (cf. Wis 4:7; Sir 6:28; 30:17; Heb 3:18; 4:1–6). Still others focus on the collective, and hence ecclesiological, dimension; such are expressions like the Kingdom of heaven, the heavenly Jerusalem (cf. Heb 12:22; Rev 3:12; 21:2, 10), or the Church triumphant. There are then some that underscore the anthropological dimension (perpetual happiness, beatific vision). Finally, there are overtly theological terms, such as the Kingdom of God, the glory of God, and communion with God845.
None of these expressions can on its own fully account for the divine promise, which finds its most exact formulation in the words of Jesus: 'And this is eternal life, that they know you the only true God, and Jesus Christ whom you have sent' (John 17:3). Here all aspects are synthesised, since Jesus is the God-Man who brings the cosmos and man to completion toward the life of God. The dialogue in the traditional rite of Baptism unfolded thus: '“What do you ask of the Church?” Answer: “Faith”. “And what does faith bestow on you?” Eternal life' (SS 10). So also in the Apostles' Creed, whose final article is 'life everlasting' (DS 30).
We shall see how Scripture speaks of eternal life, examining the objections that such an expression provokes, in order then to undertake a theological reflection: first, on what life means in its highest operation, namely knowledge, studying the discussions on the vision of God and the debate regarding the relationship between knowledge and love. Taking into account the adjective *eternal*, we shall then address the discussions concerning the eternity of this life, and thus the freedom enjoyed in heaven, and what type of succession it may imply. We shall conclude with the connection between eternal life, the present life, and hope.

### 8.1.1. Eternal Life in the Bible

It is characteristic of Israelite faith to praise YHWH as creator and source of life, especially in the Psalms. This leads back to the primordial history, which recounts the creation of the world and of man by the living God who is master of life. The Yahwist makes assertions regarding the origin of life 'that find no parallel elsewhere in the OT'846 when he says that 'the Lord God formed man of dust from the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living being' (Gen 2:7), who will have at his disposal the tree of life precisely so that he may preserve it (cf. Gen 2:9). The Book of Wisdom highlights that 'God did not make death, and he does not delight in the destruction of the living. For he created all things that they might exist, and the generative forces of the world are wholesome' (Wis 1:13–14). Salvation here is to be understood as the protection and preservation of life by the benevolent God who created it847.
Life is a gift whose preservation depends also on the choices of the creature, as is evident not only from the Adamic narrative, but also from the history of Israel: 'I call heaven and earth to witness against you this day, that I have set before you life and death, blessing and curse; therefore choose life, that you and your descendants may live, loving the Lord your God, obeying his voice, and cleaving to him; for that means life to you' (Deut 30:19–20).
Thus it comes to be understood that 'man does not live by bread alone, but that man lives by everything that proceeds out of the mouth of the Lord' (Deut 8:3), which 'is no trifle for you, but it is your very life' (Deut 32:47). In the Psalms, expectations of the promise of a long and happy life clash with the contradictory nature of daily experience. The idea gains ground that true life is found in union with YHWH, 'for your steadfast love is better than life' (Ps 63[62]:3[4])848. Life in the full sense is that of God, which is eternal. In late Judaism, 'under Hellenistic influence, true life came increasingly to be understood in the sense of eternal life (*zoē aiōnios*), an endless life [...]. From the time of the Maccabees, faith in the hereafter, in the resurrection, and in eternal life characterised Jewish theology'849.
Jesus echoes this Israelite conception when, regarding the hypothetical question of the seven brothers who successively had the same wife, he declares: 'He is not God of the dead, but of the living; for all live to him' (Luke 20:38). God created the living; life depends on Him inasmuch as He Himself is life (cf. John 14:6). It is a theological novelty of the NT to designate this life as eternal, in contrast with and at the same time in relation to the present life, since both are gifts of God. While this life is limited, the future life appears characterised by perennial duration and is described as *zoē aiōnios* in several passages (cf. Mark 10:17, 30; Matt 25:46). Its occasional designation simply as *zoē* without an adjective implies that it is the true, original, divine life (cf. Mark 9:43, 45), which is won here, as highlighted by the parable of the talents (cf. Matt 25:21–30) and the final judgment (cf. Matt 25:31–46)850.
Saint Paul sees in Christ, above all in his Resurrection, the beginning of a new life for humanity (cf. Rom 5:17–21; 1 Cor 15:22, 45), which is realised in Christians according to a paradox: 'It is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me' (Gal 2:20; cf. Phil 1:21; 2 Cor 4:10). This new life is not exhausted in time, but has as its goal eternal life (cf. Rom 6:22), which is the gift of the Spirit (cf. Gal 6:8)851.
The theme of eternal life is prominently present in the Johannine writings. 'In this the love of God was made manifest among us, that God sent his only Son into the world, so that we might live through him' (1 John 4:9). The Son is 'he who comes down from heaven, and gives life to the world' (John 6:33); not merely any life, but 'that they may have life, and have it abundantly' (John 10:10). This is the life of God, which is eternal, and which He gives us through his word (cf. John 6:68; 10:28; 12:50; 17:2). If he can do this, it is because 'he is the true God and eternal life' (1 John 5:20), as manifested by several of the 'I am' formulas characteristic of the Fourth Gospel: 'the bread of life' (John 6:35, 48), 'the light of life' (John 8:12), 'the resurrection and the life' (John 11:25), 'the way, and the truth, and the life' (John 14:6).
This life is received in faith: 'He who hears my word and believes him who sent me, has eternal life' (John 5:24), which is given in Baptism; Jesus speaks of water becoming a spring 'welling up to eternal life' (John 4:14); the acceptance of this gift transforms man into a child of God, as begotten by Him and reborn (cf. John 1:12–13; 3:5), which is manifested in charity: 'We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brethren' (1 John 3:14). Whereas in Gnosticism eternal life is transposed into an almost unreachable beyond, John considers eternal life in the present, united to the word and person of Christ who gives it to us in the Eucharist: 'He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day' (John 6:54). Whoever lives this eternal life no longer fears death or judgment (cf. John 5:24; 11:25–26). The fullness of this life entails resurrection at the end of time, an existence preserved forever (cf. John 6:27; 12:25) and characterised by the fulfilment of the promise of the vision of glory (cf. John 17:24).

### 8.1.2. The Paradox of Eternal Life

The term 'life' denotes activity, dynamism, whereas 'eternal' evokes rest, peace. In our existence, activity and rest are intertwined: intense activity produces fatigue and prompts a search for rest, while rest engenders boredom and a tendency to move. Activity and rest exist in a tension difficult to integrate in this life.
One must ask: do we really want a life that lasts forever? Would that not be an eternal boredom? Would this not be an obstacle, a condemnation, rather than a gift?
Perhaps today many people reject the faith simply because eternal life does not seem to them something desirable. They do not want eternal life at all, but the present life, and faith in eternal life seems, for this purpose, rather an obstacle. To continue living eternally—without end—appears more a condemnation than a gift. Death, certainly, one would wish to put off as far as possible. But to live always, without an end—this, when all is said and done, can only be boring and ultimately unbearable (SS 10).
The paradox is that both death and eternal life frighten us:
There is a contradiction in our attitude, which points to an interior contradictoriness in our very existence. On the one hand, we do not want to die; above all, those who love us do not want us to die. On the other hand, however, neither do we desire to continue to exist indefinitely, and the earth too was not created with this prospect. What, then, do we truly want? This paradox of our own attitude raises a deeper question: what is 'life' in reality? And what does 'eternity' truly mean? (SS 11).
Philosophy and religions have always sought answers to this dilemma852. While certain forms of Eastern religiosity hope for a perfect tranquillity and peace after death, with the cessation of all conscious human activity, certain exponents of Enlightenment philosophy place themselves at the antipodes of this view, maintaining that happiness consists in full, conscious, individual activity; life would not be the capacity for movement, but movement itself. In the former case, eternity is stressed as rest at the expense of the subject and his activity; in the latter, the life of the subject and his activity are privileged at the expense of rest. The two dimensions do not appear to be well harmonised; yet both visions coincide in presupposing that the attainment of happiness is a task to be achieved by human effort.
These oscillations testify that 'we want only one thing—“the blessed life”, the life that is simply life, simply “happiness”' (SS 11), yet 'we do not know at all what we in the depth of our being desire, what we would properly wish for. We do not know this reality at all [...]. Yet in not knowing we know that this reality must exist. “There is therefore in us a, so to speak, learned ignorance” (*docta ignorantia*)' (SS 12)853.
What does Christianity propose in the face of this paradox?
We can only attempt to step out from the temporality of which we are prisoners in our thoughts and somehow anticipate that eternity is not a continuous succession of calendar days, but something like the moment replete with satisfaction, in which the totality embraces us and we embrace the totality. It would be the moment of immersing ourselves in the ocean of infinite love, in which time—the before and after—no longer exists. We can only try to think that this moment is life in the full sense, a constantly new plunging into the vastness of being, whilst we are simply overwhelmed with joy [...]. We must think along these lines if we want to understand the goal of Christian hope (SS 12).
While religions and philosophy, in their disparity of solutions, consider that it is man who attains happiness—and for this reason fail to resolve the paradox—Christianity, maintaining the mystery, points to a different perspective: birth into life is a gift, and eternal life is a new birth, participation in a higher life, that of God. It is not a matter of achieving by one's own strength a state of perfection, which is impossible854, but of opening oneself to a superior gift, bestowed by Him who is capable of making us happy: 'Well done, good and faithful servant [...] enter into the joy of your master' (Matt 25:21).
The Boethian definition of eternity as 'the simultaneous and perfect possession of boundless life'855 describes the life of God. Man, who lives in temporality, is open to surmounting his limits by integrating the past and the future into the present. The divine gift of eternal life will resolve, in a way unknown to us, the contradictions of our present condition, as the Bishop of Hippo suggests in speaking of the 'holy and perpetual rest from all toil and all trouble', which 'does not consist in a sluggish inactivity, but in an ineffable tranquillity of restful activity [...] which preserves that which constitutes the characteristic of rest, namely to act without toiling, to think without worrying'856.

### 8.1.3. The Vision of God

'For living beings, to live is to be' (*vivere viventibus est esse*)857. With this Aristotelian statement, which Thomas adopts as his own858, the fact is expressed that living is not a mere quality of individuals, but is identified with their being. In the world there is an ontological differentiation from inanimate entities to living beings, and among the latter, in the scale of life, the higher degree includes the lower, from vegetative to animal life, reaching the summit in intellectual life. The vital principle (the vegetative, animal, or spiritual soul) causes the living being to possess an activity proper to itself. In the most perfect living beings, those of an intellectual nature, there exists a hierarchy of operations, the highest rank being occupied by intellectual activity *par excellence*: the contemplation of truth859, which is, for beings of an intellectual nature, the supreme end, the highest good, happiness, coinciding with the vision of God860.
To describe the ultimate end and happiness as contemplation means indicating the primacy of the cognitive faculty in beings of a spiritual nature. Is this not a lapse into intellectualism? It seems not to consider sufficiently the role of the other spiritual faculties, the will and freedom, which lead to love—seemingly a higher activity than knowledge. In any case, it is usually specified that this is a *beatific* vision, one that makes us happy, and this cannot occur by discarding the volitional and free dimension of a being endowed with a spiritual nature.
We shall seek to understand why, in describing the final state of communion with God, the language of seeing or contemplating has prevailed over that of loving. There are two reasons: one philosophical, namely the need to preserve divine transcendence (cf. 8.1.3.1), and the other biblical, because Scripture speaks in this manner (cf. 8.1.3.2). We shall then address several historical controversies (cf. 8.1.3.3) in order to grasp both the dogmatic definition of the vision of God and how it is possible, as well as the relationship between knowledge and love in the definitive state.

### 8.1.3.1. The Natural Desire to See God

'The glory of God is a living man, and the life of man is the vision of God'861. Irenaeus describes the end of the intellectual creature as the vision of God. Augustine expressed it in an existential manner: 'You have made us for yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you'862. God created us to see Him and thus to rest in Him; nothing else can constitute man's end, and nothing else can satisfy his desire.
Medieval theologians affirmed that man has a natural desire to see God. Saint Thomas wished thereby to show that faith does not contradict reason, since, from a rational point of view, it is possible to say that there exists in man a desire to know God that is not stilled until God is known, not only in his existence, but also in his essence863. The characterisation of the desire to see God as a natural desire stands at the heart of the relationship between nature and grace, which is central to Christian theology864. To see God is supernatural for man, because he does not possess by nature the capacity to attain it; at the same time, the divine vision is the end that the Creator has appointed for his creature, and He has therefore inscribed in its nature that desire for the end that draws it865. God created man in a state of grace that included original justice, which was subsequently lost through sin. Created in grace, human nature never existed as pure nature. It is logical that, even though transmitted in a fallen condition, it preserves the desire for the end for which it was created866.
Whereas in love one speaks of union between lover and beloved, in knowledge, according to Aristotle, there is an intentional identification in act between knower and known, while the ontological distinction is preserved867. 'Otherness is constitutive of knowledge. In this, the intentional possession of forms is distinguished from material possession. To know is to possess intentionally; it is to possess while keeping the possessed object before oneself'868. The final state, when God will be all in all, will not mean the disappearance of the distinction between God and creatures869. The language of vision to describe the state of the blessed has the indispensable function of safeguarding the transcendence of God and the autonomy of creatures. The fundamental distinction between the Creator and creatures is maintained even in consummated eschatology.

### 8.1.3.2. The Vision of God in the OT and NT

'My heart says to you: “Seek his face!” Your face, Lord, do I seek. Hide not your face from me. Turn not your servant away in anger' (Ps 27[26]:8–9). 'As a hart longs for flowing streams, so longs my soul for you, O God. My soul thirsts for God, for the living God. When shall I come and behold the face of God?' (Ps 42[41]:1–2[2–3]). Seeking the face of the Lord is a characteristic not only of the psalmist, but of the entire people of Israel, which can be described as 'the generation of those who seek him, who seek the face of the God of Jacob' (Ps 24[23]:6). After the loss of communion with the Lord who 'was walking in the garden in the cool of the day' (Gen 3:8), it is said of only a few figures in the OT that they had a special relationship with God. Among them Moses stands out, 'whom the Lord knew face to face' (Deut 34:10). While the people 'saw the pillar of cloud standing at the door of the tent, and all the people rose up and worshipped, every man at his tent door', nevertheless 'the Lord used to speak to Moses face to face, as a man speaks to his friend' (Exod 33:10–11).
When Moses asks to see the glory of the Lord, he receives a refusal in reply:
'You cannot see my face; for man shall not see me and live'. And the Lord said: 'Behold, there is a place by me where you shall stand upon the rock; and while my glory passes by I will put you in a cleft of the rock, and I will cover you with my hand until I have passed by; then I will take away my hand, and you shall see my back; but my face shall not be seen' (Exod 33:20–23).
His access to God was in reality limited. In the OT God is completely transcendent, and therefore man cannot see Him. The promise of a prophet greater than Moses (cf. Deut 18:15–18) contains a greater expectation: to him will be granted what was previously denied, namely to see the face of God directly, which will make him the mediator of a superior covenant. That promise is fulfilled in Jesus, who lives before the face of God not merely as a friend, but as the Son, in intimacy with the Father870.
Only God can bridge the distance and make Himself visible: 'No one has ever seen God; the only Son, who is God and is in the bosom of the Father, he has made him known' (John 1:18). Jesus' response to Philip's request to show them the Father is significant: 'Have I been with you so long, and yet you do not know me, Philip? He who has seen me has seen the Father' (John 14:9). Jesus promises the vision of God to the 'pure in heart' (Matt 5:8), to the humble, to the poor, to the little ones, whose angels 'always behold the face of my Father who is in heaven' (Matt 18:10).
Jesus' promise echoes in the writings of Paul and John. 'For now we see in a mirror dimly, but then face to face. Now I know in part; then I shall understand fully, even as I have been fully understood' (1 Cor 13:12). In the tension of the 'already and not yet', between present faith and future vision, the emphasis is not on the vision belonging to the righteous, but on the participation in God's own knowing ('even as I have been fully understood'). The direct vision of God is proclaimed immediately following the hymn to charity, establishing a connection that we shall consider shortly. John connects divine filiation and the vision of God: 'Beloved, we are God's children now; it does not yet appear what we shall be, but we know that when he appears we shall be like him, for we shall see him as he is' (1 John 3:2). The vision is not foreign to charity; rather, it is its cause: 'See what love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God; and so we are!' (1 John 3:1).

### 8.1.3.3. The Debate on the Immediate Vision and the Definition of the *Lumen Gloriae*: St Thomas's Explanation

'What will that glory be, and how great the joy, to be admitted to see God!'871. The vision of God was taught by the Fathers of the Church as the source of happiness for man, and thus it can be called *beatific*, as Augustine and the Cappadocians, among others, teach.
Eunomius, an Arian bishop of the fourth century, asserted that we know God is unbegotten just as He knows it of Himself; the Son, being begotten, cannot be God, and indeed, as a creature, does not fully know the Father. John Chrysostom reacted against this assertion, and, to defend Christ's divinity, taught that the divine substance is seen by Christ, but neither by us nor by the angels. The Father is seen only by the Son and the Spirit. Theodoret of Cyrrhus shared this opinion, explaining that the vision of God in the Bible is not of his essence (*ousia*), but of his glorious splendour (*doxa*)872. Gregory the Great perceived in this doctrine the danger of compromising divine simplicity:
Some have asserted that even in that blessed homeland God may indeed be contemplated in his splendour, but not in his nature. However, a rather superficial enquiry has led them into error. For in that simple and unchangeable essence, splendour is not one thing and nature another, but his very nature is his splendour, and his splendour is his nature873.
These discussions reflect the effort to maintain two elements that seem contradictory: divine invisibility, affirmed by Scripture and the Creed; and the actual gift of the divine vision to man as the fulfilment of his innermost longing, likewise affirmed by the Bible and the Church's Tradition.
Eastern theology has been inclined to emphasise the former aspect. In the line of Theodoret, Gregory Palamas would insist on the invisibility of God by means of the concept of the divine energies—that is, the glory emanating from the divine substance, which is not identified with his essence, the latter remaining unknown as transcendent874. Following an unjust accusation of ditheism, the doctrine of the divine energies was accepted at the Synod of the Byzantine Church in 1352. By this means an attempt is made to safeguard the transcendence of God, and for this reason it is seen as problematic to affirm that the righteous see God directly, as Christ sees Him.
The Latin Church explained God's invisibility differently, taking into account the force of the promise of vision. Certain Fathers (such as Irenaeus and Augustine) pointed out that while creatures cannot see God by their own powers, this does not imply that God cannot make Himself visible to them. Invisibility is not a limitation on God's part, but a way of indicating divine otherness and transcendence. God, 'invisible by nature, is visible by his will; for he makes himself seen only if he so wills'875. It is not denied that speaking of God's visibility could appear to grant creatures a certain power over God, but due weight is given to the Johannine affirmations concerning God's revelation in Christ: 'That which we have looked upon and touched with our hands, concerning the word of life [...] that which we have seen and heard we proclaim also to you, so that you may have fellowship with us; and our fellowship is with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ' (1 John 1:1, 3). Since God is unapproachable for man, the latter stands in need of a special, higher gift in order to reach Him: 'What is impossible with men is possible with God' (Luke 18:27).
The Council of Florence (1439), in view of the reunion between Latins and Greeks, did not accept the doctrine of an indirect vision: 'The souls of those who have been purified clearly behold God Himself, one and triune, just as He is' (DS 1305); yet it admitted a gradation in the vision, welcoming a doctrine widespread in the East: 'some more perfectly than others, according to the diversity of their merits' (DS 1305).
The Magisterium had already taught, in the constitution *Benedictus Deus* of Benedict XII (1336), that the blessed in heaven see the divine essence without mediation, not by their own power, but with the divine essence showing itself immediately (*divina essentia immediate se ostendente*, DS 1000). Even earlier, the Council of Vienne (1312) had taught that the direct vision of God is not due to our natural powers, but to a supernatural gift: 'The soul needs the light of glory (*lumen gloriae*) to elevate it to see God and enjoy Him beatifically' (DS 895). This dogmatic definition embraced a notion that had been developed by the great scholastic masters of the thirteenth century and was suggested by certain biblical texts in the version then prevalent, such as Ps 36(35):9[10] ('*in lumine tuo videbimus lucem*') and Rev 22:4–5 ('*et videbunt faciem eius [...] quoniam Dominus Deus illuminabit super illos*')876.
The Angelic Doctor explains that 'it is impossible for any created intellect to attain the vision of God's substance, without an action on the part of God, who transcends all creatures'877. Just as we know objects through intelligible species, and God is not a created object, so 'given that the essence of God is seen, it must be that the intellect sees it through the divine essence itself: so that in this vision the divine essence is both the object seen (*quod videtur*) and the medium through which it is seen (*quo videtur*)'878. This requires a gift 'of a supernatural order'879, which may be called the light of glory (*lux gloriae*)880. Divine infinity is no obstacle to this vision, because the negative connotation of the concept refers to the absence of material limits, so that for God, infinite means maximally knowable881. Precisely for this reason, the human intellect will never comprehend the divine essence in an exhaustive manner882.
The infused light does not imply an ontological assimilation of the soul to God, as Henry of Ghent maintained. Thomas teaches, in continuity with Aristotelian epistemology, that the intellect is brought by the *lumen gloriae* into the being of God in an intentional and not in a real sense883.
This explains the diversity of degrees of participation in the divine vision884. That there are differences is grounded in the words of the Lord, who states that in his Father's house there are many dwelling places (cf. John 14:2)885, while the reward remains the same for all, as is inferred from the parable of the labourers in the vineyard, who receive the same denarius (cf. Matt 20:1–16)886.
The merging of the 'I' with the Body of Christ, becoming an instrument of the Lord and of others, does not mean a dissolution of the 'I', but rather its purification, which simultaneously realises its highest possibilities. For this reason, heaven is individual for each person. Each sees God in his own way; each receives the love of the All in his unmistakable uniqueness887.

### 8.1.3.4. Beatific Vision: Knowledge and Love

If 'love never ends' (1 Cor 13:8) and the rest passes away, why has the Church defined our definitive state of communion with God in terms of knowledge rather than love?
Thomas and Bonaventure diverge in their positions on the priority of knowledge or love in the description of eternal life.
For Saint Thomas the supreme end toward which our desire is directed is: to see God. In this simple act of seeing God all problems find their solution: we are happy, nothing else is necessary. For Saint Bonaventure, by contrast, the ultimate destiny of man is: to love God, the encounter and union of his love and ours. This is for him the most adequate definition of our happiness. Along these lines, we might also say that the highest category for Saint Thomas is the true, whereas for Saint Bonaventure it is the good. It would be mistaken to see in these two answers a contradiction. For both, the true is also the good, and the good is also the true; to see God is to love, and to love is to see. It is therefore a matter of differing emphases within a fundamentally shared vision. Both emphases have formed different traditions and different spiritualities, thereby demonstrating the fruitfulness of the faith, which is one in the diversity of its expressions888.
There is a circularity between knowledge and love. It is true, with Thomas and Augustine, that 'nothing is loved unless it is known' (*nihil diligitur nisi cognitum*)889; but it is also true, with Bonaventure, that love impels toward knowledge890; as Pascal says, 'the heart has its reasons of which reason knows nothing'891. In the Hebrew language, moreover, the term 'to know' (*yāda‘*) has connotations that bring it close to 'to love', as occurs in passages where 'knowing' denotes spousal love892. In the case of God, in whom truth and goodness coincide, eternal life is the gift that brings about a participation in the Trinitarian life of knowledge and love.
It is appropriate to understand the reasons why Thomas insists on emphasising the priority of contemplation, or rather, in what sense, in the relationship between intellect and will, the one takes precedence over the other. His conclusion is that, while the intellect is superior to the will *simpliciter*, the will is superior to the intellect *secundum quid*893. 'The ultimate end of the intellectual creature consists in seeing God and not in taking delight in Him, which is instead concomitant with the end and as it were perfective of the end. Still less can desire or love be the ultimate end, for these are possessed prior to reaching the end'894. The vision encompasses all these dimensions:
For the reward and end of all our labours is God Himself [...]. This union consists, then, above all in a perfect vision of Him [...]. It consists, furthermore, in a most fervent love, because the more one is known, the more one is loved; and in supreme praise of Him [...]. In eternal life every blessed soul will find the fulfilment of all that it has desired and hoped for895.
The Father 'gazes upon the Son and pronounces the words of knowledge and of love. In that light the Son is turned toward the Father [...]. The space that lies between them, its intimacy, its silence, and its fullness, are true eternity. Eternity is the centre of all things. All things proceed from eternity; all things return to it'896. Thanks to the Holy Spirit, 'entering into communion with Christ, the believer is admitted through him into the relationship of Christ Himself with the Father [...]. The goal of every human journey is the Father. There is no other; it accomplishes all things'897.

### 8.1.4. The Eternity of Heaven

If 'life' implies activity engaging both knowledge and free will, 'eternal' expresses the permanence of that activity. This poses two problems, since it seems to do away with two dimensions of our existence: namely, freedom and temporality. Once we share in the eternal life of God, do we retain our freedom of choice? How will the eternity of life with God be compatible with our character as beings marked by temporality?

### 8.1.4.1. The Controversy over Freedom in Heaven

Observing the diversity of conditions among spirits in this world, and wishing to safeguard divine justice, Origen traced this diversity back to the use of freedom, since God had created them all equal and free from the beginning898. But since freedom entails mutability, the diversity of spirits arises from the diversity of their choices. Origen was convinced that, with the sole exception of the pre-existent soul of Christ, all spiritual creatures sinned with varying degrees of intensity899, and this was the cause of their present state of diversification, manifested in the assumption of a body900, which is not an evil in itself, but the expression of such diversity. This does not remove the punitive character of being in a body, yet Origen held a pedagogical conception of punishment as a means of God's love to restore creatures and bring sinners to conversion901. The reason God did not prevent evil and sin in creation is not only his respect for freedom, but also his foreknowledge that they would serve his design.
The utility of punishment applies not only to this life, but also to the hereafter. Origen thought that after the present age there would follow a succession of ages902, of which the coming age would be only the beginning; and he interpreted Jesus' words on the unforgivability of the sin against the Holy Spirit in the sense that, if the sinner's salvation does not occur in the age to come, it might take place in one of the countless ages that will follow. Since sin appears in time, and since everything that has a beginning must also have an end, God's patient love will eventually wear down the infidelity of creatures, who will consent to love Him, so that at the end God will be all in all and creation will be restored in its integrity because evil will vanish: this is the final reconciliation or *apokatastasis*903. Certain texts seem to suggest that, since spiritual creatures remain always free and freedom entails mutability, relapses will be eternally possible904. This attempt to harmonise God's merciful love with creaturely freedom lapses into paradox: 'The metaphysical necessity of the end of evil saves love but destroys freedom [...]; the eternal instability of freedoms saves freedom but destroys love'905. The Origenist view thus raises the problem of the compatibility between the eternity of heaven and human freedom. If we are free, we can reject God, and thus heaven is no longer eternal. But if it is eternal, it seems we forfeit our freedom. In the Middle Ages two answers were proposed.
Duns Scotus posed the question concerning the cause of the perpetuity of beatitude and the link between the two. If the perfection of happiness includes its perpetuity, at least in an extensive sense, this entails the absence of what is opposed to it, namely sin. But one must ask whether the absence of sin in the blessed soul is due to an internal or an external cause. Scotus's answer is that this cause is not a necessity imposed by the vision within the blessed, but the action of the divine will continuously preventing him so that the vision is maintained and sin avoided. The blessed is impeccable in a composite sense (*in sensu composito*)—that is, he cannot see God and simultaneously sin—but he is not impeccable in a divided sense (*in sensu diviso*), because theoretically he could sin, given that the cause of his impeccability does not reside within himself, but comes from an extrinsic cause. This is a remote, physical capacity that will never be actualised thanks to the free divine prevention906. The will always remains free in its exercise with respect to God, because the act through which the blessed love God is a contingent and not a necessary act. This solution poses difficulties, in that God appears obliged to constrain or correct human freedom extrinsically to maintain it in achieving its end. Freedom seems more a problem than a solution, conceived as indifference towards the good907. We examined the deficiencies of this conception of freedom in the protological section; it suffices here to note that, in such a proposal, the intrinsic bond between freedom, truth, and goodness is insufficiently emphasised.
Thomas Aquinas offers a different explanation, reflecting his distinct conception of freedom. Since the vision of God is eternal and not temporal, it cannot be lost908. There can be no change, either on the part of God, the object of the vision, who is immutable, or on the part of the intellect that sees, because it is made a partaker in the divine stability909. But 'neither can the will cease to enjoy such a vision, since it understands that in it consists its ultimate happiness: just as it cannot will not to be happy'910. In the fruition of the vision of God, who is the supreme good and supreme truth, nothing evil can be perceived, nor anything merely partially good that could move the will to desire something better911.
For since the object of the will is the good, it is impossible for the will to tend toward anything that does not have the character of good. Now, it is possible that in any particular good there may be some defect, so that whoever knows it can seek the good elsewhere [...]. But in God, who is the universal good and goodness itself, there is no lack of any good that could be sought elsewhere. Whoever therefore sees the essence of God cannot turn his will away from Him and tend toward other goods except by reason of Him912.
The possibility of sinning in heaven is definitively excluded913, and thereby the error of the Origenist position from which we set out is exposed914.
The fact that, once attained, it is impossible to abandon the vision of God does not remove freedom, but removes the possibility of sinning. According to Thomas, sinning is not part of human freedom, but a sign of a defect in freedom915. In the truest sense, the blessed freely will not to sin, because they are fully satisfied. In them the present condition no longer obtains, in which, not seeing God, man easily deceives himself by making disordered choices regarding his good.
Freedom is not a mere possibility of choice, but openness to the infinite and self-transcendence916, man's mastery over himself and over his acts and inclinations, dominion over what enslaves him, and openness to the other917. Christianity understands freedom as the liberation that Christ has wrought in us (cf. Gal 5:1), the 'freedom of the glory of the children of God' (Rom 8:21), which is a state rather than a mere act or capacity. It is a paradox that those who desire a freedom without restraint often become prey to slavery; at root, they identify freedom with the mere capacity to choose, with keeping all options open, given the essential dissatisfaction inherent in any particular choice. The vision of God removes all insatiability, being the perfect realisation of one's identity. The good guides choice; it is an illusion to imagine that choice creates the good.
Augustine spoke of the various states of freedom in salvation history. Whereas in paradise man was able not to sin (*posse non peccare*), and now in our fallen condition inheriting Adam's guilt we are unable not to sin (*non posse non peccare*), the liberation brought to us by Christ will obtain for us, in the final consummation, the inability to sin (*non posse peccare*), which is the greatest freedom (*libertas maior*) we can enjoy. 'Not because sins will have no power to allure them will the blessed have no free choice; rather, free choice will be all the more free from the attraction of sin because it has been set free to the point of an unbending attraction towards not sinning [...]. Must it be denied that God has free choice because He cannot sin?'918.

### 8.1.4.2. Eternity and Temporality

Temporality is our creaturely condition, whereas eternity belongs properly to divine being. How will it be possible to enter into eternity without ceasing to be creatures? By virtue of our spiritual nature, we can in some measure transcend our temporal limits. This natural capacity is elevated by God's gift to a participation in his own life, which is eternal919. In heaven the temporal condition of this life—the pilgrim state of growth and conversion—no longer exists. It yields to the enjoyment of the contemplation of God, according to Irenaeus: 'It is impossible to live without life, and the existence of life is possible through participation in God; and participation in God consists in seeing God and enjoying his goodness'920.
The notion of participation helps us understand that vision does not imply an ontological fusion. The persistence of our creaturely being entails a real succession of our acts, even if that succession and our perception of it will be profoundly transformed. 'All the days are not added up by coming and passing away, and the beginning of one is not the end of another: they are all together without end, where the life to which those days belong also has no end'921. Theology has coined the term *aeviternity* (*aevum*)922 to indicate that the existence of the blessed and the angels does not coincide with the eternity of God, yet is not our temporality: it is a participated eternity (*aeternitas participata*, *aeternitatis participatio*)923. In this way expression is given to the mysterious harmony between the fact that there will be no further progress924—because the gift has been reached and cannot be lost—and the fact that there will be an infinite growth, because the vision of God constitutes an inexhaustible enrichment for us:
In this truly consists the vision of God: in never finding satiety in one's desire. But it is necessary, by looking always through what it is possible to see, to be rekindled with the desire to see more: thus no limit could ever cut short the progress of the ascent to God, inasmuch as no limit is found to the beautiful, and the advance of desire directed toward the beautiful is not severed by any satiety925.
Even though eternal life begins already on earth, and the martyrs and the perfectly purified righteous see God before the end of time, only with the gift of the resurrection will the joy of the blessed be complete. The saints await us in order to receive their full reward926. 'On the day of judgment they will have an increase of beatitude: for this, while currently enjoyed only by the soul, will afterwards be communicated also to their bodies; so that the blessed may rejoice also in their flesh, in which for love of the Lord they bore travails and martyrdoms'927.

### 8.1.5. The Ecclesial Dimension of Eternal Life

The vision and love of God are not so exclusive as to preclude the addition of love for neighbour as a manifestation of his love, as He Himself commanded us. Love of neighbour in the hereafter will not be absorbed, but integrated by divine love. God employs mediations in salvation history; He constituted the people of Israel in the Old Covenant and the people of the New Covenant, his Church, which is the Body of Christ, the sole Mediator between God and men. Between the members of Christ there exists a union not only on this earth, but also in heaven. 'The Christological affirmation, however, includes also an ecclesiological moment: if heaven is grounded upon man's incorporation into Christ, it follows that it entails as well communion with all those who together form the one Body of Christ'928.
The prospect of heaven does not entail asking questions about privileges, 'but rather the commitment to expand as far as possible the vessel of one's own life'929. Charity as God's gift is capable of expanding the creature's heart: the more one loves God, the more one loves what God loves, namely other creatures. One must not reason in purely human terms, as though loving God above all things meant loving creatures less. The Christological perspective means that divine being does not stand in contradiction with human being, but both are united in their mutual relationship and distinction.
In Christology, the 'theological' and 'anthropological' lines of dialogue, of the search for love, merge [...]. God's dialogue with us is an authentically human dialogue, because God conducts it as man to man. And vice versa: men's reciprocal dialogue contains something of eternity, since the *communio sanctorum* makes it share in the Trinitarian dialogue. Hence the communion of saints is the place where eternity opens up for man930.
In this Christological context Thomas Aquinas suggests that to see God implies seeing all other things in God, just as God knows Himself and in Himself all things931. Whoever loves God loves others also in God. 'In the love of God you will rediscover all the pure loves you have had on earth'932.
The names by which the Bible describes heaven from a 'social' point of view are by no means negligible, such as the 'heavenly Jerusalem', and above all the 'Kingdom' (of God, of Christ, of heaven, etc.), in which the divine glory will be manifested (Ps 145[144]:11; cf. Rev 1:6; 19:6), which is the end of creation. Since God is the King of ages, the blessed united to Him will be called kings and will possess that glory which men so often seek in vain933. It forms part of the joy of eternal life 'to be together with all the blessed [...] so that, with the increase of the joy and happiness of one, the happiness of all increases, as the psalmist says: “Those who dwell in you are all glad and joyful”'934.

### 8.1.6. The Presence of Eternal Life in the Christian Journey

The rich young man's question: 'Good Teacher, what must I do to inherit eternal life?' (Mark 10:17) highlights that eternal life stands in relation to present conduct, but also that it is Christ who grants this gift, being in some way identified with Him935.
The vision of God proceeds from the *lumen gloriae*, which stands in relation to the light of reason (*lumen rationis*), enabling us to know God through creatures, and to the light of faith (*lumen fidei*), which prepares us for vision. The contemplation of God begins in this life, especially in liturgical worship.
'Heaven', understood as 'becoming one' with Christ, possesses therefore the character of adoration; in it the prophetic content of all worship is realised: Christ is the eschatological Temple (John 2:19) [...]. Worship includes the inseparable immediacy between God and man which theological tradition defines as 'contemplation of God'936.
The Church has always affirmed the superiority of the contemplative life over the active life937.
The contemplation of God through faith extends to all situations and persons. The inquiries of the judgment turn upon the capacity to see Jesus in one's brethren. 'For he who does not love his brother whom he has seen, cannot love God whom he has not seen' (1 John 4:20). Put positively, 'by loving your neighbour you purify your eye to be able to see God [...]. Love your neighbour, therefore, and look within yourself for the source from which love of neighbour springs: there you will see, so far as you are able, God'938.
Heaven is the realm in which God's will is done (cf. Matt 6:10). Following the expulsion from the garden due to the rejection of God, human life became a desert waste. Jesus, the Son of God who entered the wilderness of God's absence, through his identification with the Father's will—'not my will, but yours, be done' (Luke 22:42)—brought heaven once again down to earth939. Through the fulfilment of the divine will, we can anticipate, within the limits of our condition, something of heaven on earth, as occurs in the saints who, sharing in the divine light, illumine the darkness of this world. Beatitude is not merely a future reward for those who suffer 'with Christian resignation' the adversities of this world; it is a happiness that begins already here, because we are with Christ through faith and the sacraments. 'The happiness of Heaven is for those who know how to be happy on earth'940.
In the beginning God planted in Eden the tree of life, from which our first parents could eat to preserve their life. Certain medieval Doctors were able to call it a sacrament, in connection with the Eucharist, the new tree of life. Even though in the resurrected state the need for food no longer exists, the image of the banquet, used by Jesus, indicates that true food is doing the will of God—that true food is therefore wisdom, which is the Word Himself941.
Heaven 'is not a place without history, “whither” one arrives; the existence of “heaven” is grounded in the fact that Jesus Christ, who is God, is man and has given human being a place in the very being of God. Man is in heaven when and to the extent that he is with Christ'942. This means that
the glorified Christ is not 'taken away from the world', but is above the world and thereby in relation with the world. 'Heaven' means participation in this mode of existence of Christ [...]; no topographical definition can be given, nor can it be situated outside or inside our spatial structure; nevertheless it cannot be detached, understood merely as a 'situation', from the cosmos as a whole943.
It does not suffice to say that heaven is a state. 'With the proclamation of a new heaven and a new earth [...] the whole of created reality is included in beatitude. God's creature, the world, is—as the scholastics say—an “accidental” part of definitive joy'944.

### 8.2. The Second Death: Hell, the Perpetual Failure of Man

The time has come to direct our gaze toward those who hear Christ say: 'Depart from me, you cursed!' (Matt 25:41). Gathering the unanimous faith of all ages, the Catechism teaches:
We cannot be united with God unless we freely choose to love him [...]. This state of definitive self-exclusion from communion with God and the blessed is called 'hell' [...]. Immediately after death the souls of those who die in a state of mortal sin descend into hell, where they suffer the punishments of hell, 'eternal fire' (cf. DS 76). The chief punishment of hell is eternal separation from God, in whom alone man can possess the life and happiness for which he was created and for which he longs (CCC 1033, 1035).
Reward for the good and punishment for the wicked are expressions of God's justice and mercy. It is this latter point that creates difficulties for us. We can readily grasp the meaning of temporal punishments, which serve for the purification and expiation of the guilty; but it eludes our comprehension that punishment should have no end, because this excludes that medicinal character essential to penalties. The irrevocability of the penalty appears to us in contradiction with God's mercy. We can understand that the will of the blessed remains fixed in the good, but how is it possible that one should choose to remain perpetually attached to evil?
If eschatology concerns the object of hope, it is impossible for hell to be included within it. We hope to be saved and we desire that others be saved; it is not Christian to hope for condemnation. If hope extended to the object of faith, one would have to conclude that hell, not being part of our hope, would not even constitute a matter of faith. 'What can one hope for without believing in it? On the other hand, one can believe something that one does not hope for: for what Christian does not believe in the punishments of the wicked, without nevertheless hoping for them?'945.
It seems a contradiction in terms to speak of redemption while maintaining the existence of hell as a perpetual punishment for demons and for all men who die estranged from God. Hell renders eternal that which Christian faith affirms is not eternal, but had an origin in history: namely, evil. If evil had a beginning, it is supposed that it will also have an end, so that ultimately 'God may be all in all' (1 Cor 15:28). From the time of Origen and subsequently across various epochs, including our own, voices have arisen suggesting a universal reconciliation at the end of time. How can a merciful God permit that there should be persons forever estranged from Him? Yet here other questions arise no less pressing: what meaning would it have if all were saved regardless of their deeds? Where does creaturely freedom end? What would become of the justice God has promised? What real meaning would mercy retain?
In this section we shall proceed by studying the biblical affirmations, which will enable us to undertake a theological reflection on the punishment of hell in relation to sin, so as to address both the question of the harmony between justice and mercy and the possibility of a final reconciliation. We shall then conclude by reflecting on how to approach this question in the contemporary world.

### 8.2.1. The Biblical Message

The Latin term *inferi* stands in continuity of meaning with the Hebrew *Sheol* and the Greek *Hades*. In common parlance it came to be rendered as 'hell' and passed into usage to indicate the hereafter in which the dead who will not share in beatitude dwell. But these terms have translated distinct biblical concepts: the underworld (*Sheol*, *Hades*), which designates a distant place, forsaken by God and hostile to men; hell properly so called (*Gehenna*), the place of future, final punishment; and the abyss (*abyssos*), which is the abode of demons946.
Regarding *Sheol*, it must now be added that a certain evolution can be discerned in its understanding947. In late Judaism, the emergence of the doctrine of the immortality of the soul altered the conception of *Hades*. Reward and punishment begin after death, in *Hades*; and this was, according to Flavius Josephus, the view of Pharisees and Essenes, in contrast to the Sadducees. According to a later conception, the souls of the righteous after death enter into heavenly beatitude, while the souls of the wicked are punished in *Hades*, which 'loses its character as the abode of all souls and becomes the place of punishment for the wicked'948. It appears that 'the thought of eternal damnation had developed rapidly in Judaism during the last two pre-Christian centuries'949.
The term *Gehenna* is the Greek transcription of the Aramaic *Gē-hinnōm*, which designated a valley south of Jerusalem, the valley of the son or sons of Hinnom (cf. Josh 15:8; 18:16; Jer 32:35), in which children had been offered in sacrifice (cf. 2 Chron 28:3; 33:6; 2 Kgs 21:6; Jer 7:31), and which was therefore also called the 'valley of Slaughter' (Jer 7:32; 19:6); so that this should never recur, Josiah defiled it (cf. 2 Kgs 23:10). Jewish apocalyptic literature came to view this valley as the site of hell after the universal judgment. Thence *Gehenna* came to signify the definitive hell, becoming *par excellence* the place of punishment and assimilating the analogous conception of *Hades*. Closely linked to this is the idea of a final judgment in *Gē-hinnōm*950.
In the NT Jesus uses the term *Gehenna* to describe the place of condemnation for the devil and those who follow him. It is a reality that pre-exists man (cf. Matt 25:41), an abyss of fire (cf. Matt 13:42, 50), the sphere of eternal punishment after the judgment (cf. Matt 23:15, 33; 25:41, 46)951.
*Gehenna* is accompanied by descriptions that include 'unquenchable fire' (Matt 5:22; cf. 5:29; 13:42, 50; Mark 9:43–48), or 'eternal fire' (Matt 18:8–9; 25:41), where both body and soul perish (cf. Matt 10:28), and which in the parable of the weeds is described as a 'furnace of fire' (Matt 13:42)952. Jesus uses other images, such as 'weeping and gnashing of teeth' (Matt 8:12; cf. 13:50; 22:13; 24:51; 25:30; Luke 13:28), or a place 'where their worm does not die' (Mark 9:48). The Book of Revelation describes the fate of the beast and those who worship it: 'thrown alive into the lake of fire that burns with brimstone' (Rev 19:20), which is identified with the 'second death'—that is, the destiny of those whose name, on the day of judgment, is not found written in the book of life (cf. Rev 20:10–15; 21:8).
Descriptions of physical or psychic pain are not the most distressing element of this doctrine. Its dramatic force is seen in Jesus' words concerning exclusion from eternal life: 'Depart from me, you cursed!' (Matt 25:41), already prefigured in the parable of the virgins when the bridegroom says to the foolish ones: 'I do not know you' (Matt 25:12), and likewise in the Sermon on the Mount, speaking of those who on the day of the Lord will say they had frequently invoked him: 'And then will I declare to them: “I never knew you; depart from me, you evildoers!”' (Matt 7:23). Paul speaks of those who are perishing (cf. Phil 3:19; 1 Cor 1:18; 2 Cor 2:15; 4:3; 1 Tim 6:9), who 'shall suffer the punishment of eternal destruction and exclusion from the presence of the Lord and from the glory of his might' (2 Thess 1:9), 'because they refused to love the truth and so be saved' (2 Thess 2:10; cf. 1 Cor 6:9). It is a self-exclusion, which Jesus describes as the only unforgivable sin—that against the Holy Spirit, namely the sin of one who places himself outside God's forgiveness (cf. Matt 12:32). 'He who does not believe is condemned already' (John 3:18). Thus, 'the thought of eternal damnation recurs constantly both in the teaching of Jesus Himself [...] and in the writings of the Apostles'953.

### 8.2.2. The Sufferings of the Damned and the Relationship between Hell and Sin

The nature of condemnation is understood in relation to the theology of sin, which is the reverse side of the coin of the doctrine of grace. Hell as a permanent state reveals the terrifying depth of sin. It is the definitive rejection of God, which is not a mere theory, but the actual condition of the devil and his followers. If the first sin brought with it man's death—both as the death of the soul (separation from God) and as physical death (separation of body and soul)—the 'second original sin'954 brought about the death of the God-Man Jesus Christ upon the cross. Hell stands revealed as the outcome of unrepented sin, the mystery of the fullness of opposition to God and his creation. What does it entail that this dreadful mystery (*mysterium iniquitatis*) should be shared in by men?
Following Augustine, the Angelic Doctor considers that the formal aspect of sin lies in detachment from God. In the state of innocence, in which man's lower powers were subject to his higher powers, and the body to the soul, by virtue of the soul's perfect union with God, there could be no disorder within man that was not caused by a prior disorder in his relationship with God955. Detachment from God is the principal aspect of sin, which entails a disorder in man's relationship with creatures. Sin 'presents both of these aspects: turning away from God and adhering or turning toward created good'956. Between these two dimensions there exists a 'vicious' circle:
In sins where the enjoyment of a mutable good is principally intended, as in lust, avarice, and the like, the turning toward comes naturally before the turning away, which is not principally intended, but follows outside the intention by reason of the disordered turning toward. In sins, however, where turning away from God is directly intended, as in unbelief, despair, and the like, the turning away from the immutable good comes first, and then the turning toward a mutable good957.
If condemnation to hell is the outcome of sin, two types of punishment will correspond to these two aspects. To the turning away from God (*aversio a Deo*) corresponds the pain of loss (*poena damni*), while the disordered turning toward creatures (*conversio ad bonum creatum*) receives as retribution the pain of sense (*poena sensus*)958. Since the *aversio* and the *conversio* are intertwined in the nature of sin, so too in its punishment the pain of loss and the pain of sense are intertwined, which provides a reason for rejecting the hypothesis of the annihilation of the damned959.

### 8.2.2.1. The Pain of Loss

The chief penalty of hell is eternal separation from God: 'Depart from me, you cursed!' (Matt 25:41). It is a divine act of casting away from Himself one who has obstinately persisted in rejecting Him. 'It would be better to endure a thousand thunderbolts than to see that sweet face turn away from us and those serene eyes refuse to look upon us'960.
Perpetual separation from God is not merely the lack of an optional gift for human happiness. If in this life estrangement from God is not always perceived in its true gravity—conversion, the fruit of awareness of one's sins, is always a grace from God—in the life to come, once time has ended, the terrible consequences of not having reached the vision of God will be revealed in their depth. 'Extreme misery will then consist in the fact that the intellect will be totally deprived of the divine light, and the affection will be obstinately opposed to God's goodness: this is the specific misery of the damned, which is called the pain of loss'961. The darkness of the intellect that will never again behold the light, and the malice of the will separated forever from goodness, will provoke in the human spirit a permanent state of frustration at not having attained its end, of despair in understanding that there is no longer any possibility of attaining it, and of loneliness from having enclosed itself within itself962.
The Church's teaching stands in contrast with the notion that hell is merely the continuation of man's sinful state, the fruit of a choice that would thereby find its own happiness963. It has been argued by some that
hell is not [...] something that God imposes after examining our misdeeds [...]. It is the mode of existence of a man who is pleased with himself: for an entire eternity. Who no longer has anything and aspires to nothing other than himself [...]. There is no tragedy to hell, because at root hell cannot exist as a place964.
It is true that man bears responsibility for his own destiny. But this explanation fails sufficiently to appreciate that sin is an offence against God, a separation from Him and from others. Hell is not simply the continuation of a lifestyle, but a true punishment. This theory presupposes a certain individualism and spiritualism, whereas Christian anthropology maintains that man is not self-made, but constituted in dialogue.

### 8.2.2.2. The Pain of Sense. The Fire

The pain of sense 'corresponds to guilt in its aspect of disordered turning toward a transient good, just as the pain of loss corresponds to guilt in its aspect of aversion or detachment from the imperishable good'965. Scripture employs various expressions to refer to this bodily affliction, but fire has taken precedence over all others966. While they do not deem it improper to interpret other descriptions metaphorically967, the Fathers and Doctors, as well as various ecclesial documents, have taught that the fire of damnation is to be understood in a real sense and not as a mere metaphor968. This raises the question of how it is possible for a spiritual soul to suffer a bodily punishment. After the final judgment there will be the bodily suffering of the damned, but the existence of fire and its punitive character, not only toward the damned but also toward demons, precedes the final resurrection.
Augustine notes the paradox that while 'the first death expels the unwilling soul from the body, the second death retains the unwilling soul in the body. Both deaths share in common that the soul suffers from its own body what it does not wish'969. Thus he explains that
even spiritual beings, devoid of bodies, can in a real though astonishing manner be tormented with the punishment of sensible fire; for if the spiritual being of men, which is likewise certainly incorporeal, could in time be united to the parts of a body, it will also be able outside time to be bound indissolubly in relationship to its own body970.
Saint Thomas notes that this difficulty led some to view fire as a metaphor and not real971, and explains that incorporeal substances suffer corporeal fire *per modum alligationis* (by way of binding): if God created the soul bound to the body as its form, all the more is it possible for Him to bind the spirits of the damned to fire; this is their affliction—to know themselves bound to the lowest things as a punishment972. The reasoning is similar to Augustine's. Sin is the failure of submission to God; the punishment consists in the soul being afflicted by that which caused it to sin. If the soul turned away from God by turning toward creatures, the punishment is to remain subjected to creatures973. Just as the pain of loss corresponds to the *aversio a Deo*, provoking an *aversio Dei ab homine*, so the pain of sense coincides with the *conversio ad bonum creatum*, provoking a *conversio boni creati in hominem*. Creatures turn against the one who sought to dominate them in a disordered manner. The *alligatio* expresses how created goods will eternally oppress the damned.
Man is called to exercise dominion over creatures by way of use, not abuse. In our experience, when one stands at a prudent distance, fire enlightens and warms; when one is too close, it burns and destroys. So it is with created goods: when a prudent use is made of them, one lives the detachment necessary to enjoy them rightly; when their use becomes disordered, creatures rebel, already on this earth. It is commonly said that 'God always forgives, men sometimes forgive, but nature never forgives'974. One who eats to excess suffers indigestion; one who gambles excessively becomes an addict, and so on. The fire of hell represents this reality: the revolt of creation against the man who makes a disordered use of it. This seems to us to be the meaning of the *alligatio* of Thomas and Augustine.

### 8.2.3. The Perpetuity of Damnation: The Fathers, St Thomas, and Contemporary Debates

### 8.2.3.1. The Fathers and the Position of Origen

The NT texts concerning the definitiveness of punishment left no room for doubt among the early Fathers of the Church. Irenaeus teaches: 'The ruin of those who disobey the Word of God, despise his coming, and turn back, has increased, being no longer temporal but eternal. Those to whom the Lord will say: “Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire”, will be condemned forever'975.
The idea of eternal punishment provoked reactions, because it seemed in conflict with the Christian portrayal of God and man. We have already referred to Origen, who advanced the hypothesis of a universal reconciliation (*apokatastasis*) following the medicinal, and therefore temporal, penalties of hell. Even though the depth of evil makes its sinister reality felt in history and caused the death of God in Christ, it cannot possess a definitive substance; hence Origen 'could not, however, totally renounce the hope that precisely in this suffering of God a limit was set to the reality of evil, whereby the latter would have lost its definitiveness'976. This hope was embraced by a number of Fathers and ecclesiastical writers of the fourth and fifth centuries (Gregory of Nyssa, Didymus, Diodore of Tarsus, Theodore of Mopsuestia, Evagrius Ponticus, Ambrose, Jerome), who upheld various versions of the so-called 'doctrine of mercy'977, whether by exempting Christians from the possibility of damnation or by granting the lost, in virtue of God's mercy, some mitigation of their punishments.
Other Fathers were sharply critical of Origen. John Chrysostom reiterates, speaking of punishment, that 'for now what takes place is for correction, but hereafter it will be for retribution'978. Augustine maintains that the permanence of hell and the eternity of heaven are reciprocal. 'It is unreasonable to reckon eternal punishment within the limits of a prolonged fire and to believe eternal life to be without end, since Christ in the very same passage, in one and the same sentence, encompassing both destinies, said: “And these will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life” (Matt 25:46)'979.
The Church, while admitting that it is possible to infer an expectation of universal reconciliation from certain elements of doctrine taken together, has always accorded primacy not to theological speculation, but to the Scriptures. Notwithstanding Origenist resonances through the centuries, 'dogma rests on solid ground when it speaks of the existence of hell and the eternity of its punishments'980. Thus the edict of Justinian to the Patriarch of Constantinople, promulgated at the Synod of Constantinople (543): 'If anyone says or holds that the punishment of demons and impious men is temporary and that it will have an end after a certain time, that is to say that there will be a restoration (*apokatastasis*) of demons or impious men, let him be anathema' (DS 411). The principal conciliar definition in this regard is found in the profession of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council: 'All will rise again with their own bodies which they now bear, to receive according to whether their deeds were good or evil, the latter eternal punishment with the devil, the former eternal glory with Christ' (DS 801).

### 8.2.3.2. St Thomas's Reflection on the Perpetual Character of Damnation

Just as in heaven it is no longer possible to sin, so analogously, in the state of condemnation, repentance is no longer possible981. 'The souls that immediately after death are struck by punishment are rendered immutable in their will [...]. The penalty of the damned souls would not be perpetual if they were capable of changing their will for the better'982. Joining in Augustine's critique of the Origenist view, Saint Thomas observes that 'this opinion is wholly unreasonable. For just as the demons are obstinate, and on that account deserve to be punished eternally, so too the souls of men who die without charity are obstinate in evil: for “what the fall was to the angels, death is to men”, as the Damascene says'983.
While it is understandable that, in the case of the blessed, whoever has reached the end no longer wishes to lose it, the reason for the obstinacy of the damned will in evil is not equally obvious, resulting as it does not in a participation in God's eternity, but in an interminable duration of a suffering existence984. Why can there be no repentance in the future life? Why is a punishment without end inflicted, incapable of generating conversion? Why does the will remain fixed in evil? It would be easy to appeal to a divine decree that renders the sinner's will immovable, but this does not seem to respect the realism of the free nature of the human being; here we touch upon the paradox of a will that, in its obstinacy against God, obtains what it wants: the slavery of no longer being able to love God, while He maintains in existence those who hate Him forever.
In the damned, the penalty is the disorder of the will that they have set against God, whereby they paradoxically obtain what they willed: separation from God forever985. Exclusion from grace, without which one cannot escape from sin, ensures that they cannot 'change their will for the better'986. The reprobate 'will not repent of sin directly and properly, because attachment to the malice of sin will remain in them; but they will repent of it indirectly, inasmuch as they will grieve over the punishment they suffer on account of sin'987. Since the 'worm' does not die, the soul of the damned is 'tormented by a useless repentance'988.
We can now approach the severe and mysterious words of the Lord concerning the traitor: 'It would have been better for that man if he had not been born' (Matt 26:24). Saint Thomas observes that non-existence is not desirable in itself, being the pure privation of the good that is existence. However, if it is considered as the elimination of a painful life, it bears an aspect of good, because the privation of an evil is a good, as Aristotle said. 'For the damned it is better not to exist than to exist miserably [...]. From this point of view, the damned can prefer not to exist, by a deliberate act of reason'989. The question remains: why does God permit such a state? How is this reconciled not only with his justice, but above all with his mercy?

### 8.2.3.3. Contemporary Debates

Perpetual condemnation is a mystery difficult to believe, perhaps even more acutely so in the contemporary world. Why does God permit the eternal existence of beings who hate Him? How can good and evil coexist, not in history, but for all eternity? There are two ways of evading this problem: one is to maintain that the damned will be annihilated (conditionalism); the other is to affirm that in the end all will be saved (universalism)990.
The annihilation hypothesis, maintained by the Gnostics, was defended by certain liberal Protestant theologians in the nineteenth century, and revived in recent times by certain Adventist sects and the Jehovah's Witnesses. Eternal survival is made conditional upon man's state of righteousness at the moment of death, and immortality is viewed as a gift of divine grace even for the soul. It follows that the wicked, dying outside grace, will be annihilated either immediately or after a temporary hell.
Faced with this theory, it must be noted that while the damned, by reason of their rejection of God, are failed beings, one cannot ignore what Scripture affirms: 'For you love all things that exist, and have loathing for none of the things which you have made, for you would not have formed anything if you had hated it' (Wis 11:24)991. The annihilation of sinners would run counter to the divine plan of creating beings with a spiritual and immortal soul, and would fail to respect the distinction between nature and grace that holds true even in consummated eschatology992.
The hypothesis of universal salvation, upheld in the past by the Albigensians and later by certain liberal Protestant theologians under the influence of Schleiermacher, has become common in certain sectors of contemporary theology. Karl Barth remarked that its possibility should not be excluded, given the overwhelming power of divine grace and the fact that Jesus on the cross expiated the estrangement of all men993. Here the Origenist idea carries weight: if God did not eliminate all evil, evil would be an eternal principle alongside Him.
It must be observed that, while it is possible to hope regarding each individual person that he may be saved, it makes no sense to believe in an *a priori* assertion of the salvation of all, because faith teaches us that whoever dies in mortal sin is damned. Certain authors, conscious of this, speak of hell as a hypothesis rather than an existential reality, basing themselves on the fact that, while the Church can declare the holiness of certain of her members, never—not even in the case of Judas—has she deemed herself to possess the authority to speak of the actual condemnation of anyone994. Even if the existence of hell must be admitted, one may hope that it is empty. According to H.U. von Balthasar995, this doctrine was taught not only by Origen, but also by Gregory of Nyssa and Maximus the Confessor. The reasoning is that, since the end must coincide with the beginning, evil, which is a finite reality, cannot endure forever. Balthasar adds his own spiritual interpretation of Christ's descent into hell996, the supreme expression alongside the cross of the *kenosis*, revealing its incredible power to transform reality and the hearts of men.
Several scholars have contested that Gregory and Maximus ever adopted the Origenist doctrine of *apokatastasis*997, for which, according to certain biblical scholars, no scriptural foundation exists998. Furthermore, it must be borne in mind that the object of Christian hope is twofold: we hope for the coming of Christ, and we hope to be personally saved999. The act of hope entails trust in God's benevolent action toward each person, but it cannot encompass the free response of another person. We desire the salvation of others; indeed, we commit ourselves to it through evangelisation; yet the salvation of everyone is not an adequate object of hope in the strict sense, inasmuch as God does not override human freedom. The individual aspect of salvation stands in relation to the collective aspect, but the two are not confused.
The question of the number of the saved and the damned is not an article of faith, but a spiritual conviction depending on historical epochs and individual sensibilities. If today many believe that hell is for few, in the past the prevailing opinion was the opposite. Such was the case in the patristic era, as a reaction to Origenist doctrine. Later, at various moments, the laxity of Christian life prompted pastors to preach with insistence on the gravity of damnation. Among the saints we find diverse sensibilities, from those tending toward pessimism to those who are more optimistic. Several mystics (cf. Saint Catherine of Siena, Saint Teresa of Jesus, Sister Lucia of Fatima) described the vision of hell granted them by Jesus as an experience of spiritual benefit1000.
The Lord had already answered the question concerning the number of the saved with the admonition to 'strive to enter by the narrow door; for many, I tell you, will seek to enter and will not be able' (Luke 13:24), specifying that 'the gate is wide and the way is easy, that leads to destruction, and those who enter by it are many. For the gate is narrow and the way is hard, that leads to life, and those who find it are few' (Matt 7:13–14); 'many are called, but few are chosen' (Matt 22:14). Without entering into statistics, Jesus indicates the way, while the antithesis many/few possesses not a quantitative, but a personal value: for Him, as for the shepherd of the sheep, one is already many, so that he 'leaves the ninety-nine in the wilderness, and goes after the one which is lost' (Luke 15:4).
One must consider the strong symbolism of the number 144,000 saved (cf. Rev 7:4), 'a great multitude which no man could number' (Rev 7:9), signifying the superabundance of salvation offered by God to men1001, which may lead one to think that the lost will be few and the saved many, as the encyclical *Spe salvi* suggests (cf. SS 45–46). The Church's doctrine on hell is 'a call to responsibility' and also 'a pressing call to conversion' (CCC 1036).

### 8.2.4. The Second Death between Divine Justice and Mercy

According to Augustine, at the root of the incomprehensibility of the idea of perpetual damnation lies our inability to perceive the enormity of original sin. 'If all persisted in the punishments of just condemnation, in no one would merciful grace be made manifest; and if all were brought back from darkness to light, in no one would the reality of punishment be manifested'1002. While the existence of the blessed manifests divine mercy, the damned would also be 'necessary' in order that justice might be shown. These words sharpen our perplexity regarding what meaning the existence of hell can have in God's design. Let us reflect first on whether justice and mercy can exist separately in God, and specifically whether there is an absence of mercy toward the damned. We shall then seek to cast some light on the subject by considering the questions regarding the order and freedom of creatures.

### 8.2.4.1. On Justice and Mercy

Thomas asks whether justice and mercy can be attributed to God1003. In God there is no commutative justice, which governs exchanges between equals, because He is the Creator and we are his creatures. Distributive justice, proper to the ruler who maintains order by administering goods according to what each deserves, belongs to God in the highest degree1004. On the other hand, mercy likewise does not belong to God if considered as a feeling of sorrow for another's misery; but it belongs to Him by reason of the effect that such a feeling produces, namely the will to deliver the other from evil1005.
Having said this, Thomas asks whether both attributes are present in every work of God, and answers that every work of God presupposes both the one and the other. In his action, God cannot disregard either his wisdom or his goodness, and this makes Him just toward Himself; nor can He disregard the fitting order in creation, and this makes Him just toward creatures. Being the Creator, his relationship with creatures transcends justice, because He created them freely out of his goodness; hence 'every work of divine justice always presupposes the work of mercy, and is founded upon it', so that 'man's very being has no other reason than the divine goodness. And thus in every work of God mercy appears as its primary root'1006.
Recalling Augustine's idea from which we set out, Thomas notes that 'certain works are attributed to justice and others to mercy, because in some justice appears more evident, and in others mercy'1007; in the former case he cites the damnation of the wicked, and in the latter the justification of the sinner. Both divine operations manifest both attributes: in justification justice is also manifested, 'since God forgives sins on account of love, which nevertheless He Himself mercifully infuses'1008; and, coming to the point that concerns us, 'even in the damnation of the reprobate mercy appears, not indeed by way of total remission, but by a certain clemency, inasmuch as God punishes less than is deserved'1009.
Condemnation is not only not unjust; it is not even unmerciful. It stands in relation to the order of justice and to the order of personal freedom. The guilt of damnation lies in man's self-sufficient closure against a God who patiently offers the possibility of repentance. Condemnation is the effect of that obstinacy of the free will that keeps itself estranged, and thus divine justice is mysteriously accomplished, restoring order within a creation damaged by sin. Let us examine these two aspects, which, if they do not explain everything, can at least shed light on this tremendous mystery.

### 8.2.4.2. Justice and Order

Eternal punishment seems useless for the sinner in his individuality, but one must bear in mind the cosmic and social order against which sin offends. Evil deeds not only harm those who commit them, but introduce deleterious effects into the world. Condemnation forms part of the re-establishment of the cosmic and social order of justice. Hell has a meaning for the whole, even if it seems to have none for the individual.
In the very moral conscience of man there is something that reacts against the loss of such a perspective. Is not the God who is Love also definitive Justice? Can He allow these terrible crimes to remain unpunished? Is not definitive punishment in some way necessary to achieve moral balance in the deeply entangled history of mankind? Is not hell in a certain sense the 'last life-raft' for man's moral conscience?1010.
Saint Thomas argues that it is an error to contrast the good God with the God who punishes, as the Gnostics asserted. The punishment of sins contributes to the restoration of the order of justice1011. Eternal condemnation is of no benefit to the damned soul, but it can be of benefit to those journeying on this earth, 'since punishments serve to correct not only when they are inflicted, but also when they are decreed'1012. That evil should be punished serves to 'maintain divine justice'1013, which is not contradictory to his mercy: 'Almighty God, precisely because He is good, does not delight in the torments of the wretched damned. Yet, since He is also just, He is never appeased by the punishment of the sins of the reprobate'1014.
These words may seem incomprehensible to our contemporary sensibility. In them is hidden a wisdom belonging to a higher order, yet one that does not oppose the deep truth inherent in the creature thirsting for justice. While it would be a failure for God if all were damned, 'nothing prevents some among men or angels from perishing eternally: since the intent of the divine will is attained in the others who are saved'1015.
Saint Thomas rejects Origen's error not only for its contradiction of Scripture, but also for its paradoxical notion of God's mercy, which on the one hand was excessively extended and on the other excessively restricted:
For the reason is identical why the good angels are held to abide in eternal beatitude and the bad angels are punished eternally. Hence, just as he thought that the demons and the souls of the damned would at a certain moment be delivered from punishments, so he held that the angels and the souls of the blessed would at a certain moment pass from beatitude to the miseries of the present life1016.
One must seek to understand, so far as one can, that 'he has rendered himself deserving of an eternal evil who has destroyed in himself a good that ought to have been eternal'1017. If man sins against the infinite God, 'since the punishment cannot be infinite in intensity, because the creature is incapable of an infinite magnitude, it remains only that it be infinite in duration'1018: guilt remains forever, because it cannot be remitted except through grace, which man can no longer recover after death.

### 8.2.4.3. Justice and Freedom

Ultimately, we find ourselves before the mystery of freedom, both of God and of creatures. God created freely, without constraint, so that the existence of creatures is the fruit of his free will to love and to be loved. Human and angelic creatures enjoy the inestimable gift of freedom, which God cannot take away without doing violence to the nature of the beings He created.
It cannot be that God fails to take creaturely freedom seriously. God is truly God, omnipotent and good, and therefore He cannot treat in the same manner one who has acted in accordance with the good and one who has obstinately persisted without repentance in committing terrible evils. It is a belief common to the great religious traditions that God is a rewarder. If in this life punishment has a curative purpose, when one enters the definitive dimension of existence, the time for repentance is exhausted, because the decision has become eternal: such is the awesome power of freedom.
Mercy cannot nullify the realism of human action; it is neither indifference nor tolerant neglect, for God committed Himself to our salvation even unto the death of Jesus on the cross. 'Mercy does not destroy justice, but is a certain fullness of justice'1019. Kierkegaard explained that omnipotence is manifested in the highest degree in the creation of free beings1020. It is a risk that God willed to take1021, because the freedom of his creatures is real, and proof of this is the ability to choose not to love—indeed, to hate—the Creator; and that not only for a time, but forever. Benedict XVI spoke of freedom as an 'omnipotence in reverse'1022.
Eternal punishment stems from the sin against the Holy Spirit, which consists in the hardening of heart of one who closes himself against repentance and forgiveness. It is not merely that bad actions depend on the inheritance of sin in our nature. Scripture teaches that man can definitively close his heart to God. It cannot be taken for granted that human freedom is incapable of grave crimes; on the contrary, hell remains always a real possibility, because we are capable of closing ourselves totally to God. This reveals paradoxically how far the power of human freedom can reach.
A view in which grave crimes are not the fruit of responsibility, but due to a fatally corrupted nature, is inadmissible. In such a perspective, evil would be part of the constitution of reality, and this would lead to a pessimistic and despairing ontological vision. Christianity maintains that history is the setting of a drama, of a struggle between good and evil, between grace and sin. It does not say that the two powers are of the same kind, but that the one permits the existence of the other without annihilating it. It is a dualism of freedom, an existential dualism, not an ontological dualism1023. Only the good is primordial.
One must recognise
the absolute respect that God shows for the freedom of his creature. Love is a gift that man receives; it is the consequent transformation of all his misery, of all his insufficiency; not even the 'yes' to such love springs from man himself, but is prompted by the power of this love. But the freedom to refuse the maturation of this 'yes', not to accept it as something of one's own—this freedom remains [...]. He [Christ] does not treat men as minors who, at root, cannot be held responsible for their own destiny; rather, his heaven is founded on freedom, which leaves even to the lost soul the right to will its own perdition1024.

### 8.2.5. Speaking of Hell Today?

Pope Francis has mentioned hell on several occasions. Thus he once admonished members of the mafia: 'Change your lives, convert, stop, cease doing evil! [...]. Convert, there is still time, so as not to end up in hell. That is what awaits you if you continue on this path'1025. In the commemoration of all the faithful departed, the Pontiff prayed to the Lord:
Look not upon our many human poverties, miseries, and weaknesses, when we appear before your tribunal to be judged for happiness or condemnation [...]. May none of your children be lost in the eternal fire of hell, where there can be no more repentance1026.
Benedict XVI explained that there can be
people for whom everything has become a lie; people who have lived for hatred and have trodden love underfoot within themselves. This is a terrifying prospect, but certain figures of our own history let us discern such profiles in a horrifying way. In such individuals there would be nothing left to remedy and the destruction of the good would be irrevocable: this is what is indicated by the word *hell* (SS 45).
John Paul II noted that 'the thought of hell—much less the improper use of biblical images—must not create psychosis or anxiety'. Yet one must not fall into the opposite extreme of expunging it from the Church's preaching, because 'it represents a necessary and salutary reminder to freedom, within the proclamation that the Risen Jesus has conquered Satan'1027.
It is not a matter of falling into a Manichaean view, but of not silencing a part of the Gospel proclamation, whose ultimate purpose is to exhort to vigilance, to responsibility, and to conversion, in harmony with the other aspects of eschatology (cf. CCC 1036).
