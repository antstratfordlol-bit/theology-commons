## Chapter 6 Visible Creatures: Man, Created in the Image and Likeness of God, in the Material World {#sanz-alfa-omega.6}

It is now time to turn our attention to visible creation, in which we human beings live immersed. In recent decades, awareness of the importance of respecting creation has grown keener. Technological development, positive in itself, has placed man in a position where he is capable of destroying nature. Ecological consciousness has gained strength, bound up with concern over the negative effects of certain human behaviours on the ecosystem and on climatic alterations across the earth. Pope Francis’s encyclical *Laudato si’*, in continuity with the teachings of John Paul II and Benedict XVI523, addresses itself to believers and all people of good will in order to foster a "responsibility for creation", a sensitivity towards an "integral ecology"—which includes human ecology—capable of facing contemporary challenges.

These considerations transcend the boundaries of dogmatic theology, touching directly upon ethical questions. Frequently this topic is situated within the study of the Social Doctrine of the Church. The teachings of the aforementioned pontiffs are found for the most part in their social encyclicals. Here we shall confine ourselves to a few considerations of a dogmatic-theological nature.

The work of creation is common to the whole Trinity, and this implies that a sign of their Author ought to be found in creatures. Theology has traditionally spoken of the *vestigia Trinitatis*, that is, of the fact that the footprints of the Persons of the Trinity are found in the visible created world. In a spiritual and free creature such as man, these footprints become the *imago Trinitatis*, because the human being is created in the image and likeness of God (cf. Gen 1:26).

In studying the divine creative act, we have made numerous considerations concerning the created world, its goodness, intelligibility, contingency, and so forth. We have likewise reflected upon its temporality and, on several occasions, upon evolution. There is no need to fall into repetition; we shall confine ourselves to an introduction to theological anthropology in two stages: the insertion of the human being into the cosmos, together with the question of dominion over it (as emerges from Gen 1), and certain distinctive characteristics of the creation of man and his original state (broadening the consideration to Gen 2). We must impose certain limitations upon ourselves, since, on the one hand, certain fundamental questions of Christian anthropology are shared with philosophical anthropology, and are therefore studied in greater detail there; and, on the other hand, the doctrine of grace and original sin will form the subject of another manual in this series (cf. TE 6).

The chapter will proceed by dealing first with the question of man’s dominion over the cosmos, then with the distinctiveness of the human being's constitution which serves as its foundation, concluding with a reflection on the Trinitarian dimension of creation.

### 6.1. The Human Being in the Cosmos (cf. Gen 1): Care for the Common Home

In the Priestly account, the emergence of the various material realities is not the outcome of a battle or of an ontological diminution, but rather of the divine word, whose creative efficacy produces things in their diversity: heaven and earth, light and darkness, the firmament, the waters of the seas and the dry land, sprouts and herbs, seeds and trees, the stars, the days and the nights, living creatures—that is, the birds in the heavens, the fish in the seas, wild animals, and cattle upon the earth (cf. Gen 1:1–25). A foundational characteristic is that "everything created by God is good" (1 Tim 4:4).

The Priestly account reaches a crucial turning point when it states:
> God said, "Let us make man in our image, after our likeness: and let them have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the birds of the air, and over the cattle, and over all the wild beasts, and over every creeping thing that creeps upon the earth." So God created man in his own image, in the image of God he created him; male and female he created them. And God blessed them, and God said to them, "Be fruitful and multiply, and fill the earth and subdue it; and have dominion over the fish of the sea and over the birds of the air and over every living thing that moves upon the earth" (Gen 1:26–28).

Previously, God had addressed living beings commanding them to multiply (v. 22), but here we find ourselves before a living being distinct from the others, yet sharing in the same world and the same material condition, and therefore he too is invited to fruitfulness. God addresses this new being in a special manner: to him alone does He give the command to exercise dominion over the other creatures, because of him alone is it said that he was created in His image and likeness524. Certain Fathers and various exegetes have highlighted the relationship between both statements, given that, if God is the true and sole *Dominus*, Lord of creation, being made in His likeness implies a participation in this dominion of His525. Man finds himself in the unique position of being under the divine lordship, the object of God's paternal and provident care, whilst simultaneously endowed with the capacity to exercise care over creatures, with a dominion that is not absolute, but participated.

These reflections are of striking relevance particularly in recent years, when the awareness has become firmly established that we find ourselves for the first time in history in a situation in which man appears capable of committing an authentic "terricide". Theology is not called upon to offer practical solutions to the crisis, but it must reflect upon the problem, all the more so when others have pointed to the worldview grounded in the Bible as a potential cause of the destruction of the earth.

### 6.1.1. The Roots of the Ecological Crisis

In the development of the diverse environmentalist currents and movements of recent decades, ideological responsibility for the ecological disaster has often been attributed to Christianity. Mircea Eliade observed:
> The alienation and estrangement of man from the earth are unknown and, what is more, inconceivable in all cosmic-type religions, whether "primitive" or Oriental; in such cases (that is to say, in the majority of religions known to history), religious life consists precisely in exalting the solidarity of man with life and nature526.

By contrast, "modern science would not have been possible without the Judeo-Christian doctrine that removed the sacred from the Cosmos and 'neutralised' and 'banalised' it [...]; with its anti-pagan polemic, Christianity desacralised the Cosmos"527. "Technics" in Western civilisation is the indirect result of Christianity, which took the place of myth in antiquity528.

Explicit criticism of Christianity arrived in a 1967 article on the historical roots of the ecological crisis, published in the journal *Science* by Lynn White. This author maintained that the victory of Christianity over paganism represented the greatest revolution in the history of culture—an anthropocentric turn that, by establishing a dualism between man and nature, regarded the exploitation of nature by man as the will of God529. In light of the fact that modern technology must be considered the fruit of the Christian idea of man's transcendence over nature, and that this technology has been at the root of ecological problems, the conclusion is clear: Christianity bears its share of blame530. White points out that the figure of Francis of Assisi also belongs to the Christian tradition, representing a departure from orthodoxy, because through his humility he preached the fundamental equality between man and other creatures. "Since the roots of our trouble are so largely religious, the remedy must also be essentially religious"531; he therefore proposed proclaiming Saint Francis the Patron Saint of ecology, which took place some years later during the pontificate of John Paul II532.

The German writer and activist Carl Amery coined the expression *the ruthless consequences of Christianity*533, which reveals the author's underlying premise: the biblical mandate to subdue the earth led man to destroy nature, steering towards the ecological catastrophe we experience today. Amery proposed turning one's gaze towards Eastern religions, which cultivate the idea of communion with nature.

From the standpoint of the history of ideas, the worldview originating from Judeo-Christianity was not responsible for the ecological crisis, because it is an understanding of the world and of man that dates back more than three thousand years, whereas the problem of disproportionate dominion has arisen only in recent centuries, coinciding with the progressive secularisation of Western societies, characterised by certain theories that may be termed post-Christian, such as Marxism or radical liberalism.

The roots of the problem lie in modern anthropocentrism534, which transformed the image of the world held by medieval *Christianitas*535. In the latter, characterised by a contemplative paradigm, man's sovereignty over nature was conceived as care and fulfilment, having the task of reconciling all creation with God536. In the Renaissance, this perspective was altered, and man came to be seen as the "God of the elements and animals"537. Whilst Francis Bacon could still assert that "nature is commanded only by obeying her"538, Galileo conceived his working method as subjecting nature to rigorous interrogation in order to compel her to give us the answers she must give539. Descartes was convinced that thanks to progress in technical knowledge "we might employ them in the same manner for all the uses to which they are adapted, and thus make ourselves, as it were, masters and possessors of nature"540.

Guardini emphasised that the statement in the first chapter of Genesis, that all was created by God, could be translated as follows:
> There is no nature in the modern sense. The modern idea of nature falsifies all determinations of existence; recognising that the world is a work, and that behind it stands the will of the One who willed it, puts it in order [...]. Man does not have to come first to order it, as though it were chaotic in itself, as the modern age has asserted; ordering it by means of the categories of the human spirit and the sense-giving power of his will. This is conceived so as to make God superfluous541.

### 6.1.2. The Understanding of Dominion in the Church's Teaching

Christian doctrine leads to overcoming fear of nature. The affirmation that God created everything for man, who is superior to the rest of created beings, implies that there are no dark, mysterious, and evil forces directing the course of events. Christianity demythologised the world once and for all (even if it obviously recognises demonic presence and activity, this is a superior, angelic power, not an intra-mundane one)542.

It is not fitting to return to a sacralisation of nature, as some have proposed, propagating a vague pantheistic mysticism with Gnostic overtones that ends by identifying God and nature543. Rather, it is a matter of accepting that man possesses the capacity to dominate nature, and of recognising that in the exercise of this capacity there emerges the ambivalence of human freedom, which can exercise a dominion of respectful and prudent service (political dominion), or a dominion of destruction, unbridled and selfish (despotic dominion), a clear consequence of sin544.

The problem is not dominion in itself, but what kind of dominion is exercised. As Paul VI recalled, "the time has come for him [man] to master his own mastery"545. It is an ethical question, and in this sense the Church teaches that dominion includes respect for the integrity of creation. John Paul II underlined that ecological disaster is the result of a false interpretation of the principle of dominion, leading to selfish use that fails to respect created order and ends. In *Sollicitudo rei socialis* (1987), the Pontiff affirmed that the power given by God to man is not absolute, as symbolised by the prohibition against eating from the tree, since we are creatures and must respect the natural law given by the Creator546. In *Centesimus annus* (1991), John Paul II clarified that man, as God's co-worker, must respect things in so far as they are created by God547. In his Message for the 1990 World Day of Peace, he had already recalled that peace is bound up with respect for the environment, and that sin has cosmic consequences, entailing a rupture of the harmony of creation.

Benedict XVI referred to this question on many occasions, earning the moniker, coined by some journalists, of the "Green Pope". Like his predecessor, he drew attention to the care of creation as a prerequisite for world peace, because "the deterioration of nature is closely connected to the culture that shapes human coexistence"548. Francis has made this theme one of the most characteristic elements of his teaching, from his inaugural homily through to the encyclical *Laudato si’*, on care for our common home.

All three emphasise that the concept of ecology, to be properly understood, must be broadened. There is not only an "environmental" ecology, but also a "human" and "family" ecology, or, as Paul VI put it, a "moral" ecology549.
> Human ecology also implies something very profound: the necessary relationship between the life of the human being and the moral law inscribed in his own nature, a relationship indispensable for being able to create a more dignified environment. Benedict XVI affirmed that there exists an "ecology of man" because "man too has a nature that he must respect and that he cannot manipulate at will" (Address to the Deutscher Bundestag, 22.09.2011). Along these lines, it must be recognised that our body places us in a direct relationship with the environment and with other living beings. The acceptance of one's own body as a gift from God is necessary for welcoming and accepting the entire world as a gift from the Father and our common home; by contrast, a logic of dominion over one's own body turns into an often subtle logic of dominion over creation. Learning to accept one's own body, to care for it and to respect its meanings, is essential for a true human ecology. Appreciating one's own body in its femininity or masculinity is likewise necessary in order to recognise oneself in the encounter with someone who is different. In this way it is possible joyfully to accept the specific gift of another man or woman, the work of God the Creator, and to be mutually enriched. Therefore, an attitude is not healthy that pretends to "cancel out sexual difference because it no longer knows how to confront it" (Catechesis, 15.04.2015)550.

Man will not be able to exercise a just dominion over nature if he does not know how to master himself, if he does not know how to recognise himself as part of created nature by virtue of his body, which is not an instrument external to his own identity. Here emerges the distinctiveness of the call to exercise dominion, proper to the human being, which points to the specificity of his creation, manifested, alongside other elements, in the second chapter of Genesis.

### 6.2. The Peculiarity of the Human Being in Gen 2

The Yahwist account (cf. Gen 2:4b–25) is, in comparison with the Priestly account, of a markedly anthropocentric character. From the outset it appears that God's creative work has the human being at its centre. Man comes from the earth and, at the same time, God breathes into him His breath of life (v. 7), taking him from the earth to place him in His garden (v. 8), where He enters into dialogue with him in order to entrust him with the task of tilling it and keeping it (v. 15), and hence of exercising dominion over the animals, which receive their names from him (vv. 19–20), and over the rest of creatures. He is not to do this alone, but in the company of one who is not subordinate to him, but like him, namely woman (v. 23), with whom he is called to form a unity (v. 24).

There thus appears the uniqueness of the creation of the human being, composed of the materiality of the body and the spirit breathed in by God. This character makes him a supple being, open both to the external world and to his fellow human beings, and ultimately to God Himself, who places him in a position to have a particular dialogical relationship with Him.

All these aspects can be subsumed within the deepest meaning of the term "culture", from the Latin *colere*, which includes, according to a common interpretation, three dimensions: cultivating the earth, cultivating oneself, and giving worship to God551. Let us examine, in reverse order, how these three aspects characterise man.

### 6.2.1. Giving Worship to God

"The Lord God formed man of dust from the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living being" (Gen 2:7). These words contain a fundamental teaching concerning man in his twofold relationship with nature and with God. Man is a creature like the others: God forms him taking him from the earth, from inanimate matter, and breathing into him life (*nefesh*).
> He does not exist except in so far as he derives from the breath of God. For him, to exist means to be in relationship with God, and this relationship is constitutive of his being. This is the first feature of biblical anthropology, which excludes, from the very beginning, any claim on man's part to be the creator of himself and to dispose of himself552.

After having fashioned man, "the Lord God planted a garden in Eden, in the east; and there he put the man whom he had formed" (Gen 2:8). Theology has traditionally understood this twofold divine action as a distinction between the creation of man with his natural capacities (dust and the breath of life), and his elevation to a state of communion with God (placement in the garden)553.

There is here a connection with creation in the image and likeness of God in Gen 1. According to Irenaeus, the image refers to the natural gifts that accompany human life, whereas the likeness designates the special gifts with which God had adorned human nature, which were lost through sin554. Among these, immortality stands out:
> For he created all things that they might exist, and the generative forces of the world are wholesome, and there is no destructive poison in them, and the dominion of Hades is not on earth. For righteousness is immortal [...]. God created man for incorruption, and made him in the image of his own eternity, but through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his party experience it (Wis 1:14–15; 2:23–24).

The classic theological distinction between nature and grace finds its foundation here. The affirmation of faith is that God created man in a state of original justice: grace is not merely a remedy for sin, but also precedes it, because it is given together with creation555. Although the Council of Trent did not wish to pronounce on the concrete moment of the infusion of grace, it is a truth of faith that prior to sin God bestowed upon the human being the gifts of nature and grace (cf. DS 1511).

This is an essential affirmation of Christian anthropology. God did not wish to create the human being in a purely natural condition (even though He could have done so), but from the first moment He granted the human being a special proximity to Himself556. God created man to draw him close to Himself, to bring him into the garden, to establish a covenant with him, showing that His absolute transcendence does not prevent Him from drawing near to His creatures. "Just as Yahweh is in contact with the people of Israel, so the Creator is in contact with man"557.

The covenant is manifested through the imagery of the trees of the garden: the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. God does not prevent man from feeding from the former; on the contrary, that is precisely what He desires: that man should live forever. But there is a boundary, expressed in the prohibition against eating from the second tree, which indicates to man the duty to acknowledge that God is the Creator, and he is His creature. The distinction between nature and grace, between creation and elevation, manifests the distinction between Creator and creature. After sin, man forfeits this privileged condition and is denied access to the tree of life (cf. Gen 3:22). Guardini emphasises that knowledge was not forbidden to man; on the contrary, it was the beginning of the dominion he was called to exercise over creatures, as shown by his giving names to the animals. What was forbidden to him was a particular mode of knowing: that which takes place in rebellion and pride, rather than in obedience.
> What was to be accomplished at the tree was not the renunciation of knowledge, but on the contrary, the foundation of all knowledge: the recognition, sustained by personal seriousness, that God alone is God, and that man, by contrast, is only man. Assenting to this fundamental truth or rejecting it was that "good and evil" before which everything had to be decided [...]. What, then, does the tree mean? [...] It says to man: in your knowledge, in your attitude, in your entire existence, the decisive factor in everything must be the fact that God alone is "God", and you, by contrast, are a creature; the fact that you are indeed His image, but only an image; He alone is the model. You can and must be lord of the world; but by His grace, because Lord by essence is He alone558.

Had man remained obedient to the divine commandment, he would not have lost communion with God; indeed, he would have reached, sooner or later, a state of perfect beatitude. Man was called to grow, to develop his capacities in the garden, cultivating the earth and family life—spheres that formed part of his relationship with God. The Priestly account illuminates this vision with the Sabbath, the day of God's rest, which becomes a day consecrated to the creature's praise and recognition of the Creator, to the worship of God. This worship is not separate from the rest of human activity: just as the six days of creation are fulfilled in the seventh, so the days of work look towards the day of rest, of worship. The dimensions of existence are not separated, but point to the eschatological dimension present from the beginning, which is also, remarkably, Christological. Let us examine this.

God desires to draw near to man, made in His image, and man is called to the fullness of life and beatitude in obedience to God. It may seem surprising that in Genesis man is not presented as a son of God. According to Renckens, this was due to the polytheism with which the formula was imbued at that time. The concept of "son" indicates a community of nature, whereas the notion of "image" also accommodates an inferior nature, and thus presented itself as more suitable for describing man's relationship with God, which involved both closeness and the necessary ontological distance559.

The notion of image is also powerful; it is not said that man *is* the image of God, but that he is created "in the image of God" (Gen 1:27)560. The theme of the image is little present in other books of the Old Testament (cf. Sir 17:3; Wis 2:23), but gains prominence in the New Testament, where it is said that Christ "is the image of the invisible God" (Col 1:15); this expresses the Christological perspective of protology561.

That man was created in the image of God is related to his having been created in Christ, through Him and for Him (cf. Col 1:16–17), who is the Image *par excellence*. Adam is "a type of the one who was to come" (Rom 5:14). Christ is "the last Adam" (1 Cor 15:45), who brings to full completion man's call to communion with God, which the first Adam, through sin, failed to realise. Through Christ's obedience unto death on the cross (cf. Phil 2:8), man is reconciled, recapitulated. The Paschal mystery constitutes the beginning of the new creation, in which man recovers access to the tree of life562.
> If creating belongs to God alone, sanctifying and quickening soul and body with an incorruptible life, plunging them into the life-giving fire of the Spirit, is likewise properly divine. It is this divine vocation of man, to which Adam was called from the origin, that Christ will fully realise through His resurrection563.

"Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s, and to God the things that are God’s" (Mt 22:21). Beyond the immediate sense, these words also contain a deeper meaning:
> The image of God is stamped not on gold, but on the human race. Caesar's coin is gold, God's coin is humanity [...]. Therefore, give your material wealth to Caesar, but preserve for God the unique innocence of your conscience, where God is contemplated [...]. Caesar, indeed, required his image on every coin, but God chose man, whom He created, to reflect His glory564.

> If Caesar demands his own image stamped on the coin, will not God require of man the divine image sculpted within him? [...]; just as the coin is returned to Caesar, so the soul, illumined and imprinted with the light of His countenance, is returned to God [...]. For Christ dwells in the inner man565.

"Man, every man, bears within himself another image, that of God, and therefore it is to Him, and to Him alone, that each one owes his own existence"566. Man's end consists in recognising his Creator and living with Him and for Him, as Christ taught by His life and His words, becoming Himself not only the life and the truth, but also the way (cf. Jn 14:6). Rendering worship to God, ultimately, means confessing that "Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father" (Phil 2:11).

### 6.2.2. Cultivating Oneself

#### 6.2.2.1. Human Individuality Composed of Matter and Spirit

"The Lord God formed man of dust from the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living being" (Gen 2:7). Behold the mystery of human existence, which, on the one hand, comes from the earth, and on the other, shares in the divine breath. Man, composed of body and spirit, comes into being. In the text, the term used is not *ruah*, but *neshamah*, which signifies "breath", complemented by the word "life", and thus man becomes a living person. This is linked both to the fact that in the Old Testament God alone lives by Himself and is the principle of all life, and also to the observation of the phenomenon of respiration: when a person breathes his last breath, he dies; life depends upon breath567.

The formation of man from the earth points to the fragility of the human condition, destined to return to the earth from which it was taken, and to the gratuitousness of the gift God makes to this creature when He elevates him to a higher condition of life in the garden. It signals a complementary methodology with respect to Gen 1: there God creates by means of His word; here we see God at work through the image of the craftsman, who moulds the human being as though out of clay—a motif that recurs in other Old Testament texts568.

From the text we can extract neither a contradiction nor a concordance with contemporary theories on the origin of humanity569. To think that man comes from the earth, that he participates in the evolutionary process, is compatible with the conviction that the emergence of man in the material world is an event resulting from a particular intervention of God's creative power. Rather than saying that spirit comes from matter, it seems more reasonable to think that matter comes from Spirit or Creative Reason, and that it is created as the presupposition for the appearance of the human spirit in the world.
> Becoming human is the uprising of the spirit, and such an event cannot be dug up with a spade. The theory of evolution does not take away faith; nor does it strengthen it. But it invites faith to understand itself more deeply, and thus to help man to understand himself and to become ever more what he is: the being who for all eternity can say "Thou" to God570.

Man is created as a being in whom matter and spirit, body and soul, belong to his original nature. Throughout history various explanations have been proposed in this regard; that of Saint Thomas has been adopted by the teaching of the Church571. Following Aristotle, the Angelic Doctor defined the soul as the *forma corporis*, in order to emphasise the substantial unity of soul and body. This formula indicates the original goodness of creation, which entails the goodness of matter and the body. We have already reflected upon this aspect, which the Church had to defend against various dualistic philosophies that regarded the body and matter as originating from an evil principle, as the source also of the evil within man, and therefore as something from which it is good to be liberated. This teaching of the Church is always accompanied by the affirmation of the transcendence of the soul over the body, which is manifested in two characteristics: its spirituality and its immortality. We shall address these aspects in the eschatological section.

The composition of spirit and matter is extended in the human being in the composition of diverse faculties and capacities: memory, intellect, will, and then the realm of passions, feelings, and affections, which were classically understood under the categories of the concupiscible and irascible appetites572.

If God had created man to exercise dominion over creation, it is logical to think that this dominion had first to concern himself—that man might integrate his capacities and faculties within the unity of his being and live in harmony with himself. The Church has taught that the state of original justice involved the presence of other gifts, in particular the gift of integrity (*donum integritatis*): man's submission to God implied the subjection in man of the lower faculties to the higher ones, and therefore freedom from concupiscence573.

After sin, the rupture of the vertical relationship with God leads to the rupture of man's horizontal relationships with himself and with others.
> God created man in His image, so that he might be lord of the world by grace, as God is by essence. Things were to bow to his will, just as he himself was to be obedient to his Lord [...]. Man cannot reign over God's work if he is disobedient to the Lord of this work. While man showed obedience to God, nature obeyed him574.

The consequence of his attitude of disobedience is that "man remains subject to that nature which he imagines he dominates"575. Man's life becomes a struggle between the spirit and the flesh, as Paul attests in expressive and realistic terms (cf. Gal 5:16–26)—a struggle, ultimately, between good and evil (cf. Rom 7:18–25). We have already observed that this is a historical-salvific, existential dualism of freedoms, not an ontological dualism576.

The pursuit of the fullness of human life requires the intellectual and moral virtues. Since man no longer possesses the gift of integrity, he must strive to attain it through the acts of the various virtues577. In his concrete conduct, man follows and/or reinforces his habits (*habitus*) which, if good, are virtues, and if inclined towards evil, are called vices. Virtues and vices do not necessarily determine man's concrete actions, which always remain free, but they condition them—and, in the case of vice, heavily so. As Pope Francis teaches, sin is one thing, corruption another578.

#### 6.2.2.2. Human Individuality in Its Relationship with Others

After creating the animals and establishing their inferiority with respect to man, God caused a deep sleep to fall upon him in order to form, from one of his ribs, woman, who becomes the fitting companion for man because she shares his very nature (cf. Gen 2:21–23). Sexual differentiation belongs to the human being, as the Priestly account had already specified: "Male and female he created them" (Gen 1:26).

Man is not a solitary being. To his original relationship with God there is added his relationship with the cosmos, as well as the relationship between male and female, which lies at the root of all other relationships within the human condition: between parents and children, among siblings, across different families and peoples. There exists a single humanity, originating from the same source, which ultimately traces back to the Creator. The fulfilment of man in marriage and family belongs to the divine creative plan, and this means that sexual reality is, like the rest, good and willed by God. The Church has reaffirmed her convictions on numerous occasions against those who consider sex and marriage as deriving from the evil one579. John Paul II devoted a significant part of his teaching to the so-called "theology of the body"580.

In the Priestly account, sexual differentiation is presented in terms of the transmission of life: "Be fruitful and multiply" (Gen 1:28); in the Yahwist account, the creation of woman responds to the problem: "It is not good that the man should be alone" (Gen 2:18), without reference to fecundity. Marriage emerges more as an expression of the communion of persons than of the transmission of life581. In both accounts, the complementarity between male and female has as its purpose mutual help, not only in the sense of procreation directed towards populating the earth582, but also in the management of family life, inasmuch as neither of the two exhausts the potentialities of human nature583. The task of cultivating and keeping implies that the preservation and conservation of life is inseparable from its growth and transmission. The human being is not called to shut himself up within the wealth of his own interiority; rather, his inner growth is bound up with openness to the diversity implied by the existence of the other. Whilst the body is the means of personal expression, man's spiritual condition allows him to open himself to the other584.

The description of the creation of woman from man's rib does not denote subjection or inferiority, but on the contrary, reciprocity and complementarity. The New Testament perspective provides the key to a deeper interpretation. Another Adam fell asleep upon the wood of the cross, and from his pierced side flowed blood and water, symbolising the sacraments of the Church (cf. CCC 766). There is a parallelism between Christ's spousal self-giving to the Church in the Paschal mystery and spousal love between man and woman: Saint Paul teaches that the latter is called to be a sign of the former (cf. Eph 5:21–32). Marriage is a sacramental reality of creation elevated to become a sacrament of the new creation585, showing once again the importance of the link between creation and salvation.

The subsequent chapters of primeval history recount the genealogies (*toledot*) that give rise to the birth of the various nations. The intertwining of the individual and collective (familial and social) dimensions of the human being is constant. This is shown dramatically by the story of Cain and Abel, which opens the great biblical theme of fraternity under the sign of an ever-timely question: "Am I my brother's keeper?" (Gen 4:9). We might add the story of Joseph, mistreated by his brothers (cf. Gen 37). The light of the New Testament will be definitive, inasmuch as God Himself, in Jesus, becoming our brother, restores our intimacy with God the Father and teaches us what it means to be human586. Giving worship to God and cultivating oneself in relationship with others are two dimensions that go hand in hand, as the Law of Israel states and Jesus brings to fulfilment: to love God above all things, and to love one's neighbour as oneself (cf. Mt 22:37–40; Deut 6:4–9 and Lev 19:18).

The diversity of peoples forms part of the richness of creation. In Gen 10, which presents the table of the descendants of the sons of Noah, "every conception that would see in human diversity a consequence of sin is excluded"587. The notion of one culture supplanting all others and reducing humanity to uniformity would be foreign to the biblical text. If in the first chapter of Genesis a demythologisation of nature was accomplished, something analogous now occurs for the world of nations, in that there is a "denunciation of all racism and of every claim of superiority on the part of a race"588. This is a singular fact in the history of religions, implying a fundamental truth: "Every man is a brother to man"589. Hospitality is proof that human fraternity is more important than national differences, because it recalls the universal unity of destiny of the human race. "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28). The character of Israel does not derive from a natural, quasi-mythical superiority, but from the divine election that takes place in history. "The people of Israel does not belong to the world of creation, to the natural order; it belongs to the order of sacred history, to the design of Salvation"590.

In this history, evil also grows, as shown by the episode of the Tower of Babel (cf. Gen 11:1–9). The punishment of the dispersal of peoples and the confusion of languages displays the ambiguity inherent in both diversity and unity: "It can be an expression of humanity's richness and it can also be a sign of its divisions"591 and its temptations, which manifest themselves in countless antagonisms and wars. In the Church too, the new People of God, diversity will manifest the Spirit, but it can degenerate into a source of misunderstandings that generate schisms. "The world presents itself simultaneously in a process of advance and in one of decay. It tends concurrently towards the perfection of good and towards the culmination of evil"592. Ultimately, "the dividing line between culture and barbarism is precisely this: whether or not there is unconditional respect for man"593.

### 6.2.3. Cultivating the Earth

Another aspect of the Yahwist account is the task given to man to till and keep the garden (cf. Gen 2:15), which subsequently becomes the mandate to cultivate the ground from which he was taken and to which he was sent back after sin (cf. Gen 3:23). In the subsequent chapters of primeval history, the various professions emerge (cf. Gen 4:2), beginning with the distinction between Cain the tiller of the soil and Abel the keeper of sheep. "In the creation account, as throughout the Old Testament, work is considered an essential constituent of man. A life without work could not be, in this perspective, a full and successful life; it would not be an existence worthy of man"594. The task of cultivating and keeping the garden forms part of the blessing of the human creature595.

In the Priestly account, God's creative work is presented as a labour, within which there is a time for rest. The days of activity find their end in a different day, which reveals the meaning of that activity. "The separation of the seventh day from the flow of daily occurrences is intended to indicate the end of the creature, whom God made to be His partner. The activity assigned to man is not his end; his end is rather the rest of eternity, to which the rest of the seventh day alludes and points"596.

Is it possible to find a connection between the six days of activity and the seventh day? In the *Epic of Atrahasis*, the creation of humans is celebrated as the liberation of the gods from the yoke of labour, and is therefore conceived as service to the gods, that is, in view of worship. In the Genesis account, man is created not for the cult of the gods, but for civilising activity upon the earth. Even though this is the Priestly document, worship is inserted into a broader context, which is man's dominion over the earth597. Following the interpretation of the Hebrew terms we translate as "to cultivate" and "to keep" (*‘abodah* and *shamar*), Adam has been described as the "priest of the cosmic temple" through his vocation to work the earth, collaborating with that God whose creative work is depicted as an activity of labour598.
> Nothing is more in keeping with man's biblical vocation than work, by means of which he transforms the material world. In this sense we say that nothing is more biblical than technology. Its development is profoundly in accord with God's plan, even when it is the work of men who do not believe in Him. But if technology is an aspect of man, it is only one aspect. The man of technology must also be the man of adoration. Six days are given to him by Yahweh to dominate the world, but one day also to acknowledge the Lordship of God. To suppress adoration means to mutilate man of half of himself599.

The Old Testament perspective takes on a new light from the fact that Jesus spent the greater part of His earthly life working; He lived a life marked, so to speak, by secularity600, in the hiddenness of daily family and working life at Nazareth. The first decades of Christ's life, upon which the Gospels dwell little and theological tradition has dwelt even less, cannot be separated from His redemptive mission. The Paschal mystery, the beginning of the new creation, cannot be detached from the early years of Jesus' life, just as in Genesis the seventh day is not severed from the first six, but is their crown. The cross of Jesus, lifted up from the earth and destined to be raised high by His disciples, is the supreme manifestation of love, in which there intersect the fruitfulness of work, understood as effort and sacrifice, and spousal fruitfulness which, as the fruit of the gift of one's own life, is capable of generating new life—that which springs from the pierced side of the Saviour.

Only in recent times, beginning with the emergence of the Social Doctrine of the Church, have the Magisterium and Catholic theology focused upon this point. In the past, the motto *ora et labora* had its meaning within the monastic context in which it arose. The Reformation involved an appreciation of daily industriousness in the lived experience of faith, which concerned above all the inner dimension of the subject, whilst society witnessed a process of secularisation wherein work and religion became increasingly estranged. With the precedent of the development of the Church's social doctrine, certain twentieth-century authors distinguished themselves by proposing a theology of work—indeed, a theology of earthly realities—echoed by ecclesial teaching, both in *Gaudium et spes* of the Second Vatican Council and in John Paul II's encyclical *Laborem exercens*. Spurred on also by the charisms raised up by the Spirit, the invitation to the sanctification of work and ordinary life has today become the common teaching of the Church, thereby taking up one of the challenges raised by the reformers601.

### 6.2.4. Participation in Creative and Provident Power

The special dignity of the human being, created in the image and likeness of God, makes him a creature who belongs to the material world and at the same time is called to transcend it—not in the sense of "abandoning" it, but of bringing it to fulfilment through the accomplishment of the task of governing it entrusted to him by the Creator.

Man is created "creator", in the sense that the fruitfulness proper to procreation and production is a participation in the power of the Creator602. "God, who is love and life, has inscribed in man and woman the vocation to a special participation in his mystery of personal communion and in his work as Creator and Father"603. If creation is participation in being, greater closeness to the divine Being and a more specific creaturely dependence upon Him translate into greater participation in His action in time. Through his work, man becomes a participant in divine providence604, a partner in a history that God wishes to share with His free creatures, which is a history of salvation. Man's collaboration in the divine plan of salvation is never his exclusive work, but is the consequence of his special dependence upon God605.

The reality of sin and human fallibility, individual and collective, renders an intra-historical culmination upon this earth illusory. The utopian character of the modern myth of progress is by now evident (cf. SS 17–23). In the course of human life, the transition from the aesthetic stage of childhood and youth to the ethical stage of maturity in work and family brings awareness of one's own mortality, which is inevitable and profoundly unjust. There appears the value of what has been called the "common life"606, that sphere in which so many persons have lived and live in an exemplary and often silent manner. The world of our experience opens up to the hope of the possibility of an existential continuation in the hereafter607.

The Christian, who experiences these stages of human life, feels himself a participant in the building of the Kingdom of God in history, in the hope of new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells608. Here the way opens to Christian eschatology, which we shall address shortly.

### 6.3. Creatures, Reflection of the Trinity: Vestigium and Imago

From the very beginning of our treatise we have affirmed, with Revelation, that creation is the work of the Trinity, exploring the Trinitarian dimension of the creative act. At the conclusion of this part on creatures, the question arises whether a trace of the Trinitarian creative action can be found within them.

The doctrine that recognises in created things the footprints of the Trinity (*vestigia Trinitatis*) is present in Augustine, who speaks of various "created trinities" in the world and, in a special way, in the human being (*imago Trinitatis*)609. Thomas Aquinas follows this doctrine when, proceeding from the principle that every effect must in some way represent its cause, he maintains that "we find in all creatures a representation of the Trinity by way of vestige"610. Earlier he had described it as a representation of the causality of the cause, not of its form, just as smoke represents fire. He then explains that this representation occurs
> inasmuch as there are found in each creature aspects that must be attributed, as to their cause, to the divine persons. For every creature subsists in its own being, has also a form that determines its species, and finally has an order towards something else. We say, then, that inasmuch as it is a created substance, it represents the cause or principle: and thus it points to the person of the Father, who is the principle without principle. Inasmuch as it has a given form or species, it represents the Word, since the form of a work of art derives from the mental concept of the artist. Finally, inasmuch as the creature has an order or tendency, it offers a likeness to the Holy Spirit, who is Love: for the order or aptitude of a creature towards something else derives from the will of the Creator611.

The consideration regarding rational creatures is different; by virtue of their more intense participation in the divine being, they are the image of the Trinity (*imago Trinitatis*):
> The processions of the persons present themselves as acts of the intellect and of the will, as has been said: for the Son proceeds as the Word of the divine intellect, and the Holy Spirit as Love of the will. Therefore in rational creatures, in which will and intellect are found, there is a representation of the Trinity by way of image, inasmuch as there is found in them a mental word and a love proceeding from it612.

The human and divine, natural and supernatural dimensions of our being make us understand not only the inviolable value of man, but also "in what sense the acts that enable the human being to come into existence, in which man and woman mutually give themselves to one another, are a reflection of Trinitarian love"613.

Only once Revelation has occurred can we perceive the reflections of the Trinity in creation. The Angelic Doctor will always maintain that neither created things nor man in particular lead to the knowledge of the divine persons, since this can only be the fruit of a supernatural Revelation. Created realities, including man, lead to the knowledge of the Creator, and this holds true once Revelation has taken place. The use of the psychological analogy to penetrate the mystery of the Trinity is therefore legitimate. At the same time, only supernatural Revelation allows us to grasp the profound reality of the Triune God, and of His creative and salvific design; hence, only from the Trinitarian perspective is there a full understanding of the mystery of creation and of the being of man as *imago Trinitatis*, which lies at the foundation of our understanding of the salvific mystery of our divine sonship in Christ. Saint Thomas expounds the link between Trinity and creation from the perspective of a circularity between faith and reason614.

Today these reflections may appear insufficient, or at least open to further development, but they shed light on proposals to elaborate a Trinitarian ontology615—proposals that must reckon with the irreducibility between the essential and personal dimensions of our discourse on God, which lies at the root of the Thomistic method described as *redoublement*616.

According to the Fourth Lateran Council, there is a single principle of reality, the Triune God. Behind this declaration lay the vision disseminated by Joachim of Fiore, according to which salvation history is divided into three stages: of the Father (Old Testament), of the Son (the Gospel), and of the Holy Spirit (the spiritual Church). This division implicates the Trinity entirely within the history of creation617. Hegel and Schelling, for different reasons, align themselves with Joachim's vision in so far as they conceive the being of God as implicated in the unfolding of history. Against this, it must be clarified that "dogma maintains the contrary: that God is not becoming, but being, that He is the full foundation of historical becoming"618.

Keeping these clarifications in mind, nothing prevents us from perceiving, as Pannenberg points out, that at the origin of the order and stability, distinction and structure that we observe in the world stands the Son/Word, and that the principle of dynamism, activity, and unification is the Holy Spirit619.

What holds for the world in general can be applied in some way to the life of human communities and of the Church. In diverse societies there exists the principle of tradition, in relationship with the memory of the past and with identity in the present as a people. Alongside it there is the principle of renewal, which points towards the future, towards progress understood as the fulfilment of promises. In the Church we find a similar analogy: on the one hand, she is hierarchically configured, and as an institution requires structure; at the same time, she is essentially charismatic, inasmuch as the dynamism of the charisms opens her to new historical realisations.

In societies, tradition and renewal are called to integrate, and it is not good to abandon one in favour of the other, because a constitutive element would then be lacking. The same holds true for the Church, in which neither of the two elements can be missing: she is simultaneously hierarchical and charismatic. Historical experience teaches us that the combination of these elements is not without tension, and that it is not always easy, from a natural point of view, to hold these perspectives together. This realisation helps us understand that perfect unity can come only from God, in whom the Son and the Holy Spirit are precisely one, living in a perfect communion of truth and love and acting together in perfect synergy620. Our history journeys towards that perfect integration, confident as we are in the Lord's promise of a future eschatological fullness.

Therefore, returning to questions already treated, whether regarding the ecological crisis or other events that may appear negative in human history, the Christian must not allow himself to be overcome by anguish or mistrust in the future. He believes in the creative and re-creative power of God and knows that the destiny of the world is under the dominion of Him who is a merciful Father, which provides the foundation for his hope. Man dwells in a world he did not create and to which he did not impart the order and intelligibility it possesses. God created all things through the Logos and with Love. Man is called to discover, recognise, and respect this truth.

### Concluding Reflections on Protology

Daniélou pointed out two important characteristics of the primeval history in Genesis: its missionary character and its prophetic nature. The missionary character derives from the fact that this teaching concerning man, created by God in His image to worship Him and to dominate the world, belongs to all peoples and to all times621. Man is neither a celestial being fallen into the realm of matter, nor an animal gradually evolving in intelligence, but a spiritual creature at the centre of a material creation. "This message [...] concerns the human condition as such [...]. Adam belongs as much to Jews as to Christians and Muslims, and, even if they do not call him by that name, to men of every race and every culture"622. Had Christ been connected only to Abraham, He would be considered solely in His relationship to the Jewish people. "The reference to Adam seals the universal character of the Word’s action and guarantees the possibility of salvation for the non-evangelised"623.

The prophetic dimension concerns the entire Bible, because whenever it looks back to the past, it does so in view of the future624. Unlike pagan religions, Genesis does not express nostalgia for a lost past, but directs hope towards future glory. What God will accomplish is greater than what He has already accomplished. Far from dissolving history into nature, as pagan myths do, the Bible incorporates nature into history. What is awaited is not only the liberation of Israel, but the new heavens and the new earth. The Genesis narrative "is not merely a remembrance of the past, but a prefiguration of the future"625.

The divine creative action manifests a radicality that demands a corresponding response: "You shall love the Lord your God with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your might" (Deut 6:5; Mt 22:37). "He who made you demands the whole of you"626. Responding to the call to love Him with one's whole being goes hand in hand with the task of bringing His love to the whole world: "Go into all the world and proclaim the good news to the whole creation" (Mk 16:15). Apostolate is the "overflow" of the interior life627, in analogy with the dynamic *ad intra* – *ad extra* of divine action. To this reality refers the concept of "missionary discipleship" (cf. EG 119–121).

The greatness and beauty of creatures awaken the question concerning origin and end, allowing the reality of the Creator to be glimpsed. The Christian, in his dialogue with non-believers, can kindle these questions so that minds and hearts may open to the light of the Creator. In dialogue with believers of other religions, the Christian finds in creation an excellent starting point for the consolidation of certain fundamental moral values of the person.

For this faith it is worth giving one's life, as the martyrs have demonstrated from the earliest days of the Church. They surrender themselves by entrusting themselves to the Creator, as seen in the dialogue between Justin and his judge628. To have the strength to resist the temptation to idolatry and continue worshipping God the Creator and the Lord Jesus, we have the prayer that Christ taught us, in which we ask for the bread that He Himself gives us. "And as we say 'Our Father'—since He is the Father of Christians and believers—so we likewise say 'our bread', because Christ is bread for us who are united to His body"629. Prayer, trust, and thanksgiving are the consequences of faith in creation630.

The Magi from the East were certain that God can be glimpsed in creation and knew that it is possible to encounter Him, not with any telescope, but with the profound eyes of reason in search of the ultimate meaning of reality and with the desire for God moved by faith.
> In the beauty of the world, in its mystery, in its greatness and in its rationality, we cannot fail to read the eternal rationality; and we cannot help being guided by it to the one God, Creator of heaven and earth. If we adopt this gaze, we shall see that He who created the world and He who was born in a cave in Bethlehem, and continues to dwell among us in the Eucharist, are the same living God, who calls upon us, loves us, and desires to lead us to eternal life631.

### Part II: Introduction to Eschatology

"Man cannot be understood solely on the basis of his origin or from an isolated fragment that we call the present; he is rather directed towards his future. Only his future makes fully manifest who he is"632. The "Deuteronomic dimension of faith" (cf. EG 13; EG 142 and 288) entails approaching the past through the memory of the people, reflecting upon the wonders the Lord has worked; but the Israelite does not halt at the past; rather, he turns his gaze towards the future of God's promises, with an attitude of hope. The theocentric nature of the question of origin, identity, and destiny acquires a new light in Christ, who is "the same yesterday and today and forever" (Heb 13:8), "the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end" (Rev 22:13); in Him is realised "the mystery hidden for ages and generations but now made manifest to his saints [...], Christ in you, the hope of glory" (Col 1:26–27; cf. Eph 3:9; Rom 16:25–26).

If in protology we looked towards the "first things", we now turn our gaze towards the "last things" (*tà ésckhata*)633, with an apophatic and vigilant humility, as Jesus counsels when He declares that neither He nor much less His listeners know the day or the hour, but only the Father (cf. Mt 24:36), and therefore that one must watch (cf. Mt 25:13). Only God can reveal through His Spirit His secrets, "what no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor the heart of man conceived, what God has prepared for those who love him" (1 Cor 2:9).

If in the truths of origins there is a connection with human rationality, so too with regard to eschatological truths one must "always be prepared to make a defence to anyone who calls you to account for the hope that is in you" (1 Pet 3:15). This attitude is especially timely in a world that, in recent centuries, has undergone a process of secularisation of the Christian *eschaton*634. If in the past the influence of the Christian vision of man's destiny shaped art and literature (from Dante's *Divine Comedy* to Michelangelo's *Last Judgement*), today we witness the alteration of the divine promise into human demands, with the disillusionment of not seeing the expectations of a better world realised (cf. SS 16–23). In the recent past, Marxism was an example of this phenomenon; today transhumanism is in vogue, setting itself three objectives: super-longevity, super-intelligence, and super-wellbeing635. It is thought possible to recover the gifts of paradise without waiting for the end of time, as an outcome of our own progress.

The study of the *novissima* (the Last Things) can be structured in various ways636. We shall follow the outline that begins from the Parousia of Christ, which includes the resurrection of men and of the cosmos in view of judgment (cf. Chapter 7), continuing with the final states, which depend upon the free response of the creature: heaven and hell (cf. Chapter 8); there will follow the intermediate state between the end of the earthly life of individual persons and the *eschaton*: death, purgatory (cf. Chapter 9). The final chapter (cf. Chapter 10) relates the human desire for immortality to the Christian promise of resurrection, seeking to justify the necessity—stemming from Revelation and in agreement with reason—of speaking not only of a universal eschatology and a consummated eschatology, but also of an intermediate eschatology637.

The synthetic principle guiding our reflection, applied to the study of eschatological realities, expresses itself in the necessity of conjoining dimensions that ought not to be separated: body and spirit, individual and society, continuity (identity) and discontinuity (novelty); in summary, the integration between the present and future dimensions of eschatology, the "already and not yet", to use the terminology of Oscar Cullmann638, which has been received into Catholic theology.

The expression refers to the central reality of Christianity, the Resurrection of Christ: "If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain" (1 Cor 15:14). The Paschal mystery of Jesus' death and resurrection, the beginning of the new creation, is a reality already present but not yet fully manifest: "He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day" (Jn 6:54). This promise constitutes the core of Christian eschatology: eternal life already begun, but not yet arrived at completion.

Here lies the characteristic Christian tension between the horizontality of present life, marked by a transience that does not fully satisfy the longings of the human heart, and the verticality of the divine breaking into history of a new life that presents itself as fulfilling plenitude (*fascinans*) while at the same time evoking a holy awe (*tremendum*)639. The conjunction between the horizontal and the vertical is effected upon the cross, whose power extends from origins to the end, as indicated by the depiction of the striking Sator magic square, found in many medieval churches, with an origin dating back to the early Christians640. The letters (A, M, and Ω) indicate that Jesus embraces the beginning, the middle, and the end of all things641. The cross is the new tree of life, to which we have access in the sacrament of the Eucharist642.
