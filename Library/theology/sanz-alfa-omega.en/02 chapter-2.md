## Chapter 2 The Creative Act of the Trinity in Revelation and Theological Reflection {#sanz-alfa-omega.2}

*«Fecemi la divina podestate / la somma sapienza e ’l primo amore»*88.
Thus Dante renders in verse the truth of Revelation concerning the creative act as the fruit of omnipotence, wisdom, and love.
The attribution of creation to the Father, redemption to the Son, and sanctification to the Holy Spirit is well known. This appropriation of the works *ad extra* to each of the Persons of the Trinity, which has its own justification, must be harmonised with the principle that the works *ad extra* of the Trinity are common to the three Persons, and therefore "creation is the common work of the Holy Trinity" (CCC 292). If one inquires into the specific role of each Person who accomplishes the creative act according to their personal property, one arrives at the appropriation of the essential attributes of omnipotence, wisdom, and love, respectively, to the creative work of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit.
In the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed we confess our faith "in one God, the Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth"; "in one Lord Jesus Christ [...]; by whom all things were made"; and in the Holy Spirit, "the Lord, the giver of life" (DS 150). Christian faith speaks not only of a creation *ex nihilo* (out of nothing), which indicates the omnipotence of the Father, but also of a creation made with wisdom—the Word (*logos*) through whom "all things were made" (Jn 1:3)—and of a creation *ex amore* (cf. GS 19), the fruit of freedom and love, the Spirit who proceeds from the Father and the Son. "The eternal processions of the Persons are the cause and reason of the whole production of creatures"89.
The outline of the present chapter will follow this Trinitarian dimension of the creative act, which constitutes one of the cornerstones of the renewal of contemporary theology of creation90. On the basis of this pedagogical scheme, we shall review the principal texts of Scripture, Tradition, and the Magisterium in this regard.

### 2.1. YHWH Creator and Saviour in the OT

The faith of the people of Israel displays a distinctiveness in comparison with the worldviews of surrounding peoples, even though it shares certain elements with them. Many authors, following the soteriological understanding of creation in the Old Testament proposed by G. von Rad, have reiterated that the initial experience of the Israelites was not faith in the Creator, but the saving action of YHWH. If this approach, sound in itself, were taken to extremes, creation would be regarded as a subordinate theme, secondary to the historical-salvific core of faith; von Rad himself realised the limitations of such an understanding when he turned to study the sapiential literature.
Westermann emphasised that Israel's thought cannot be detached from that of neighbouring peoples, with whom it shared, as a presupposition of their thinking, belief in the existence of a Creator of the world prior to any saving experience. In this approach, there is the risk of sundering the doctrine of creation from that of redemption, or at least of conceiving their relationship in an extrinsic manner.
It must be affirmed that the *proprium* of Israelite faith lies in the assertion that the Creator is the Saviour, and that the Saviour is the Creator. The theme of creation appears connected in diverse ways with that of salvation, while simultaneously possessing its own distinctiveness, which is not exhausted by that connection.
Let us seek to frame this concept more clearly, analysing first the Genesis narratives, and then other Old Testament texts.

### 2.1.1. Creation and Covenant in Genesis

In recent decades, besides insisting that Israel's primary experience was the saving intervention of YHWH, scholars have also recalled that Genesis was not the first biblical book to be written and that other canonical books likewise speak of creation. It is advisable not to focus attention solely upon the narratives of origins.
Nevertheless, it cannot be denied that the placement of primeval history at the beginning of the sacred books has a very precise theological significance91. The intention was to link Israel's faith with the origins (*genesis*) of the world and of humanity. Barth's formula concerning the relationship between creation and covenant, so influential in contemporary theology, is set forth in his *Church Dogmatics* as a theological commentary on Gen 1 and Gen 2 respectively: whereas the first chapter shows that creation is the external ground of the covenant, the second reveals that the covenant is the internal ground of creation92.
The Book of Genesis can be divided into two major blocks: the first eleven chapters describe the origins of the nations (God creates the world and man), while from chapter 12 through to chapter 50 the origin of the chosen people is presented (God creates Israel)93.
With regard to the first part of the book, although attention has traditionally focused on the first three chapters—which contain the two accounts of creation and the fall—it is now held that they should not be read in isolation from the whole of the first eleven chapters. These present themselves as a literary composition of two traditions, the so-called Yahwist (J) and the Priestly (P)94.

### 2.1.1.1. Which Literary Genre? Symbolic History and Prophecy of the Past

Clarifying the literary genre of the first chapters of Genesis is vital for grasping the message they seek to convey. The question poses itself in the form of an alternative: is it myth or history?95
There are convergences between Genesis and texts from the Mesopotamian milieu: *Enuma Elish* presents similarities with Gen 1:1–2:4a, even though the same cosmographic framework is subjected to very different religious interpretations. The flood narrative is found both in Gen 6:5–9:7 and in the Sumerian poem of Ziusudra and the *Epic of Gilgamesh*, with a similar scheme, but with a different religious message96.
There are particular details exclusive to Genesis: the Garden of Eden, the story of Cain and Abel, the episode of the sons of God and the daughters of men, the Tower of Babel. Beyond these differences of content, according to scholars, one finds the different manner in which the Bible frames the narrative, namely with the intention of constructing a coherent didactical framework: the conception that Israel, under the impulse of its religious faith (founded upon divine Revelation), had formed of entire prehistory. There is no other ancient document with such systematic coherence. In any event, the most important distinguishing element is its rigorous monotheism97.
Contemporary exegesis rejects the idea of a literal historicity of the texts, since there are evident anthropomorphisms, naiveties, and expressions that preclude such an interpretation. In recent decades it has been increasingly well understood that these texts possess a didactical intention: the authors wish to transmit a doctrine as well as to recount facts. They refer to historical data that truly took place, recounting them not in a descriptive but in a cryptic manner, through the metaphors available to them. And this for two reasons: first, it is obvious that there are no witnesses to those primordial events; second, the symbolic garb aids the understanding of the simplest minds.
If the first eleven chapters of Genesis are neither myth nor history, how can their literary genre be defined? Rahner proposed a middle way with the expression "historical aetiology"98, which he distinguishes from mythical aetiology and which can be deemed valid if historicity is understood not merely as possible, but as real and actual.
It seems preferable to use the expression "symbolic history": this makes it possible to maintain the sense of the texts (which is not modern historiographical), the character of reality belonging to historical facts, and the enduring validity of the message. One is thus in harmony with the teaching of Pius XII in the encyclical *Humani generis* (1950):

> The first eleven chapters of Genesis, although properly speaking they do not conform to the historical method used by the best Greek and Latin historians or by authorities of our own time, nevertheless belong to the historical genre in a true sense, which must, however, be further studied and determined by exegetes; the same chapters [...] in simple and metaphorical language, suited to the mentality of a people of little culture, recount both the principal truths that are fundamental for our salvation, and also a popular description of the origin of the human race and of the chosen people. If the ancient sacred writers took anything from popular narratives (which may be conceded), it must never be forgotten that they did this with the help of divine inspiration, which preserved them from all error in the selection and evaluation of those documents. Therefore, the popular narratives incorporated into Sacred Scripture cannot by any means be placed on the same level as mythologies or the like99.

Benedict XVI offered another key for understanding these texts when, referring to the Liturgy of the Word of the Easter Vigil, he recalled that traditionally "all these readings were called prophecies. Even when they are not direct foretellings of future events, they have a prophetic character: they show us the inner foundation and direction of history"100. Since the first reading is precisely the first chapter of Genesis,

> by this the liturgy intends to tell us that the creation narrative is also a prophecy. It is not information about the external development of the becoming of the cosmos and of man. The Fathers of the Church were well aware of this. They did not understand this account as a narrative about the chronological unfolding of the origins of things, but rather as a pointer to what is essential, to the true principle and the end of our being101.

Benedict XVI thus took up the expression *prophetia de praeterito*, used by Gregory the Great to refer to the opening words of the Bible: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Gen 1:1). Even if etymology restricts prophecy in the strict sense to the future, it is legitimate to broaden the notion to the present and the past102. In truth, both types of prophecy, of the future and of the past,

> fall under the peculiar legitimacy of prophetic discourse [...]. Prophecy is not primarily an expansion of knowledge, but a placing of oneself under the power of the coming God. This now applies equally to prophecy of the past. It does not provide information about details of the past, but is man's placement of himself under the justice and grace of his origin in God and of His acting in history, in such a way as to determine the past in its fundamental theological character103.

Gen 1–11 is a symbolic and prophetic history, narrated not in a modern historiographical manner, but in an ancient sapiential one. The mythical-symbolic clothing impairs neither the historicity of the facts nor the substance of the doctrine. Augustine, commenting on Genesis, maintained that "our sacred authors knew what was in accordance with the truth concerning the shape of the heavens, but the Spirit of God, who spoke through them, did not wish to teach men these things, which are in no way profitable for the salvation of the soul"104. Appealing to these considerations, Galileo wrote in his *Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina of Lorraine* (1615) that "I heard from an ecclesiastical person of the most eminent degree that the intention of the Holy Spirit is to teach us how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes"105.

### 2.1.1.2. Some Theological Elements of the Priestly Account

In the first chapters of Genesis there are two accounts originating from different redactional traditions. Whereas the first (cf. Gen 1:1–2:4a) belongs to the Priestly tradition, the second (cf. Gen 2:4b–25) is an expression of the Yahwist tradition, of which the subsequent narrative of the intrusion of sin into the world also forms an integral part. While the first, termed the *Hexaemeron* because it recounts the creation of the world in six days, is connected to the cultic sphere (it may be thought to have been transmitted in the form of a liturgical hymn) and is considered theologically profound, the second is anthropocentric in character, not only because it centres on the creation of Adam and Eve, but also because it describes God's acting with anthropomorphic features; in any case, it presents itself as sapiential teaching that also possesses the nature of a prophetic exhortation106.
Thousands of pages, from the Fathers of the Church to the medieval Doctors down to recent exegesis, have been written to comment on these narratives, which may without exaggeration be counted among the most influential in the history of humanity. We shall confine ourselves here to pointing out certain elements of the Priestly account that are of particular importance for a theology of creation107. We shall deal with the Yahwist account, which is more specifically anthropological, in Chapter 6.
The first verse affirms that the existence of everything we see ("the heavens and the earth") had an absolute beginning ("in the beginning"), due to the action of God as sole author ("God created"). The verb *bara'* is used, which in the Bible is exclusively reserved for divine action, both in creation and in salvation history. In the face of Hellenistic and pagan thought, it later became necessary to make explicit that if God could act upon created elements, this was because He is the Creator of matter as well. The expression in Gen 1:2 (*tohu wa-bohu*: "formless and void"), referring to the earth as primordial chaos, presupposes the preceding verse that asserts its created character. Even if a doctrine of creation *ex nihilo* is not yet formally asserted, the foundations are laid for regarding such a development as entirely consistent with this text108: "this *tohu-bohu* is intended to be nothing other than a graphic representation of absolute nothingness"109. In any case, we are far removed from explanations of the genesis of the world as a combat of the Creator against the powers of chaos.
At the origin of all things lies the divine action that creates through its word. "God said" is repeated exactly ten times in this narrative (cf. Gen 1:3, 6, 9, 11, 14, 20, 24, 26, 28, 29). Such a number is not accidental, but indicates the connection with the covenant. God's covenant with Israel rests upon the Law (*Torah*) given to the people, which consists of ten words (*Decalogue*)110. The Priestly account correlates the ten words that sanction the creation of the chosen people with the ten words by which God created all things at the beginning111. Aleph and Tav being the first and last letters of the Hebrew alphabet, a rabbinic commentary asserts that Adam transgressed the entire Law from Aleph to Tav (the initial of *Torah*)112, whereas, on the other hand, it is said that Abraham observed the entire Law from Aleph to Tav113.
Another fundamental affirmation of the account is the original goodness of all creatures: "God saw that it was good"; this expression likewise recurs a significant number of times, namely seven (cf. Gen 1:4, 10, 12, 18, 21, 25); the final occurrence, referring to the whole of creation together, including the human being, employs the adverb "very": "God saw everything that he had made, and behold, it was very good" (Gen 1:31)114. The number seven signifies fullness, perfection, and completeness in the Bible, exactly like the number ten, and both express God's covenant with mankind115.
These two motifs (God creates by speaking and God declares that it is good) converge in the concept of blessing (*bene-dictio*). Creation bears the character of a divine blessing. There is no trace of a primordial struggle; at the origin there is only the divine blessing action, both towards living creatures in general (cf. Gen 1:22) and towards the human being, who is tasked with exercising dominion over the rest of creatures (cf. Gen 1:28). The divine blessing implies the transmission of the "energy to procreate"116; work and the generation of life are an essential part of the task assigned by the Creator to human beings, who are fashioned in His image and likeness (cf. Gen 1:26–27) and invited to share in some measure in His power117.
The divine blessing also concerns the seventh day (cf. Gen 2:3), the *Shabbat*, which constitutes another link with the covenant. "For Israel, the Sabbath was the day on which all could share in God's rest, in which man and beast, master and slave, great and small were united in the freedom of God. Thus the Sabbath was the expression of the covenant between God, man, and creation"118. The weekly scheme does not derive from the material of creation, since in reality the divine works are eight119. If they have been grouped into six days, this occurs with a precise didactical intention rooted in the narrative's connection to the institution of the Sabbath: creation is presented as a work of God that includes a day of rest, so that it may serve as an example for man, whose Sabbath rest dedicated to worship becomes a sacred law grounded in God's will and in the nature of things.
The divine blessing is present from the beginning to the end of the creative work. In Leviticus, God associates His blessings with Israel's observance of the Law in the most comprehensive manner possible, namely from Aleph to Tav (cf. Lev 26:3–13), whereas the curses linked to unfaithfulness to the Law, although occupying greater space, are actually "shorter", running from Vav to Mem (cf. Lev 26:14–43)120.
Another element in this account is the creation of the stars, fashioned on the fourth day (cf. Gen 1:14–19). This is striking, given the divine character attributed to them in the foundational myths of religions. They are simple lamps placed by God in the firmament to give light to men. They are not regarded as hostile powers, but are "demythologised", reduced to their natural reality121. This is an "anti-mythical" element, as well as a confirmation of the goodness of all creation. The sources of light are not deities, but creatures122.
The first creative word had been: "Let there be light!" (cf. Gen 1:3), because creation is a reflection of the being of God, who is Light (cf. 1 Jn 1:5). Goodness of creation and light coincide: "Light is therefore also the expression of the good that is luminosity and creates luminosity [...]. The fact that God created light means that God created the world as a space of knowledge and truth, a space of encounter and freedom, a space of goodness and love"123.

### 2.1.2. Creation and the Historical-Salvific Action of God in Other Books of the OT

Creation is not exclusive to Genesis. Israel's primary experience, recounted in Exodus, is the totality of God's saving interventions that configure the history of the covenant. In that setting, faith in the Creator grows and matures; this faith nevertheless precedes the saving experience, because otherwise YHWH could not be acknowledged as the Almighty124. The *proprium* of Israelite faith lies in the double affirmation: the Creator is the Saviour, the Saviour is the Creator125.
In the deliverance from Egyptian bondage (cf. Ex 13:17–18:27) and the establishment of the Covenant at Mount Sinai (cf. Ex 19–24), the Hebrews had a palpable experience of divine power. The recognition of God occurred in the extraordinary wonders over nature that He wrought on their behalf: the pillar of cloud and fire to guide them (cf. Ex 13:21–22), the parting of the waters of the Red Sea (cf. Ex 14:21ff.), the manna as an answer to their prayer (cf. Ex 16:4ff.), and so on. These events were signs of God's dominion over all things, of His constant care for the people126. At first they did not perceive God in the order of nature, but in those moments when He acts above its laws—not in a magical sense, but as a demonstration of total power.
We find a deepening of this theme in the prophetic writings of the exile. In a difficult period of Israel's history, the Babylonian deportation, there are very rich texts concerning the doctrine of creation, particularly in the second part of the Book of Isaiah127.
The crisis of faith in God provoked by defeat and exile stimulated the prophets' exhortation to conversion and observance of the Law, inviting the people to recall the saving events of their history. If God had liberated them in the past, He could do so again. Their infidelity to the Covenant had brought about the loss of divine protection, but if they turned back to the Lord and trusted in Him, He would once more be at their side.
Thanks to the preaching of the prophets, the Hebrews began to rethink and renew their faith in God as Creator and Saviour. Because God is the Creator of heaven and earth, He is capable of saving man from any misfortune. Thus the Hebrews perceived creation as the foundation of all God's interventions. In the Book of Consolation, comprising chapters 40–55 of Isaiah, a vision of creation close to the first chapter of Genesis is presented, manifested also in the frequent use of *bara'*:

> Thus says God, the Lord, who created the heavens and stretched them out, who spread forth the earth and what comes from it, who gives breath to the people upon it and spirit to those who walk in it: "I am the Lord, I have called you in righteousness, I have taken you by the hand and kept you; I have given you as a covenant to the people, a light to the nations" (Isa 42:5–6).

> Israel is saved by the Lord with everlasting salvation; you shall not be put to shame or confounded to all eternity. For thus says the Lord, who created the heavens (he is God!), who formed the earth and made it (he established it; he did not create it a chaos, he formed it to be inhabited!): "I am the Lord, and there is no other" (Isa 45:17–18).

In these texts salvation and creation are interwoven, as they are likewise in Jeremiah:

> Ah Lord God! It is you who have made the heavens and the earth by your great power and by your outstretched arm! Nothing is too hard for you. [...] You brought your people Israel out of the land of Egypt with signs and wonders, with a strong hand and an outstretched arm, and with great terror; and you gave them this land, which you swore to their fathers to give them, a land flowing with milk and honey (Jer 32:17, 21–22).

Unlike Exodus, here faith in the Creator is clearly articulated; on the one hand, God's omnipotence is extolled not only over things but also over the entire being of man; on the other hand, God's saving power, since He is the Creator of all, begins to broaden, embracing not only the chosen people, but also the rest of the nations.
In the sapiential books these motifs are expanded with new developments linked to the deepening of faith in the Creator128. Whereas the historical and prophetic books emphasise the experience of the sovereign and creator God who acts in the history of the people, here creation (of man and of the world) will be spoken of as the work of divine wisdom. If previously God was experienced in history, in a historical-salvific "actualism", now God is perceived in nature: "from the greatness and beauty of created things their original author, by analogy, is seen" (Wis 13:5).
Without the salvific dimension disappearing, attention here focuses upon creation as such, upon the intelligibility of things, which are the fruit of divine wisdom, endowed with personal traits:

> The Lord created me at the beginning of his work, the first of his acts of old. Ages ago I was set up, at the first, before the beginning of the earth. When there were no depths I was brought forth, [...] before the mountains had been shaped, before the hills, I was brought forth; before he had made the earth with its fields, or the first of the dust of the world. When he established the heavens, I was there; [...] when he marked out the foundations of the earth, then I was beside him, like a master workman; and I was daily his delight, rejoicing before him always (Prov 8:22–27, 29–30).

Certain psalms praise God first for the work of creation and then for His saving deeds, without any break in continuity (cf. Ps 135 [134] and 136 [135])129. Others, such as Psalm 104 (103), are centred upon the praise of God the Creator without any reference to salvation history130:

> Bless the Lord, O my soul! O Lord my God, you are very great! You are clothed with honour and majesty, who cover yourself with light as with a garment, who have stretched out the heavens like a tent, who have laid the beams of your chambers on the waters, who make the clouds your chariot, who ride on the wings of the wind [...]. O Lord, how manifold are your works! In wisdom have you made them all; the earth is full of your creatures (Ps 104 [103]:1–3, 24).

It has been debated whether this approach represents a corruption of the original Israelite faith, the fruit of contact with the Hellenistic world; but it is more reasonable to think of a process of deepening and maturation of the faith of the people (under the guidance of the Holy Spirit). This could be described as the transition "from phenomenon to foundation"131. The recognition of God's presence and power in the wondrous interventions He accomplishes in history leads to the realisation that all that exists is the fruit of the creative intervention of divine omnipotence; hence God can be recognised not only in extraordinary phenomena, but also and above all in the regular course of natural events. The fact that the contemplation of creatures leads to the praise of the Creator, rather than to a self-sufficient closure, proves that there is no surrender here to pagan naturalism, but rather an underscoring of the dependence of the world and of man upon the wise Creator.
The story of Job132 displays in paradigmatic fashion an awareness of the superiority of divine wisdom over human wisdom, which provides the interpretive key for the moments of tragedy, suffering, and death that accompany earthly existence:

> Where were you when I laid the foundation of the earth? Tell me, if you have understanding! [...] Have you commanded the morning since your days began, and caused the dawn to know its place? [...] Have you entered into the springs of the sea, or walked in the recesses of the deep? Have the gates of death been revealed to you, or have you seen the gates of deep darkness? (Job 38:4, 12, 16–17).

These questions demonstrate that man does not always comprehend God's ways, and must therefore adopt a disposition of openness and hope towards Him—specifically, towards His justice.
Although not belonging by genre to sapiential literature, the Second Book of Maccabees offers the testimony of a pious mother who exhorts her sons about to undergo martyrdom to place their hope in God and in the future resurrection, using trust in the Creator as an argument to convince them:

> The Creator of the world, who shaped the beginning of man and devised the origin of all things, will in his mercy give life and breath back to you again [...]. I beseech you, my child, to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them, and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed. Thus also mankind comes into being. Do not fear this butcher, but prove worthy of your brothers. Accept death, so that in God's mercy I may get you back again with your brothers (2 Macc 7:23, 28–29).

This is an existential expression of the faith that will later be formulated conceptually as creation *ex nihilo*.

### 2.2. Creation and Christ in the NT

### 2.2.1. Continuity with the OT

"By faith we understand that the worlds were prepared by the word of God, so that what is seen was made from things that are not visible" (Heb 11:3). This confession makes clear that the early Christian community situated itself in a perspective of continuity with respect to Israel's faith in the Creator. In the New Testament, references are not numerous because this was an underlying presupposition of their relationship with God, as expressed in the prayer following the release of the Apostles: "they lifted their voices together to God and said, 'Sovereign Lord, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them...'" (Acts 4:24).
On several occasions Jesus grounded some of His teachings upon this truth. To defend the indissolubility of marriage in the context of the Mosaic concession of a bill of divorce, He appealed to the fact that "from the beginning it was not so" (Matt 19:8), with an obvious reference to Genesis. In debates with the Pharisees concerning the true interpretation of the Law regarding the purity of foods, He explained that true defilement proceeds from the human heart, not from material things: "Thus he declared all foods clean" (Mark 7:19). It would be difficult to understand these words without the background of the Genesis narrative concerning the original goodness of all things and the ruin introduced into creation by human sin.
Jesus' ministry can be considered a renewal of creation, both in His teachings and in His actions. On the one hand, He recalls the original design of God, and therefore His words are in continuity with the creative word; indeed, the Fourth Gospel will identify Jesus with the Word, the *Logos*, who was in the beginning. On the other hand, the wonders He worked have the significance of restoring the qualities present in the original creation, which are no longer present in a creation wounded by sin: He makes the lame walk, the mute speak, the deaf hear, the blind see, and cleanses lepers (cf. Matt 11:5; 15:30–31; Luke 7:22); of particular significance is the miracle of restoring sight to the blind, specifically the healing of the man born blind (cf. Jn 9); Jesus' gesture of making mud from the earth and applying it to the blind man's eyes has evident Genesis resonances, to the point that many have interpreted it as a creative gesture akin to Gen 2:7.
Upon seeing these wonders, the astonished crowd exclaims: "He has done all things well" (Mark 7:37). Jesus heals from other diseases (cf. Matt 9:20–22; Luke 14:1–6; etc.) and from every other physical suffering, such as hunger and thirst (He feeds the multitudes: cf. Matt 14:13–21 and 15:32–39). He has power even over that evil for which there is no remedy, death: He raises the dead (cf. Mark 5:21–43; Luke 7:11–17; Jn 11:1–44). Jesus' word is efficacious just as God's creative word was, for the simple reason that they are identical. Thus He can also perform actions upon the created world, such as commanding the wind and the sea to be still (cf. Mark 4:39–41), or walking on water (cf. Matt 14:25–33), which show that He is Lord and Master of all.
This efficacy does not stop at the external and visible dimension, but reaches being in the depths of its inner, spiritual dimension. Jesus' power extends not only to the body, but also to the soul. He is able to cast out demons and forgive sins, and this is a sign of the presence of the Kingdom of God (cf. Luke 11:20; Mark 2:5–11).
In Jesus, what we considered in the Old Testament concerning the depth and universality of the power of divine action upon the world and upon man is fulfilled in fullness. Jesus' power extends to the whole world and to all men, and also to the whole of man. His dominion over the visible world is a sign of His dominion over the invisible and inner world: "Did not he who made the outside make the inside also?" (Luke 11:40). The Creator of the spirit is also the Creator of matter.
Jesus, therefore, on the one hand refers back to the first creation and, on the other hand, labours so that creation, damaged by sin, may be renewed; in Him, in His Paschal Mystery, there takes place a "new creation", the Father's design "to unite all things in him [Christ], things in heaven and things on earth" (Eph 1:10). One belongs to this new creation by virtue of a new birth (cf. Jn 3:5).
Jesus' saving work claims universality; that is, it concerns the entire cosmos and all peoples; this means that Jesus transcends the concrete history He lived: He is God, the Son beloved of the Father. Just as in the Old Testament the experience of historical-salvific action was the occasion for ascending to God as Creator, so in an analogous way in the New Testament, the experience of faith in the Risen Jesus is the occasion for the new people, the nascent Church, to link the mystery of Jesus to the mystery of God the Creator. There is a progression from noting Jesus' power over creation to affirming His role in the creative act as the Son-Word of the Father. For this reason, the Book of Revelation can refer to Him as "the Amen, the faithful and true witness, the beginning of God's creation" (Rev 3:14).

### 2.2.2. Creation in Christ, Through Him, and For Him: A Trinitarian and Eschatological Christocentrism

"In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God; all things were made through him, and without him was not anything made that was made" (Jn 1:1–3). The Johannine Prologue confirms what we have just stated. For John, the initial experience was personal contact with Jesus: "Come and see" (Jn 1:39). At the beginning of his Gospel, John feels the need to return to the origins, just as in the Old Testament the narrative of Israel's salvation history is preceded by the primeval history of Genesis. The relationship between the two is obvious, both by virtue of the opening words ("in the beginning") and in the description of the creative act through the word, the *Logos*, as the Fathers of the Church observed133. The Johannine Prologue may be called "the New Testament account of creation"134.
We stand here before the central question of protology: the bond between creation and salvation in Christ, the centre of the Trinitarian design of love. There is no conflict between the Creator God of the Old Testament and the redeeming, merciful God of the New Testament, as Gnosticism asserted, presenting the novelty of Jesus, the Gospel, as a rupture with the ancient Law. The dialectic between old and new, between Law and Gospel, between creation and redemption, is old, not new, even though since the time of Marcion in the second century it has never ceased to reappear in varied forms within Christianity. The correct Christian view moves between continuity and discontinuity, between "treasures new and old" (Matt 13:52); on the one hand, Jesus is the Novelty of God135, and brings this new life to the old man; on the other hand, Jesus did not come to abolish the Law but to fulfil it (cf. Matt 5:17). The novelty of Jesus is not in contrast with creation, but constitutes its healing and perfection.
Of the utmost importance are those New Testament passages in which the person of Jesus is associated with the creative act through three prepositional phrases: "in him", "through him", and "for him"136. Their value has been rediscovered in contemporary theology, and they must be understood with balance, in order to avoid swinging from the extreme of an independence of creation from Christ (extrinsicism) to that of a total absorption of creation into Christ (Christomonist intrinsicism).

### 2.2.2.1. IN HIM: Christocentrism

"For in him (*en autō*) all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible [...]. All things were created through him (*di’ autou*) and for him (*eis auton*). He is before all things, and in him (*en autō*) all things hold together" (Col 1:16–17). Only in the Christological hymn of the Letter to the Colossians do we find together the three prepositions with which the New Testament traditions describe the creative action in relation to Christ137.
The initial—and therefore emphatic—position of *en* with the dative reveals a deliberate insistence highlighting the uniqueness of the mediation. The expression could be understood instrumentally, but the *en* placed at the beginning of verse 16 stands in relation to the other *en* at the end of verse 17. The meaning to be accorded to this first preposition is the subsistence of all things in Christ; He is the reason for being of all that exists, their permanent foundation138.
Here the phrase "creation in Christ" finds its foundation, summarising the renewal of the treatise on creation from the second half of the twentieth century onward, associated with the positions of von Rad and Barth. Certain Catholic theologians argue that Christ is the condition of possibility of creation (cf. Rahner and Balthasar), inasmuch as His mediation is not merely instrumental, but of metaphysical and personal import139. From the Protestant side, Pannenberg adds that Christ, as the Son distinct from the Father, is the condition of possibility of all that is truly other than God140. Summarising this perspective, it has been written that "the possibility of creation is objectively grounded in the fact of the Incarnation: for the Incarnation reveals the possibility of the communication of God's own being to a nature that is not the divine nature: this is creation"141.
These developments must reckon with the perspective that claims a certain independence for the notion of creation with respect to the covenant. Auer criticised the proposal to assign a new theological placement to the treatise on creation, positioning it after Christology rather than before it, as was traditional in manualist theology142. While recognising in this proposal a justified critique of the "separation of the problem of salvation from the problem of creation"143, he expressed disagreement with the ambition "to understand the problem of creation starting from the problem of salvation"144. The risk would be to conceive the unity of the divine plan of creation and redemption as a necessary system, thereby imperilling God's freedom in His action. Auer argues that the New Testament affirmation of creation in Christ aims to reinforce the understanding of His divinity, by inserting Him into God's creative act145.
Furthermore, in the new proposal there is a tendency to regard as the theological object "not primarily the thing itself, but 'the meaning of the thing'"146. One would find oneself in the position of having to affirm in Christology the Christological meaning of creation starting from faith in the one YHWH, and having to wait until the end of the treatises on Christ before speaking of the mystery of creation. Auer maintains that "if one wishes to understand aright Christ's cooperation in creation, one must first of all clarify, in its own proper standing, the meaning of the revealed mystery of 'creation out of nothing'"147.
Athanasius, in his treatise on the Incarnation, seems to support Auer:

> It is proper that we speak first of the creation of the universe and of God its Creator, so that it may be adequately understood that its renewal was accomplished by the Word who created it in the beginning. Indeed, no contradiction will be seen if the Father wrought the salvation of the universe in him through whom he created it148.

The question is whether Christ, the incarnate Word, is Creator as the Son/Word of the Father or also in His humanity. Regarding the passage in the Letter to the Colossians, it is undisputed that the subject of the statements is Christ, and that no distinction is made between the pre-existent and the incarnate Word. It does not appear that the author intended to assert that Jesus as man is creator. These issues, linked to the use of the phrase "in Christ", can be illuminated if attention is paid to the other two prepositions, "through him" and "for him". The subsistence of all things in Christ leads us in two complementary directions: both towards the mystery of the Trinitarian God and towards eschatological fulfilment.

### 2.2.2.2. THROUGH HIM: Trinitarian Christocentrism

The second preposition indicates Christ's mediation in creation: "All things were created through him (*di’ autou*)" (Col 1:16). It is the same preposition as in the passage from the First Letter to the Corinthians, where the Lord Jesus Christ is assigned a role in the creative action, which has first been attributed, with respect to its origin, to the Father: "yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom (*ex hou*) are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom (*di’ hou*) are all things" (1 Cor 8:6). What in Rom 11:36 is said of God alone ("from him [*ex autou*] and through him [*di’ autou*] and to him [*eis auton*] are all things") receives further specification through the distinction of the Father's role as origin from that of the Son as mediator.
This reading suggests a progression from Christ as mediator of salvation to His creative mediation and pre-existence as the eternal Son and Word of the Father. Thus the Johannine Prologue: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God. He was in the beginning with God; all things were made through him (*di’ autou*)" (Jn 1:1–3); and likewise the Letter to the Hebrews:

> In many and various ways God spoke of old to our fathers by the prophets; but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son, whom he appointed the heir of all things, through whom (*di’ hou*) also he created the world. He reflects the glory of God and bears the very stamp of his nature, upholding the universe by his word of power (Heb 1:1–3).

From an exegetical standpoint, Col 1:16–17 constitutes a unit of meaning. The three prepositions express in a complementary manner the scope of the Son's creative mediation, and must therefore be taken together149. In the passage there is a kind of successive specification: *en autō* is complemented by *di’ autou* and *eis auton*150. Thus it is explained that the initial *en* does not imply mere instrumentality, but that the creative act is permeated by the presence of the Son, of which the other prepositions indicate the modalities.
The perspective of the phrase "in Christ" is thereby developed: all things subsist in Christ because they were made in Him as the eternal Word, through whom the Father creates. The New Testament texts lead us to a Trinitarian Christocentrism, to the affirmation that the subsistence of all things in Christ is due, in the final analysis, to His belonging to the Trinity. The historical-salvific perspective guides us towards the Trinitarian perspective, according to the classical schema dear to the Fathers regarding the relationship between economy and theology151. Contemporary theology of creation, alongside and following the recovery of the Christological dimension of creation, has regained the Trinitarian principle of creation, already present in patristic and medieval tradition, which linked the role of the Son and of the Spirit in the creative act152. The creative mediation of the Word was explained by analogy with His procession *per modum intellectus*, and therefore exemplar causality was appropriated to Him, consistently with the preposition *dia* (through).
In this way, the risk of an instrumental interpretation of *dia* is overcome, and a particular relationship of created things to the Logos is affirmed, as the "exemplar idea (*ratio factiva*) of what God makes"153. The meaning of *en autō* is thus clarified: if its subject is Christ, it refers directly to His divinity, since it belongs exclusively to God to create and sustain creatures154.

### 2.2.2.3. FOR HIM: Eschatological Christocentrism

The final preposition indicates finality towards Christ: "All things were created [...] for him (*eis auton*)" (Col 1:16). While the Vulgate translated imprecisely *in ipso*, the Neo-Vulgate has translated *in ipsum*, more faithfully to the Greek original which employs the accusative, thereby respecting the sense of dynamic finality of the expression155. This preposition goes beyond the sapiential background (it is never said in the Bible that everything was created *eis sophian*), and admits of an exegesis in the sense both of a Christological finalisation of creation and that all things were created to be subjected to the Son156.
These interpretations do not oppose one another, but are understood in the light of other New Testament passages. The ultimate reason for the divine plan is given: the redemptive Incarnation of the Word, which reveals the design "to unite all things in him [Christ], things in heaven and things on earth" (Eph 1:10) at the end of time, when the new creation will be fulfilled in fullness, and which has already become present through salvation. He is "the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end" (Rev 22:13). Creation is therefore revealed as a preparation for the Incarnation, directed towards Christ157.
Other Pauline texts speak of a new creation in Christ (cf. 2 Cor 5:17; Gal 6:15; Eph 4:22–24; Rom 8:19–24)158. From them emerges the strong link in the divine design between creation and salvation, since the former is destined for the latter and the latter is described as a new creation, as Leo the Great teaches:

> The Lord Jesus Christ, being born true man without ever ceasing to be true God, established in himself the beginning of a new creature [...]. Awake, O man, and recognise the dignity of your nature. Remember that you were made in the image of God, which, though deformed in Adam, has nevertheless been renewed in Christ159.

Between creation and salvation "there is discontinuity, because the latter is not the outcome of an immanent evolution of the former; yet there is also a certain continuity, since the first creation will not be annihilated, but transformed"160.

### 2.2.3. A Christological Realism

If the temptation in the past was to exaggerate the distinction between creation and salvation, with the risk of a dualistic view of God's plan (extrinsicism), today the risk is to exaggerate their unity and arrive at a monism that blurs distinctions (intrinsicism). This is the risk of Barth's Christomonism161 and, in the Catholic sphere, of the thought of Teilhard de Chardin, which tends to present salvation history in terms of a quasi-necessary process. The influence of idealism has not been foreign to Catholic theology on this point162. In such a perspective, it is difficult to find room for the distinction between nature and grace, between faith and reason, that makes visible the novelty of the Christ-event in salvation history—not to mention the problematic nature of going further and attributing a creative character to Christ's humanity as well.
The present-day situation calls for a dialogical sensitivity. If one begins from the premise that the foundation of everything is found solely in Christ, the way to dialogue on values seems closed to persons who do not share our faith. It is necessary to reason about the nature of man and of the world, because, in the final analysis, the new creation in Christ does not destroy the first creation, which was made in Him, the eternal Logos of the Father, but rather elevates and brings it to fulfilment. The vigorous resurgence of the debate on the natural law, the interventions of Benedict XVI aimed at emphasising the bond between faith and reason163, as well as the teachings of Francis, especially in *Laudato si’*, appear to confirm this line of thought.
We propose a Christocentrism capable of grounding the autonomy of created reality and the path that leads from our reason to God. The journey from creatures to the Creator (characteristic of the sapiential books of the Old Testament and noted in Rom 1:18–20) is not cancelled by Christ. The book of nature continues to remain open to all who wish to read it, even though it will be fully understood only in the light of the book of Scripture.
The Christocentrism of Revelation does not negate the fact that, precisely because Christ is the eternal Logos of the Father and the principle of the intelligibility of the real, there are dimensions of *logos* recognisable even by those who lack faith in Christ, because they are present in the creation made in the Logos and through the Logos. The "relation of creatures to the Word, the divine Logos, is at the very root of the logic of the world: of its truth, its intelligibility, and therefore of its being the starting point of man's natural knowledge of God"164.
The theological interpretation of the New Testament data concerning creation in Christ, through Him, and for Him, must avoid the extremes of intrinsicism and extrinsicism; expressed positively, it must unite without confusing, and distinguish without separating. This is the Christian form of thinking, which led the Fathers of the Council of Chalcedon (451) to formulate clearly, within the limits of human language, the mystery of Christ.

### 2.3. Ex amore creatus: The Creator Spirit

If the presence of God the Father in the creative act is explicit in both the Old and New Testaments, and the presence of Christ is not entirely foreign to the Old Testament and becomes explicit in the New, one may say that the presence of the Holy Spirit in the creative act remains implicit in both Testaments.
Only after the full Trinitarian Revelation of the New Testament was it possible, in the Christian tradition, to reread certain Hebrew terms of the Old Testament as an incipient manifestation of the existence of the Third Person of the Most Holy Trinity. Attention came to focus on the *ruah* of YHWH, which can signify both wind and breath, and whose privileged field of action is the history of the people of Israel, in which the Lord shows His protection through the presence of His "spirit" in the judges, kings, and prophets165.
From the experience of the *ruah* of God in history, one advances to the awareness of its presence in the work of creation: "the Spirit of God moved over the face of the waters" (Gen 1:2). The link between word and spirit in the act of creating involves an anthropomorphism, since breathing has a function in speaking. "The spirit and the word are, so to speak, an emanation of the person, a kind of immaterial extension thereof, by means of which man, while remaining wholly within himself, can enter into contact with other persons and things"166. This anthropomorphism, as in other biblical passages, contains profound spiritual content. In intuiting that God created the universe by the power of His Word, "the role of the Spirit emerges, the perception of which is fostered by the very analogy of language which, by association, joins the word to the breath of the lips"167; indeed, "by the word of the Lord the heavens were made, and all their host by the breath (*ruah*) of his mouth" (Ps 33 [32]:6). God's life-giving breath is not confined to the initial moment of creation, but sustains and vivifies the whole of creation, continually renewing it: "When you send forth your Spirit, they are created; and you renew the face of the earth" (Ps 104 [103]:30). The prayer of Judith is significant: "You spoke, and they were made; you sent forth your Spirit, and it formed them" (Jdt 16:14).
The quality of the Spirit (*pneuma*) as lifegiver is manifested in the New Testament, where, in the description of the birth of the Messiah as the fruit of the power of the Spirit (cf. Luke 1:35 in relation to Matt 1:20), one can perceive an analogy with the first creation, in which "the Spirit of God moved over the face of the waters" (Gen 1:2) and "the Lord God formed man of dust from the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living being" (Gen 2:7)168. When on Easter evening the Risen Jesus breathes upon the Apostles to communicate the Spirit to them (cf. Jn 20:22), He "renews upon them the same action that God the Creator had accomplished upon Adam. God had 'breathed' upon man's body to give him life [...]. Jesus' human breath thus serves the accomplishment of a divine work even more wonderful than the initial one"169.
The Spirit is at the origin of the first creation, of the continuity in history of God's creative action, and of the new creation accomplished in the mystery of Christ, as emerges from several Pauline texts, foremost among them the emblematic passage from the Letter to the Romans:

> For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God [...] to obtain the glorious liberty of the children of God. We know that the whole creation has been groaning in travail together until now; and not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies (Rom 8:19, 21–23).

John Paul II, in the encyclical *Dominum et Vivificantem*, speaks of the Spirit as Person-Love and Gift:

> Inasmuch as he is consubstantial with the Father and the Son in divinity, he is Love and Gift (uncreated), from which derives as from a source (*fons vivus*) every bestowal with regard to creatures (created gift): the donation of existence to all things through creation; the donation of grace to men through the entire economy of salvation170.

The Father brings creation to completion not only through His Word, who is the Son, but also "through his Love, who is the Holy Spirit"171, who is the *principium creationis rerum* and the *ratio in qua Deus omnem effectum amoris largitur*172. "The Holy Spirit represents the *ratio* of a creation out of love"173.
Creation, especially that of the human being, takes place out of love, as the Second Vatican Council teaches: "For if man exists it is because God has created him through love, and through love continues to hold him in existence. He cannot live fully according to truth unless he freely acknowledges that love and devotes himself to his Creator" (GS 19). The phrase *ex amore* allows us to complete the meaning of the characterisation of creation *ex nihilo*.
From the experience of the action of the Spirit, one ascends to His creative presence. The parallelism *a contrario* between the event of Pentecost (cf. Acts 2:1–13) and the narrative of the Tower of Babel (cf. Gen 11:1–9) has often been noted. Whereas the meaning of Babel is that if men build life against God they end up turning against one another and destroying themselves, Pentecost signifies the contrary, namely unity; life can only be constructed as a gift of the Spirit of God174.
The faith of the Church has deemed that sufficient foundation is found in Scripture to speak of the *Spiritus Creator*175. In the profession of faith we refer to Him as *Dominum et vivificantem*. "Faith in the Creator Spirit is an essential content of the Christian Creed"176, in connection with the action of the Son:

> It is divine love, incarnate in Christ, that is the fundamental and universal law of creation. This is to be understood not in a poetic sense, but in a real one. Dante understood it thus when, in the sublime verse that concludes the *Paradiso* and the entire *Divine Comedy*, he defines God as "the Love that moves the sun and the other stars" (*Paradiso*, XXXIII, 145). This means that the stars, the planets, the entire universe are not governed by a blind force; they do not obey the dynamics of matter alone. It is not, therefore, cosmic elements that are to be divinised; rather, on the contrary, in all things and above all things there is a personal will, the Spirit of God, who in Christ has revealed Himself as Love (cf. SS 5)177.

### 2.4. Creation and the Trinity

### 2.4.1. The Creating Trinity in the Fathers of the Church and Medieval Doctors

*"Unus mundus factus est a Patre per Filium in Spiritu Sancto"*178. This Augustinian formula may be considered "the theological interpretation of the New Testament message"179. The Bishop of Hippo argues, employing perhaps for the first time the expression *Trinitas creatrix*:

> Every creature endowed with intellect, soul, and body, insofar as it exists, draws its being from this creating Trinity. [...] This, however, is not to be understood as though, of the whole of creation, the Father made one part, the Son another, and the Holy Spirit yet another, but in the sense that the Father, through the Son, in the gift of the Holy Spirit, created simultaneously all things and each singular nature180.

Irenaeus of Lyons referred to creation as the common work of the three Persons, affirming that the sovereign God carries out His creative plan with His "own hands", namely the Son and the Spirit181. The Cappadocians of the fourth century developed a Trinitarian and historical-salvific vision according to which the properties of the Persons are reflected in creation. Gregory of Nyssa writes:

> We have not learned that the Father does anything by Himself without the Son being united with Him, or that, in turn, the Son operates anything in isolation without the participation of the Holy Spirit. No: every operation that proceeds from God and extends through creation and receives its name according to the various notions concerning it, has its starting point in the Father, proceeds through the Son, and attains its perfection in the Holy Spirit182.

Medieval Scholasticism integrated these insights into its initial theological syntheses. Thierry of Chartres distributed the causal attributions of creation among the divine Persons in a manner that, with certain modifications, was to become classical: the Father as the efficient cause, the Son as the formal cause, and the Holy Spirit as the final cause of the creative work183. Bonaventure sees created things (in their unity, truth, and goodness) as traces (*vestigia*) of the *Trinitas fabricatrix*; spiritual creatures are also an *imago* of the Trinity (memory-intellect-will, a scheme already present in Augustine), and through grace the *similitudo* is likewise formed in them184. This expression is infrequent in Scholasticism: in the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard it appears but once185.

### 2.4.2. The Creating Trinity in the Creeds and Councils

The history of the confession of faith in the creating Trinity in ancient creeds and ecclesial documents186 attests that only from the fourth century onwards is there an explicit reference in the first article of faith: *"Credo in unum Deum Patrem Omnipotentem, factorem caeli et terrae"* (DS 150)187. In these creeds, which possess a Trinitarian structure, the work of creation is appropriated to the person of the Father, from whom all proceeds, both *ad intra* and *ad extra* of the Trinity. This Trinitarian order is developed through the inclusion of more or less explicit mentions of the creative role of the Son and the Spirit in the respective sections of the Creed, as occurs in a complete manner in the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Symbol188.
With the passage of time, it became necessary to emphasise the complementary aspect: the Trinity is a single creative principle. The *Symbolum Toletanum I*, from the first half of the fifth century, confesses faith "in the one true God, Father and Son and Holy Spirit, maker of things visible and invisible, through whom all things were created in heaven and on earth" (DS 188). Canon 1 of the First Lateran Council (649) speaks of a single creative power189. Not many years later, in the profession of faith in the Trinity of the Eleventh Council of Toledo (675), it is affirmed that the three divine Persons are "inseparable both in what they are and in what they do" (DS 531).
The clearest expression is found in the definition of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) against the Cathars and Albigensians:

> We firmly believe and simply confess that there is only one true God, eternal and immeasurable, omnipotent, unchangeable, incomprehensible and ineffable, Father, Son and Holy Spirit, [...] one single principle of all things, Creator of all things visible and invisible, spiritual and corporeal (DS 800).

The theological sensitivity changed in comparison with the early centuries, because interest now lay not in highlighting the differentiated role of the Persons, but their unity in being and in operation. There is only one *creatrix essentia*190, and this does not mean positing a principle distinct from the Persons. The same Council teaches, following Peter Lombard, that in God there is a Trinity—Father, Son, and Holy Spirit—and not a quaternity, "since each of the three Persons is that reality, namely divine substance, essence or nature; which alone is the principle of all things, and outside of which nothing else can be found" (DS 804).

### 2.4.3. Unus creator, tres creantes: The Reflection of Saint Thomas

The originality of the Angelic Doctor's synthesis consists not in novelty of doctrine, but in the balance with which it is presented: the affirmation of the Trinitarian principle of creation, the insistence upon the single principle of creation, and their harmonisation in the doctrine of appropriations.
For Thomas, the first reason rendering knowledge of the divine Persons necessary for us is "in order that we may have a right conception of creation", while the second (and principal) reason is "that we may hold a right idea of the redemption of the human race, accomplished by the Incarnation of the Son and the outpouring of the Holy Spirit"191. On this foundation one can appreciate his formulation of the Trinitarian principle of creation: "The procession of the Persons, which is perfect, is the cause and reason of the procession of creatures"192. Thomas uses the same word (*processio*) to refer to the origin of the Persons of the Trinity and of creation, even though an ontological chasm exists between them. This represents the Christianisation of the Neoplatonic *exitus-reditus* schema. The Angelic Doctor envisions salvation history as the process of the outgoing of creatures from God and their return to God, within which our historical existence unfolds in freedom, in a sphere wherein the most appropriate arguments are those of fittingness (*convenientia*), not necessity.
Thomas likewise affirms a single creative principle, because there is only one divine creative essence:

> To create is properly to cause or produce the being of things. Now, since every agent produces things resembling itself, the principle of an action can be determined from its effect: for fire produces nothing but fire. Therefore, to create belongs to God in virtue of His being: which is nothing other than His essence, common to the three Persons. And thus creating is not proper to any single Person, but is common to the whole Trinity193.

By this Thomas does not exclude the specific operation of the Persons in creation. While the personal processions are *ad intra*, and therefore belong to the distinct Persons, the procession *ad extra* belongs to the three Persons, and only in this sense does one speak of the essence as the single principle: it is not conceived as another principle alongside the Persons (which would incur the risk of positing a quaternity in God, as Lombard had cautioned).
Thomas's affirmation of the single creative principle, grounded in metaphysical reasoning, is consistent with his conviction that human reason can attain by its own powers the truth of creation. What reason by its unaided powers can by no means know, on the other hand, is the Trinity of Persons194.
Thomas's distinction between the knowledge of God's unity through reason and the knowledge of the Trinity of Persons through faith does not imply a separation between the One God Creator and the Triune God Saviour, but rather a dual, intensive and non-exclusive level of consideration between the One God Creator and the Triune God Creator195.
The Aquinate states that the existence of *unus creator* does not necessarily imply that He is *unus creans*196. Creation is the work of the three Persons, and therefore there is not *unus creans*, but *tres creantes*, since the adjective/participle indicates number according to the subjects; at the same time, creation is the work of the Three not insofar as they are distinct (as they would be by their processions) but insofar as they operate in virtue of the common essence, and therefore there are not *tres creatores*, but *unus creator*, since the substantive indicates the number of the essence.
How are *unus creator* and *tres creantes* to be harmonised? Thomas derives from Scripture and theological tradition the doctrine of appropriations, which allows one to attribute to one Person an action, or an aspect thereof, that is common to all three, because it corresponds more closely to His personal characteristics. In the first place, the Aquinate refers to the essential attributes of God:

> The divine Persons have a causal influence on creation according to the nature of their respective processions. For, as we demonstrated above when treating of the divine knowledge and will, God is the cause of things by His intellect and His will [...]. God the Father produced creatures through His Word, who is the Son, and through His Love, who is the Holy Spirit. And in this respect the processions of the Persons are the reason for the production of creatures, inasmuch as they include the essential attributes of knowledge and will197.

The Augustinian psychological analogy, which regards the generation of the Word *per modum intellectus* and the procession of the Spirit *per modum voluntatis*, serves to describe the production of creatures. If we add that to create requires infinite power, and that the power to beget is the same power to create198, there emerges the threefold appropriation of essential attributes to the Persons: to the Father omnipotence, to the Son wisdom, to the Spirit love.
Saint Thomas takes up from the theological tradition a second series of appropriations that refer to the exercise of creative causality199. The Aquinate analyses the contents assigned to the causal prepositions *ex quo*, *per quem*, and *in quo*. The various prepositions used indicate, respectively, efficient causality, which is appropriated to the Father, in relation to the attribute of divine power; exemplar causality, attributed to the Son, in connection with the wisdom of the divine Artist; and final causality, which is attributed to the Holy Spirit, as is also goodness, since it indicates the divine governance that directs every thing to the attainment of its end, which is identified with the good.
The doctrine of appropriations enables the affirmation of the Trinitarian principle of creation, highlighting the resemblance between creative causality and the procession of the Persons, without forfeiting the uniqueness of the creative principle; for the foundation of such resemblance, alongside the primary source of Revelation, is the appropriation to the Persons of attributes that our reason distinguishes as belonging to the single divine essence.
In conclusion, the distinction between reason and faith, between nature and grace, is consistent with the progression from the One God Creator to the Triune God Creator. The work of the Creating Trinity is the presupposition of the work of the Re-creating or Saving Trinity. Creation and redemption are distinguished because not all, by the mere fact of being created, are immediately redeemed. "God who created you without you, will not save you without you"200. Within creation the salvific design unfolds in history, to which, through the exercise of freedom, one must adhere (it remaining possible to oppose it). What is at stake here is the relative autonomy of created reality and created freedom. At the same time, identifying the Creator with the Redeemer is decisive, because Christianity proclaims Christ as the sole Saviour and mediator between God and men. There are no other mediators or redeemers. Christocentrism is therefore a Christocentrism of creation and redemption. Revelation concerning God the Creator came into contact in history with diverse philosophical perspectives. It is to this that we turn our attention in the next chapter.
