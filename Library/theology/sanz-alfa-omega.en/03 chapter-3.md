## Chapter 3 The Characteristics of the Creative Act in Dialogue with Philosophical Thought {#sanz-alfa-omega.3}

IN DIALOGUE WITH PHILOSOPHICAL THOUGHT

The study of the creative act in Revelation has led to its identification as the work of the almighty Father from whom all things proceed, of the Son-Word through whom all things were made, and of the Spirit-Love. Without departing from this Trinitarian framework, we propose, in virtue of the relationship between faith and reason, to explore these dimensions in their dialogue with various philosophical and religious perspectives throughout history. We shall examine the meaning of creation *ex nihilo* (omnipotence), of its intelligibility (wisdom), and of its freedom and goodness (love). This final point will lead us to conclude with the classical theme of the glory of God as the end of creation.

### 3.1. Creation *ex nihilo*

Even if the development of the human being's "creative" capacity might seem to suggest otherwise, the assertion remains valid that to speak of creation in its original sense necessarily implies accepting the existence of God. The world and man owe their existence to the infinite divine power to bring all things into being without presuppositions. Strictly speaking, the sole creator is God, who is "the one principle of all things, creator of all things visible and invisible, spiritual and corporeal; who by his almighty power at the beginning of time created from nothing both orders of creatures" (DS 800).

This formula of the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) reflects something far older. Under the influence of 2 Macc 7:28, we read in the *Shepherd of Hermas*: "First of all, believe that there is one God, who created all things and set them in order from non-being into being"201. The opening of the rule of faith handed down by Tertullian confesses that there is "one only God, and no other besides the creator of the world, who brought forth all things out of nothing (*de nihilo*) through his Word, sent forth before all things"202. In his description of God the Creator and of the reality created by Him, Theophilus of Antioch maintains that "He brought all these things into existence from nothing (*ex ouk ontōn*), so that through His works His greatness might be known and perceived"203. The expression "creation from nothing" soon became an evident element of the Christian faith204.

It is debated whether the precise moment of its formulation took place in second-century Christianity, in the controversy against Gnosticism and other emanationist philosophical currents205, or whether one should recognize the implicit presence of this doctrine in the earliest teachings of the Church206. More recently, it has been argued that creation *ex nihilo* was already present in the New Testament tradition207. What cannot in any case be called into question is the Judeo-Christian origin of the expression208.

### 3.1.1. Christianity and Greek Philosophy Confronting Creation from Nothing

From the outset, the Christian proclamation laid claim to universality. It was logical to seek points of contact with pagan religion and culture, as witnessed by Paul's speech at the Areopagus. At the time Christianity appeared, the explanation of reality in the Greek world divided the sensible realm of bodies—changeable, mortal, non-divine, and the principle of evil—from the supersensible realm of the spirit—permanent, immortal, divine, and the principle of the good. While Plato's philosophy taught men to focus upon the second, of which the first was a poor emanation, Aristotle sought to value the first. Christianity opted for certain elements of Platonism that appeared to be in accord with revelation, such as the primacy of the spirit, or the awareness that man's end lies in God. Yet there were obscure points in the Platonic vision: the quasi-identification of the human spirit with the divine spirit, the conception of God in terms of the Good as an impersonal idea, the negative view of the material cosmos, and the conception of salvation as an achievement attained by man through his own powers.

In this context, Gnosticism presented itself as a spiritualist tendency within Christianity209, wherein the soul originates by emanation from God and therefore participates in a certain sense in divinity, whilst matter is the prison of the spirit. Leading exponents of Gnosticism were Marcion and Mani, the founder of Manichaeism. In the latter especially, there exists a stark dualism between matter as the principle of evil and spirit as the principle of the good. The ascetic conduct of struggling against material elements to attain the spirit conceals a deception; for subsequently, upon noting that the body does not belong to divinity and is therefore irrelevant to salvation, one easily lapses into hedonism.

In these perspectives, God has nothing to do with the material world, but only with the human soul. One may wonder: did the idea of God as creator of the world truly exist? To what extent did pagan thought, especially Greek, possess the idea of creation?

Thomas Aquinas posed this very question. It is debated whether, in the texts where he traces the history of the question starting from the Greek philosophers, he attributed the notion of creation to Plato and Aristotle210. If the Christian notion of creation *ex nihilo* implies an absolute beginning, Thomas could not attribute this to the Greeks, particularly Aristotle, since this is an aspect accessible only through supernatural Revelation. This does not mean that he could not in some way detect the presence, among Greek thinkers, of the notion of creation, insofar as it contains a dimension attainable by reason: namely, the universal causality of all things. Here emerges Aquinas's optimism regarding human reason, which historically, prior to Christianity, managed in some measure to intuit what is contained in the metaphysical notion of creation.

One of the first metaphysical consequences of Christianity consisted in affirming that the fundamental distinction in reality is that between God the Creator and His creatures. In the first centuries of the Christian era, this constituted a radical novelty. In the ancient schools of thought, the radical distinction between matter and spirit gave rise to diverse currents (materialism and spiritualism, dualism and monism). Christianity shattered these frameworks by asserting that matter too (like spirit) is the creation of the one transcendent God. This represented genuine philosophical progress, which can be formulated, in Heideggerian terminology, as the transition from the ancient axiom "from nothing comes nothing" (*ex nihilo nihil fit*) to the other, "from nothing is made created being" (*ex nihilo fit ens creatum*)211.

Irenaeus emphasized, against Gnosticism, that God is the author of all that exists:

> To attribute the substance of created things to the power and will of Him who is the God of all things is credible, acceptable, and consistent; and in this regard it may rightly be said that "what is impossible with men is possible with God" (Lk 18:27). For while men cannot make anything out of nothing, but only out of underlying matter, God is superior to men first and foremost because for His work of creation He has Himself supplied the matter, which previously did not exist212.

Augustine distinguishes creation from generation (whose effect is of the same nature as the generator) and from production (which implies the pre-existence of a material). Creation is the bestowal of being and existence *ex nihilo*213. This explanation stands in stark contrast to Manichaeism and Neoplatonism. There is a single principle, and this suffices to overcome both dualism and pantheism, because the Creator is absolutely distinct from His creatures. Being as such is a good, because Supreme Being is also the Supreme Good. This thought remains heir to Neoplatonism, albeit adapted to the requirements of the Christian faith: "There was Thou, and nothing else; from which nothingness Thou createdst heaven and earth; two creatures, one near unto Thee, the other near to nothing; one unto which Thou only shouldest be superior, the other unto which nothing should be inferior"214.

Thomas Aquinas developed, within his theology, a metaphysics of creation that united in a new synthesis, thanks to the notion of the act of being, Platonic participation and Aristotelian causality. God is presented as Subsistent Being Itself (*Ipsum Esse Subsistens*), who as First Cause is absolutely transcendent with respect to the world; at the same time, in virtue of the participation of His being to creatures, He is intimately present in them, as they depend in their being and operation upon Him who is the source of being. With Augustine, one can affirm that God is "more inward than my innermost self and higher than my highest part" (*interior intimo meo et superior summo meo*)215.

With respect to Greek philosophy, Christianity overcomes both monism (which asserts that matter and spirit merge, and that the reality of God and the world are identical) and dualism (according to which matter and spirit are opposed primordial principles). Created reality is one, because it proceeds from a single principle, and at the same time diversified. The primordial non-opposition of matter and spirit finds expression in man, composed of both. Christianity has in fact supplied metaphysical theses to universal thought, theses which nevertheless do not cease to belong to the proper realm of reason216. We shall examine Thomas's synthesis and then turn to the theme in philosophical modernity.

### 3.1.2. Key Elements of Saint Thomas's Metaphysics of Creation

#### 3.1.2.1. Participation and Causality

Thomas considers that creation from nothing in the metaphysical sense can be demonstrated rationally, whereas this does not hold for the question of creation understood as a temporal beginning. We shall treat the latter in Chapter IV, whereas our interest here is to demonstrate the former, which is founded upon the participation of being217.

The argument begins from the fact that beings possess participated being, which is really distinct from their essence; one must ascend from beings by participation to Being by Essence, who is unique and the cause of all being by participation. Participation is understood here in a transcendental, not a predicamental sense. While predicamental participation implies that what is participated exists only in the participants and is found in them according to its essential content (for example, the species in individuals), transcendental participation presupposes that what is participated exists outside the participants, and the latter do not possess it in its totality, but according to diverse degrees (for example, accidents with respect to the being of the substance).

Participation in being cannot be called predicamental, because in that case being would belong to the substance of the thing, and therefore to its definition. Being does not enter into the definition of an entity because it is neither a genus nor a specific difference. It is participated without belonging to essence218, and this means that participation in being is transcendental. Being—specifically Being by Essence—is a separate perfection, and can be only one, because it coincides with the metaphysical notion of act as pure and absolute perfection, unlimited by any potency. Being is the act of all acts, the perfection of all perfections; participated being is limited, whereas Being by Essence is subsistent, unique, and separate.

The argument maintains that it is necessary that Being by Essence be the cause of beings by participation. To participate in something means not to be it by nature; it implies having received it, and this demands a cause. From the notion of being by participation one deduces the necessity of a cause, even though the relationship to the cause does not enter into the definition of being, because there exists a Being that is uncaused219.

The pivotal principle that allows Thomas to demonstrate creative causality is participation in being: only He who is Being by Essence can communicate being to that which possesses it by participation220. Aristotle is thus brought to completion by apprehending God not merely as the first cause of motion, but also as the first cause of being. The novelty of the Thomistic synthesis consists in integrating Platonic participation and Aristotelian causality by virtue of his original notion of being as act. If in Platonism participation obscures causality, in Aristotelianism causality extinguishes participation221.

#### 3.1.2.2. Creation, Act of Being, Real Distinction

A decisive element of Saint Thomas's metaphysics is the so-called "real distinction": while in God essence and being are identical, other beings are characterised by the composition of essence and the act of being (*esse*, *actus essendi*), *esse* being a participation in the divine Being; *esse* is received in an essence that is actualised by it. The real distinction surpasses the Aristotelian conception of act and potency; for—given the distinction between an essential order and an existential order or order of being—forms, although acts at the essential level, are nevertheless in potency with respect to *esse*, whereas Thomistic *esse* is the act of every act. Participation points back to causality because, if something is possessed by participation, it must necessarily be caused by Him to whom it belongs by essence. The proper cause of being is Being by Essence Himself, *Ipsum Esse Subsistens*.

Aquinas's reasoning is situated in the order of being, which is the act of every act and the perfection of every perfection. This confirms the genuine universality of the starting point of the proof of creation and, consequently, the necessity of a universal cause. Saint Thomas moves beyond the purely formal order and truly attains the order of the real. The originality of the Thomistic notion of *actus essendi* in comparison with Greek thought thus becomes manifest. Notwithstanding Aquinas's optimism in certain passages, Greek philosophy never attained the notion of creation in the strict sense, insofar as its metaphysical journey toward the foundation of reality halted at the level of forms. Stated succinctly: the Greek philosophers could explain *why* something is *what* it is, but not *why* it is at all.

"Saint Thomas relies directly and systematically upon the composition of essence and *esse* to demonstrate creation"222. Even when not explicitly mentioned, the real distinction is implicit in all the proofs: every creature is characterised by the distinction between essence and being, because every creature has a received being, limited by essence; whence the contingency of creatures, which do not possess being of themselves, and point to a higher cause that bestows being.

Thus "creation and participation in being are identified." Creation designates neither a mere *factum* nor a state of ontological precariousness, but a stable and positive condition, like the *actus essendi* that confers existence upon every thing. "Creaturely being does not derive from a thrownness into existence, nor is it reduced to the actuality resulting from some sort of primordial event; rather, it resides in the deepest structure of reality, whose being is act"223. The infinite difference between God and the creature is expressed in a consummate manner in the Thomistic distinction between essence and *esse*, articulated through the notion of participation, which entails the creature's total dependence upon God, thanks to the emergence of *esse* upon which creation is grounded.

#### 3.1.2.3. Creation as Relation

The notion of relation possesses considerable importance in Thomistic metaphysics and theology of creation224, in connection with the notions of participation and causality: "Creation is nothing other than a certain relation"225. In virtue of the real distinction between essence and being, together with the radical distinction between beings by participation and Being by Essence, and once motion has been eliminated from the causality proper to *Ipsum Esse* which is creation, what remains in the creature of that creative action is the relation of dependence of the being by participation upon Being by Essence. In harmony with the distinction between creation in the active sense (God's own action, inaccessible to us) and in the passive sense (the effect of that divine action, which is created reality, perceptible to us), Saint Thomas establishes that the created world has a real relation to God, whereas this relation, considered from God's point of view, is only a relation of reason with respect to created reality226.

This assertion does not establish a separation between God and His creatures, but explains the asymmetrical reality of creation: creatures depend on God (this is creation in them), while God does not depend on creatures, because His acting derives solely from His love. God is the Absolute who bestows gratuitously. The freedom and love of divine creative action do not give rise in God to a relation of dependence, but to solicitude for His creatures227.

The relation to God is fundamental in the creature, because it constitutes creation in the passive sense (*passive sumpta*)228. The created act of being proceeds from God (*esse a Deo*) not only historically, but permanently. The relation to God (*esse ad Deum*) marks the origin, dependence, and finality of creatures. This relation is grounded neither upon essence nor upon other formalities, but upon being; so that while it is true, on the one hand, that the creature can be considered in itself "without its relation to God" (*sine respectu eius ad Deum*)—which shows that this relation does not belong to essence—it is equally certain, on the other hand, that in that case, the creature considered without that reference appears as "not having being" (*non habens esse*)229. The relation to God is not secondary, but absolutely necessary in the creature.

The proper effect of creation is *esse*. If one loses sight of this by according primacy to essence, one commits the metaphysical error of attempting to introduce relation into the essence of things, in order to affirm—albeit with the best theological intentions—that the relation to God is constitutive of the creature. The error of essentialism is to think that essence is constitutive of things, while existence is pure facticity. One must not confuse substance with its relations, but understand that the relation to God is constitutive in creatures because it is anchored in the act of being. Thomistic thought on creation is relational not because it reduces created being to relation, but because it perceives the relation of creation as inherent in the innermost core of the creature230.

#### 3.1.2.4. Immanence and Transcendence

How is it that being, the innermost principle of the entity (*interior et intimius*), is received from without? If being is that which actualises *ens qua ens* from within, entirely immanent to the thing, how can it be received? Here arises the problem of "extrinsecism" in relation to the causality of being: if being is what is most proper to an entity, it seems difficult to affirm that it is caused by another; if, on the other hand, being is understood as residing in the entity as an external addition, then the first assertion becomes unintelligible.

In Thomistic metaphysics, the affirmation of creative causality is paradoxical, because it concerns a causality that is at once intrinsic and extrinsic. Creation entails an intrinsic causality not because it is one of the so-called intrinsic causes (material and formal), but because it acts from within, unlike creaturely efficient causes which necessarily act from without. Given that God exercises efficient causality, creation also entails an extrinsic causality, and indeed a transcendent one, by virtue of the radical distinction between Being by Essence and participated *esse*. "Intrinsic and extrinsic causality, which not only do not exclude each other but mutually imply one another: only the infinite divine transcendence makes possible His intimate causal immanence in all things"231. The compatibility between absolute divine transcendence and His profound immanence in all creation, in Aquinas's perspective, can be attained by the natural light of reason at the metaphysical level232.

For Saint Thomas, the notion of participation constitutes the decisive rational proof of creation, in which the immanence of being in entities is harmoniously integrated with the transcendence of the Being who gives them origin. "In this way, immanence and transcendence meet: the meeting point is the metaphysics of creation, the theoretical foundation of this metaphysics is the Thomistic notion of intensive *esse* through the notion of participation"233.

### 3.1.3. Creation from Nothing and Philosophical Modernity: Return to Dualism and Monism

In modern philosophy, the notion of creation underwent a shift toward the idea of continuous creation understood in an actualist sense. Creation from nothing does not imply an absolute beginning; rather, everything constantly begins anew under the divine creative impulse, which is the only real causality. This represents an exaggeration of the *ex nihilo*, the fruit of the nominalist exaltation of divine omnipotence, before which every creature is radically contingent. This exasperation would paradoxically lead to the progressive disappearance of the notion of creation from nothing, marking a return both to dualism and to monism of a pantheistic stamp.

In Descartes, the rational demonstration of God's existence from the conservation in being of all things coincides with the division, inaugurated by the *cogito*, between *res extensa* and *res cogitans*, which brings us back to the dualism between matter and spirit. Malebranche's occasionalism would assert the sole causality of God in creation, while all other beings are merely occasions for God's action. To place God at the origin of everything in this manner means placing Him at the origin of nothing; consequently, from this point forward a reaction developed that would end by expelling God from the causal order of the world: deism, which grew alongside the development of the scientific capabilities of the era.

Leibniz conceives essences as possibles; as such, they possess their reality and determination in the divine intellect and are therefore uncreated. Creation signifies the transition from possibility to actuality, that is, an extrinsic placing of essences into existence. Here is reflected the primacy of possibility over actuality, which attributes to possibility an exigency to exist (*omne possibile exigit exsistere*), translating into an essentialism wherein essence (fundamentally eternal and uncreated) has primacy over existence, which is a mere extrinsic result. Even though the expression *ex nihilo* is maintained, creation is not truly from nothing—at least not absolute nothingness—but at most from existential nothingness, because essences precede the decision to create.

Baruch Spinoza, a seventeenth-century Dutch Jewish philosopher, represents a type of rationalist philosophy in which the human mind, overcoming Cartesian dualism, attains the unity of knowledge, insofar as the order and connection of things (*res extensa*) coincides with the order and connection of ideas (*res cogitans*)234. This unification translates into the maxim *Deus sive Natura*, the identification between God—who is the infinite substance upon which everything depends—and Nature, the totality of all that exists, the substance and all its attributes and modifications. Spinoza distinguishes *natura naturans* (which is God) from *natura naturata* (the things):

> By *natura naturans* we must understand that which is in itself and is conceived through itself, or those attributes of substance which express eternal and infinite essence, that is, God, insofar as He is considered as a free cause. And by *natura naturata* I understand whatever follows from the necessity of God's nature, or of each of God's attributes, that is, all the modes of God's attributes insofar as they are considered as things which are in God, and which without God can neither be nor be conceived235.

For Spinoza, God is the cause of the being of things in the same sense in which He is cause of Himself (*causa sui*), understanding by cause a necessary deduction, the unfolding of what is already contained in the immanence of substance. Spinoza rejects the idea of creation because he denies the transcendence of God over the world: "God is the immanent, and not the transitive, cause of all things"236. He upholds a pantheism, or rather a "panentheism", which can be described by the formula: "Whatever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be or be conceived"237. Some have evaluated this position as implicit atheism: in the Spinozist system, it suffices to replace God with Nature. Spinoza was excommunicated by the Jewish community because of the implications of his theses.

Hegel believed that rather than denying God, Spinoza had divinised the cosmos, and that his position was an "acosmism" rather than an "atheism"—a denial of the autonomy of Nature with respect to God, and therefore a reaffirmation of the uniqueness of divine substance. Hegel lamented that Spinoza had not found a dialectic capable of integrating affirmation and negation and of guaranteeing the autonomy of the finite with respect to the infinity of divine substance. Hegel, on the other hand, proposed precisely such a dialectic: "What is rational is actual; and what is actual is rational"238. This is the formulation of absolute idealism, which unites the fixism of rationalism (the idea) with the dynamism of empiricism (nature).

Dialectic, partly retrieved from Fichte as the law of thought and reality, is composed of three moments: a positive one (thesis), a negative one of opposition (antithesis), and one of reconciliation of opposites through limitation (synthesis). Hegel does not typically use these terms, but others of a more ontological nature: in itself (*an sich*), for itself (*für sich*), and in and for itself (*an sich und für sich*). The first moment (abstract intellectual) concerns the idea in itself, which can be identified with God prior to the creation of finite entity (the world), and is studied by logic; the second (negative rational or dialectical) contemplates the idea outside itself, namely Nature, which is the externalisation or alienation of the Idea in the spatiotemporal realities of the world, studied by the philosophy of nature; the third (positive rational or speculative) unites the idea in and for itself, namely Spirit, which is the idea returning to its initial state, enriched with concreteness after its passage through Nature, and is studied by the philosophy of spirit.

Hegel explains the fusion of finite and infinite: the infinite estranges itself, merges into the finite, and reclaims itself. This unitary dynamic is a secularisation of the Christian idea of creation and redemption, as would become evident in the disciples of the Hegelian Left (Marx and Feuerbach). We witness here a confusion of God's immanence in creation with ontological fusion. The notion of creation from nothing has disappeared. Things do not proceed from the Absolute; rather, the Absolute is this very proceeding. The history of the world is the history of God, of the advent of the Spirit, of the realisation of reason. Even if he rejected the false representations associated with a less deeply conceived pantheism, his was an authentic and acutely grounded pantheism239.

### 3.1.4. Creation from Nothing in the Contemporary Context: Nihilism, Evolutionism, Existentialism

The historical process that began with the arbitrary exaggeration of God's omnipotence led to the radical separation between God and the world, but also to a confusion of the two. All this has brought about a divinisation of man, which has prompted certain currents in contemporary philosophy to speak of creation as applied to human action240. The Kantian ideal is radicalised in those approaches that eliminate the Creator and put man in His place, as occurs paradigmatically in Nietzsche's "nihilism", which constitutes a return to the ancient pre-Christian formula *ex nihilo nihil fit*.

The idea of creation has been thrown into crisis by those who, in confrontation with the natural sciences, have theorised evolution at a metaphysical level. In this perspective, it would no longer be necessary to explain the leap from non-being to being. One must distinguish evolution as a scientific description concerning the properties of things, from evolution as a philosophical explanation (metaphysical evolutionism), which seeks to go further and offer an explanation of the very existence of things. Evolution as a scientific theory cannot contradict faith in creation (the so-called "creationism" typical of certain fundamentalist Christian sectors, which favours complete literality in interpreting Genesis, is an entirely different matter). The omnipotence of a God who creates an evolving world evokes even greater admiration, as Darwin himself witnessed:

> There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed by the Creator into a few forms or into one; and that, whilst this planet has gone cycling on according to the fixed law of gravity, from so simple a beginning endless forms most beautiful and most wonderful have been, and are being evolved241.

It has been pointed out that while in *The Origin of Species* the words "creation" and "Creator" appear more than a hundred times, the word "evolution" does not appear242. Even today some perceive antagonisms that are more ideological than real. The scientific theory is one thing; its philosophical and theological interpretation is quite another.

As a reaction against idealism, an existentialist current developed which interprets the dogma of creation in a psychological-existential sense, stripping it of its ontological dimension. Creation, or rather the sense of creatureliness, is taken to be the experience of one's own dependence, of existential poverty, a feeling of nothingness before God. By placing present experience at the centre, one runs the risk of ending up denying the absolute beginning of created being, as happens in the thought of Schleiermacher243. To achieve a balanced view of the question, one must establish a clear epistemological differentiation between the natural sciences and faith, with philosophy serving as mediation.

### 3.1.5. The Denial of Divine Omnipotence in Recent Proposals

"By granting freedom to man, God relinquished His power"; "by renouncing His inviolability the eternal ground permitted the world to be"244. After the terrible experience of Auschwitz, Hans Jonas deemed it necessary to deny omnipotence, viewing it as incompatible with divine goodness and rationality. "Only of a completely incomprehensible God could it be asserted that He is absolutely good and co-originally absolutely omnipotent"245.

Some theologians have followed this path, abandoning the divine attribute of omnipotence in favour of the weakness of the Cross: "Today various theologians say that God cannot be omnipotent, otherwise there could not be so much suffering, so much evil in the world [...]; for many, for us, it becomes problematic, difficult, to believe in God the Father and to believe Him to be omnipotent"246.

To renounce divine omnipotence would mean abdicating petitionary prayer; if one no longer believes in God's lordship over all things, then the world is no longer in His hands247. One must follow a different path, which allows us to grasp how "His omnipotence is not expressed in violence, it is not expressed in the destruction of every opposing power as we might desire, but is expressed in love, in mercy, in forgiveness, in accepting our freedom"248.

In order to legitimise his image of God, Jonas believed he had to renounce the traditional doctrine of divine omnipotence:

> Total, absolute power means power that is not limited by anything, not even by the existence of something in general, alien to it and distinct from it [...]. Absolute power has therefore, in its solitude, no object upon which it can act. But as power devoid of an object, it is power devoid of power, power that negates itself249.

These words reveal a certain manner of understanding the medieval distinction between God's absolute and ordained power, which became exasperated with the voluntarist decay of nominalism250, and which led:

> to the assertion that we know only God's *voluntas ordinata*. Beyond it would lie God's freedom, in virtue of which He could have created and done the exact opposite of everything He actually did. Here positions emerge that [...] could lead to the image of a God-as-Caprice, who is not bound even to truth and the good251.

Against Jonas's notion that God, given the existence of evil, cannot be both omnipotent and good, Kierkegaard observed *ante litteram* that God's omnipotence is identical to His goodness. Starting from the observation that "the highest thing that can be done for a being [...] is to make it free", he maintains that in order to do this, omnipotence is required. This seems strange to us because, according to our ordinary way of reasoning, omnipotence ought to make dependent. Contrary to every finite power which makes dependent, infinite goodness manifests itself in giving in such a way that, by holding itself back in an omnipotent manner, it simultaneously renders the recipient independent.

> Only omnipotence can make independent, can bring forth from nothing that which has subsistence in itself, by virtue of the fact that omnipotence always holds itself back. Omnipotence is not bound by a relationship to something else, for there is nothing else to which it relates; no, it can give without losing the least bit of its power, that is, it can make independent.

While for Socrates the art of power is to make men free, and this is a prerogative of omnipotence, the widespread idea in modern dialectic is that power grows in proportion to the capacity to compel and make dependent. For Christianity, on the contrary:

> creation from nothing expresses in turn that omnipotence can make free. He to whom I absolutely owe everything, while nevertheless absolutely preserving all in being, has precisely made me independent. If God, in order to create men, had lost something of His power, He could no longer make men independent252.

### 3.2. The Intelligibility of Creation

The specificity of the creative act is not captured merely by describing it as the production of being from nothing. Omnipotence is necessary to create, but it is not sufficient. The exaltation of divine power as the sole configuring element of the creative act can lead to the idea of a God-as-Caprice, to whom creatures submit merely in order to subsist.

The Old Testament presents the world as the fruit of God's wisdom (cf. Wis 9:9). It "is not the product of any necessity whatever, nor of blind fate or chance" (CCC 295), but possesses an intelligibility that human reason, participating in the light of the divine Intellect, can grasp—not without effort, and in a spirit of humility and respect before the Creator and His work (cf. Job 42:3). This attains its full expression in the identification of the Son, Jesus Christ, with the Logos (cf. John 1:1). The wisdom of God is a Person, the incarnate Word, through whom all things were made (cf. John 1:3). Christianity from the beginning made an option for rationality. God's creative work is intelligible because it is the fruit of wisdom, of the Logos, who coincides with God's very being. There exists a creative Reason, or *Spiritus Creator*, which prompts the affirmation: "not to act reasonably, not to act with *logos*, is contrary to the nature of God"253.

### 3.2.1. The Logos Between Greek Philosophy and the New Testament

The New Testament doctrine concerning the presence of the Word (Logos) in creation, together with the translation of the Hebrew term *bārā’* as *poiein*, was destined to make creation appear linked to Greek terminology deriving from Platonism, Stoicism, and Neoplatonism, in close contact with the doctrine of the demiurge. We must ask whether this was a simple borrowing of a term and a doctrine, or whether, upon a common terminological foundation, a genuine novelty of thought was wrought.

In Plato the supreme principle is the One, identified with the Good, which is not a person; it is not an intellect, but an intelligible. This does not mean that there is no divine intellect: that intellect is the demiurge (*dēmiourgos*, literally, "public worker"; also translated as "artisan"), above whom stands the One. This hierarchy would change with Aristotle, for whom the first mover is intellect, the thought of thought (*noēsis noēseōs*), not the intelligible.

Plato's demiurge is *poiētēs*, the efficient cause of produced realities, into which he introduces order and unity. His intervention presupposes the higher principle, which is the Good or One, to which he must look and by which his work is measured. The ultimate ground of beings is not the demiurge, but the One. At the same time, there is a material principle antithetical to the intelligible principle. This material principle is eternal, whereas generated things are not, and it is also indeterminate. The demiurge works upon a receptacle, which for material entities can be called matter, but which for mathematical entities is indefinite extension: this principle is the Dyad. Thus, the demiurge operates having above him the One and beneath him the Dyad, both being first and eternal principles of reality254. The demiurge is the mediator between the intelligible dimension and matter, the intelligence that designs the world, looking to the ideas as models and using matter as an instrument255. The ideas, the object of true intellection, are eternal and necessary; the sensible world is generated and corruptible. The demiurge does not create from nothing, but transmits form to pre-existing matter according to an order that transforms chaos into cosmos.

The distinction between the sensible and supersensible worlds appears in the allegory of the cave256. True reality lies on the supersensible side, wherein dwell divine beings and many perfections. Perfection implies distance from matter, spirit and matter being antithetical. Nevertheless, there is a relation between the two worlds, as seen in the tension between spirit and matter in man. The work of the demiurge ensures that in the sensible world there exists some intelligibility, a certain order.

Philo, a Jewish philosopher of the first century of the Christian era, introduced the biblical idea of creation into philosophy for the first time257. When God "generated all things, He not only brought them to light, but created what did not exist before, not handling matter like a demiurge, but being Himself its creator"258. Without renouncing Platonic metaphysics, he went further in his rereading of the Genesis account. His writings, in dialogue with the principal philosophical schools of the day, exerted a considerable influence and are important for contextualising the Christian doctrine of the Logos.

While according to Plato ideas are ungenerated and eternal, serving as models or exemplary causes, Philo conceives them as produced by divine thought. Whereas the Platonic demiurge had to look to the Ideas, being measured by them in his activity, Philo's God has nothing above Himself. "The intelligible cosmos is nothing other than the divine Logos already engaged in the act of creation"259. Here is a first difference between Philo and Plato: at the summit of reality stands not the impersonal One, but the personal God.

The biblical doctrine of divine transcendence leads to conceiving matter as created and not eternal. "It is the proper characteristic of the supreme Craftsman to provide sufficient material before beginning to fashion it"260. Creation occurs in two moments:

> God [...] having determined to create this visible world, first formed the intelligible world, so that, using this incorporeal and most godlike pattern, He might then fashion this corporeal world, a younger likeness of the elder model [...]. But the world composed of ideas can have no other dwelling-place than the divine Logos who disposed these things261.

The Philonic interpretation of the Logos is a matter of no easy exegesis262. For Plato, intelligible form is not inherent in the thing, but an occasion for the intellect to elevate itself to the contemplation of ideas. Other philosophical schools affirmed an intelligibility intrinsic to things. Aristotle called it *entelecheia*. The Stoics spoke of the *logos spermatikos*, the "seminal reason", a living (active) principle that diffuses itself through inert matter (passive principle), bringing diverse entities to life. This would be the counterpart of Plotinus's "world soul", present in all things, guarantor of the rational unity of the entire cosmos.

Between the Platonic demiurge (*logos theios*) and the intelligibility of things (*logos spermatikos*), Philo situates the Logos, who proceeds from God as the first creature. God creates the world and governs it through the Logos, characterised by biblical terms such as wisdom, son, or angel, who has "the extraordinary gift of separating, by standing in the midst, the created from the Creator"263. This explanation, while retaining Platonic elements and obscure points, goes beyond Platonism, insofar as it seeks to establish contact between divinity and the material world, and to answer the question of mediation between God and the created world. It is a doctrine that prepares the way for the Christian Revelation of the Word.

The idea of a Creator who has no need of pre-existing matter is present in the New Testament264. In continuity with 2 Macc 7:28, the Epistle to the Romans affirms that God "calls into existence the things that do not exist" (Rom 4:17). In the second century, this would lead to the formulation of creation *ex nihilo*, grounded upon the salvation accomplished by the risen Christ. This implies that one can no longer conceive of a salvation that leaves matter outside. There is no longer room for a dualism of principles; one must affirm that matter is not eternal, but created.

In the New Testament perspective, as shown by the Pauline texts that treat the relationship between Christ and creation, prior to the foundation of the cosmos there is no longer a material principle; there is the Son, beloved of the Father. The Epistle to the Colossians (1:15–17) establishes the primary distinction between God and created reality, whether visible or invisible. God's design in Christ grounds reality in every dimension. If God is the primary source of the salvation bestowed in Christ, it follows that God is also the sole and exclusive source of the gift of existence bestowed in Christ, the unique principle of creation. Material reality is fully involved in divine creation.

Whereas in Philo creation comprises two moments (the conception of the intelligible cosmos and of matter, followed by the formation of the visible cosmos), in the Epistle to the Ephesians (1:3–10) the formation of the cosmos is preceded by the election of the saints in Christ by the Father. If, according to Plato, the demiurge forms the sensible world by gazing upon the realm of ideas, here the reality that serves as the model for creation is Christ. The Johannine Prologue, which identifies the Son with the Logos and declares His divinity, states that "all things were made through Him, and without Him was not anything made that was made" (John 1:3).

### 3.2.2. The Question of the Logos in the Fathers and in Saint Thomas

In the second century, the Gnostic repudiation of the Old Testament led to the debasement of the creator into a demiurge, kept distinct from the good God, the Father of Jesus Christ. The Nicene Creed would profess faith "in one God, the Father almighty, maker of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible" (DS 125). The development of Christological doctrine in the first centuries had as its central point the affirmation of a single Logos, divine and human, who creates and saves the entire universe265. The Fathers of the Church viewed with interest the Platonic doctrine of the demiurge and Philo's doctrine of the Logos, insofar as they seemed useful for explaining the nature and role of Christ, the incarnate Word: His presence in creation guaranteed and grounded the universality of the salvation He accomplished.

In this encounter between Christianity and philosophy—which entailed not only an interest in rational concepts to explain reality, but also a discernment to grasp seeds of truth and set aside what did not conform to Revelation—there arose two errors incompatible with Christian faith: on the one hand, the subordination of the divinity of the Word to that of God (cf. Arius); on the other hand, the suppression or absorption of the human element of the Word into His divinity (cf. Apollinaris). Confronted with these errors, the dogmatic formulations of the Council of Nicaea in 325 were defined regarding the full, consubstantial divinity of the Word; followed by the Council of Ephesus in 431 and Chalcedon in 451 on the perfect humanity of Christ and His inseparable, unconfused union with divinity (cf. DS 300–303).

The difference between the Greek and Christian *logos* was understood more clearly during the Arian crisis. For Arius, Christ, the incarnate Logos, is the first of creatures, which is why "there was a time when [the Logos] was not"266: the influence of Philo here appears evident. The Council of Nicaea, alongside Athanasius, rejected this position, emphasizing the condescension of God, which is revealed in creation and redemption, becoming man in Christ to save mankind. Arius's God was too intellectual, incapable of entering into contact with the material world267.

It became increasingly clear that in the Platonic vision there was an ontological continuum that proceeded from God through many mediations to matter, by means of a process of emanation. In such an outlook, the figure of Christ risked appearing as an intermediate being, neither God nor man. The idea of a demiurge is therefore utterly alien to the Christian vision. God creates matter directly, without instruments; He is the sole principle of all things. The ancients could not conceive that spirit and matter could touch in this way.

Augustine, in his confrontation with the religious philosophy of the Stoic Varro, and in continuity with the New Testament and the Apologist Fathers, maintained that Christianity had nothing to do with mythical theology or civil theology (the realms of myth or civil religion), but with natural theology, that is to say, the domain of philosophical rationality268.

Augustine could not completely set aside Platonic thought269. Creation participates in the ideas that have existed in God from all eternity, and this he calls creation according to eternal reasons (*rationes aeternae*). Each thing is what it is by virtue of the idea that God has of it270. Augustine attributes importance to ideas or exemplary causes, which are the immutable reasons of things or primary forms (the *logoi* of Greek philosophy) existing in the mind of the Creator. To conceive them, as Plato did, outside the divine mind would be sacrilege. The Platonic world of ideas is thus situated within the divine intellect. Nevertheless, the affirmation of the simultaneous creation of matter and form represents an overcoming of Platonism.

In order to maintain the balance between the initial creation and constant divine action in the world, Augustine teaches that although God created all things simultaneously, He created them not in themselves, but in their "seminal reasons" (*rationes seminales*)271. The idea carries Neoplatonic and Stoic resonances (*logoi spermatikoi*), but its content is original. Augustine conceives them as seeds implanted in things, containing virtually all the being that would unfold over time; they are prior to seeds themselves, quasi-seeds of seeds; that is, directing forces and laws from which proceed seeds and all the perfections deriving from them272. Nothing appears in the universe that was not virtually created at the beginning.

The Christian vision of a divine creative act steeped in rationality found various luminous expressions during the medieval period, most notably in Thomas Aquinas, for whom the Word, proceeding *per modum intellectus* from the Father, is the cause and reason of all that was made, its *ratio factiva*.

Commenting on the Johannine Prologue according to one possible reading (*quod factum est in ipso vita erat*), he affirms that just as in God His act of understanding (*intelligere*) is His life and His essence, so everything in God not only lives, but is life itself, because everything in God is His essence; "in God the creature is the creating essence" (*creatura in Deo est creatrix essentia*)273. One recognizes an intentional, intellectual pre-existence of things in God, like the house in the mind of the architect. The Word is the *raison d'être* of things, which pre-exist in Him from all eternity. "Through Him" is not understood as an instrumental cause (the demiurge) acting extrinsically alongside the efficient cause. God creates through wisdom and on account of wisdom; God does nothing except through His concept, His eternal Word.

If to this we join the idea of creation as participation, every creature becomes a *vox Verbi*274; this enables us to speak of a Revelation in creation, in relation to the doctrine of the seeds of the Word (enunciated by certain Church Fathers) and the analogy of being, and in general of the harmony between faith and reason, which causes Thomas to oppose in the most absolute manner the theory of double truth.

The doctrine of the creative Logos is a necessary complement to creation *ex nihilo*. Creation through the Logos enables us to understand that divine omnipotence is not a blind force, but is ordered by rationality. Beyond what God actually does, one can always conceive what He could do, because divine being cannot be reduced to His action. For this reason, there arose in the medieval period a complex distinction, used with prudence by Saint Thomas: that between God's absolute and ordained power (*potentia Dei absoluta et ordinata*)275. Without entering into the details of such a question, it should be stressed that the major figures associated with the nominalism that followed, specifically Scotus and Ockham, who made extensive use of the distinction, in any case upheld the fundamental principle that God always acts in an ordered manner276. The subsequent decline of nominalist thought introduced a rupture between omnipotence and rationality277 destined to grow and provoke further fractures that lie at the foundation of the modern process of secularisation.

### 3.2.3. The Modern and Contemporary Secularisation of Creative Reason: Between Rationalisms and Irrationalisms

After Aquinas's synthesis, the balance between faith and reason, though defended by the greatest nominalist thinkers, could no longer be sustained. An exaggerated emphasis upon divine omnipotence in creation and the governance of the world relegated to the background the fact that divine wisdom is participated to creatures. Creatures were now perceived in their radical contingency, stripped of intrinsic intelligibility: they stood in a relationship of absolute dependence upon the almighty God, who can do (and command to be done) whatever He pleases in the created world, with no rule other than His own will.

The doctrine of the analogy of being was replaced first by univocity and then by equivocity, leading to the conclusion that the only possible science is that of sensible reality. The fission between omnipotence and rationality in the conception of God gradually spilled over into the worldview born with modernity. While from the humus of British nominalism empiricism took root, on the European continent French rationalism and subsequently German idealism emerged.

Immanuel Kant sought a synthesis between the demands of rationalism and empiricism. "Without sensibility no object would be given to us, and without understanding no object would be thought. Thoughts without content are empty, intuitions without concepts are blind"278. In order for true knowledge to be possible (on the model of physical science, which with Newton had attained unprecedented advances), one must accept the existence of *a priori* forms of sensibility (space and time) and of the understanding (the categories, to which Kant adds the modal categories), which make possible those synthetic *a priori* judgments that ground the scientific character of our knowledge, beyond which the thing-in-itself (the noumenon) remains an unknown reality. Here in brief is Kantian transcendental idealism, which entails an agnosticism of reason regarding the existence of the Creator, who must then be postulated by practical reason (along with freedom and the immortality of the soul) as the guarantor of the moral order, even though He is incapable of intervening either in the course of events or in the free choices of men.

If Kant's transcendental idealism represents an advanced form of deism that curtailed God's creative influence, Hegelian absolute idealism moved in the direction of absorbing the reality of God into the process of the constitution of Spirit. Phenomena associated with the Enlightenment, such as the cult of the Goddess Reason or of the Supreme Being, constitute in some measure a kind of return to the Greek *logos*, whether in the version of Platonic dualism or in that of the Stoics' *theologia civilis*. We witness a progressive loss of the Christian doctrine of creation in the Logos. "The idealisms, by privileging everything that concerns the mind's access to things, beyond questions relating to the existence and intelligibility of things in themselves, inevitably place a robust doctrine of creation in parentheses"279.

While rationalism and idealism can be grouped under the heading of essentialism, because in some way they uphold the primacy of essence (idea, reason) over existence, which is treated merely as another characteristic of essence (one recalls Kant's example of the hundred thalers)280, there have also been assertions of the foundational role of existence in diverse forms of thought such as romanticism or existentialism, which accentuate existence, history, and tradition as values superior to pure rationality. Along this path, recent times have arrived at explicitly irrationalist currents, culminating in the aforementioned Nietzschean nihilism. Thus it is confirmed that the loss of creation *ex nihilo* is also the loss of creation *ex Verbo* (Logos). The contempt for reason coincides with the exaltation of the will, which Schopenhauer identifies with the Kantian noumenon or thing-in-itself, and Nietzsche characterises as the will to power. This is where the rupture between rationality and omnipotence, arising at the dawn of modernity, ultimately leads.

This irrationalism must reckon with the human desire to grasp the intelligibility of things, manifested, among other things, in scientific inquiry. Without detailing the various advances made by the natural sciences over recent centuries, one must recall the development and consolidation, in the twentieth century, of an evolutionary paradigm of thought that entered into dialogue with the philosophical and religious idea of creation. Examples of this dialogue can be found in the vitalist philosophy of Henri Bergson, who speaks of "creative evolution", as well as in the so-called "process philosophy (and theology)" inspired by the works of A. N. Whitehead281.

Joseph Ratzinger addressed evolution within the context of the dialogue between philosophy and Christian faith in creative Reason. Faith in creation does not consist in empirical assertions, but in the question of meaning: "the conviction that the world as a whole, as the Bible says, derives from the Logos, that is, from creative Reason"282. The domains are thus distinguished:

> Faith in creation seeks after the being of reality as such; its question is why there is something rather than nothing. The idea of evolution, by contrast, is concerned with why these particular things exist and not others, whence they received their determination, and in what relationship they stand to other configurations283.

If there is no distinction between physics and metaphysics—if physics sets itself up as metaphysics, as occurs in perspectives that turn the theory of evolution into an all-encompassing philosophy—it appears that matter is the principle and origin of the development of spirit. If this were true, the Creator and creation would vanish. Christian doctrine maintains that "spirit is not an accidental product of material developments, but rather that matter is a moment in the history of spirit"284.

Ratzinger affirms that Christian faith entailed from the outset the discovery of the separation between universal nature and the Being that grounds it, and therefore the distinction between physics and metaphysics285. This distinction was progressively abandoned, to the point where, in certain formulations, "the evolutionary theory crystallized as the way to make metaphysics disappear once and for all, to render the 'hypothesis of God' superfluous (cf. Laplace), and to formulate a strictly 'scientific' explanation of the world"286.

Confronted with this, the believer must ask whether "the evolutionary doctrine can present itself as a universal theory of all reality, beyond which further questions regarding the origin and nature of things are no longer legitimate or necessary"287. It is a matter of knowing whether reason and the rational stand at the beginning of all things as their foundation, or whether, on the contrary, at the base of reality lie chance and necessity (cf. Monod)288, or blind chance and foresight (cf. Popper and Butler)—and therefore that which is devoid of reason.

The question is whether "reason is an accidental byproduct of the irrational, ultimately insignificant in the ocean of the irrational, or whether that fundamental conviction of Christian faith and its philosophy remains true: *In principio erat Verbum*—at the beginning of all things stands the creative power of reason"289. On this question, the natural sciences and, at its own level, philosophical thought encounter their limits. Even if faith cannot be empirically demonstrated, the question remains: "Can reason really, without renouncing itself, renounce the priority of the rational over the irrational, of the Logos as first principle?"290. While the attempt to deduce the rational from irrationality patently founders, the Christian proposal of the coincidence between love and reason as the fundamental pillars of reality emerges with compelling force.

* * *

The journey through the history of philosophy has shown us how, paradoxically, a reason turned in upon itself, believing it can find within itself the answer to its deepest inquiries, ends by affirming the absurdity of existence and failing to recognize the intelligibility of the real (nihilism, irrationalism, etc.).

The discovery of the philosophical consequences of the truth of creation through the Word allows us to affirm that the Logos is found in God as creative Reason, and is simultaneously present in the world, which participates—especially in the intelligent creature—in the divine Logos. It is not the Logos that exists on account of the world, but the world that exists on account of the Logos; a world which therefore possesses a Christocentric structure, since the Logos became flesh. In virtue of the balance between divine transcendence and His immanence in creation, proper to the understanding of creation as participation in being, and in virtue of the Incarnation of the divine Logos in Christ, it is possible to recognize that the Logos is both transcendent and immanent, without opposition and without ontological confusion. Spirit does not proceed from matter; reason does not derive from the irrational; rather, at the origin of all things, of matter and of the spirit that emerges in the world, stands the creative Spirit, creative Reason.

This vision helps overcome the one-sidedness of essentialism, which presumes to find the reason for existence in essence, as well as the inadequacy of existentialism, which believes it can locate the cause of essence in existence. In the perspective of creation, the reason for both essence and existence is found in God the Creator. Creatures are not merely existences subjected to divine omnipotence, but also essences flowing from divine ideas. Yet this alone is not sufficient to characterise the Christian notion of creation: it is an act born of divine freedom, which out of love bestows essence and existence upon its creatures. It is precisely to this that we now turn.

### 3.3. The Freedom of the Creative Act and the Glory of God

In the Christian view, God is under no constraint to produce an intermediate being in order to bring order into chaos. Whereas in the Greek outlook the *logos* is contingent (its *raison d'être* being to order pre-existing matter) and the world necessary, in the Christian outlook the Logos is necessary (participating in the necessity of divine being) and the world is contingent. God requires no instruments to create: He is the sole and exclusive origin of the universe. The mediation of the Christian Logos is in no way comparable to that of the demiurge; the Logos is Creator insofar as He is in full possession of the divine nature (*homoousios*). Any creature, by the mere fact of having received being, cannot strictly speaking communicate it, although, as we shall see, it can cooperate through a participation in divine causality.

The creative act depends solely, immediately, and exclusively upon the omnipotence and wisdom of the Creator. It is an act that is also free and, consequently, the fruit of love; for love is the characteristic act of freedom, to be connected with gratuity rather than necessity. Having considered creation *ex nihilo* and creation *ex Verbo* (Logos), our reflection upon Christian Revelation reaches its summit in contemplating creation *ex amore*.

Christian reflection upon the Holy Spirit, the Love of the Father and the Son, took up certain characteristics that the term *pneuma* possessed in Greek thought, as a source of life, breath, respiration, wind291. Unlike the Logos, there was nothing in Greek philosophy comparable to the Holy Spirit, for the simple reason that while Aristotle intuited that we can love God, neither he nor Greek philosophy in general was in any way capable of grasping that God "first loved us" (1 John 4:19). Their God could be loved, but He could not love292. The created world is the fruit of the love of the Father toward the Son in the Holy Spirit. The Creator Spirit is love that bestows existence. "The Lord is good to all, and His compassion is over all that He has made" (Ps 145 [144]:9). The Scriptures attest in countless ways that God's fundamental disposition toward His creatures is love, which in the face of sin reveals itself as mercy293.

If God is Being and Love, in Christianity there was no primordial conflict; rather, all created being is good, because it proceeds from the God who is love and who is good. "We believe that the world proceeds from the free will of God [...]: 'For thou didst create all things, and by thy will they existed and were created' (Rev 4:11)" (CCC 295); "Because creation comes forth from God's goodness, it shares in that goodness [...]. For God willed creation as a gift" (CCC 299).

Care must be taken not to absorb being into love. While the perspective of creation as participation in being has enabled us to understand that there is a distinction between God and His creatures, and that God's transcendence is the foundation of His immanence, the perspective of creation as the fruit of love could blur this distinction. Revelation tells us that in the end God will be "all in all" (1 Cor 15:28), because love tends toward union with the beloved. Within the Trinity there exists a perfect union of love between the Persons, and the divine design is to make us partakers of that life and union. If to this we add the novelty of the Incarnation of the Word and His redemptive work through death on the Cross, it might seem that God has taken the permanence of His creation so seriously that He regards it on a par with His own life, as though it formed part of it.

This point, if not harmonised with what was said earlier, could lead—and has in fact led—to views that end by identifying God with creatures, and therefore considering the process of salvation history as a necessary emanation for the constitution of the divine being: that is, pantheism. One may ask: what has become of the divine creative freedom from which we began? With the precedent of Kant's transcendental idealism, the clearest example is Hegel's absolute idealism, which entails the denial of divine freedom, or rather, the identification of freedom and necessity in God regarding His external action (*ad extra*) as well as His inner being; both approaches influenced contemporary theologians such as G. Hermes and A. Günther294.

These theological attempts prompted the fathers of the First Vatican Council (1870) to take up once more the profession of faith in God the Creator formulated by Lateran IV (1215), adding the clause *liberrimo consilio* to underscore God's creative freedom:

> The one, only, true and living God, Creator and Lord of heaven and earth [...] really and essentially distinct from the world [...]. This sole true God, of His own goodness and "almighty power", not for the purpose of increasing His own beatitude, nor for the sake of acquiring it, but in order to manifest His perfection by means of the good things which He bestows upon creatures, by a most free decision, from the beginning of time created from nothing both orders of creatures295.

The idealist doctrines condemned by Vatican I were not far removed from the ancient Gnosticism combated by Irenaeus:

> It is proper to begin from the first and most important article, God the Creator, who made heaven and earth and all things that are therein [...] and to demonstrate that there is nothing either above Him or after Him: that He was moved by no one, but made all things by His own free decision, being the only God, the only Lord, the only Creator, the only Father, and the only One who contains all things and Himself grants to all things their existence296.

The question of existence, and therefore of the *why*, does not allow itself to be dissolved into the question of essence, of the *how* and *what* of creatures, especially when one experiences evil, notwithstanding the confession that at the origin of all things there is only good. How can the omnipotence and wisdom of the Creator be reconciled with His goodness, when we constantly encounter the presence of evil in the world? It is not enough to know that God accomplishes all things by His omnipotence, for this alone could be understood as arbitrariness. Nor does it suffice to know that all things proceed from wisdom, for this does not explain the disorder and evil we perceive in the world. Confronted with the experience of evil, it is not enough to investigate the origin of our existence, nor what we are; one must ask the *why* of existence, which is the metaphysical question *par excellence*.

### 3.3.1. Contingency, Evil, and the Freedom of the Creative Act in the History of Thought

The question of evil (its origin, scope, and effects) is intimately connected with the question of existence297. Experience teaches us that when everything is as it ought to be, we do not ask momentous questions, but simply carry on. When a negative experience is undergone, when things do not happen as they should, questions arise: "Why did this happen? Why did it happen to me, and right at this moment?" This is the experience of the privation of a good considered due—a privation perhaps felt even more acutely by the Christian. If on the one hand the Christian is grateful to God for the good, on the other, when confronted with the experience of evil, he questions the reason *why*, perhaps with greater urgency than others: "Why evil, if everything was created by God and is good, and there is no eternal principle upon which to lay the blame for the evil occurring in the world? What place does negativity hold in the process of history and in creation?"

The answers provided to this problem in modern and contemporary philosophy are not encouraging. Kant stated that the existence of evil is in some way functional to the progress of humanity, even while considering it a question insoluble by reason without the aid of religion298. For Hegel, negativity plays a crucial role, belonging to the very process of the constitution of the Absolute. Schopenhauer observes that the law of the strongest governs the world, and that the will to survive entails destruction299. In the Darwinian outlook, the law of evolution is the survival of the fittest. Expressed by Camus in an atheistic existentialist key, this perspective leads to recognizing that existence is absurd300; or upholding, with the consistency of Sartre, that our destiny is destruction, and that man is a useless passion301. Heidegger would say that man is a being thrown into existence, a being-towards-death302.

These answers are permeated with pessimism and negativity, yet their underlying premise implies a Christian presupposition: the world is contingent, not necessary, the fruit of a free divine creative act. Inasmuch as they are contingent, the world and man are subject to the power of negativity and non-being from which they were drawn. Creation is not the Creator; it carries with it limit and privation, lest it be confused with Him. The contingency of creatures is the other side of the coin of the creative act of God who acts freely.

This perspective was not present in Greek philosophy. The eternity of matter, co-originary with divinity, implied that the world was necessary. Aristotle thought that the laws of nature were as necessary and immutable as God Himself303. The formation of the world does not result from a free act of God. Christian faith in creation introduced a new dimension: by emphasizing the absolute transcendence and alterity of God, it stripped the world of necessity. While the world needs the Creator in order to exist, given its contingency, God, who is Necessary Being, has no need of the world.

Thomas Aquinas affirmed that the universe does not possess intrinsic necessity (as God does), rejecting the Averroistic interpretation of Aristotle according to which the necessity of natural laws coincides with the necessity of God. This would be clarified in the question of the eternity of the world. Thomas distinguishes between necessity, which belongs to God alone, and eternity: it is conceptually possible to think of a creation accomplished by God *ab aeterno* (even though through revelation we know that it took place in time). The world is contingent, metaphysically speaking, because it is the fruit of a free divine act.

The Ockhamist insistence upon divine freedom in creation, in opposition to Greek-Arabic necessitarianism, would lead to an extreme conception of freedom in terms of arbitrariness, and to an absolute contingency of things (radical contingentism)304. Natural laws would no longer be a participation in divine reason, but mere extrinsic impositions of divine omnipotence. In the most radical nominalism, this culminated in ethical voluntarism, where good and evil are not inherent in actions, but depend upon the divine decree that sanctions the goodness or malice of a given behaviour305.

Leibniz's rationalism considers that essences possess reality in God. Creation would be the work of the divine will which, in virtue of metaphysical optimism, chooses to bring into existence the best of all possible worlds306. Perfection lies not on the side of existing being (a mere extrinsic outcome), but on that of essence (fundamentally eternal and uncreated); this implies that the creative act itself must submit to a principle or reason of convenience, and create the best of all possible worlds. It is difficult to see how divine creative freedom can still be maintained in such a framework.

From the late Middle Ages onward, reflection upon creation seemed forced to choose between two opposing directions: the path of rationality, which declares the necessity of the world; or the path of arbitrariness, which declares the radical contingency of the world. If creation is logical, it is not free; if it is free, it is not logical. We must ask: can this dilemma be overcome? Is there room for creation as a divine action that is free without being arbitrary, rational without being necessary?

### 3.3.2. Harmony Between Truth and Freedom, Reason and Love

In modern thought, the conception of divine freedom as arbitrariness is linked to the conception of human freedom as indifference. It is thought that the hallmark of freedom is independence from rules and reason. This is an idea of freedom conceived as opposed to whatever, being established, might determine or constrain it. In this outlook, to follow reason would be a symptom of a lack of freedom. Action should attain indifference, independence from everything, in order to enjoy a boundless freedom that keeps all possibilities open; in short, a creative freedom akin to divine freedom.

The consequences of separating rationality and freedom are felt powerfully in Kantian philosophy. Freedom is a postulate of practical reason, disconnected from theoretical reason, which deals with truth as certain and scientific knowledge in the phenomenal world. Freedom consists in the good will of the agent who acts on the conviction that the principle guiding his action should be capable of being elevated to a universal law (the categorical imperative). In this theory, which has the merit of underscoring the dignity of every human person, freedom—the good of free action—is severed from any truth-content that precedes the action. It is a purely formal ethics, which rejects any principle external to the subject in favour of the decision of good will alone. This approach underpins contemporary views of freedom when it is asserted, for example, that a choice is right if made without coercion, without reference to a law, a *logos*, a truth that in some way precedes it. The subject becomes the court of final appeal for morality.

Without discounting the positive elements proposed in this vision, which partly coincide with classical reflection on conscience, here "we witness the sacrifice of truth upon the altar of freedom"307. The teleology of action has been lost, collapsing into the subject's self-referentiality as the foundation of freedom. An act is thought to be free if performed without reasons originating from outside. Yet experience teaches us that every action has a motivation, as expressed in the Aristotelian axiom *omne agens agit propter finem*. The end has the character of a cause, but also of a good, and is therefore what is pursued by the action; last in execution, it is first in intention, and this constitutes the tension proper to free action.

In the Kantian perspective, it is difficult to speak of an improvement or growth of the subject. Whoever does good does so because he possesses a good will, free from coercion, and thus diffuses good around him; but his action does not make him good. It is presupposed that the subject has already attained perfect freedom and that, like the law, the truth of his acting resides within him, making him a quasi-divine subject. If this path is pursued to its ultimate consequences, one arrives at the idea that freedom makes the person true308, in contrast to Jesus' affirmation: "You will know the truth, and the truth will make you free" (John 8:32). "Modern nihilism, on the contrary, preaches the opposite: namely, that it is freedom that makes you true. Some even maintain that no truth exists, thereby opening the way to stripping the concepts of good and evil of all substance and rendering them entirely interchangeable"309.

This conception of human freedom—which tolerates no constraints, demands to be absolute (that is, unconditioned), and seeks to create its own nature and truth—is an attempt to elevate human freedom to the status of divine freedom; but in doing so, it only disfigures the genuine notion of freedom, not only human, but also divine.

God creates without constraint; there is nothing outside Him that obliges Him to create. Yet this does not mean that divine freedom is arbitrary, as the heirs of nominalism claim. The creative act is mighty, wise, and free. "The absolute, yet sweet and gentle power of the Lord responds to the depths of man, to his highest aspirations of intellect, will, and heart. It does not speak with a language of force, but expresses itself in charity and truth"310. In God, reason and freedom, truth and love coincide, as they do in His creative action; consequently, the authentic Christian idea of creation is not forced to choose between a free but illogical creation, or a logical but unfree creation. The Father creates all things through the Son-Logos in the Holy Spirit-Love, and therefore the free creative act pursues an end that cannot be other than what God is: omnipotent, wise, and loving.

> God, who was alone and had nothing co-existing with Him, willed to create the world. He conceived it, willed it, and by His word made it, and forthwith what was made stood present to Him as He willed, having accomplished it as He willed. For us it suffices to know only that nothing co-existed with God except Himself. But He, though being alone, was manifold; for He was not lacking in word, nor wisdom, nor power, nor counsel. All things were in Him, and He was the all311.

An important aspect of the novelty of Christianity consisted in orienting religion toward a rational vision of reality, which included the *ethos* and its concrete application under the primacy of love. "The primacy of the Logos and the primacy of love proved to be identical. The Logos no longer appeared merely as a mathematical reason at the basis of all things, but as creative love, even to the point of becoming compassion toward the creature"312.

The Christian affirmation of creative freedom enables us to overcome the narrowness of visions that, by placing necessity in God, end by upholding fatalism or determinism; or that, by untying reason and freedom from one another, conceive free action as indeterminate and without an end. Now we must ask: if there is nothing, either "within" or "outside" God, that obliges Him to create, can one speak of an end that moves Him in His action? What was His purpose in creating? How does the end of God's action relate to the end pursued by creatures?

### 3.3.3. The Glory of God, the End of Creation

The First Vatican Council points out that God created "not for the purpose of increasing His own beatitude, nor for the sake of acquiring it, but in order to manifest His perfection by means of the good things which He bestows upon creatures"313.

The question could lead to an impasse: if, to safeguard God's greatness and freedom, we say that there is nothing outside or above Him that moves Him, and that the creative act depends solely upon Him, it would seem that He created everything having Himself as end; we would thus be confronted with a God who desires everyone to adore and contemplate His perfection, His glory. Out of love for whom did God create? Out of love for Himself, or out of love for creatures? Did God create all things for Himself, or for man?

Calvin maintained that the world, "created as a manifestation of the glory of God"314, has as its end to be the theatre of His glory315. Man is, rather than a son, a servant of this God, and must not presume to enjoy goods, but must annihilate himself so as not to detract from God's glory. This perspective tends toward the first answer: God created everything to manifest His glory, and the end of creatures consists in seeking solely the glory of God. An heir to this approach in the twentieth century was Karl Barth, who retrieved the expression *soli Deo gloria*—to God alone the glory316. Man would have to renounce his own happiness in order to adhere to and seek in all things the glory of God.

This theocentric vision is characteristic of Protestantism, which places great weight upon the degraded condition of human nature after sin. Within the Reformation, forms of thought also developed that, in the dialectic between God and man, emphasized the opposite aspect: the autonomy of the subject and his decisions with respect to any moral authority. The Kantian good will admits no external principle; its criterion is self-grounded. Here it seems that everything was made for man and the power of his decision. The dilemma remains: does everything have God as its end, or man? And does man's happiness come from the unconstrained exercise of free will?

This dilemma is the fruit of the conviction that, in order to exalt God, man must be annihilated; or conversely, that to exalt man means rejecting God. This is the *aut-aut*, a dialectical mode of thought that opposes God and man, characteristic of certain philosophical currents of modernity. Once the balance between faith and reason, between grace and nature, has been lost, thought oscillates between the exaltation of an omnipotent God whose will must be followed absolutely, and a reaction toward the rejection of this God in favour of the glorification of man and his freedom, brooking no limitations or dependencies.

The Bible conceives the glory of God (*kābôd* in Hebrew, *doxa* in Greek)317 as His self-manifestation in splendour and goodness. It can be recognized in creation (cf. Ps 19 [18]:1; Isa 6:3), in salvation history (cf. Exod 14:17–18), and in liturgical worship, through His presence in the sanctuary (cf. Exod 40:34–35; 1 Kgs 8:10–11; Ps 26 [25]:8). At the end of time, an appearance of the *kābôd* is awaited to save Israel (cf. Isa 60:1–2; Ezek 39:21–22) and the nations (cf. Ps 96 [95]:3; Zech 2:9–10). Glory is the exclusive property of God, though in certain cases angels seem to share in similar attributes.

In the New Testament, reference to the glory of God is frequent, in continuity with the Old Testament (cf. Matt 16:27; Acts 7:55; Rom 1:23; 6:4; Eph 3:16; 1 Tim 1:11; Rev 15:8). The concept is applied both to the earthly Jesus (cf. Luke 9:32; John 1:14; 2:11; 1 Cor 2:8) and to the exalted Lord (cf. Luke 24:26; John 17:5; Rom 8:17; Phil 3:21; 2 Thess 2:14; 1 Tim 3:16), or to the pre-existent Son (cf. John 12:24; 17:5). In the New Testament as well, glory extends to angelic beings.

Here one moves far beyond the Greek *doxa* understood as reputation or renown: one does not seek the applause of men, but the glory of God, which is not mere outward appearance, but a property of His being that reveals itself outwardly. The New Testament concept includes the manifestation of God's power and salvation in salvation history, above all in Christ and His redemptive work (cf. Matt 17:2–3; John 1:14; 2:11; 2 Cor 4:4, 6), but it also entails a significant eschatological amplification with respect to the Old Testament *kābôd*. Redemption consists in the fact that man and creation will share in this mode of being. The glory of God is already present with its transformative power in the believer, in virtue of the Resurrection of Christ and communion with Him, the "first fruits of those who have fallen asleep" (1 Cor 15:20).

From these biblical data it emerges that God's plan is to make creatures share in His being, His goodness, and His glory: conformation to the Son, the Image of the Father, through the Spirit. If this is the end of the creative work, it is evident that, on the part of the creature, giving glory to God consists in acknowledging Him as Creator, the source of being and life. This is accomplished in irrational creatures, which manifest the glory of God objectively, while the human being is called to do so formally, becoming the conscious voice of praise of creatures to the Creator. Man is called to recognize God not only as Creator, but also as Father, being destined to be conformed to the Son, Jesus Christ, the perfect Image of the Father, through the work of the Holy Spirit. Precisely in this consists man's finality, his beatitude.

Irenaeus expressed this in celebrated words: *Gloria Dei vivens homo, vita hominis visio Dei*—"the glory of God is man fully alive, and the life of man is the vision of God"318. Upon this insight is founded the theological tradition that speaks of a natural desire for the vision of God in man (*desiderium naturale videndi Deum*)319. Glory thus reveals itself as a protological-anthropological, Christological, and eschatological term. The various stages of salvation history are interwoven. Hence the axiom regarding the relationship between nature and grace (*gratia supponit naturam*) can be expanded to include glory: *gratia praeparat naturam ad gloriam aeternam*320.

That the glory of God and the happiness of man coincide in reality means that the relationship between God and man is not to be seen in terms of rivalry, but of communion. "God is the origin of our being and the foundation and culmination of our freedom, not its opponent"321, as Mary manifests, who "is not afraid that God may be a 'competitor' in our life, that He could take away something of our freedom, of our vital space by His greatness. She knows that if God is great, we too are great"322.

The foundation of this assertion is Christological: the union of Creator and creature in the Person of the incarnate Word, which shows how communion with God brings man to his true fulfilment, since he was made in Him, through Him, and for Him (cf. Col 1:15–17). If God created everything for Christ, who is God and man, the question of the apparent opposition between the glory of God and human happiness finds its Christological resolution. "The world was created for man, but not for just any man—who could have competitors—but created for the man Jesus Christ, for Him, through Him, and in Him"323. This implies a dynamic conception of the world and history, journeying toward the Omega point of union with God in Jesus Christ.

> If from the concrete history of salvation we say: God created the world in view of Christ, then the two seemingly contrary positions spoken of at the beginning meet. If this is so, it means after all that He created it in view of the man who is at the same time eternal God from eternal God. If God created the world in view of Christ, then He created the world truly for Himself; and then He created truly for creatures; then Christ is true man as well as true God; then He created it in view of the creature who is to become a partaker of God. Thus it will also become visible that the happiness of the creature and the glory of God are not two individual ends that are conjoined, but are complementary aspects of a single total reality324.

The reconciliation of both perspectives was present in Saint Thomas when he wrote that "God does not seek His own praise for His own sake, but for our benefit"325. In this last affirmation we can discern a connection with the classical question of the motives of the Incarnation. Creation is made simultaneously for God (to manifest His glory) and for us (to give glory to God), because it is made for Christ. In the divine design, the work of creation is ordered toward the Incarnation of the Word, without there being a necessary link between the two. The Incarnation is accomplished for God (to manifest His redemptive mercy) and for us (to liberate from sin and restore divine filiation). The various theological positions are Christocentric, whether one emphasizes the link between creation and the Incarnation, or stresses the bond between the Incarnation and redemption326. Irenaeus underscores the unity of the design of creation and Incarnation, but also affirms the redemptive purpose of the actual Incarnation. Thomas highlights the actual redemptive finality of the Incarnation of the Word, but also speaks of creation's final orientation toward Christ327.

That the Christocentric dimension helps overcome the dilemma between theocentrism and anthropocentrism means that the formulation of the glory of God as the end of creation must be inclusive. Balthasar suggests understanding the expression *soli Deo gloria* in the broader sense of the Ignatian motto *ad maiorem Dei gloriam*. While in the first case creatures seem to be excluded (*soli*), in the second, without detracting anything from the glory of God, it is signified that creatures can associate themselves with it (*ad maiorem*)328.

Following the Eucharistic doxology, Saint Josemaría used the expression *Deo omnis gloria* in a Christocentric sense: "‘*Deo omnis gloria*.’ — All glory to God. — It is an emphatic confession of our nothingness. He, Jesus, is everything. We, without Him, are worth nothing: nothing. Our vainglory would be just that: vain glory; it would be sacrilegious theft; the ‘I’ must not appear anywhere"329.

This "I" is to be understood in the sense of "the fundamental law of human existence: 'He who loves his life loses it, and he who hates his life in this world will keep it for eternal life' (John 12:25)"330. We stand before the mystery of the call to live existence as self-giving, as an exodus from self toward God and toward others, avoiding the inward curving of the self proper to sin. It is, ultimately, not the annihilation of the person—who as such is good because blessed by God in creation—but the affirmation of the glory of God revealed in creatures, who freely offer Him praise by opening themselves to Him and to others and renouncing self-enclosure. The divine blessing upon the creature calls forth the creature's corresponding response in blessing God: that is, glorifying Him, rendering Him praise in thanksgiving for the gifts received.
