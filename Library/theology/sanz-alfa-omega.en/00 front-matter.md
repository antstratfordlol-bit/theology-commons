# Presentation

At the very outset of this presentation, I wish to express my gratitude to the Author of the present manual, Fr Santiago Sanz Sánchez, Associate Professor of Dogmatic Theology at the Pontifical University of the Holy Cross in Rome. This gratitude is due for at least three reasons. The first is for having welcomed with enthusiasm the proposal I made to him some years ago to write this manual of protology and eschatology—an undertaking which, for various reasons, is less straightforward than one might imagine. Secondly, I thank him for having produced a manual that respects the principal hallmarks of the *Ecclesial Theology* (*Teologia Ecclesiale*) series to which this volume belongs: a concise length, scholarly scientific rigour in its treatment, an up-to-date bibliography, and a level of exposition well suited to the primary addressees of the series, namely students in the first cycle of theological studies [the STB / baccalaureate level]. The third reason for which I feel moved to express my gratitude to Professor Sanz is that, by means of this manual, he directs our attention to Trinitarian Christocentrism and, even more, to the fact that, although we live on this earth, even «midway upon the journey of our life» we must never avert our gaze from our roots and our ultimate destiny. On these two aspects, which constitute the third reason for my gratitude, I intend to add some further observations below.

First of all, Trinitarian Christocentrism. I find myself in profound agreement with the general approach of this treatise, because Fr Sanz expounds the entire protological and eschatological tract on the basis of the centrality of Christ, the incarnate Word. Without entering here into the classical dispute concerning the primary motive of the Incarnation,[^1] it is an established fact that, concretely—that is to say, in the one actual economy of salvation that has taken place, and not in others that might have been possible—the Word was made flesh for our redemption, and that, consequently, the whole history of salvation revolves around this pivotal hinge: the Incarnation of Him who is *Unus de Trinitate*.[^2] Both aspects must be maintained: both Christocentrism and its Trinitarian 'grounding'. For the Trinitarian perspective is the only one that enables us to grasp the true identity of Jesus, that is, His Person. A Christocentrism that is not Trinitarian is condemned to run aground on the shoals of mere 'Jesuan' studies, which are far removed from authentic Christology.

On the other hand, an absolute theocentrism that is not constantly correlated with the historical-salvific reality determined by the Incarnation would lose sight of the fundamental truth that—to put it in the words of Pope Gregory XVI—«it can never happen that without God we come to know God».[^3] In other words, we know the divine Trinity because God the Trinity has revealed Himself historically, and this revelation reached its culmination and insurmountable perfection in the mystery of the incarnate Word. Within the present economy of salvation, an absolute theocentrism, in which Christ were to represent merely an optional appendix, constitutes an attempt to know God (Trinity) without God (the incarnate Word); that is, to acknowledge the centrality of God without upholding the centrality of the concrete manner in which God has made Himself manifest to us in His otherwise inaccessible mystery. Professor Sanz remains far removed from such an error, presenting the mystery of creation and that of the final consummation in the light of the centrality of Christ, the incarnate Word. Moreover, just as he avoids the shoals of a non-Christic theocentrism, so too he steers clear of the misunderstanding of a Christomonist approach, such as that proposed by, among others, Karl Barth or Pierre Teilhard de Chardin.

With this, another element of Trinitarian Christocentrism has already emerged, one likewise taken up by the Author in the present treatise: the synthetic principle. It is a matter for satisfaction that the protology and eschatology studied here make use of this principle, which I personally have sought to bring back to attention, making it, so to speak, the architectonic principle of my *Dogmatics*.[^4] By the expression 'synthetic principle' is meant that which is classically termed *et... et*, or indeed—as Leo Scheffczyk puts it—«the Catholic *et*».[^5] Reference to this 'both/and', so typical of Catholic faith and theology, arose when it was observed that the correct method of approaching theological questions must be both Christocentric and Trinitarian. For the *et... et* is realised primarily and originally in Christ Himself, who is both God and man, and is, in this sense, the synthetic Principle in Person. Precisely because He is both the Second Person of the Trinity and true man, fundamental theological methodology must embrace both the Trinitarian and the Christological dimensions. From this foundational *et... et* there derive, in accordance with the *analogia fidei* or *nexus mysteriorum*, all the other countless instances of *et... et* found throughout Catholic doctrine.

Professor Sanz highlights some of the principal instances of *et... et* present in protology and eschatology. He does so, for example, when, following Saint Thomas, he emphasises the harmony of faith and reason, of grace and nature, of resurrection and immortality; or when he notes that God is the Creator of matter as well as spirit; or where he points out that the glory of God and the freedom of man are not in opposition.

The second aspect mentioned above, upon which it is appropriate to dwell briefly, concerns the fact that this book helps us never to avert our eyes from our roots and our ultimate destiny. In this sense, Fr Sanz's treatment demonstrates its timeliness. Indeed, the current existential outlook appears marked by an intense flattening onto earthly life and the present moment, whilst those social and religious forces that seek to keep our generation vigilant regarding its remote origins and its ultimate destiny seem increasingly to fade away. Not only the social movements of our time, but frequently even religions present themselves to humanity as agencies of humanisation, rather than of the divinisation of the human being. One remains flattened along the horizontal axis and confined to the fleeting moment, in a new, almost bizarre declension of the ancient *carpe diem*. Yet a religion that confines its mission to the immanent has thereby declared its own redundancy as a religion. Nothing remains for it but to be swallowed up into the churning vortex of earthly causes of social promotion, unable any longer to speak a word or provide an example that is alternative, prophetic, and critical, albeit in a constructive perspective. A religion, and Christianity in particular, has a different and higher calling. This manual helps us, whilst respecting its own literary genre, to remember this: that the beginning and end of humanity certainly have to do with the past and the future, but also with the present—and this by means of faith and the reception of Christ's grace, in the Church and through the Church. The believer must bear witness, in the midst of the world, to this supernatural vision that enables him to confront the present life with a gaze fixed upon eternity.

With regard to this witness-bearing character, proper to true religion, it will be useful to recall the Old Testament narrative concerning that pious mother persecuted by the king together with her seven sons on account of their fidelity to the Law. That great Jewish woman was neither shaken by the terrible fate inflicted upon her offspring, nor intimidated by the king's threats; and she exhorted her sons to face martyrdom with these words: «I do not know how you came into being in my womb. It was not I who gave you life and breath, nor I who set in order the elements within each of you. Therefore the Creator of the world, who shaped the beginning of man and devised the origin of all things, will in his mercy give life and breath back to you again, since you now forget yourselves for the sake of his laws» (2 Macc 7:22–23). And when the first six sons had already been slain, she exhorted the last, saying: «I beseech you, my child, to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them, and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed. Thus also mankind comes into being. Do not fear this butcher, but prove worthy of your brothers. Accept death, so that in God's mercy I may get you back again with your brothers» (vv. 28–29). In both exhortations, the mother refers both to the origin of all life in God (creation) and to the ultimate destiny of every human being in Him (consummation). It is an exemplary passage, in which the present is not devalued by looking to the past and the future, but quite the reverse: precisely by remembering his origins and his ultimate end, the human being is able to face the present in the right manner, namely by living the moment with a vision of eternity. The reply that the seventh martyred son gave to the king was worthy of such a mother. The young man said: *Non oboedio praecepto regis, sed obtempero praecepto legis* (Nova Vulgata). Here is his answer, cited more fully:

> «I do not obey the king's command, but I obey the command of the law that was given to our fathers through Moses. But you, who have contrived all sorts of evil against the Hebrews, will certainly not escape the hands of God. For we are suffering because of our own sins. And if our living Lord is angry for a little while, to rebuke and discipline us, he will again be reconciled with his own servants. But you, unholy wretch, you most defiled of all men, do not be elated in vain and puffed up by uncertain hopes, when you raise your hand against the children of heaven. You have not yet escaped the judgment of the almighty, all-seeing God. For our brothers after enduring a brief suffering have drunk of ever-flowing life under God's covenant; but you, by the judgment of God, will receive just punishment for your arrogance» (vv. 30–36).

An eschatologically oriented faith entails not only a constant readiness for suffering and, should it be required, even martyrdom, but also the awareness that, alongside divine mercy, there exists divine justice, which metes out equitable retribution. The martyr, who accepts the loss of earthly life in order to attain eternal life, will receive a judgment of salvation, whereas the wicked man, who lords it over others and often prevails here on earth, will receive a sentence of condemnation unto the second death. That this does not represent (as the Marcionites of various eras would have it) an Old Testament retributive vindictiveness superseded by the image of the merciful God preached by Jesus is proved by the words of the Redeemer Himself:

> «When the Son of man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on his glorious throne. Before him will be gathered all the nations, and he will separate them one from another as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats, and he will place the sheep at his right hand, but the goats at the left. [...] Then he will say to those at his left hand, 'Depart from me, you cursed, into the eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels' [...] And they will go away into eternal punishment, but the righteous into eternal life» (Mt 25:31–32, 41, 46).

In the face of an age such as our own, in which the only fear many people experience is that arising from a terror-inducing eschatologism—couched in ecological, pacifist, epidemiological, or cosmological terms—it is necessary to return to cultivating the healthy and holy fear of God; that fear which is neither terror nor anguish, but an awareness that all of us will ultimately be judged by the just and merciful Judge. It will benefit all, but particularly ordained ministers who are called to exercise the ministry of preaching and spiritual direction, to re-read and meditate upon this recent magisterial text, which felicitously unites the *et... et* of justice and mercy, of holy fear and hope:

> «A protest against God in the name of justice is not helpful. A world without God is a world without hope (cf. Eph 2:12). Only God can create justice. And faith gives us the certainty: he does so. The image of the Last Judgement is not primarily an image of terror, but an image of hope; for us it may even be the decisive image of hope. Is it not also an image of fear, however? I would say: it is an image of that fear which calls upon responsibility. An image, then, of that fear of which Saint Hilary says that all our fear has its place in love. God is justice and he creates justice. This is our consolation and our hope. But in his justice there is also grace. This we know by turning our gaze to the crucified and risen Christ. Both—justice and grace—must be seen in their correct inner connection. Grace does not exclude justice. It does not make wrong into right. It is not a sponge which wipes everything away, so that whatever someone has done on earth ends up being of equal value. Dostoevsky was right to protest against this kind of heaven and this kind of grace in his novel *The Brothers Karamazov*. Evildoers, in the end, will not sit at table at the eternal banquet indistinguishably beside their victims, as though nothing had happened».[^6]

The custom has become widespread of remaining entirely silent, in preaching, catechesis, and spiritual direction, regarding 'negative' eschatological truths or those that 'might cause alarm'. While one must certainly avoid styles of preaching *à la* Dante Alighieri—that is, excessively minute and luridly descriptive—glossing over entirely the possibility (which remains ever real) of eternal damnation is assuredly not healthy at the pastoral level, besides failing to respect the integrity of the content of Revelation. Even if the intentions may be good—in the sense that whoever acts in this manner perhaps does so to offer grounds for hope rather than despair, for consolation rather than affliction—it is well known that the end does not justify the means, and that a good intention does not automatically make an action good. If, for the purpose of uplifting souls, one were to deny or conceal revealed dogma, this would certainly not represent sound pastoral method. Here too, there is need of an *et... et*: we must help people to live in hope, without falling silent about or disowning the truths of the faith. It is possible to do so today, just as in the past. It is possible, and it is necessary.

This book by Professor Sanz represents a valuable aid in preparing for such an apostolic and pastoral style, offering theological students a treatment of protology and eschatology that is clear, honest in its content (presented from a synchronic, that is thematic, perspective), traditional (drawing upon the Fathers and Saint Thomas), yet at the same time thoroughly up to date, thanks to copious citations of very recent authors such as Pannenberg and Ratzinger. We wish our readers every profit from the study of the present volume, so that, by thoroughly assimilating sound theology, they may also be equipped to disseminate in the right manner the truths of our faith concerning the origin and the consummation of the present age.

# General Introduction

Our title evokes the words of the Apocalypse: «I am the Alpha and the Omega» (Rev 1:8; 21:6; 22:13). The subject of this declaration appears in the preceding words: «says the Lord God», and in those that follow: «who is and who was and who is to come, the Almighty!» (Rev 1:8). The application to God of the first and the last letters of the Greek alphabet, the original language of the text, signifies—as other occurrences of the phrase indicate—that He is «the Beginning and the End» (Rev 21:6; 22:13), indeed, «the First and the Last» (Rev 22:13; cf. Is 44:6; 48:12). In this way, the being of God is designated not only in an absolute sense, but also in His relation to reality: with Him, the Almighty, all things begin and all things will end.

God has not remained enclosed within His eternal existence («who is and who was» [Rev 1:8]), but is «[he] who is to come» (Rev 1:8), revealing Himself at the beginning, at the end, and throughout history. The subject of the last two occurrences («he who sat upon the throne» [Rev 21:5]) is the Lamb, Jesus Christ Crucified and Risen, who is «the same yesterday and today and for ever» (Heb 13:8). From the very beginning the Church, in the writings of the Fathers, in poetic compositions, and in artistic monuments, has alluded to Christ as the Alpha and the Omega.[^7]

In these words is condensed the content of a manual that seeks to address the first things (protology) and the last things (eschatology). Here too is indicated the fundamental principle that must guide it: Trinitarian Christocentrism.[^8] Christ, the Word of the Father, together with the Spirit, created all things; and as Creator He can draw near to His creatures in order to guide them—inasmuch as He is incarnate, died, and risen—to eschatological fullness in the Kingdom. In the cross one may discover a third letter (M) together with the other two (A and Ω), signifying that the beginning, the middle, and the end of all things are embraced by Him.[^9] The expression in the Apocalypse may be regarded as the Hellenised form of a rabbinic saying, according to which the seal of God is the word *emet* ('truth'), which is formed from the first letter, the middle letter, and the final letter of the Hebrew alphabet. God is «the beginning, the middle, and the end of all things».[^10] Christ is at the centre of the Father's design to bring us into eternal communion with Himself in the Spirit.

It is customary to refer to the three fundamental questions of human existence: who am I, whence do I come, and whither am I going? These are metaphysical questions concerning our origin, our destiny, and our identity. Ancient myths and modern scientific approaches seek answers to them, colliding with the limits of human explanatory capacities when confronted with mystery.[^11] Christian Revelation tells us that we originate from the creative act of God, that we are journeying towards definitive communion with Him, and that our human identity receives decisive illumination from Jesus Christ, «the last Adam» (1 Cor 15:45), who «fully reveals man to himself and makes his supreme calling clear» (*Gaudium et Spes*, 22).

The light radiating from Christ enables us to understand a paramount idea of these pages. One tends to think that creation concerns the past, whereas the future pertains to eschatology. In reality, neither is creation an event belonging exclusively to the past (even though it unquestionably implies a beginning), nor is eschatology merely a matter of the future (even though it speaks of the end). Both coincide in Christ, the mediator of creation and redemption. In His Paschal Mystery the new creation has already begun for us (cf. 2 Cor 5:17; Gal 6:15; Rom 8:19–24), as has eternal life, and therefore eschatology, which is not solely a future reality but also a present one: «he who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day» (Jn 6:54). With the Resurrection of Christ in the fullness of time, the Father's plan to recapitulate all things is accomplished (cf. Eph 1:10). This new creation does not annul the first, but brings it to fulfillment, inasmuch as in Sacred Scripture creation is not merely the initial work of God but a permanent condition, for «my Father is working still, and I am working» (Jn 5:17), not only at the beginning, but also throughout history and at its consummation.

Protology illuminates eschatology, eschatology illuminates protology, and both receive light from the mystery of Christ. Creation is the foundation of redemption and of the final consummation, because both the Incarnation and the Resurrection presuppose the existence of a created reality that is both material and spiritual. The Incarnation and the Resurrection constitute the fullness of creation, the goal of the divine plan. A classical axiom states that grace not only presupposes nature, but also elevates it and brings it to perfection.[^12] It belongs intrinsically to the Christian faith to uphold both the unity and the distinction of the various dimensions of the Trinitarian design in Christ: God created a nature, elevated to grace in history, and destined for glory throughout eternity.[^13]

The fundamental existential questions to which we have referred give voice to philosophical enquiry, with which theological reflection maintains a close bond. Leibniz's question: «Why is there something rather than nothing?»,[^14] or the similar inquiry posed by Heidegger: «Why are there beings at all instead of nothing?»,[^15] together with Kant's questions: «What can I know? What ought I to do? What may I hope?», in short, «What is man?»,[^16] are provocations that theology, illuminated by the light radiating from Christ, must endeavour to answer. We are of the opinion that only where metaphysics, the history of salvation, and eschatology coexist harmoniously is the principle of concord respected between faith and reason, between grace and nature, between God the Creator and man the creature. In the course of these pages we hope to demonstrate that, in the face of the oscillations of a pseudo-pantheistic Gnosticism and a pseudo-scientific deism, Christianity gives an adequate account of the real dualities of the world and of man; in other words, Christianity acknowledges the historical-existential dualism of human life without lapsing into an ontological dualism. The adoption of the synthetic principle (*et... et*) avoids the false dichotomy (*aut... aut*) that leads alike to dualism and to monism.[^17]

Protological and eschatological discourse must recognise its intrinsic limitations from the outset. The apophaticism required in all discourse concerning God[^18] applies pre-eminently to the questions of the beginning and the end, which are so close to the mystery of God as to be more unknown to us than known. Qoheleth tells us that men cannot «find out what God has done from the beginning to the end» (Eccles 3:11). The more the empirical sciences advance, the more questions find an answer, yet other inquiries, perhaps of greater moment, arise and remain unresolved.[^19] The human capacity to know reality, though potentially infinite, reveals that the more we know, the more we know that we do not know.[^20] For this reason, protology and eschatology must be sober and restrained in their speech.

This is not merely the acknowledgement of a limit, but also the discovery of an aspect to be appreciated. Protology and eschatology possess a pronounced prophetic and symbolic dimension, as is made evident by the language of the sacred writings—above all Genesis and the Apocalypse—concerning the first things (prophecies of the past) and the last things (prophecies of the future).[^21] This does not mean renouncing the truth; on the contrary, it bears witness that the Truth, which has been unveiled for us in the history of salvation, remains in great measure still veiled, until that day shall arrive when God will be «all in all» (1 Cor 15:28). Let us therefore approach what Revelation teaches us concerning the first and last truths, and compare it with what our reason is able to discern along its path of enquiry.

# Part I: Introduction to Protology
### (Theology of Creation and Anthropology)

«Creation is the foundation of "all God's saving plans," the "beginning of the history of salvation" that culminates in Christ» (CCC 280).[^22] Sacred Scripture (cf. Gen 1:1) and the Symbols of Faith, both the Apostles' Creed (cf. DS 30) and the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed (cf. DS 150), begin with God the Creator.

The necessity of fixing our attention upon the first article of the Creed is perennially timely. «The first heresy, the foundational heresy of Christianity, is not the heresy connected with the corpus of doctrines concerning the Son, or Trinitarian theology, or the theology of the Incarnation; rather, it is the heresy concerning God as Creator»,[^23] namely Gnosticism, which recurs repeatedly throughout history and in an extraordinary manner today. Pope Francis shared with several bishops a reflection confided to him by the Pope Emeritus: «This is the epoch of sin against God the Creator».[^24]

In recent centuries, as we shall see, a conception has gained ground which denies not creation itself, but its bond with history. Deism, by banishing the creative act to the initial moment, severs any ongoing relation in the history of the world with the Creator.[^25]

Our treatment will follow the schema grounded in Thomas Aquinas's distinction between creation *active sumpta* (actively considered) and *passive sumpta* (passively considered).[^26] We shall examine first creation in the active sense—the creative act of God—and then creation in the passive sense—creatures in their relation to God. This schema underscores the strictly theological character of protology, the primary object of which is God, not man nor the world. If created beings are the object of theology, this is solely on account of their relationship to God the Creator, as authors as diverse as Saint Thomas and Wolfhart Pannenberg have pointed out.[^27] Ours is not faith in creation, but faith in the Creator.

We have chosen to give primacy to a synthetic-synchronic approach in the exposition of individual themes. The progression of the chapters will not proceed as do other manuals—which already do so admirably—by surveying every aspect in Scripture (OT and NT), then in magisterial documents, in the Fathers, in the medieval Doctors, etc.; rather, it will integrate the fundamental fruits of such an approach within a synchronic perspective that allows everything, as far as possible, to be viewed in relation. We shall seek to prioritise a pedagogical exposition that fosters comprehension of the individual topics under consideration. In this regard—as the reader will soon perceive—we have availed ourselves in a particular way of the guidance of Thomas Aquinas and Joseph Ratzinger / Benedict XVI: two great masters who reflect two complementary theological approaches.

For purely methodological reasons linked to the distribution of subject matter across the volumes of this series, we have had to leave aside the question of original sin, which will be treated in the manual on grace. We shall not, however, be able to avoid offering reflections on evil, which, like the shadows in a painting that set off the lights, constitutes an obligatory point of reference in any theological consideration of creation.

The structure of this first, protological part envisions, therefore: after a first chapter contextualising the truth of creation in its relationship with both faith and reason, a second chapter examining the creative act of God in Revelation, before exploring systematically in the third chapter certain core aspects of the Christian idea of creation. There will follow an investigation into the prolongation of God's eternal creative act in time (cf. Chapter 4), encompassing the classically related themes of conservation and providence. Once the creative act has been analysed, we shall proceed to the study of creatures, beginning with the invisible (cf. Chapter 5) and arriving in the final chapter of this part, Chapter 6, at visible creatures (man in the world).
