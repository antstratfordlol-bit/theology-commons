## Chapter 5 Invisible Creatures: The Angels, Participants in God's Governance {#sanz-alfa-omega.5}

THE ANGELS, PARTICIPANTS IN DIVINE PROVIDENCE

The profession of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council confesses that God "by His almighty power from the beginning of time created from nothing both orders of creatures: the spiritual and the corporeal, that is, the angelic and the earthly, and then man, who partakes in a manner of both" (DS 800). In this way, it specifies what the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed professes concerning the one "Maker of heaven and earth, of all things visible and invisible" (DS 150). Having studied the divine creative act, we now turn our attention to created reality, beginning with the order of spiritual creatures.

Faith in the angels has always constituted a challenge for human reason432. Whilst the affirmation of God's existence is philosophically necessary once it is admitted that everything is created, the same cannot be said regarding these beings, whose existence is not necessary. In times past, philosophy occupied itself with separate substances, whose existence, if not strictly necessary, appeared congruent with the perfection of the universe. Greek thought on the demiurge and divine ideas made it difficult to comprehend the existence of purely spiritual beings that were neither God nor part of the being of the godhead itself.

In the context of the development of science and technology, some find it difficult to admit the doctrine concerning invisible creatures, dismissing it as part of an infantile stage of faith. "It is impossible to use electric light and the wireless and to avail ourselves of modern medical and surgical discoveries, and at the same time to believe in the New Testament world of spirits and miracles"433. What ought one to say in the face of the digital developments of recent years, when thanks to the internet we are able to speak with others through the screen of a tablet or a smartphone?

During the years of theology's so-called "anthropological turn", the transition from the treatise on God the Creator to theological anthropology led, in more than a few instances, to the disappearance of the chapter on angels from textbooks: "From a kerygmatic point of view it is no longer necessary today, in preaching and teaching, to place the truth of angels in the foreground"434. In response, it has been observed that "angelology is not a mere appendage to anthropology [...]. Only the reality of the angelic world guarantees the reality of man's insertion into a spiritual world that is greater than himself"435.

Analogous considerations apply to the affirmation of faith according to which certain of these beings rebelled against God in a definitive manner436, constituting the kingdom of darkness, which exerts a considerable influence over mankind and the cosmos by virtue of the power of the sin instigated by them. The profession of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council states: "For the devil and the other demons were created by God good in their nature, but they became evil of their own accord. And man sinned at the instigation of the devil" (DS 800). Faced with the objection that in the twenty-first century we would do well to leave behind certain mythical doctrines, Francis has reaffirmed the Church's faith: "His presence is found in the first pages of Scripture, which end with God’s victory over the devil. [...] Hence, let us not think that he is a myth, a representation, a symbol, a figure, or an idea"437.

In this chapter we shall synthesise the central elements of angelology within the Christian tradition, with particular reference, as regards the ontological question, to the Angelic Doctor, and, as regards the liturgy in connection with the angels, to J. Ratzinger / Benedict XVI. We hope to demonstrate, with all due respect to Bultmann, how the technological advances of the digital age, far from constituting an obstacle, actually place us in a position to grasp this truth of faith more effectively.

### 5.1. The Angels in Salvation History: Their Creation and Elevation

### 5.1.1. The Angels in the Bible

The denial of the existence of angels is nothing new. It suffices to recall that Christ Himself confirmed their existence (cf. Matt 22:30; Acts 23:8) against the opinion of the Sadducees. The affirmation of Gregory the Great is well known: "The existence of Angels and Archangels is attested on almost every page of Sacred Scripture"438. Whatever qualifications might be made, "the fact remains that one would have to revise radically Sacred Scripture itself, and with it the whole of salvation history, if one wished to rid oneself of the angels"439.

The term "angel" translates the Latin *angelus*, from the Greek *ággelos*, which means "messenger" or "envoy". This word was employed in the Septuagint to render the Hebrew equivalent *mal’ak*, which in the Bible is applied to various categories of beings fulfilling the mission of a messenger, meaning "delegate" or "ambassador"440. This name indicates that "what counts most in Revelation is the truth concerning the tasks of the angels"441. Augustine observed that "angel" is a name that describes an office rather than a nature442.

This must not lead hastily to declaring the impossibility of knowing their nature, or to setting the two dimensions (ontological and functional) in opposition to one another. Scripture speaks of these beings as spirits (*pneumata*) in several places (cf. Heb 1:14; 12:9; Rev 1:4). Pannenberg, who like Barth had shown himself opposed to any reflection upon the angelic nature, was ultimately forced to admit that the phrase in Hebrews 1:14, "spirits ministering" (*leitourgika pneumata*), is virtually a definition of the nature of angels443. The Bishop of Hippo demonstrated the harmony between both aspects: "If you inquire the name of their nature, it is spirit; if you inquire their office, it is angel: according to what he is, he is spirit; according to what he performs, he is angel"444. This statement leaves several questions open, because, as purely spiritual beings, angels appear in close relationship to the nature of God, who "is spirit" (John 4:24). Given the rigorous distinction in Christianity between Creator and creatures, and the monotheism it entails, it will not be easy, as we shall see, to maintain both the created and the purely spiritual character of the angels, thereby avoiding dualism. In any case, in the Bible they are presented as the creatures nearest to the divine exemplar.

Returning to the Old Testament, where one witnesses "a progressive taking root of faith in angelic beings after the exile"445, the tasks undertaken by the angels are described. On the one hand, they participate in the celebration of the glory that the Creator receives from the created world: "Praise the Lord from the heavens; praise him in the heights! Praise him, all his angels" (Ps 148:1–2). On the other hand, the angels take part in God's governance over creation: "Bless the Lord, O you his angels, you mighty ones who do his word, hearkening to the voice of his word!" (Ps 103 [102]:20). To the angels is entrusted a special solicitude for men: "For he will give his angels charge of you to guard you in all your ways. On their hands they will bear you up, lest you dash your foot against a stone" (Ps 91 [90]:11–12). The Book of Tobit speaks of the protection exercised over him and his family by Raphael, "one of the seven angels who stand ready and enter before the glory of the Lord" (Tob 12:15), presenting their petitions and prayers to God (cf. Tob 3:17 and 12:12). The Book of Daniel mentions another celestial figure, Michael, "the great prince" (Dan 12:1), indicating that the duties of angels as ambassadors of the living God extend not only to individual human beings and those endowed with special offices, but also to whole nations (cf. Dan 10:13–21).

The New Testament stands in continuity with the Old Testament conception of angels as God's messengers. Their apparition suggests that the transcendent irrupts into the world, which occurs in its fullness in the Incarnation of the Word. "Since God is present in Jesus, his earthly journey is accompanied by the angels"446. We see this in the account of the annunciation of the birth of John the Baptist to Zechariah and of Christ to Mary, both entrusted to the angel Gabriel (cf. Luke 1:19, 26); an angel announces the birth of Christ to the shepherds in the night whilst a heavenly multitude praises God (cf. Luke 2:9–14); an angel explains to Joseph in a dream the mystery that Mary carries within her and gives him directions for the protection of the newborn Child in the face of the danger of Herod's persecution (cf. Matt 1:20–21; 2:13).

The angels are present at the beginning and the end of Jesus' public life, comforting and ministering to Him: following the temptations at the close of the forty days of prayer and fasting spent in the wilderness (cf. Matt 4:11), and during the agony in Gethsemane, after He had overcome the temptation to withdraw from the Father's will (cf. Luke 22:43).

The angels announce the good news of the Resurrection to the women who hastened to the tomb (cf. Mark 16:5–7), particularly to Mary Magdalene, who is granted a personal appearance of Jesus (cf. John 20:12–17). After the Ascension, the angels appear to the Apostles to reveal to them that "this Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven" (Acts 1:11). At the return of Christ the angels will be at His side, collaborating with Him at the decisive moment of judgement (cf. Matt 13:49; 16:27; 25:31; 2 Thess 1:7f).

"In comparison with the intertestamental writings, the sobriety of references to the angelic world in the New Testament"447 does not mean that the heavenly spirits are denied; they "continue to exercise their traditional functions, but henceforth at the service of the sole Mediator, the Christ"448; for this reason, in the Gospel they are always connected with the mission of Christ: "You will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man" (John 1:51).

Since the mission of Christ extends into the nascent Church, it is entirely natural that in the Acts of the Apostles the angels are present, guiding the first steps of the community: following the arrest and imprisonment of the Apostles, "an angel of the Lord opened the prison doors" (Acts 5:19); this scene is repeated later with Peter alone (cf. Acts 12:7–11); an angel subsequently appears in a vision to Paul, revealing that he and his companions will survive a shipwreck (cf. Acts 27:23–24); an angel guides the deacon Philip in his apostolic mission (cf. Acts 8:26); the encounter between Cornelius and Peter is brought about by an angel (cf. Acts 10:3–7). In these latter instances it becomes explicit how these beings convey the divine will to men.

The Book of Revelation is rich in references to the angels. Presiding over each of the seven churches to which the seer writes is an angel (cf. Rev 1:20). Their two functions are recalled there in various ways: on the one hand, they surround the throne of God and fill the celestial realm with their hymns of praise (cf. Rev 5:11; 7:11); on the other hand, they transmit Revelation and impart visions (cf. Rev 1:1; 10:1, 8f; 14:6f; 17:1); lastly, they participate in the judgement of God (cf. Rev 11:15–18; 14:6f; 20:4).

### 5.1.2. Creation and Supernatural Elevation of the Angels

Jesus, as the Son of God, is superior to all the angels (cf. Mark 13:27; Heb 1:4ff; Phil 2:9f). In several texts polemic is directed against the Gnostic worship of angels (cf. Col 2:18; Rev 19:10; 22:8f), because it obscures knowledge of the unique status of Christ the Mediator, and insistence is placed upon the supremacy of Christ over them449. The angels belong to Christ, inasmuch as "in him all things were created, in heaven and on earth, visible and invisible, whether thrones or dominions or principalities or authorities—all things were created through him and for him" (Col 1:16).

The struggle against Gnosticism, which blurred the boundaries between the divine and the created, led the Fathers of the Church to reiterate that, according to Scripture, angels are mere creatures450. It was necessary to explain their apparent absence from the Genesis narratives. Origen thought that they had been created prior to the world, since the creation of the material world was the consequence of their fall451. Augustine hypothesised that their creation was implicit in Genesis 1:1: in the beginning God created heaven (the angels) and the earth (the material world)452. Furthermore, the Bishop of Hippo suggested that the mention of the light created on the first day (cf. Gen 1:3) indicated "the formation of the spiritual creature"453. In this way he departed from the common opinion regarding the priority of the angels' creation relative to the material world, in favour of a simultaneous creation—a view that would become the standard position of the Scholastics, including St Thomas, in whose worldview "something would be lacking in the angelic world without the corporeal world, and in the corporeal world without the angelic world"454.

As creatures, the angels are good and blessed. Having been created *viatores* (wayfarers), their creation did not initially include the communication of the grace of the vision of God, even though, as Thomas says following Augustine, God created them "producing at once their being and bestowing grace" (*simul eis et condens naturam et largiens gratiam*)455. Their very first act could in no way be deficient. If the sin of some among them, to be discussed below, is described in Scripture as a fall (cf. Isa 14:12; Ezek 28:13–17), this implies a transition from a positive to a negative state. Only in a second moment did certain angels, by rebelling, nullify the merits of the first moment456.

The angels had to undergo a trial, after which those who remained faithful to God entered into the everlasting enjoyment of the beatific vision; they can no longer sin, nor can they grow in holiness, inasmuch as they have attained the consummation of their destiny. Jesus says of them that they "always behold the face of my Father who is in heaven" (Matt 18:10). The Church has never accepted the Origenist notion of a possible reversibility of choice, either for the rebel angels or for the good ones457.

### 5.2. Reflections on the Nature and Mission of the Angels

### 5.2.1. Some Properties of the Angelic Nature According to St Thomas

How can purely spiritual beings exist that are not divine? In the patristic era there were difficulties in defining an adequate ontological status for the angels that could account for both their inferiority with respect to God and their superiority over ourselves. Ambrose thought that "nothing is immune and exempt from material composition, except the sole substance of the adorable Trinity, which is truly pure and simple"458. Augustine, having to concede a certain corporeity, specified that in angels it is a matter of *corpus non caro* (a body, not flesh)459. Gregory the Great aptly expressed this dilemma: "In comparison with our bodies, they are pure spirits; but in comparison with the supreme and boundless Spirit, they are merely bodies"460.

Bonaventure wrote that "angels are incorporeal by nature"461. Nevertheless, following his master, Alexander of Hales, the Seraphic Doctor specified that, in order to preserve divine transcendence and simplicity, one must admit that in the angel substantial form enters into composition with some kind of spiritual matter462. Only with Thomas was the definitive transition achieved towards the clear affirmation of the pure spirituality of angels, without any kind of matter entering into the definition of their nature, thanks to his insight into the metaphysical structure of created being463.

Indeed, the earlier reflections assumed that every created being is composed of matter and form. Yet this is not the primary composition of act and potency in a created subject. Creation implies the real distinction between being (*esse*) and essence (*essentia*), which relate to each other respectively as act and potency. In Thomas's metaphysics of creation, in continuity with the data of faith, a created composition without matter is entirely possible, and therefore a created being that is purely spiritual.

> Although in the angel there is no composition of matter and form, there are nonetheless in him act and potency. And this can be made evident by observing that in material realities a twofold composition is found. The first is the composition of matter and form, which constitutes a determinate nature. This nature, so composed, is not however its own *esse* (act of being), but *esse* is its act. Hence the nature is related to its *esse* as potency to act. If therefore we remove matter, and suppose that a form subsists without it, there still remains between the form and *esse* the relation that exists between potency and act. And in this sense we must understand the composition proper to the angels [...]. Only God, therefore, is Pure Act464.

From this affirmation several consequences follow. One is the incorruptibility of the angels, and hence their immortality465. It is to this that Jesus refers when He speaks of the final eschatological condition of the resurrected: "For they cannot die any more, because they are equal to angels and are sons of God, being sons of the resurrection" (Luke 20:36). This does not mean that they enjoy divine immutability, but rather it must be affirmed that in them there is always progression.

Being incorporeal, angels cannot be circumscribed or localised in a place466; this does not mean that they cannot act upon matter, as the human spiritual soul likewise does. From this one can understand, as also from certain biblical texts (cf. Exod 25:20; Isa 6:2–6; Ezek 10:3–22; 28:14), why angels (specifically cherubim and seraphim) have traditionally been depicted with wings, which signify their agility, analogous to that of the risen body of Jesus. It also follows that—since what is higher can assume and support what is lower—an angel can manifest himself in bodily form, as Scripture leads us to suppose when speaking of certain apparitions (Raphael in the Book of Tobit or Gabriel in the Gospel of Luke). In such circumstances, the body can be conceived "as a sacrament of angelic presence"467.

Finally, there is a characteristic that is difficult to grasp: every angel is unique in its species. If one follows the principle of individuation by virtue of matter signed with quantity (*materia quantitate signata*), the argument proves unassailable:

> For those things that agree in species and differ numerically possess the same form, and are distinguished by matter. If therefore the angels are not composed of matter and form, as has already been explained, it is logically impossible that there should be two angels of one and the same species468.

Perhaps we may grasp the meaning of this affirmation if we consider that the plurality of individuals within a corporeal species is not in itself a perfection, but a kind of "ruse" of nature to mitigate an imperfection, since no single individual is capable of realising in itself the entire perfection of the species. Each angel fulfils and exhausts this unique grade of being, this exceptional manner of participating in the divine perfection. Consequently, each angel exhausts its own species469.

The intellect and will of angels are different from ours470. On the one hand, they know intuitively (by species infused by God or through higher angels) and not in a discursive manner; on the other hand, they act with clarity and immediacy, fixing their will not by vacillating between alternatives as we do, but definitively and forever. Intellect and will make them personal beings.

For Thomas, the existence of angels is not only a truth of faith, but is also reasonable from the perspective of the perfection of the universe471. If God exists as the Pure Act of Being, and at the other extreme of reality there are corporeal substances, there ought to be an intermediate category of beings: spiritual substances. How does one move from fittingness (*convenientia*) to a cogent demonstration? The first step of the argument is to uphold the necessity of the existence of incorporeal, and therefore purely spiritual, substances. If God created everything by His intellect and will, there must be creatures in the world that imitate this aspect of the divine perfection, capable of intellectual knowledge and of love. One might object that this is precisely what man is. Here the second stage of the argument enters: what is accidental to a nature is not coextensive with all subjects that share that nature. Union with a body is accidental to a spiritual substance, because not all intellectual substances are necessarily united to a body. The principle of participation affirms that, if an imperfect realisation of a perfection exists, there must also exist a perfect realisation of that perfection. Since the human intellect is imperfect, in that it is in potency and destined to be actualised progressively, there ought to be a created intellectual power that is fully in act; and this is the angel.

### 5.2.2. The Angels and the Liturgy

Metaphysical considerations are not the gratuitous fruit of speculative thought detached from the lived experience of faith; rather, they arise from the exercise of reason within faith, understood as a spiritual and ascetical praxis that likewise recognises the importance of the symbolic elements present in the Bible. Just as we spoke earlier of the angels' wings, we must now speak of their voice (cf. Job 38:7; Luke 2:13–14; Rev 5:9–12), inasmuch as, as Ratzinger affirmed in his lectures, their abiding with God takes the form of flying and singing, where singing is seen as the highest form of flight472.

Faith in the angels stands in close relation to the liturgy of the Church473, as is deduced from their "definition": "Are they not all ministering spirits (*leitourgika pneumata*) sent forth to serve, for the sake of those who are to obtain salvation?" (Heb 1:14). "All cultic manifestations of the Church ought therefore to be understood either as the participation of the angels in earthly worship or, conversely, every earthly worship of the Church as participation in the worship offered to God by the angels in heaven"474. The Fathers and the liturgical tradition of the Church suggest that the angels are actively and efficaciously present in each of the seven sacraments475, and in a most particular manner in the Eucharistic celebration. In the Roman Canon the prayer is made: "command that these gifts be borne by the hands of your holy Angel to your altar on high"476. John Chrysostom observes that the angels surround the altar when the priest approaches to offer the sacrifice477. Following upon the reference to the wilderness manna as *panem angelorum* (Ps 78 [77]:25 Nova Vulgata), it became customary to call the Eucharistic food the "bread of angels" (*panis angelorum*, *panis angelicus*)478. The union between what Origen considers the two Churches479—gathered together so that the angels may bear our prayers to God—is made explicit in the conclusion of the Preface: "with Angels and Archangels, with Thrones and Dominions, and with all the hosts and Powers of heaven, we sing the hymn of your glory, as without end we acclaim"480.

Liturgical prayer is not exhausted in the sacraments, but continues in the praise of the Divine Office or Liturgy of the Hours, the official prayer of the Church, especially entrusted to those under obligation to choral recitation. "The medievals were convinced of the angelic presence in the monastic office"481. Monks imitate the existence of the angels; theirs is "the song of men who have elevated their entire existence above the natural order, drawing near to the angels' mode of being"482. The expression "to sing like angels" is widely known in popular parlance.

The Fathers speak of a complex hierarchy among the angels based upon various texts of the Old and New Testaments, particularly the Pauline epistles (cf. Eph 1:21f; Col 1:16)483. Pseudo-Dionysius was to popularise the scheme, derived from Proclus, of an arrangement into three hierarchies and nine choirs or orders484. The widespread influence of this description was due to the fact that Pseudo-Dionysius (likely a Syrian monk of the fifth to sixth centuries) was long believed to be Paul's disciple mentioned in the Acts of the Apostles, and his writings consequently enjoyed immense authority throughout the Middle Ages485.

According to the classification of Pseudo-Dionysius, which Thomas adopts, in the first hierarchy we find, in descending order: Seraphim (cf. Isa 6:2–6), Cherubim (cf. Gen 3:24; Ezek 1:6–11, and many other Old Testament passages), and Thrones (cf. Col 1:16), who, being directly turned towards God and contemplating the plan of divine works in its very source, receive this illumination directly from God without intermediaries. The second tier comprises, likewise in descending order: Dominions (cf. Col 1:16), Virtues (cf. Eph 1:21), and Powers (cf. Col 1:16; Eph 1:21), who perceive the divine plans in their highest created causes, specifically through the angels of the first hierarchy. In the final hierarchy we discern: Principalities (cf. Col 1:16; Eph 1:21), Archangels (cf. 1 Thess 4:16; Jude 9), and simple Angels, who execute the divine plans they contemplate as these are determined to particular effects486.

The Church honours in her liturgy three figures who bear proper names and are accounted archangels487—even though the word *archangelos* occurs only in the two texts just cited, the concept is nonetheless present in the Book of Revelation (cf. Rev 8:2f; 11:15). They are characterised by having a distinct role in salvation history. The first is Michael (cf. Dan 10:13, 20; Rev 12:7; Jude 9). *Mika-El* means "Who is like God?", manifesting the essential posture of the good spirits who made the salvific choice by virtue of which they "behold the face of the Father" who is in heaven. The second is Gabriel, whose presence in the mystery of the Incarnation (cf. Luke 1:19–26) expresses the meaning of his name, *Gabri-El*, that is, "power of God" or "my strength is God". The Incarnation, as the fullness of creation, is the supreme sign of the almighty Father. The third archangel is called Raphael. *Rapha-El* means "God heals". He is made known to us through the story of Tobit (cf. Tob 12:15f), which is "significant regarding the entrusting to the angels of God's little children, who always stand in need of custody, care, and protection"488.

### 5.2.3. The Angels and Providence: The Guardian Angel

A circularity exists between the two missions of angelic beings, whose beholding of God is not incompatible with their being sent forth on behalf of men. "The closer a creature is to God, that is, the more perfect and elevated it is within the hierarchy of beings or within the hierarchy of grace, the more important is its participation in the execution of the benevolent designs of providence"489.

The angels accompany the events of salvation history, in a pre-eminent manner the mystery of Christ and of the Church. This angelic assistance concerns not only the broad sweep of great events, but also the lives of individual persons. The Acts of the Apostles recounts that when Peter, who was known to be in prison, knocked at the door of the house of Mary the mother of John, the maid Rhoda, on hearing his voice, instead of opening, ran in for joy to announce to the others that Peter was there, and they reacted by saying that it was perhaps his angel (cf. Acts 12:12–16). Jesus, speaking of the little ones who must not be scandalised, says that "their angels in heaven always behold the face of my Father" (Matt 18:10). The conviction gradually took root during the patristic period that every human community, and indeed every single individual, enjoys the special protection of an angel. "Some of them are set over nations, whilst others accompany the faithful individually [...]. That an angel stands beside each of the faithful as a kind of pedagogue and shepherd directing his life, no one will deny"490. This is the doctrine of the guardian angels, which received fresh impetus during the medieval period in connection with monastic life. Following Pseudo-Dionysius and in continuity with Gregory the Great, the Aquinas is convinced that missionary activity *ad extra* concerns the angels of the lower orders; whilst the angels sent to guard individual human beings belong to the lowest angelic choir, those appointed to watch over communities belong to higher hierarchies491.

Bearing in mind Augustine's admonition that we must honour the angels whilst strictly reserving to God alone the worship of *latria*492, devotion to the guardian angels developed especially within the Benedictine order; Bernard of Clairvaux is the pre-eminent doctor of devotion to the guardian angels. Their liturgical feast appeared in the sixteenth century, and in 1670 Pope Clement X extended it to the universal Church, assigning it to 2 October493.

### 5.3. The Trial and Fall of the Rebel Angels

In the profession of faith of the Fourth Lateran Council it is stated that "the devil and the other demons were created by God good in their nature, but they became evil of their own accord" (DS 800). These words were directed against the Cathars, a medieval group of Gnostic tendencies who considered the devil to be evil by origin, thereby contradicting the primordial goodness of all creation. The intention was to make clear that the malice of the demons is the fruit of the perversion of their free will, and not of their ontological nature. Evil does not exist prior to sin. There are no creatures created evil. This follows directly from the affirmation of the goodness of creation against every form of metaphysical dualism.

The doctrine concerning the rebel angels does not stand at the centre of the Church's faith; rather, it represents a secondary, subordinate aspect that makes sense only in its dependence upon the primary and central reality: the goodness of God the Creator and His revelation in Christ. The Christian economy has at its centre the incarnate Son of God and, subordinately, human beings as the recipients of His salvation. An obsessive mentality that constantly sees the presence of the evil one as the direct cause of every ill, whether real or perceived, does not accord with the Christian vision.

Nevertheless, the affirmation of the existence of the devil is crucial for understanding that the responsibility for the existence of evil in the world is not to be laid upon God, but rather upon the negative outcome of the exercise of freedom on the part, primarily, of certain spiritual creatures. In recent times, two challenges have called this doctrine into question. Firstly, given its secondary character, relative to the positive mystery upon which it depends, it has been suggested that it would be improper to speak of "faith in demons", and that, in view of their sheer negativity, one might think of their non-existence494. Secondly, Barth challenged the traditional faith of the Church:

> Everything that we call evil, death, sin, the devil, and hell is not God's creation, but is on the contrary what God's creation excludes and to which God has said "no" [...]. That which is not good was not created by God and has no creaturely being; if any existence at all is to be attributed to it, it is preferable to define it as non-being; then there remains to it no other name than that of the power of being that springs from the force of the divine "no"495.

Thus, demons were never angels, and the doctrine of the fall is a baseless invention. One would have to ask, however, what ontological consistency and ambiguous relation to God belong to this "non-being"—a construct that raises more problems than it resolves, hovering as it does under the shadow of metaphysical dualism496.

In short:

> There are two equal and opposite errors into which our race can fall about the devils. One is to disbelieve in their existence. The other is to believe, and to feel an excessive and unhealthy interest in them. They themselves are equally pleased by both errors and hail a materialist or a magician with the same delight497.

### 5.3.1. The Rebel Angels in Scripture498

It is commonly thought that in the Old Testament the chosen people, partly through their intense reflection upon evil in connection with the experience of exile, gradually became aware of the moral dispositions of heavenly beings, so that only from a certain point onwards was the angelic world regarded as divided into two camps: that of the good angels and that of the evil demons. The Septuagint translation employs the Greek word *daimon*, which could designate both the malign powers dominating existence and pagan deities. In the Book of Tobit it is described how "Asmodeus the evil demon" (Tob 3:8) causes harm to human beings.

The Hebrew word is *śāṭān*, which in the Old Testament indicates in a general sense an "adversary". Two texts demonstrate how a celestial figure assumes such a role: at the beginning of the Book of Job, Satan must seek permission from God to act against this righteous man (cf. Job 1:6–7); in the Book of Zechariah, the prophet recounts that the Lord showed him "Joshua the high priest standing before the angel of the Lord, and Satan standing at his right hand to accuse him. And the Lord said to Satan: 'The Lord rebuke you, O Satan!'" (Zech 3:1–2). In another passage, the use of the noun without the definite article indicates a proper name and thus reinforces the notion of an evil personal being (cf. 1 Chr 21:1 in relation to 2 Sam 24:1). This malice, according to the Book of Wisdom, is not natural but caused by vice: "through the devil’s envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his party experience it" (Wis 2:24); in this way Satan is identified with the serpent of Genesis 3.

In the New Testament, Satan along with his demons is prominently present: he is named thirty-four times, whilst on another thirty-three occasions he is referred to by the Greek translation *diabolos*, "the divider" or "slanderer". Satan recapitulates the figures of evil from the Old Testament: the dragon, the ancient serpent (cf. Rev 12:3, 9; 13:2; 20:2). And he reveals himself as the enemy of Christ, the Antichrist *par excellence* (cf. 1 John 4:3).

The words of Jesus: "I saw Satan fall like lightning from heaven" (Luke 10:18), spoken upon the return of the disciples, who rejoiced that "even the demons are subject to us in your name!" (Luke 10:17), reveal His mission as a combat, an eschatological conflict between light and darkness. In the Gospel of Mark, Jesus' first public act after His baptism at the Jordan is the expulsion of an unclean spirit from a possessed man (cf. Mark 1:23–28). Jesus had already experienced this combat in His own person when He had to confront the tempter in the wilderness (cf. Mark 1:12–13), who then waited until his opportune time (*kairos*, cf. Luke 4:13) in the moments preceding the Lord's Passion, returning to tempt Him in Gethsemane (cf. Mark 14:32–42). Just as Jesus chose to do the will of God, so each human being must decide his allegiance to one or the other, for neutrality between good and evil is impossible. Dualism in Christianity is moral and existential in character, but never ontological499.

Paul underlines the superiority of Christ not only over the good angels, but also over those powers with clearly negative connotations of which he speaks in several of his epistles500. Christ "delivers the kingdom to God the Father after destroying every rule and every authority and power" (1 Cor 15:24). Paul exalts the power of Christ who is exalted above these spirits which, though seemingly neutral at first glance (cf. Eph 1:20–21), are in reality demonic and hostile to God (cf. Eph 6:12).

### 5.3.2. The Sin of the Angels and Its Effects

Why, if they were created good, does the Bible speak of the existence and destructive, negative activity of these beings? When and why did they rebel against God? The sin of the angels is shrouded in a mystery concerning which we possess few indications. In the Old Testament there are two prophetic oracles concerning the fall of the king of Babylon, styled Lucifer (cf. Isa 14:12–15), and of Tyre (cf. Ezek 28:12–19), which probably draw upon certain mythological motifs of the Ancient Near East, and which have traditionally been understood as depictions of the sin of the angels501. Two New Testament passages refer to this mysterious event. Speaking of the condemnation awaiting false prophets, the Second Epistle of Peter recalls that God "did not spare the angels when they sinned, but cast them into hell and committed them to pits of nether gloom to be kept until the judgment" (2 Pet 2:4). In the Epistle of Jude we read that God "has kept by eternal bonds under darkness for the judgment of the great day the angels that did not keep their own principality, but left their proper abode" (Jude 6).

The biblical texts shed little explicit light upon the nature of this sin. This opened the way to various hypotheses formulated since patristic times on the matter502. In the earliest centuries a sin of lust was proposed, based upon the episode of the union between the sons of God and the daughters of men in the context of humanity's increasing corruption, which led God to repent of having made man (cf. Gen 6:1–6). This theory was, as a rule, rejected by the Fathers503.

A second hypothesis is grounded upon the devil's envy as the cause of the entry of death into the world (cf. Wis 2:24), maintaining that it was a sin of envy or jealousy directed against the creation of human beings. Irenaeus asserts that the devil "envied man and became apostate from the divine law; for envy is alien to God"504.

A variant of this hypothesis, maintained by St Bernard, specifies that the devil's envy was provoked by the elevation of man to his own rank of glory505. On this basis, Suárez and others suggested that the object of this jealousy was the grace of the hypostatic union, which introduces a Christological reference to the fall of Satan506. From the perspective of St Thomas, however, who links the Incarnation to the remedy for Adam's sin, one may question whether angels *in statu viae* were granted explicit knowledge of the mystery of the redemptive Incarnation.

Gradually the conviction emerged that at the origin of the angel's sin lay pride, the root of all sin (cf. Sir 10:12–13). This view became dominant in Augustine507, and was adopted by Thomas, who defined the angelic sin as one of pride, interpreting the alternative hypotheses in light of this principle. The Aquinas explains the object of diabolical pride by means of the words of the king of Babylon, the type of Lucifer: "I will ascend to heaven; above the stars of God I will set my throne on high; I will sit on the mount of assembly in the far north; I will ascend above the heights of the clouds, I will make myself like the Most High" (Isa 14:13–14). Thomas explains that Satan wished to be like God508, not in the sense of a creature made in His image and likeness, but by coveting for himself that perfection which belongs exclusively to God and is incommunicable (namely, being Creator and Lord)—a disordered, and hence sinful, desire in which Satan embroiled other angels. The Aquinas is convinced that the prince of the fallen angels was the highest in the natural order. He explains that, even though sin depends solely on the will, if one considers the motive of sin, namely pride, it seems reasonable that there should be greater occasion (not cause) for pride among the most elevated angels. He who was the best in the order of nature became the worst by his own choice509.

The New Testament passages on the sin of the angels speak of their punishment until the Day of Judgement. What will occur at that moment? They did not cease to be angels after their fall, continuing to retain their natural perfections, which have been darkened forever510. In the context of the Last Judgement, the Lord speaks of an "eternal fire prepared for the devil and his angels" (Matt 25:41) and of their "eternal punishment" (Matt 25:46). Origen's theory regarding a limited duration of this condemnation in favour of a final universal reconciliation (*apocatastasis*) was definitively rejected by the Church. The Synod of Constantinople in 543 leaves little room for doubt: "If anyone says or holds that the punishment of demons and impious men is temporary and will have an end after a certain time, that is to say, that there will be a restitution (apocatastasis) of demons or impious men, let him be anathema" (DS 411).

Theologians offer two reasons for the irrevocability of the choice made by Satan and his followers: one extrinsic, the other intrinsic. The first is that God ceases to offer them the grace of conversion; the window of opportunity for decision has passed. The second is that God respects their nature, since an angel, by virtue of his purely intellectual nature, stands from the outset before everything he can know, and his free will is fixed from the first moment upon the object of his choice in a total and irreversible manner. The cause of the unforgivableness of their sin is the definitive character of their choice, not a defect in divine mercy. "What death is to men, that the fall is to the angels; for after the fall there is no repentance for them, just as there is no repentance for men after death"511. This immutably perverse orientation of their mind constitutes the cause of their constant and terrible suffering512.

### 5.3.3. The Rebel Angels in the Mystery of Providence

God did not annihilate the creatures that rebelled against Him. In a mysterious, surprising, and paradoxical manner, the existence of these creatures, their hatred towards God, and their opposition to His designs are integrated into God's governance of the world513. We must acknowledge that "all things work together for good" (Rom 8:28).

Sin did not destroy the nature of the rebel angels. Despite their definitive obstinacy, the being they received from God remains good. A certain hierarchical order is preserved among them, such that Satan's sin was an exemplary cause of the sin of his followers, among whom some exercise authority over others. This "union" of theirs is by no means genuine love; on the contrary, it is driven by hatred, by the desire to destroy God's work with greater effectiveness. The paradox is that this peculiar "society" cannot be self-sufficient, but depends for its very existence upon God the Creator, who makes use of their activity to accomplish His plans514.

The permanence in existence of the rebel angels is bound up with the understanding of the Christian life as a struggle in history between the power of darkness and the light. The Christian must constantly exercise discernment, as Francis recalls in continuity with the Desert Fathers and the spiritual tradition, to distinguish whether what presents itself before him comes from the good spirit or from the evil spirit515.

The primary manner in which demons seek to influence human beings is temptation. This is attested throughout Scripture, from the story of Adam and Eve in paradise to the temptations Jesus underwent in the desert and in Gethsemane. Although in certain passages it seems to be asserted that God tempts men, according to Thomas such texts must be interpreted with good sense, for they actually indicate that God tests or instructs His people and individual persons516.

There are extraordinary manifestations of diabolical activity upon human beings: obsession and possession. The former consists of physical or psychic harassments, denoting how the devil besieges a person or a group. The latter, which appears frequently in the Gospels, can be understood in a broad sense or in a more technical sense, as "the state of a person—who may, moreover, be in a state of grace—whose body or even active sensitive faculties the devil seizes in order to use them as a passive instrument with the aim of carrying out his evil works"517. One must act with prudence so as to distinguish genuine cases from others that fall within the competence of psychiatry. For example, in the sixteenth century, a nun who was in strange spiritual conditions was examined by St John of the Cross, who concluded that she was not tormented by a demon, but merely by an excess of melancholy518.

It is worth recalling that Christianity presented itself from the beginning as a message of liberation from fatalism. The devil has no absolute power over us, and our freedom is always capable of rejecting his temptations, with the grace of God. The Christian, sharing in the Paschal victory of Christ over the powers of evil, knows that a life of union with Christ in the sacraments and in prayer is the best way to respond to his Master's invitation to live in calm and peaceful vigilance. The Church has always preserved the ministry of exorcists, in imitation of Jesus, who performed frequent exorcisms during His pastoral ministry. These are not magical rites, as might have occurred in ancient Babylon, but prayers in which victory over the devil is attributed to God alone519, in connection with the liturgical dimension of faith, which administers Baptism in the form of promise and renunciation, of turning away from Satan and drawing near to Christ520.

The demons wage war against God, seeking to estrange His creatures from Him, driven by hatred of God and jealousy towards men. Yet unwittingly and unwillingly, they collaborate in the fulfilment of the plan of salvation, and thus paradoxically contribute to the good of those who love God. The death of Christ on the cross, instigated by the devil, became the instrument of divine omnipotence to save the world and man. God would not permit diabolical activity in history—which is a profound mystery—for He "would by no means permit any evil to exist in His works unless He were so almighty and good as to bring good even out of evil"521.

### 5.4. Final Considerations

The importance of balance between metaphysics and salvation history, between creation and redemption, was manifested in the first part of this chapter in the balance between the metaphysical nature of the angels and their mission in salvation history. In their participation in the economy of creation and supernatural elevation, it is crucial to emphasise that Trinitarian Christocentrism which makes clear that the angels belong to Christ, are His, and serve Him. Being not the protagonists but secondary actors, their pointing towards Christ is of tremendous help to us.

Faith in the angels is Christocentric, because they remind us that we are called to unite ourselves with them in praising Christ.

> They are something more than a poetic backdrop from the repertoire of folklore and fairy tales. They belong to God, to Christ, and to the Holy Spirit, but they also belong to us. For us they signify a possibility of our existence, the possibility of its elevation and intensification [...]. If we do not direct ourselves toward the angel who stands before God, we turn without fail toward the angel who has departed from God, toward the demon. Man has always existed thus: he goes out of himself and draws near either to the angel or to the demon522.

Within the logic of the mediations of which God makes use to reach us and draw us to Himself, faith in the existence of angels shows itself congruent with the vision of the Creator outlined in the preceding chapters, far removed from a deism that admits no divine intervention in the world and from a pantheism that identifies everything with God without mediation. Perhaps our technological progress, which orients us towards transcending the limiting constraints of our material condition, rather than alienating us from the angels, helps us to draw even closer to them.
