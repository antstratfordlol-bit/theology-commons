## Chapter 4 God's Creative Act in Time: Conservation, Providence, and Governance {#sanz-alfa-omega.4}

We have dealt with the divine creative act in Revelation and in theology (cf. chapter 2) and with the consequences of this affirmation of faith in dialogue with philosophical thought (cf. chapter 3). Before proceeding to created realities (cf. chapters 5 and 6), it is necessary to address the intermediate space between the eternal creative act and temporal realities. While it is true that creative action belongs to divine eternity, it is no less true that this action continues in time.

A central idea of this book is that, just as eschatology cannot be reduced to the future, because it is already actual, so creation must not be confined to a remote past, because it is a present reality open to the future. God did not create merely in the beginning; rather, his creative action embraces created temporality and historicity, guiding them towards their end. Alien to the Christian vision are both the idea of a creation concluded in the beginning (deism), such that the Creator no longer acts in the world, and the idea of a creation that, in its ontological fragility, can neither exist nor operate without the direct and constant influx of a Creator who becomes the sole actor in history and the cosmos (pantheism and occasionalism). The Christian vision opposes both the thought that God cannot act in the world, and the idea that God does everything while creatures do nothing.

In this chapter we must explain in what sense God's creative act abides in time, in continuity with his governance over creation and history—that is, divine providence. And we must clarify how this governance is compatible with the proper acting and causality of creatures in their autonomy. All this demands reflection on the relationship between God's eternity and the temporality of creatures. Let us proceed with the treatment of these topics in order.

### 4.1. Conservation in Being Between Continuous Creation and Deism

The idea of a creation prolonged in time possesses certain elements that are discernible in Greek philosophy. A precedent might be found in Philo, who asserted that without the presence of God the world would be annihilated331. But it is Christianity, from its very beginnings, that speaks of the conservation of creatures in being332. This idea would find its most complete speculative development in the Thomistic reflection on creation. Recently, the expression *creatio continua* has been introduced into theology, which must be studied in its modern philosophical origins, in its opposition to deism, and in its relationship to the classical idea of conservation.

### 4.1.1. Conservation in Scripture and the Fathers

In the Bible, the permanence of creatures in existence is not "automatic", but is due to the power of the Creator who conserves them. A hasty reading of Gen 6–9 (and also of other passages of the OT) might induce one to think the contrary—that God willed the destruction of creatures. The narrative of Noah (like others in Israel's history) indicates that, in the face of the spreading of evil and destruction in the world, God wills to save a small remnant in order to relaunch his original will to give life to creatures, "because God did not make death, and he does not delight in the destruction of the living. For he created all things that they might exist, and the creatures of the world are wholesome, and there is no destructive poison in them, nor is the realm of death on earth" (Wis 1:13–14).

Other texts describe the maintenance of the creative influx: if he "should take back his spirit to himself, and gather to himself his breath, all flesh would perish together, and man would return to dust" (Job 34:14–15). The Psalmist observes: "When you hide your face, they are dismayed; when you take away their breath, they die and return to their dust" (Ps 104 [103]:29). Still more eloquent is the Book of Wisdom: "For you love all things that exist, and loathe none of the things that you have made; for you would not have formed anything if you had hated it. How would anything have endured if you had not willed it? Or how would anything not called into existence by you have been preserved?" (Wis 11:24–25).

In the speech at the Areopagus, Paul alludes to the divine action that sustains things in being, referring to the Stoic concept that "in him [God] we live and move and have our being" (Acts 17:28). The Letter to the Colossians applies this conservative action to Christ, in connection with his participation in the creative act: "All things were created through him and for him. He is before all things, and in him all things hold together" (Col 1:16–17). The same dynamic is perceived at the beginning of the Letter to the Hebrews, when it affirms that the Son through whom God made the world "is the radiance of his glory and the exact imprint of his nature, and he upholds the universe by the word of his power" (Heb 1:3). The Father and the Son continue to be at work, according to the words of Jesus: "My Father is working still, and I am working" (Jn 5:17).

Commenting on this phrase, Augustine stated: "That God continues to work without ceasing we must believe and, if possible, understand in the sense that if his action were withdrawn from his creatures, they would cease to exist"333. Gregory the Great maintained that, since all things were created from nothing, "their essence would return to nothingness if the Author of all things did not hold them all in his sovereign hand. Thus all creatures are unable, of themselves, to subsist and move; but they subsist to the extent that they have received the possibility of existing"334.

These biblical and patristic considerations make evident how the conservation of things in being is related to creation; it is a truth present in Revelation that can be demonstrated from the viewpoint of reason335. To Thomas we owe perhaps the most complete argumentation of the fact that God not only gives being to creatures but also maintains them in being—that creation implies conservation. This idea must confront certain objections arising from subsequent philosophical frameworks, which either render the idea of conservation irrelevant or interpret it in a different manner336.

### 4.1.2. Some Difficulties with the Notion of Conservation: The Doctrine of Saint Thomas

A first objection comes from those who, admitting that God created everything at the beginning, assert that his action is not necessary to ensure that things continue to exist. A watch, once manufactured and set in motion, no longer depends on the watchmaker; a building already constructed does not require the architect to remain standing. Deism envisions the Creator as a watchmaker or architect: once his work has been put into operation, he withdraws because it can carry on by itself. Creative action remains circumscribed to the past, without influx upon the present and the future. Deism, born in the modern era, stresses the transcendence of the Creator with respect to the world, but denies relevance to any possible intervention of his in natural and historical occurrences. Deism arose as a naturalism opposed to the novelty of a revelation of God in history, tending to relegate man's religious life to the background337. This mentality is present in certain contemporary frameworks, which render it superfluous to speak of a divine action in conserving creatures in being, or of a providence that guides history. This reductionism is understandable if one bears in mind that Christian thought has sometimes linked the conservation of being to providence rather than to creation, with the risk of reducing the creative act to the beginning. The conservation of being and providence must be understood along the line of creation338.

Opposed to deism, which emphasises God's transcendence, is modern pantheism, which places the accent on divine immanence in the world, to the point of reaching an identification, as in Spinoza (*Deus sive Natura*). There is no longer any room for creation from nothing, nor even for speaking of the conservation of being: all is one.

Another position emphasises the contingency of the created order, postulating the necessity of a continuous divine acting that constantly maintains things in existence: this is *creatio continua*, characteristic of Descartes and Luther, which dilutes the distinction of creation and conservation from providence, so that God alone acts; everything depends constantly and exclusively on divine action, leaving no room for the real efficacy of secondary causes.

The various conceptions briefly described could not be understood without the context that unites them: the Christian metaphysics of creation. Each reflects in its own way the breakdown of the balance in the God–world relationship that has dominated Western thought since the fourteenth century339. We turn first to the synthesis of Saint Thomas on the doctrine of conservation, in order then to establish a comparison with the *creatio continua* of modernity.

For Thomas, creation is the "continuous metaphysical situation of the creature [...] of total dependence in being"340. Dependence on God is permanent, as is creative causality, since the creature is a being *ab alio*341. Conservation is not a new action, but the very same action by which God produces being: "God does not conserve things by a new action, but by continuing the action by which he gives being, an action that is subject neither to motion nor to time"342. The term "continuation" does not adequately express the concept, since there is continuity where there is succession, whereas creation and conservation are the same action of giving being and maintaining it. Rather than a continuous creation, it is the prolongation of creation, since conservation is not a repetition of the creative act343. The presence of Being in the being of the entity is indicated by Thomas not through the example of the architect building a house, but as the presence of light in the air due to the illumination of the sun344. If the presence of the creative cause were to cease, the entity would fall into nothingness. If the light illuminating the air disappeared, the illumination would vanish. "The being of every creature depends on God in such a manner that creatures could not subsist even for an instant, but would fall back into nothingness, were they not kept in being by the action of divine power"345.

Creative action includes conservative action. If it did not include it, it would not be a true creative action, since it would not be a creation *ex nihilo*. If things existed even in the absence of conservation, this would mean that creation had not been from nothing. What is created from nothing cannot be self-sufficient, fully *a se*, given that it is always contingent, *ab alio*. A possible annihilation of creatures would not be a positive action of God, but the cessation of his conservative action346.

The world cannot possess existence unless it receives it from God, but, once received, it possesses it as its own. Conservation is not an incessant communication of *esse* to the creature, as though the latter were at every moment losing the being it received, but "is the same creative act to which the total being of the creature remains inseparably united in a state of profound dependence [...]. The creature does not tend towards *non-esse*, but naturally tends towards being, in virtue of the cause on which it depends"347.

"[The created universe] is made by God as solid as if it had existed *ab aeterno* by virtue of its own necessity"348. By virtue of its divine origin, the world is so deeply anchored in being that of itself it neither began to be nor will cease to be, nor does it account for its beginning or its end. Only Revelation gives certainty of the peculiar divine action in both moments.

### 4.1.3. Continuous Creation in Modernity and Contemporary Theology: A Comparison with the Thought of Saint Thomas

Philosophical reflection that accepts creation has developed a growing interest, in dialogue with the physical and biological sciences, in the notion of *creatio continua*, which has its roots in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century rationalism. The birth and consolidation of the evolutionary paradigm exerted their influence on Bergson's "creative evolution" and Whitehead's "process philosophy". In different ways, these approaches emphasise that divine creative activity is not something static, limited to the world's beginnings, but an incessant and continuous activity throughout the passage of time.

Recent theology has welcomed the notion of continuous creation, which gives a more dynamic sense to the idea of conservation in being, in harmony not only with the current development of the natural sciences, but also with the biblical idea of creation, which cannot be reduced to the beginning of history. *Creatio continua* constitutes "a more precise signification of *creatio ex nihilo*"349.

To clarify this point, one must examine the historical evolution of the notion of continuous creation in modern rationalism, so as to take up contemporary proposals in the light of Christian reflection on creation, the background of all these reflections.

All rationalist authors maintained the necessity of a *creatio continua*350. Even though the idea (though not the exact expression)351 is present in Augustine and Thomas, the meaning that rationalism accords to continuous creation differs from theirs, because the metaphysical elements underlying the notions of creation and conservation have changed352.

Whereas Thomas insisted on conceiving conservation as the continuation of the creative act, Ockham underlined the contingency of every event as an immediate consequence of its dependence on God353. The rational foundation of the truth of creation is thus eliminated, and creation will be regarded as an exclusively truth of faith, grounded in divine omnipotence354. With this presupposition, faith in creation is not for Luther a positive affirmation of the objectivity and theological relevance of the creative work, but a declaration of faith in the absolute supremacy of God and the insignificance of the creature. Creation comes to be conceived in the manner of actualism, which denies the consistency and stability of the created work, in order to make it depend continuously and directly upon the creative divine Word, upon the will of the Almighty. Creation and providence are identified. The relative autonomy of creatures is imperilled.

Descartes prefers to speak of continuous creation rather than conservation, to designate God's constant recreation of everything355. The continuous dependence of things upon God is grounded in a new conception of time:
> For if we consider the nature of time, it is indeed clear that the very same force and action are needed to conserve any thing in each individual moment in which it continues to exist, as would be needed to create it anew if it did not yet exist; so that the fact that between conservation and creation there is only a distinction of reason is one of the truths made manifest by the natural light356.

For Descartes, time is a discontinuous succession of indivisible instants that depend directly on God and not on the preceding or subsequent instant. He finds himself obliged to consider conservation as a creation reiterated at every moment357. Conservation, understood as continuous creation, constitutes an argument proving the existence of God358: "From the fact that we now exist, it does not necessarily follow that we shall exist a moment later, unless some cause—namely, the same one that produced us—continues to produce us, that is, conserves us"359.

Malebranche inherits from Descartes the thesis of continuous creation and understands it in relation to occasionalism: God alone is cause, whereas natural beings are merely occasional causes. The sun is a mere occasion for God to illuminate and warm the earth. Divine causality is properly creation, and for this reason causing belongs to God alone, since causing and creating are identical360. In creating, God establishes in creatures an absolute dependence with respect to Himself. "For the world certainly depends on the will of the Creator; and therefore, if it subsists, it is because God continues to will that it be. The conservation of creatures is thus, on God's part, nothing other than their continuous creation"361. All efficacy comes from God, who in causing creates continuously. There is no room for a real efficacy of creatures. This system contains a paradox because, in subsequently affirming that without an occasional cause there can be no general law, God would thus be subjected to occasional causes, which condition the divine choice, even though they are due solely to the efficacy of God's power. The idea of God lies at the foundation of Malebranche's philosophy, but "placing God at the origin of everything leads to not recognising him at the origin of anything"362.

Bayle, a predecessor of the Enlightenment thinkers, maintained that conservation must be conceived as a reiterated creation, as though things, at every instant, fell into nothingness and began to exist anew by virtue of a new creation363.

These analyses help us grasp how the modern conception of creation, rooted in decadent nominalism, accelerated the rupture between the being of God and the being of the world. The direct and immediate contact, which was meant to emphasise the world's nothingness and God's omnipotence, separated God from the world and caused nature—previously divinised—to emerge. This historical process gave impetus to the divinisation of man, which has led certain currents of contemporary thought to speak of creation as applied to human action364.

Thomas understands the unity of creation and conservation differently from the rationalist authors who uphold "continuous creation". In the latter conception, *esse* is an extrinsic accident to essence, such that their union is ephemeral. The creature possesses a real tendency towards non-being, since it can accidentally lose it. Maintaining the creature in existence supposes that God renews its actualisation at every instant. For Thomas, one may speak of continuous creation in the sense of a divine action that continuously gives being, not as the bestowal of a new existence or as an action distinct from creation365.

Once essence becomes primary with respect to being, being is reduced to the fact of existing, losing its character of first act (*actus primus*). In order to exist, essences are required to be continuously actualised in an extrinsic manner, falling into an actualism in which being is foreign to essence. The metaphysical actuality of being is reduced to pure facticity366. In this essentialist and actualist vision, existence supervenes upon essence as the fruit of an external causality, and by virtue of this exteriority a continuous divine intervention of bestowing existence is required. Once essence has taken primacy, being—reduced to *de facto* existence—is considered extrinsic to the thing's essence. The danger for a correct understanding of creation appears evident: for if creation is described as placing an essence into existence, how can one explain the origin and created character of the essence?

### 4.1.4. The Autonomy of Creation Between Deism and Continuous Creation

Deism highlights the transcendence of creative causality, guaranteeing the consistency, autonomy, and tendency towards being of creatures. However, by confusing the transcendental causality of being proper to the creative act with the predicamental causality of secondary causes, it operates a reduction of the notion of creation, separating it from its necessary relationship with conservation (and divine governance). The influx of the creative act is confined to the initial moment, forgetting that to be a creature is not primarily to have a beginning, but to have participated, received being. The recognition of one's own dependence on God lies at the foundation of man's religious life; this is an important element that remains hidden in a deist perspective367, which emphasises causality at the expense of participation, divine transcendence over the world to the detriment of its immanence.

Pantheism stands at the opposite pole of deism. It underlines the bond of being between God and the world, the divine presence in creation, to the point of postulating their identification, collapsing the distance between the Infinite and the finite. Participation takes precedence over causality; divine immanence is asserted at the expense of transcendence.

Thomas's doctrine of creation and conservation avoids both extremes, affirming causality and participation, immanence and transcendence. Creation is not reduced to the initial moment, and at the same time its permanent character, inasmuch as it is conservation, remains far from any confusion between the Creator and his creatures, the former being necessary and the latter contingent. It is not a matter of radical contingency, because conservation implies a positive tendency of the creature towards being, and this delimits the sense to be given to the expression *creatio continua*.

If one affirms the creation of the world by God, the influx of divine action must not be limited to the initial moment; rather, by virtue of the contingency of created beings, the creative act must continue in time. A theory according to which everything was completely established at the beginning, so that divine action was no longer necessary thereafter, represents an inadequate vision of creation.

Contemporary theological reflection, in its rereading of the doctrine and in its dialogue with the natural sciences, has fostered a dynamic conception of the idea of creation. The diverse biblical traditions speak of creation looking not only to the beginning of time, but also to history and to eschatological time. There are three aspects implied in the Judeo-Christian notion of creation: *creatio originalis*, *creatio continua*, and *nova creatio*368. Articulating the biblical and scientific perspectives, it has been said that evolution has opened to theological reflection the possibility "of seeing the creative activity of God in the world created by him not merely as a creation of the order established by him once and for all, but as continuous and ever new creation"369.

In emphasising the immediacy of the divine creative action, modernity fell into an arbitrary conception of divine actuation. Pannenberg rightly affirms that creation does not take place only at the initial moment, but that everything, including time, has its "beginning" in God's creation, originates from the eternity of God370. Nevertheless, his notion of creation, inherited from Luther and Descartes, fails to avoid actualism, as if God were continuously creating, given the absolute contingency of creatures. Against the conception of a created world that evolves independently of the Creator, Pannenberg emphasises the primacy of divine action in its creation, but it is not clear how much room remains in his vision for the autonomy of created realities in their being and operating. Monod and others371 have suggested considering the conservative aspect of evolutionary processes. Rather than the actualist concept of continuous creation, it would be better to deepen the notion of "secondary causes". First, however, we must reflect on the notion of time in its relationship with eternity in the light of the notion of creation.

### 4.2. Creation Between the Eternity of God and Time and History

In the confrontation with Arianism, the Council of Nicaea reaffirmed that the Word is "begotten, not made", and that it is false to assert that "there was a time when he was not" (DS 125–126). Arius's identification between the generation of the Word and his creation in time was thus refuted. The generation of the Word belongs to the eternity of God; creaturehood implies temporality372. The Christian distinction between Creator and creatures is manifested in the fact that, while eternity belongs to God, time describes creaturely being.

In Scripture, eternity is a characteristic of God's being, indicating that he exists from always and will exist for ever. "Before the mountains were brought forth, or ever you had formed the earth and the world, from everlasting to everlasting you are God [...]. For a thousand years in your sight are but as yesterday when it is past" (Ps 90 [89]:2, 4). Indeed, God says of himself: "I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god" (Isa 44:6; cf. 41:4; 48:12). These expressions are attributed to Jesus Christ in the Book of Revelation, where he is called "the first and the last" (Rev 2:8; cf. 21:6; 22:13) and also "the Living One" (Rev 1:18), who shares the life of the Father, which embraces all ages, indicated by the first and last letters of the alphabet: "I am the Alpha and the Omega, who is and who was and who is to come, the Almighty!" (Rev 1:8).

Unlike creatures, the "everlasting God" (Dan 13:42; cf. Bar 4:8; Isa 40:28; Rom 16:26) does not have before him a future different from his present. Not only his being, but also his life, his kingdom, his power, his love, his purpose, and his Gospel are qualified by this characteristic of eternity (cf. Dan 4:34 [31]; 7:14; Ps 145 [144]:13; Jer 31:3; 2 Pet 1:11; Eph 3:11; Rev 14:6). All that was and will be is present to him, because outside him there is no future; rather, he himself is his future and is therefore completely free373. His being indicates permanence: "I am who I am!" (Ex 3:14); but also fidelity, seeing that in the end God will be "all in all" (1 Cor 15:28). "Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and for ever!" (Heb 13:8).

In Scripture, creation is linked to time, in contrast to the eternity of God. The creative act occurred at a precise moment, marking the beginning of the world's existence: "In the beginning God created the heavens and the earth" (Gen 1:1), which echoes in the Psalmist's prayer: "Of old you laid the foundation of the earth, and the heavens are the work of your hands" (Ps 102 [101]:25 [26]). References to the beginning of the world or of the ages are frequent both in the OT, particularly in Sirach, and in the NT (cf. Sir 15:14; 16:26; 36:14; 39:25; 44:2; Isa 41:4; Mt 19:4, 8; 24:21; Mk 10:6; 13:19; Lk 11:50; Heb 1:10; 2 Pet 3:4). As confirmation of the beginning of all things at a precise moment, the NT uses the expression "before the world was" (Jn 17:5) and also "before the foundation of the world" (Eph 1:4), indicating the precedence of God over all things. As we shall see, the word "before" in this context appears to indicate an ontological priority, not a "time before time", which would make little sense.

Certain Christian authors of Neoplatonic and Origenist tendencies maintained that the world has existed from always because an uncreated primary matter existed. They asserted that, just as the Word is eternally begotten, so too the world is eternally created. Meister Eckhart maintained that "as soon as God was, he immediately created the world [...]; when God was, when God begot the Son co-eternal with himself and completely equal in all things, he also created the world" (DS 951–953). These theses were rejected by the Constitution *In agro dominico* of John XXII in 1329.

In both Arius and Eckhart, confusion arises between creation and generation, between time and eternity. Scripture contains passages that seem to support their positions, insofar as certain references to Wisdom present it precisely on the boundary between God's eternity and the time of creatures (cf. Sir 24:9; Eccl 3:11; Prov 8:22), so that it might appear to be seen as the point of union between both dimensions of existing. It is necessary, therefore, to clarify the question from a speculative standpoint.

### 4.2.1. The Notions of Time and Eternity

The Christian idea of creation introduces a novelty into the conception of the relationship between time and eternity. While the Creator is eternal, his creatures are mutable and subject to temporality. The importance of time in the Christian vision of the history of salvation is bound to an astonishing affirmation: God crossed the boundary between eternity and time because, in his Incarnation, the eternal entered into time.

The vision of time in Greek philosophy is customarily described by the image of the circle, the symbol of perfection; in it, the notions of permanence, immortality, necessity, divinity, and so forth prevail. Whereas for Plato time is the "moving image of eternity"374, the version in the sensible world of ideal atemporality, for Aristotle time is an aspect of natural reality:
> When we perceive the "now" as one [...] then no time seems to have elapsed, because there has been no change. But when we perceive a "before" and an "after", then we say that time exists. For this is what time is: the number of movement according to the before and after375.

Bound to this is the understanding of primary matter as existing from always; time is as eternal as the movement it measures, and is thus divine, necessary, because it is eternal, constant, and cyclical. Immortality and eternity are explained as uninterrupted duration. Time cannot be changed, destiny is inexorable, everything is under the dominion of time (*chronos*). "Greek man perceives himself and the world primarily as nature, not as history"376.

Christianity transformed the circle into a line segment: time is no longer conceived as eternal, but as history, with a beginning, a development, and an end. The NT, besides *chronos*, has recourse to other terms: *kairos*377, which designates an occasion of special significance, such as conversion (cf. Mk 1:15), death (cf. Mk 13:33), or temptation (cf. Lk 4:13); and *pleroma*, which expresses the fullness of time in the Incarnation (cf. Gal 4:4) and in recapitulation (cf. Eph 1:10).
> In Christianity time has a fundamental importance. Within its dimension the world is created, within it the history of salvation unfolds, having its culmination in the "fullness of time" of the Incarnation and its goal in the glorious return of the Son of God at the end of time378.

Time is not easily grasped: "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I want to explain it to someone who asks me, I do not know"379. Augustine sought to complement the objective Greek vision with a description that included interiority: time as a distension of the soul (*distensio animi*). It is not important to consider things merely in their passing, but for what they leave in the soul of each person.

Everyone experiences moments of particular intensity that "fly by", and others that, because of their heaviness, seem endless. Because the spirit transcends time at a natural level, it has the capacity to connect the three temporal dimensions: we bring the past into the present through memory; and we anticipate the future through hope. The present, which centralises our experience of time, slips from our hands: what was future a moment ago becomes past in a few seconds. The experience of *tempus fugit*380 places us in the condition of desiring a fulfilled existence in a stable present, which coincides with Boethius's description of eternity: "The simultaneous and perfect possession of interminable life (*interminabilis vitae tota simul et perfecta possessio*)"381.

Augustine maintains for the first time that creation was made *with* time and not *in* time. This distinction is important for understanding the created character of temporality, which renders it absurd to ask what God was doing before creating382. There is no time before creation, because time is the measure of mutable things, and God is immutable; in him there is no before and after. God alone is eternal; the world is not, contrary to what certain philosophers thought. Everything that exists and is not God is created by him; the creature cannot be co-eternal with its Creator. Augustine adopts the Stoic idea that in the beginning there existed the seeds of what would later develop (*rationes seminales*). Thus he explains that God created at the beginning and that he continues to act in the present world and in history383.

Just as creation in its temporality shows itself to be open, so too it can be touched by the irruption of eternity and enter into some manner of participation in it. The Christian vision of time as history, with a past, a present, and a future, is illuminated by divine action, which qualifies it as original creation, continuous creation, and new creation; time marks the principal stages of salvation history as creation, redemption, and eschatological consummation384.

Sharing the Augustinian thesis on the created character of time, Thomas Aquinas would distinguish himself by defending a peculiar position, original in his era, which seems to take a step back towards Greek philosophy, insofar as he maintains that, from the perspective of reason, it is possible to think of a world created from eternity. The complexity of the question warrants a detailed study.

### 4.2.2. The Medieval Controversy on Creation ab initio temporis

Thomas affirms that the creature has non-being prior to being (*prius habet non esse quam esse*)385. This priority of non-being can be understood naturally (*ex nihilo*) or temporally, namely, that the creature previously was not and then began to be in time. While the first aspect is demonstrated by reason, the second point is presupposed by faith386. From the perspective of reason, the question of the beginning is aporetic, because it is repugnant neither to a creation *ab aeterno* nor to a creation with a beginning in time; the aporia is resolved only by Revelation, which affirms that the world began to exist.

This position was the subject of controversy among the Angelic Doctor's contemporaries387. There were two extremes: those who maintained, from a philosophical standpoint and against the faith, that the world is necessarily eternal; and those who, from a theological standpoint, affirmed that the beginning in time is a demonstrable truth.

Thomas's argumentation in rejecting both positions rests on two principles: the impossibility for man to penetrate the divine will, which by an act of radical freedom creates the world; and the creation of time as the measure of becoming, which supposes the existence of a becoming entity and excludes the possibility of an empty time prior to creation388.

The first derives from divine creative freedom. It is not necessary that the world has existed from always, and, for the same reason, it is not necessary that it began to exist. Reason finds itself confronted with an antinomy that it cannot resolve.

Facing those who claim to demonstrate the eternity of the world, Thomas underlines the contingency of creation, freely produced by the First Principle. Although in God being and operating are identical, it does not follow that creation is *ab aeterno*, because the effect is such as the divine will wills it to be. Both divine acting and divine being are eternal, but their effect is produced according to the direction of the intellect and the command of the will, which operates not according to the modality of its being but of its purpose, and thus God decides not only that the thing should be, but also when it should be. Now, since time, which is the measure of the created entity in becoming, was created with the world, because God "produced simultaneously creature and time"389, it follows that "time is created with the beginning of being, and it makes no sense to speak of a time preceding creation"390.

The same principle serves the Angelic Doctor to counter those who, relying on the contingency of the world, claim to prove rationally its temporal beginning. The confusion consists in conceiving creation as a transition that presupposes a time prior to being. But creation is not a movement; whereas in natural processes a beginning indicates that there is a *prius*, "behind" creation there is only the free divine creative act. What is necessary for the *ab alio* of creation is the relation391.

The affirmation of the world's beginning is a revealed truth, for which there are no necessary rational arguments, but reasons of probability. Sensitivity in avoiding confusion entails a positive evaluation of the capacities of reason (hence the critique of the theologians on this point) and at the same time the recognition of its limits (the aporia of an exclusively rational framing of the beginning), which implies the openness of reason to what transcends it (the credibility of the revelation of the world's beginning).

For Thomas, binding temporality to the notion of creation implied setting aside Aristotle's philosophical presuppositions, as the Franciscans had done, who considered themselves faithful followers of Augustine, in controversy with the Aristotelians of the Latin Averroist school, who upheld the world's unlimited duration. Whereas the Bishop of Hippo had to defend the rationality of Scripture against the Manichaeans and point out the incompatibility of Neoplatonic emanationism with Christian faith, Saint Thomas confronts Aristotelians who, by distorting the Stagirite's presuppositions, render them incompatible with the Christian faith, so that, in polemicising against them, one ran the risk of abandoning the path of philosophical reflection, in which a legitimate Aristotelianism is compatible with faith.

According to the Angelic Doctor, essences are so suited to exercise the act of being that, once received, they abide in it. He could find in the world created by God "no necessary reason to suppose that it had not always existed. There is no existential defect in the solid ontological structure of the created universe. It is made by God as solid as if it had existed *ab aeterno* by virtue of its own necessity"392. By virtue of its divine origin, the world is so anchored in being that, of itself, it neither began to be nor will cease to be, nor does it account for its beginning or its end. Only Revelation gives certainty of divine action in both moments.

Regarding this controversy, Ratzinger maintained that speaking of an infinite duration or not is not decisive, because the only thing the affirmation of faith really determines is that "the being of the world always includes dependence on eternity, it being unimportant to know how old the world is or how long it will endure. As long as the world endures it is becoming in contrast to Being, and for this reason exists as temporal in dependence on eternity"393.

### 4.2.3. Creation and History in the Perspective of Saint Thomas

This distinctive position of Aquinas plays an important role within the whole of his thought394, as demonstrated by the energy with which he defended it throughout his life. The distinction between the problem of creation and the problem of the beginning allows one to grasp the openness of creation to history395. Thomas has recourse to the *exitus–reditus* schema, originating from Neoplatonism396, effecting a "Christianisation" in the philosophical order, through the introduction of freedom at the origin of things; and in the theological order, through the vision of creation as the beginning of a real event in time and with time397. Only thus is the schema of emanation and return applied to real history, and the succession of events, fulfilling itself upon the foundation of a good event, can compose an economy of the historical realisation of salvation.

The true meaning of creation in Thomas is, therefore, the real God of salvation history. "In its fundamental notion, the historical dimension can be defined as the conjunction of two freedoms: that of the Creator and that of creatures, or, if one prefers, as an encounter between persons: the eternal God and the creature in time"398.

Some see in the *exitus–reditus* schema a triumph of Neoplatonism at the expense of salvation history; to us it seems simply that Thomas avails himself of reason, while indicating its limits, for the sake of understanding Revelation399.

In this sense, it is fitting to understand the importance Thomas accords to arguments from convenience and congruence (*argumenta convenientiae*)400. This way of proceeding is due, in the last analysis, to the conviction of the non-necessary but free character of history, which is open to a salvific revelation of God that surpasses, without contradicting it, the realm of necessity of the created order. The mystery of the Trinity appears congruent with our idea of God, yet transcends it; the mystery of the Incarnation of the Word is fitting with the perfection of creation and history, but is not necessary; the mystery of creation in the beginning shows itself congruent with divine goodness, without being necessary.

According to some, scientific research points us towards the affirmation that the world had a beginning in time; the hypothesis of the eternity of the world becomes increasingly unsustainable. Pannenberg thinks, however, that the beginning of the world is not self-evident. After a historical review of the question taking into account the development of the sciences, he sets forth his position, grounded in the idea of congruence or convenience: if it belongs to the finitude of every process in time to have a beginning, and we can consider the world as a unitary whole, it seems that the world as a whole had a beginning. Pannenberg seeks to avoid the extremes both of a physical necessity and of a total exteriority of faith.
> The affirmation of a temporal beginning cannot be grounded directly upon analyses or conclusions of a physical character. Nor can it be derived, however, from the nature of the finite, since it depends on the possibility that such a concept of finitude can then be applied to the world in its entirety. The affirmation of a beginning cannot be justified either as a revealed truth external to the phenomenon of nature that we can observe and reconstruct. It needs an empirical foundation, which is what modern cosmology can in fact offer401.

We are not in the realm of absolute certainty typical of a necessary demonstration. Aquinas's position, as happens elsewhere in his works, places itself, in defending the rationality of creation, in the worst possible case—namely, that the world should have a duration *ab aeterno*. This in no way detracts from the fact that Saint Thomas was convinced of the temporal beginning of the world; a fact which, without imposing itself with rational necessity, showed itself with a high degree of convenience, because it accords better with the freedom and goodness of God. "Other things receive their existence from him, because they have not always existed. It is clear, moreover, that he does not act by necessity of nature, and that his power in acting is infinite. Therefore it was most fitting for the divine goodness to give created things a beginning to their duration"402.

### 4.2.4. The Temporal and Historical Character of Being According to Ratzinger

Ratzinger starts from the affirmation that the biblical conception of creation is dynamic, salvific-historical; the term *bara’* designates both the creative act and the historical acting of God403. Created being is intimately bound to time; the nexus between creation and conservation understood as *creatio continua* becomes evident. "If being presents itself as becoming and time, then creation and conservation are not two separate acts succeeding each other, parts of a drama in two acts, but a single reality embracing all"404.
> In accordance with this, conservation or *creatio continua*, as theology puts it, is not simply an ahistorical process of a fixed influx of being; rather, this so-called conservation is a process intertwined with a reasonable historical goal, and from that goal it preserves its form. One sees here, at the roots of ontology, the orientation towards eschatology, the salvific-historical relation of the entire world405.

The openness of creation towards history implies that ontology opens itself to the free acting of God. Against the Enlightenment claim of a philosophy of history that believes it finds meaning within itself, Christianity maintains that there is no rationality immanent to history, but that within it human freedom and divine freedom interact.

The dynamic conception of created being sees openness towards the future in relation to God's eternity. "Time asks eternity, as the eternal creative present, to be kept within it"406. Temporal being stands in a relationship of dependence upon the eternity of God which, without subjecting itself to becoming or identifying with it, conserves it.

The dynamic understanding of being is the point of encounter between biblical faith in creation and the evolutionary vision. In a fixist worldview, eternity is conceived in opposition to time, as occurs with Aristotle, for whom God is the eternal power that attracts the entire universe and in this attraction keeps it in motion—a characteristic of his power without which he can exist in his being. God "has no relationship with the world, but the world does have a relationship with him, because it is drawn by *eros* and kept in motion"407. Time is the eternal succession of the revolutions of the celestial spheres, which move from eternity and towards eternity through the *eros* of God, and thus does not enter into the internal constitution of the cosmos.

This vision has little to do with the biblical idea of creation. Faith has the capacity, as an understanding of the origin of being, to renew itself in the face of successive cosmologies; one can embrace a vision in which time, like space, is constitutive of being. "In this seems to consist the authentic radical shift of the Darwinian worldview: that the factor of time is also constitutive, because being is conceived as an unfolding in becoming; being and time enter into one another"408.

Just as Thomas Aquinas had to confront the Aristotelian image of the world, so today theology confronts an evolutionary vision of the world, concluding that the first article of faith is compatible with temporality and the dynamic, progressive character of being. This optimism must be tempered by adding that "the biblical understanding of the world is determined in a core manner through the category of time, though not in so total a sense as it appears to be in the evolutionary understanding of the world"409.
> Creation understood in an evolutionary sense means that being must be understood from within as temporal [...]. Creation is therefore not a distant beginning, made once and for all so that from then on the entire block of being is there; nor is it a beginning broken into various stages; rather, creation is an affirmation that refers to being as such. It refers to being as temporal and in becoming. Temporal being in becoming is, as such, entirely embraced by the single creative act of God, which is comprehensible to us only along the line of time and temporal prolongation410.

Empirical assertions do not belong to the sphere of faith, but faith addresses itself to the question of meaning, which concerns the realm of fundamental human questions. To believe Christianly in creation means understanding the world as originating from the Logos who in Jesus Christ entered into history411.

### 4.3. Providence: An Approach to the Question of Evil

The doctrine of creation and conservation in being leads to the consideration of divine providence.
> If creation means in general the determination of all reality by the Logos, the origin of all things from a meaning, then discourse about conservation simply signifies the total dependence of all that exists on this meaning, or more precisely the permanent abiding of the world within the divine meaning. This permanent anchoring of creation to the meaning that envelops it, faith calls providence412.

To avoid confining creation to the initial moment, without influx upon history, one must understand conservation and God's providential action in history along the line of creation413.

Certain forms of religiosity, especially of the prayer of petition, can constitute distortions of the true image of the providential God of the Bible. There are those who, while all goes well, scarcely remember God, but when they experience life's difficulties, turn to God as though he had forgotten them, almost blaming him for the evils that occur, and demanding of him a kind of emergency intervention to end their discomfort. This is the conception of the "God of the gaps" (*deus ex machina* / *Dio tappabuchi*), a true caricature of the God revealed in the Scriptures.

Beyond these representations, more or less widespread at a popular level, it is legitimate to inquire into the possibility and actuality of divine interventions in our world. For some, God cannot intervene as a matter of principle; others maintain that he intervenes only at certain moments. Finally, there are those who think God is constantly in action to correct the course of a weak and now corrupted creation. Either God never intervenes, as the deists say; or he intervenes only sometimes, as actualism says; or in reality he is always intervening, as Luther and Malebranche maintain. If this is so, what has become of the autonomy and freedom of creatures in their acting? The question is to understand whether creation and providence are distinct, as deism maintains, or identical, as actualism asserts. Let us address these aspects, beginning with the Scriptures.

### 4.3.1. Providence in the Old and New Testaments

The nexus between the doctrine of conservation and that of providence can be noted in the fact that in the Bible God's action upon creation is exercised in the manner of authority and care: the authority of the Creator ("governs") expresses itself as the solicitude of the Father ("guards")414. The ancient creeds expressed the aspect of governance with the Greek term *Pantokrator*, which must be understood in harmony with the image of the Shepherd: "The Lord is my shepherd; I shall not want" (Ps 23 [22]:1). Divine care and authority are presided over by wisdom, which "reaches mightily (*fortiter*) from one end of the earth to the other, and governs the universe well (*suaviter*)" (Wis 8:1).

The properties of the creative act (power, wisdom, love) are the same for divine providence, which extends over all and over each: "The eyes of all look to you, and you give them their food in due season. You open your hand, you satisfy the desire of every living thing" (Ps 145 [144]:15–16). "I love you, O Lord, my strength. The Lord is my rock and my fortress and my deliverer, my God, my rock, in whom I take refuge, my shield, and the horn of my salvation, my stronghold" (Ps 18 [17]:1–2 [2–3]). God is the unshakable foundation upon which human existence rests: "You hold my lot" (Ps 16 [15]:5). The book of Job manifests this twofold dimension, insofar as faith in the God who creates and sustains all is the basis of confidence in his care for us: "Behold, God is exalted in his power; who is a teacher like him? [...]. For he draws up the drops of water; they distil his mist in rain, which the skies pour down and drop on man abundantly [...]. For by these he feeds peoples; he gives food in abundance" (Job 36:22, 27–28, 31).

These notions are confirmed and enriched by the NT. On the one hand, Jesus reiterates them in his teachings:
> Look at the birds of the air: they neither sow nor reap nor gather into barns, and yet your heavenly Father feeds them. Are you not of more value than they? And which of you by being anxious can add a single hour to his span of life? And why are you anxious about clothing? Consider the lilies of the field, how they grow: they neither toil nor spin, yet I tell you, even Solomon in all his glory was not arrayed like one of these. But if God so clothes the grass of the field, which today is alive and tomorrow is thrown into the oven, will he not much more clothe you, O you of little faith? Therefore do not be anxious, saying, "What shall we eat?" or "What shall we drink?" or "What shall we wear?" For the Gentiles seek after all these things, and your heavenly Father knows that you need them all. But seek first the kingdom of God and his righteousness, and all these things will be added to you (Mt 6:26–33; cf. Lk 12:24–31).

> Are not two sparrows sold for a penny? And not one of them will fall to the ground apart from your Father. But even the hairs of your head are all numbered. Fear not, therefore; you are of more value than many sparrows (Mt 10:29–31; cf. Lk 21:18).

On the other hand, Jesus reveals himself as the incarnate providence of God. He appropriates the Old Testament image: "I am the good shepherd" (Jn 10:11). He himself provides for people's needs, multiplying the loaves and fishes, changing water into wine, healing the sick, restoring sight, and so forth. His care also manifests itself in the compassion he feels for the multitudes as a whole and for individual persons, showing solidarity with misfortunes, sufferings, and anxieties, and bearing them upon himself, to the extreme of laying down his life.

The NT manifests that the same Word who created, sustains, and guides all things, once incarnate, shows his providential care in an overt and visible manner. With these considerations we have not yet reached the deepest level of revealed truth. That level is touched when we observe that the destiny to which we are called, the Kingdom that man must seek and actualise in the world, is a plan of God the Father preceding creation, because in Christ "he chose us in him before the foundation of the world, that we should be holy and blameless before him. In love he predestined us for adoption to himself as sons through Jesus Christ, according to the purpose of his will, to the praise of his glorious grace" (Eph 1:4–6). "The predestination of man and the world in Christ, the eternal Son of the Father, confers upon the entire doctrine of divine Providence a decisive soteriological and eschatological character"415 and situates it at a level distinct from the ancient conception of fate.

### 4.3.2. Metaphysics and History, Between the Negation of Providence and Providentialist Actualism

The Bible speaks of God's providence both within the horizon of his creative work and in that of salvation, in continuity with what was stated in chapter 2 regarding the bond between the creative and salvific work in the single plan conceived in Christ. God's salvific interventions in history help us understand that he never abandons the work of his hands.
> Providence is, as it were, a constant and unceasing confirmation of the work of creation, manifesting the constant and uninterrupted presence of God as Creator in the whole of creation: a presence that continuously creates and continuously reaches the deepest roots of all that exists416.

Always in continuity with the preceding reflections, a balance is maintained here between the metaphysical and the salvific-historical dimensions. The Christian concept of providence is not merely a chapter of religious philosophy, more or less linked with the concepts of Greek wisdom (such as *pronoia*), but the response of Revelation to the great questions of every human being, possessing the completeness of a vision that does justice to reason by anchoring it in the firmest certainties of faith.

From the beginning, providence has been a fundamental truth of faith. The First Vatican Council, in the Dogmatic Constitution *Dei Filius* (on Catholic faith), teaches:
> God, by His providence, protects and governs all things which He has created, reaching mightily from one end of the earth to the other, and ordering all things sweetly (Wis 8:1). For all things are naked and open to His eyes (Heb 4:13), even those things which are yet to occur by the free action of creatures (DS 3003).

The Council thus intended to counter the errors of its day, particularly deism which, while admitting God's existence and the creation of the world, maintains that he does not concern himself with creation. Deism directly attacks the truth of divine providence, effecting a drastic separation between it and the work of creation.

A correct understanding of creation in its link with conservation and providence makes evident the reasonableness of faith in God's constant sustaining and governance of creation. Having excluded the view according to which God cannot act in the world, other questions open up: does this mean that creatures, particularly free ones, are not true and real causes? If all that occurs is governed by a loving God, why does evil exist? Does God always act, only sometimes, or must we resign ourselves to the idea that he never acts?

### 4.3.3. Divine Concursus and Secondary Causes: Providence and Freedom

God conserves creatures in their being and in their acting. Things not only endure, but act in time, producing effects. Spiritual creatures act freely and their works are their own, not God's, who sustains and is the guarantor of their freedom (*concursus*).

Following Thomas, one must distinguish the creative causality of God from the causality of creatures—the primary cause (*causa prima*) and secondary causes (*causae secundae*)417, which do not stand at the same level of being: the former is transcendental, while the latter are predicamental. Each is cause a hundred percent in its own order; there is no contradiction between acknowledging that without God we can do nothing (cf. Jn 15:5) and that our actions are ours and not God's. The Church's doctrine:
> on the one hand, says that creaturely being is a being through participation, so that it does not have being of itself, but possesses it only as received; on the other hand, it simultaneously affirms that this received being is given in autonomy, with a substantiality and activity of its own418.

That God governs all things does not mean that he fails to respect the autonomy of creation. The image of a God who is too "intrusive" is typical of frameworks that confuse creation and providence by virtue of an actualist conception of continuous creation. Opposed to this image is a deist conception according to which God does not intervene in history (or, at most, intervenes only at critical junctures, acting as a "God of the gaps"). While actualism emphasises continuous divine intervention in creation, deism insists on divine transcendence and the consequent autonomy of the created order. The former binds creation and providence too closely together; the latter separates them excessively.

According to Aquinas, God, in governing the world, makes use of the action of secondary causes, respecting their proper sphere. This is a manifestation of his goodness, which wills to employ creatures to bring creation to its end419. Since God guides all things, secondary causes in some way serve the designs of providence. Creatures, especially free ones, are called to collaborate, to cooperate with God in the fulfilment of his plan420. Being free, they can also oppose the will of God or behave in a manner contrary to it. Does this mean that providence is not fulfilled?

### 4.3.4. The Question of Evil: Its Providential Divine Permission in View of Greater Goods; Trust in God; The Definitive Light of Christ

One must confront the presence of evil in a good world, created by a good God. Saint Thomas argues that divine providence does not exclude evil from things. God does not cause it, but he does not suppress the operation of secondary causes, which can fail; the defect in an effect of a secondary cause is due to that cause, and is not imputable to the first cause421.

It is customary to speak of divine "permission" of evil; this means that evil is subject to providence422. "God, in his omnipotence [...] being supremely good, would never permit anything of evil to exist in his works unless he were so omnipotent and good as to bring good even out of evil"423. The Angelic Doctor affirms that God prefers to draw good out of evil rather than not permit any evil to exist at all. To God's goodness belongs the permission of evils and the eliciting of greater goods from them. God is the "universal provider of all being (*universalis provisor totius entis*)"424.

By granting men a participation in providence, God respects their freedom even when they act evilly. It is striking that God, in his omnipotent providence, can draw goods from the consequences of evil; history can be interpreted according to this key of reading425, in connection with the Pauline words: "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good" (Rom 12:21).

Great works of literature describe how at times evil seems stronger than good; nevertheless, it is difficult to eradicate the natural human tendency to trust that in the end good must triumph and in fact does triumph, because love is more powerful. The experience of evil places before our eyes the tension between divine omnipotence and goodness in God's acting in history, which receives a mysterious answer in the event of the Cross of Christ.
> To the question: how to reconcile the evil and suffering in the world with the truth of divine Providence, no definitive answer can be given without reference to Christ. On the one hand, indeed, Christ—the Incarnate Word—confirms [...] that God is with every man in his suffering [...]. Jesus Christ reveals that this suffering possesses a redemptive and salvific value and power [...]. Thus the truth of Providence acquires through the "power and wisdom" of the cross of Christ its definitive eschatological meaning [...] in the perspective of "predestination in Christ", that is, in the perspective of man's vocation to eternal life426.

The definition of evil as privation (*privatio boni*), and not as a constitutive part of the universe, is a Christian notion427. To affirm that evil exists, but has no substance, means overcoming the dilemma in which succumb, on the one hand, those who deny the reality of evil in the name of God's goodness and infinite power; and on the other, those who deny God's goodness and infinite power in the name of the reality of evil. The truth of creation, carried to its ultimate consequences, implies maintaining that there is no privation without a being that finds itself deprived of something—that is, there is no evil without a good that supports it. Pure, absolute evil is impossible. Good is more fundamental and more powerful than evil428.

To the question "if God exists, whence comes evil?" (*si Deus est, unde malum?*), Saint Thomas replies: "If evil exists, God exists. For evil would not exist if the order of good were taken away, the privation of which is evil. And this order would not exist if God did not exist"429. The world, even though imperfect, with evil within it, exists; therefore God exists. Evil needs a subject in which to reside (there would be no blindness without someone deprived of sight). Without any paradox, evil proves the existence of God, because it reveals that the existence of a contingent subject postulating the existence of the Absolute.

Reading Scripture, we become aware of the fact that there are explicit texts on providence, above all in the sapiential books, and also implicit texts of a salvific-historical kind. In the latter, one can have the impression that God intervenes at times, and at others does not; that there are gaps, moments in which God seems to hide himself.

What are we to say? Is there a contradiction in the Bible? In reality, it is not so. Providence is constant, real, and continuous. It is human beings who do not always know how to regard what happens as God's providence. God manifests himself both in what seems good, and also by permitting evil and suffering. The OT teaches that one must seek and find the traces of God in all things, as indicated by Job's response to his wife after losing children, possessions, and health: "Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?" (Job 2:10). Gregory the Great comments:
> Good, that is, God's gifts, both temporal and eternal; evil, that is, present misfortunes, regarding which the Lord says through the mouth of the prophet: "I am the Lord, and there is no other; I form light and create darkness, I make well-being and create calamity" (Isa 45:5, 6–7) [...]. "I form light and create darkness", because, when externally through scourges darkness gathers, internally, in this school, light is kindled. "I make well-being and create calamity", because peace with God is restored for us when things created good, but not well desired, are turned into what is evil for us, into scourges. By sin we set ourselves against God; therefore it is just to return to peace with him by means of scourges; when any created thing that is good turns into sorrow for us, corrected, we humbly return to peace with the Creator430.

The apparent ambivalence of providence receives its definitive answer in Christ crucified, the "power of God and wisdom of God" (1 Cor 1:24). "If through faith we discover this power and this 'wisdom', we find ourselves upon the salvific ways of divine Providence [...]. Divine Providence is thus revealed as God walking alongside man"431.

This consideration has consequences for Christian spirituality. It is a liberation for man to know himself governed by a providential God who is Father, and not by a blind fate. The testimony of the saints (cf. CCC 313) encourages Christians to discover the necessity of understanding that "all things work together for good for those who love God" (Rom 8:28). The daily acceptance of providence is an act of theological hope, which does not exclude the responsible exercise of freedom, which enters into God's plan.
