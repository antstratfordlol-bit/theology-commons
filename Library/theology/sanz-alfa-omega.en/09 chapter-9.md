## Chapter 9 Intermediate Eschatology: Death and Purgatory {#sanz-alfa-omega.9}

Hitherto we have set forth the themes that constitute the object of Christian hope, both the events of a universal character and the definitive states of man. It remains to examine that shadow zone between our present situation and the end of the world, which will be brought about by another event: the end of the earthly life of each one of us.

This is the so-called intermediate eschatology, the "no man's land" between death and the Parousia. If these two were to coincide, many problems would be resolved immediately—indeed, they would not even exist. Since the end of the individual does not in fact coincide with the end of all things, this raises questions to which theology must provide an answer: when is retribution received, at the moment of death or at the end of time? If in the former case, what meaning is there in awaiting the end? If not, what happens while one waits? Is there a place of purification after death, or must one wait for the universal judgment? What becomes of the human being who no longer has a body and is not yet risen?

We divide this final part into two phases, which will examine, first, death as the end of the earthly pilgrimage and as the moment of retribution, and hence the so-called particular judgment. Secondly, we shall address the question of otherworldly purification as a reality distinct from the punishment of hell.

In the next chapter we shall deal with the foundation of all this, namely the necessity of affirming the immortality of the human soul, not as an isolated doctrine in its own right, but as the presupposition of the resurrection. This final part constitutes an authentic ecumenical challenge, wherein we shall be able to note the points of shared faith alongside others that remain the subject of controversy among Catholics, Orthodox, and Protestants.

### 9.1. Retribution After Death

Death appears to be a natural fact; it occurs throughout the cosmos at all levels, given the contingency of created beings. Every human being perceives himself as a "being-towards-death"1028, which is one of the few certainties one can have in this life. When we consider death close at hand, our perspective alters, and we see it as the worst of evils, because it signifies the disappearance of the greatest good, which is life. Our innate striving towards fullness is cut short by death; it happens as a matter of fact, yet it ought not to exist. Here lies the metaphysical problem, which arises from the existential predicament and which peoples of all ages have sought to confront by developing their religious sense.

There are various ways of evading this "contradiction". One is the trivialisation of death, characteristic of our age, which manifests itself in avoiding any discourse concerning it or in reducing it to a mere technical issue. "Death must become a fact so material, so customary, so common, as no longer to arouse any metaphysical problem"1029. Whereas the Christian liturgy prays for deliverance from sudden death, so that we may have time to prepare ourselves, today many would desire a death without time to think about it. "They wish to slam the door in the face of metaphysics before it can present itself"1030. Death is dehumanised, and with it life as well; paradoxically, death provides us with the key to discerning what man truly is.

Another way of concealing the dramatic character of death is to regard it as a positive reality. This is what occurs in spiritualist outlooks, in which the separation of the soul from the body is deemed an authentic liberation, to be evaluated positively as the commencement of a fuller life. Such is the tenor of Platonic thought, which finds existential expression in the philosophical martyrdom of Socrates for the sake of justice1031.

In the Christian perspective, death has been assumed by Christ. When a person dies, one often hears it said: "His departure is a loss". The death of a human being is unjust, because each individual is an unrepeatable being, and therefore his departure entails an authentic impoverishment1032. If this is true for us, the death of Christ was the most unjust event in history, because his life displayed an extraordinary exemplarity, acknowledged even by those who hold a negative view of Christianity. His death was a tragedy; and yet, in God's design, Jesus' sacrifice brought us redemption. We are placed before a precise question: is death the worst of evils, or does it possess something good, which Jesus took upon himself in order to save us? Is death something alien to God's plan for man and the world, or does it belong to his design of creation and salvation?

We shall see, first, how Scripture speaks of death in relation to sin, in order then to understand what it means that Christ assumed death. There will follow an analysis of retribution at the moment of death and hence of the particular judgment, concluding with a reflection upon the presence of this individual *eschaton* within the life of the person.

### 9.1.1. The Relationship Between Death and Sin in the Bible and in Tradition

### 9.1.1.1. Death and Sin in the Bible

Sapiential reflection makes an observation: "For everything there is a season... a time to be born, and a time to die" (Eccles 3:1-2). The mortal condition unites us with the other creatures that inhabit this world:

> For the fate of the sons of men and the fate of beasts is the same; as one dies, so dies the other. They all have the same breath, and man has no advantage over the beasts; for all is vanity. All go to one place; all are from the dust, and all turn to dust again. Who knows whether the breath of man goes upward and the breath of the beast goes down into the earth? (Eccles 3:19-21).

The reference to dust and to the breath of life recalls Genesis. In creating man, God gave him that breath of life which enables him to live and preserve life. It was a gift conditioned upon obedience to the commandment: "Of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil you shall not eat, for in the day that you eat of it you shall surely die" (Gen 2:17). This, lamentably, came to pass, and thus death entered the world as a consequence of sin: "In the sweat of your face you shall eat bread till you return to the ground, for out of it you were taken; you are dust, and to dust you shall return" (Gen 3:19).

This consequence did not belong, according to the Book of Wisdom, to the original plan: "God did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living. For he created all things that they might exist, and the generative forces of the world are wholesome, and there is no destructive poison in them, and the dominion of Hades is not on earth. For righteousness is immortal" (Wis 1:13-15). If God did not create death, and death is present in the world, then the cause of death is not to be found in Him: "For God created man for incorruption, and made him in the image of his own eternity, but through the devil's envy death entered the world, and those who belong to his party experience it" (Wis 2:23-24). Sirach is explicit: "From a woman sin had its beginning, and because of her we all die" (Sir 25:24).

The reference to the Genesis narrative indicates the dynamic of freedom, of choosing between good and evil, that has marked history from the very beginning. Here is the key to the salvation history of the people of Israel:

> See, I have set before you this day life and good, death and evil. If you obey the commandments of the Lord your God which I command you this day, by loving the Lord your God, by walking in his ways, and by keeping his commandments and his statutes and his ordinances, then you shall live and multiply, and the Lord your God will bless you in the land which you are entering to take possession of it. But if your heart turns away, and you will not hear, but are drawn away to worship other gods and serve them, I declare to you this day, that you shall perish (Deut 30:15-18)1033.

The tragedy of human existence is to find oneself condemned to a death brought about by man himself through his own conduct. In distancing himself from God, he finds no one left to save him, and thus consoles himself not only by enjoying, while he can, the fleeting goods of this life, but also—what is even worse—by inflicting suffering upon the righteous man who trusts in God:

> For they reasoned unsoundly, saying to themselves, "Short and sorrowful is our life, and there is no remedy when a man comes to his end, and no one has been known to return from Hades [...]. Come, therefore, let us enjoy the good things that exist, and make use of the creation to the full as in youth [...]. Let us lie in wait for the righteous man, because he is inconvenient to us and opposes our actions" (Wis 2:1-2, 6, 12)1034.

Confronted with the sufferings of this life, even the righteous man who trusts in the Lord experiences moments of doubt and uncertainty regarding the hereafter:

> If I look for Sheol as my house, if I spread my couch in darkness, if I say to the pit, "You are my father," and to the worm, "My mother," or "My sister," where then is my hope? Who will see my hope? Will it go down to the bars of Sheol? Shall we descend together into the dust? (Job 17:13-16).

The mission of the prophets likewise consists in arousing trust that only the God who creates life possesses the power to eradicate evil and death from the world and from history: "He will swallow up death forever, and the Lord God will wipe away tears from all faces, and the reproach of his people he will take away from all the earth; for the Lord has spoken. It will be said on that day, 'Lo, this is our God; we have waited for him, that he might save us'" (Isa 25:8-9).

The Pauline perspective confirms the strong nexus between sin and death found in the Old Testament. With reference to the Adamite event, he declares: "Therefore as sin came into the world through one man and death through sin, and so death spread to all men because all men sinned" (Rom 5:12). And again: "For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 6:23); "the sting of death is sin" (1 Cor 15:56).

Paul introduces the comparison between the deed of Adam and that of Christ, which is far more powerful: "If, because of one man's trespass, death reigned through that one man, much more will those who receive the abundance of grace and the free gift of righteousness reign in life through the one man Jesus Christ" (Rom 5:17). Christ opposes to the reign of death his own reign of life (cf. Rom 5:21) and resurrection: "For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead. For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ shall all be made alive" (1 Cor 15:21-22).

Christ's opposition to death and sin possesses a distinctive characteristic: He destroyed them in a singular manner, by taking them upon himself, by making them his own: "We were reconciled to God by the death of his Son" (Rom 5:10); "For our sake he made him to be sin who knew no sin, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God" (2 Cor 5:21).

### 9.1.1.2. Death and Sin in the Tradition of the Church

The biblical texts echo the mortal condition in which humanity finds itself, a de facto condition that appears natural. Scripture offers an explanation of the reason for this, which does not imply a passive resignation to the natural condition of contingent creatures, but rather traces it back to the initial events of human history—the turning away from God—as the causes of the historical situation of mortality. "In the NT death is not a natural process, but a historical event, bound up with the conception of man's existence as a sinner"1035.

The Church's first authoritative pronouncement came within the context of the Pelagian controversy. Maintaining that sin is not transmitted, but is merely a free personal act, the Pelagians asserted that we are all born into the same mortal condition in which Adam found himself, because death is connatural to man. This assertion was condemned in Canon 1 of the Sixteenth Council of Carthage in 4181036, the conclusions of which were approved by Pope Zosimus in his *Epistola tractoria*. The great Fathers and Doctors, together with subsequent ecclesial teaching, confirmed this perspective: there exists a causal nexus between the occurrence of sin and the onset of our mortal condition1037.

In our eyes, this condemnation might seem exaggerated; one might think that death is genuinely natural to man. Our physical and biological knowledge informs us that the life of contingent beings is by nature limited by virtue of their material condition. In a Teilhardian perspective, mortality, as an expression of evil, would form part of the dynamic of an evolving world journeying towards the Omega point. Nevertheless, if one accepts that death is natural, given its close bond with sin, then sin too would be natural to man. This appears to be the conclusion of the understanding of evolution espoused by a certain Teilhardism1038. Is death a natural phenomenon, or is it a consequence of human sin?

In his commentary on Genesis, Augustine explains in what sense death is natural and in what other sense it is the result of the first man's sin:

> For before he sinned, Adam's body could be called mortal in one respect and immortal in another: mortal, that is, because it was able to die; immortal, on the other hand, because it was able not to die. For it is one thing not to be able to die, as is the case with certain natures created immortal by God; and quite another thing to be able not to die, in the sense in which the first man was created immortal; this immortality was granted to him not from the constitution of his nature, but from the tree of life. After he had sinned, Adam was driven away from the tree of life, with the consequence that he was able to die, whereas, had he not sinned, he would have been able not to die. Adam was therefore mortal by the constitution of his natural body, yet immortal through a gift bestowed upon him by the Creator. For if the body was natural, it was certainly mortal inasmuch as it could also die, even though at the same time it was immortal inasmuch as it could also not die. In truth, only a spiritual being is immortal by the fact that it can by no means die, and this quality is promised to us only for the future, that is to say, in the resurrection1039.

Julian of Toledo teaches that "the origin of death, for man, is sin"1040, adopting Isidore of Seville's etymological interpretation of the word *mors*: "It was called death (*mors*), either because it is bitter (*amara*), or from the 'bite' (*morsus*) of the first man [...]. From bite, therefore, derives the very term death"1041. And he reads the Augustinian explanation in his own manner: "The first man was created in such a way as to be able to be immortal and mortal. Not so immortal, however, as to be able to avoid death in the event of sin, nor to such a degree mortal as to have to undergo it even without having committed it"1042.

Relying in part upon Augustine's position and upon the Aristotelian conception of the soul as *forma corporis*, Thomas Aquinas explains:

> The form of man is the rational soul, which of itself is immortal. Wherefore death is not natural to man on the part of his form. The matter of man, on the other hand, is a body composed of contrary elements, and this is necessarily corruptible. Wherefore on this side death is natural to man. But this corruptibility of the human body derives from a necessity of the matter [...]. Yet this is not a condition required by the form: for, were it possible, the form being incorruptible, it should rather have been provided with incorruptible matter [...]. God, who created man, is omnipotent. And therefore, by a gratuitous gift, he removed from man at his first creation the necessity of dying which derived from such matter. But this benefit was withdrawn on account of the fault of our first parents. Hence death is natural on account of the condition of matter, but it is a punishment on account of the loss of the divine gift that preserved from death1043.

An analogous conception of human nature, in the diverse states (integral and fallen) in which it is found in history, makes it possible to maintain without contradiction that death is both natural and a consequence of sin.

One might reflect upon what would have happened in the earthly paradise in the absence of sin. Since our first parents did not enjoy eschatological fullness, a transition to definitive communion with God was necessary. Scheffczyk suggested that "if one wishes to connect the moment of bodily death with the paradisiacal state, one can understand the natural end of man as a death in friendship with God without the terrors of sinful death"1044. John Paul II similarly affirms that, according to the faith of the Church, "without sin, the end of the earthly trial would not have been so dramatic"1045. The *nexus mysteriorum* points towards Mary, conceived without sin, who experienced a death different from ours (the Dormition, as Eastern tradition calls it). She underwent no bodily corruption, but was assumed into heaven, and thus this dogma sheds light upon what might have occurred in the beginning1046.

### 9.1.2. Death Assumed by Christ and the Death of the Christian

### 9.1.2.1. The Death of Jesus and of the Christian in the NT

Cullmann pointed out the difference between the death of Socrates and that of Jesus. Whereas the philosopher discourses serenely upon the immortality of the soul in the moments preceding his death, Jesus experiences anguish in Gethsemane and upon the cross: "In the days of his flesh, Jesus offered up prayers and supplications, with loud cries and tears, to him who was able to save him from death" (Heb 5:7)1047. The death of Jesus is not simply that of a great man who dies for a just cause; rather, "it is seen and proclaimed as the unique and fundamental saving event"1048.

The various statements of the New Testament converge in the proclamation that this death is "for" someone ("for us": cf. Rom 5:8; 1 Cor 15:3; "for many": cf. Mk 10:45; 14:24; Heb 9:28; Jn 10:15; "for you": cf. 1 Cor 11:24). In the death of Jesus, God breaks the power of sin, inasmuch as He, by entering into solidarity with mankind, deprives death of its power. God sought out man even in death, and offered Himself as the foundation of life, stripping from him the impulse towards sin and bestowing upon him his grace, the new life with Christ1049, who "himself likewise partook of the same nature, that through death he might destroy him who has the power of death, that is, the devil, and deliver all those who through fear of death were subject to lifelong bondage" (Heb 2:14-15).

This perspective was already present in the Servant of the Lord songs in the Old Testament, in which:

> death and illness are no longer solely punishments for sin, but can rather be the path of him who belongs to God—the path on which he, suffering for others, opens to them the door to life, and, as one suffering in their stead, becomes their saviour [...]. Death no longer appears as definitive annihilation and punishment, but as a force of purification and transformation1050.

"This does not signify an exaltation of death: God, by entering in Christ into the realm of death, cancelled and conquered it"1051. Here we are far removed from Buddhist religiosity, in which the thirst for life must be extinguished because it is considered the cause of our suffering. Death is the "last enemy" (1 Cor 15:26) that must be destroyed. "Its end signifies the advent of the definitive and exclusive kingdom of God"1052.

The transformation of death wrought by Christ has profound significance for the Christian, who experiences physical death as something that lies before him, but which must become a source of hope in the God who "gives life to the dead" (Rom 4:17). Faith imparts the certitude that, in Christ, "death is swallowed up in victory" (1 Cor 15:54), and thus the dialectic of the "already and not yet" of salvation is revealed anew1053. The Christian understands in faith that "his suffering and his death are a suffering and dying with Christ, so that, precisely in suffering and in death, he experiences communion with God"1054. Only in this light can one venture to pronounce a positive judgment upon death: "For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain" (Phil 1:21). In the end, what truly matters is to be with the Lord: "Whether the world or life or death or the present or the future, all are yours; and you are Christ's; and Christ is God's" (1 Cor 3:22-23); "If we live, we live to the Lord, and if we die, we die to the Lord; so then, whether we live or whether we die, we are the Lord's" (Rom 14:8); "neither death, nor life, nor angels, nor principalities, nor things present, nor things to come, nor powers, nor height, nor depth, nor anything else in all creation, will be able to separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord" (Rom 8:38-39)1055.

> The confrontation with physical death makes man aware of the fundamental structure of his own being. It places him before the choice of accepting the structure of "love", or of setting against it the structure of "power" [...]. The God who himself dies in Christ is the God who fulfils the structure of "love" beyond all expectation [...]; that power over which man has no disposal and which everywhere delimits his life is no blind law of nature, but a love that has given itself to him to the point of being immolated for him1056.

### 9.1.2.2. Interpretations of the Christian's Death in Light of Its Assumption by Jesus: Inner Power or Transformation?

Given that Jesus saved us by his death, and that death, as a transit to the definitive state, is a gain, it might seem logical to think that death possesses an inherently positive aspect that grants it a rightful place within God's original plan. On the other hand, the overwhelming majority of New Testament texts are unequivocal in their negative evaluation of death, which was assumed by Christ precisely in order to be transformed, because in itself it possesses no value. Here two lines of interpretation emerge.

#### a) The Final-Decision Hypothesis

According to a first interpretation, Jesus assumed death because it possesses an inner power for human self-realisation, which He rendered efficacious. His death surpasses that of Socrates in its quality of courage, loyalty, and love. The *kenosis* implied in his death is the reflection of a deeper *kenosis* within the Trinity itself. Christ's death is a Trinitarian act that generates grace. Along these lines, certain authors maintain that every human being enjoys in death a moment of perfect lucidity in which he is able to make his definitive choice for or against God. This is the "final-decision hypothesis", initiated by Glorieux and elaborated by Boros: "In death there opens up for man the possibility of his first fully personal act; it therefore constitutes the truly privileged locus for the dawning of consciousness, of freedom, of the encounter with God, and of the decision concerning his eternal destiny"1057. Rahner maintains that death constitutes by its nature personal self-realisation. Apart from the Heideggerian roots of this theory, its proponents advance certain theological reasons, aimed at showing that the moment of death, as in Jesus and by analogy with the choice of the angels, is the principal moment of human life, to the point that it might be regarded as a quasi-sacrament. The hypothesis appears coherent with the theory of the fundamental option and could be invoked to resolve difficult theological problems, such as the salvation of unbaptised infants and non-Christians.

One of the arguments adduced by the advocates of this hypothesis is Saint Thomas's citation of a dictum of Saint John Damascene: "Death is to men what the fall was to the angels"1058. It would seem that, in the final analysis, only the freedom of an angel could answer for an eternal destiny. Precisely this "renders the thesis problematic: it seeks to turn man into an angel and thereby tacitly judges the specific *conditio humana* to be unacceptable"1059; the problematic nature of the hypothesis is confirmed by the absence of empirical evidence and of a theological tradition supporting it.

This theory tends to concentrate eschatology, including universal eschatology, at the moment of death, which, as a fundamentally anthropological phenomenon without special consideration of divine intervention, becomes the juncture at which everything is decided. In confirming the fundamental option, relevance is stripped from ordinary life; salvation is decided in moments of exceptional intensity. In reality, salvation is not played out in a single instant, but across the span of one's entire existence. Christian common sense affirms that, ordinarily, a person dies as he has lived. This theory, which is rare today, tends towards a Platonic interpretation of death as liberation from matter1060.

#### b) The Reality of Death, Transformed by Christ

Another explanation maintains that in the New Testament:

> death is not something natural, willed by God, as it was for Greek thought, but something contrary to nature, radically abnormal, opposed to the divine intention [...]. Death as such is God's enemy. For God is life, the creator of life [...]. Behind the pessimistic conception of death lies an optimistic conception of creation1061.

Inasmuch as it is an evil, death has no efficient, final, or formal cause; at most, it has a deficient cause1062. If death can possess any value, this is due to the transformation it undergoes in the Paschal mystery of Christ, and it must therefore be understood in union with the resurrection. "With the death and resurrection of Christ, death ceased to be only death [...]. The reality of death remains; but it has entered into a new relationship with life; it has become the passage to a new life"1063.

To affirm that the death of Christ is victorious over death requires an openness to a dimension distinct from metaphysics:

> Since death could not swallow the Word without the body, nor could Hades receive him without the flesh, he was born of the Virgin, so that through the body he might descend into the realm of the dead [...]. It came to pass then that death approached him to devour him with its customary certainty and inevitability. It did not perceive, however, that within the mortal fruit it was eating, Life was concealed. It was this that brought about the end of the unwitting and reckless devourer. Death swallowed him without any fear, and he set life free, and with it the multitude of men [...]. Glory to you, who made of your cross a bridge over death. Across this bridge souls may pass from the region of death to that of life1064.

Jesus is entirely man and shares with us the natural fear of death; He "who is completely united with God, more united than any other man in the world, must feel the horror of death more than any other [...] 'Why have you forsaken me?'. At that moment he is truly in the hands of the great enemy"1065. If death is God's enemy, Christ's victory over it cannot consist in continuing to live as an immortal soul without dying.

> He can conquer Death only by genuinely dying, by entering into the very realm of Death, the great destroyer of life [...]. He who wishes to conquer someone must enter into his territory. He who wishes to conquer Death must die [...], losing the most precious good that God has bestowed: life itself1066.

The destructive power of death did not extinguish in Jesus his filial love for the Father, nor his love for mankind. It revealed instead the reality of the words addressed by Christ to the apostles: "Greater love has no man than this, that a man lay down his life for his friends" (Jn 15:13).

Voluntary acceptance out of love transformed death into a fountain of grace and redemption. Benedict XVI explains, in his meditation before the Holy Shroud, that it:

> acts as a "photographic" document, endowed with a "positive" and a "negative". And in truth it is precisely so: the darkest mystery of faith is at the same time the most luminous sign of a hope without boundaries. Holy Saturday is the "no man's land" between death and resurrection, but into this "no man's land" One has entered—the Only One—who traversed it bearing the marks of his Passion for mankind [...]. God, in Jesus Christ, shared not only our dying, but also our remaining in death. The most radical solidarity. In that "time-beyond-time", Jesus Christ "descended into hell" [...], remaining in death, he crossed the threshold of this ultimate solitude in order to guide us too across it with Him [...]; this is precisely what occurred on Holy Saturday: within the kingdom of death the voice of God resounded. The unthinkable happened: namely, Love penetrated "into hell": even in the extreme darkness of the most absolute human solitude we can hear a voice that calls us and find a hand that takes hold of us and leads us forth. The human being lives by the fact that he is loved and can love; and if love has penetrated even into the realm of death, then life has arrived there too1067.

God entered in order to illumine the darkness of death, and thus death no longer wields a maleficent power over man. By virtue of incorporation into Christ through Baptism, death is no longer a curse for the Christian, but a fountain of blessing, because it can be transformed into an act of obedient love towards God. "The death of Christ is the seriousness of the God who loves; our death is the seriousness of the man loved by God"1068. The Christian lives by dying daily to himself (cf. Mt 16:24-25; Mk 8:34-35; Lk 14:27); such is the meaning of mortification1069, of detachment from worldly goods (cf. Lk 12:16-21), which brings happiness inasmuch as it enables one to live in love towards God and others. While the fear of death is strongest in those who tend to accumulate possessions and cling to them, the Christian strives to lay up treasures in heaven (cf. Mt 6:19-20). This does not mean that he may not also experience a natural anguish, but the faith and charity infused by God into the soul cast out fear (cf. 1 Jn 4:18), enabling him to understand that he need no longer fear death, because Christ has redeemed it by entering into it (cf. Heb 2:15).

### 9.1.3. Full Retribution After Death

### 9.1.3.1. Biblical Indications

The time for meriting ends with this life, because after judgment that possibility will exist no longer. There will be a different state, of definitive reward or punishment for the works performed in this life. We can glimpse this in the Old Testament: "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might; for there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going" (Eccles 9:10). This revelation becomes clearer in the New Testament: "For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive good or evil, according to what he has done in the body" (2 Cor 5:10). These texts entail two problems. The first is whether, to receive reward or punishment, one must wait for the universal judgment or whether, since after death one enters the realm of God, there takes place at that very moment a determination of divine retribution. The second is whether this implies the existence of another judgment, personal and individual, at the moment of death. These problems do not impede acceptance of a teaching that passes from Scripture to the Fathers of the Church: namely, that one must repent of sins while on earth, because after leaving this world we shall no longer be able to perform works of penance. When a man dies, he is like a felled tree trunk that remains where it falls1070.

Thomas is of the opinion that in Scripture no obstacle is to be found against the idea of full retribution immediately after death; indeed, he maintains that there are texts in favour of such a perspective, both in reference to the wicked who immediately after death go to hell (cf. Job 21:13; Lk 16:22), and to the good who immediately enter heaven (cf. Lk 23:43; Rev 2:7)1071. The Angelic Doctor mentions other Old Testament texts that describe the present state as a time for meriting or demeriting, comparing it to military service (cf. Job 7:1; Lev 19:13; Joel 3:4 Vulgate), and indicating that, after the time of struggle, immediate reward or punishment is due according to how one has fought1072.

For Saint Thomas, it is the Apostle Paul himself who affirms that full retribution "is granted immediately after this life"1073. It would run contrary to the Apostle's intention to say that, upon the destruction of the body, we would obtain the real dwelling in heaven not immediately, but only in hope, since while we live on earth we already possess the certainty of one day possessing the heavenly dwelling; and we already possess it in hope, according to another Pauline expression: "For in this hope we were saved" (Rom 8:24). The desire to be with Christ, expressed in other Pauline letters, signifies that "the Apostle hoped to reach heaven immediately after the dissolution of his body"1074.

### 9.1.3.2. Historical Discussions

"Immediately after death, human souls receive punishment or reward according to their merits"1075. Aquinas reasons *a contrario*, showing that "there is no reason why punishment and reward should be deferred, since the soul is capable of both. Therefore, as soon as the soul is separated from the body, it receives reward or punishment for what it did while in the body"1076. The objection that one must await the resurrection for full retribution in the body does not hold:

> Merit and guilt do not belong to the body except through the soul, since no act has the character of merit or demerit except insofar as it is voluntary. Therefore it is just that both reward and punishment should derive to the body from the soul, since they never belong to the soul on account of the body. Hence there is no reason why, in punishing or rewarding souls, one should await the re-assumption of bodies1077.

These arguments are judged sufficient to exclude the error of certain Greeks who "assert that before the resurrection of bodies souls neither ascend to heaven nor are cast down into hell"1078. To what was the Angelic Doctor referring?

In the various stages of the patristic era there had been a diversity of views on this theme1079. The Apostolic Fathers and Cyprian spoke of a full retribution immediately after death, whereas Justin, Irenaeus, and Tertullian maintained that one must await the final judgment for the definitive separation between righteous and sinners, even though they accepted that their situation after death is different. There is unanimity regarding the reward granted immediately to martyrs. The period of waiting between death and resurrection was termed *refrigerium interim*, an expression due to Tertullian in his Montanist period: every soul "in that place experiences its torments and its consolations (*supplicia et refrigeria*) [...]. Why should you not believe that the soul is punished or refreshed in the underworld awaiting (*in inferis interim*) the judgment, in one sense or the other, in the anticipation and hope of it?"1080.

The majority of fourth-century Fathers defended the doctrine of full retribution immediately after death (the two Gregories, Jerome, Chrysostom); likewise, later on, did Gregory the Great and Julian of Toledo. There are authoritative exceptions, such as Ambrose and Augustine, who speaks of a hidden place of waiting for rest or punishment. "The time between man's death and the final resurrection holds souls in mysterious abodes (*abditis receptaculis*), according as each has merited rest or affliction, in proportion to what it received as its lot while living in the flesh"1081.

From the eighth century onwards, full retribution after death became a commonly accepted doctrine, though not without exceptions. In the West, the best-known exception is Saint Bernard; in the East, the figures of Photius and Theophylact contributed to a substantial portion of Byzantine theology, from the Middle Ages onwards, upholding full retribution only at the end of time.

Let us summarise the reasons invoked by various patristic and medieval authors, Eastern and Western, to sustain the delay of retribution. First, the descent of Jesus into hell and the opening of the gates of heaven transformed the notion of Sheol as an undifferentiated state of waiting, but did not alter the temporal dynamic of retribution, which must await the end to be full1082. The second reason invoked by the Fathers is that in Scripture eschatological centrality belongs to the resurrection and the day of judgment; hence, as Augustine maintains, if the triumph of Christ has not been fully revealed, neither is the reward completely consummated. Thirdly, it is understandable that certain Fathers refused to speak of an immediate retribution because this had been championed by authors of Gnostic tendency, who concentrated everything upon the liberating moment of death1083.

### 9.1.3.3. Dogmatic Definition

During the Avignon papacy, John XXII (†1334) was aware of the widespread diffusion in the West of the idea of full retribution immediately after death. Following a study of the question in the Fathers, and adhering to certain teachings of Saint Bernard, he expounded, in a series of sermons in Paris:

> his new understanding regarding a strictly Christological intermediate phase of the lot of the deceased: only with the universal judgment and the resurrection will the definitive Trinitarian status replace this intermediate stage; until the last day the saints are beneath the altar (Rev 6:9), that is, beneath the protection and in the consolation of the humanity of Christ. Only after the judgment will they emerge from the veil of his humanity and ascend above the altar to see God face to face1084.

These declarations gave rise to a controversy entangled with politics, and thus his Franciscan adversaries, especially William of Ockham, accused him of heresy.

Before his death, John XXII retracted his position, as made public by Benedict XII1085, who in 1336 promulgated, with the express intention of issuing a binding definition, the Constitution *Benedictus Deus*. Therein he taught that the souls of the departed who have no need of purification do not remain in an intermediate stage, but:

> immediately after death (*mox post mortem*) [...] even before the re-assumption of their bodies and the universal judgment, following the ascension of our Saviour the Lord Jesus Christ into heaven, have been, are, and will be in heaven, in the kingdom of heaven and celestial paradise with Christ,

in such wise that they:

> have seen and see the divine essence with an intuitive vision and, even more, face to face, without the mediation of any creature by way of object seen (DS 1000)1086.

Although this text might appear distant from the vision of the Fathers:

> one must not overlook that the central substance of the affirmation is Christology, so that it ultimately signifies an explanation of what "the ascent of Christ into heaven" means, and simultaneously an interpretation of his Passion, whose meaning is revealed precisely in the ascent of Christ. According to this text, the ascension of the Lord is an essentially anthropological-historical fact. It confirms that, from now on, heaven is no longer a closed heaven. Christ is in heaven, and that means that God is accessible to man1087.

The process of Christianising Jewish ideas was not yet closed in the era of the Fathers, given the diversity of views on the question, although a certain notion of an intermediate state would be preserved in the doctrine of purgatory. One may conclude that:

> the medieval doctrinal text remains faithful to the concept of the intermediate stage, in that it distinguishes between the personal definitive determination and the cosmic-historical determination of Christology (and at the same time of human destiny). In this sense, the text can in fact be regarded as a synthesis of the spiritual development of Patristics1088.

In response to objections concerning full retribution after death, whether in the patristic sphere or in later Eastern theology, it must be observed that it respects the principle of the "already and not yet", characteristic of Christian eschatology: against the Gnostics, not everything is concentrated at the moment of death, because neither the resurrection nor the universal judgment has yet taken place; yet neither is everything postponed to the end of time, because Christ is already risen, has already saved us, and with his Ascension has already thrown open the gates of the Kingdom. The final resurrection will change the condition of persons, but not their standing with respect to God. The dogmatic definition leaves room for the intermediate stage of those who are not yet completely purified.

### 9.1.4. The Particular Judgment

The doctrine of *Benedictus Deus* concerning full retribution immediately after death implicitly contains the affirmation of the existence of a personal judgment (termed "particular" to distinguish it from the "universal" judgment at the end of time), which is indispensable for effecting the separation between the righteous and sinners. The two truths are not necessarily linked: one could maintain the existence of a judgment at the moment of death while asserting that reward or punishment is postponed until the final consummation (this is Augustine's position). As a matter of logic, however, one cannot affirm that retribution takes place after death while simultaneously denying the particular judgment. While there has been a formal dogmatic definition regarding retribution, the particular judgment has not been the object of a specific formal decree, even though the Church has always taught it, including the most recent Council and the subsequent *Catechism*1089.

### 9.1.4.1. Some Objections

There has been no shortage of objections to the concept of a particular judgment. First, it is pointed out that, whereas the vast majority of biblical texts refer to the universal judgment, clarity is lacking with regard to the particular judgment, as witnessed by the absence of consensus among the Fathers of the Church: it would seem to constitute a superfluous duplication of the single judgment of God of which Scripture speaks.

Relying on the universal and general character of judgment in the Bible, it has also been argued that, since man is a social being who lives in community and is conditioned by it, he could be judged only as a member of that society, not as an isolated individual. His sins would not be purely individual, inasmuch as they possess an inherent social dimension.

This line of reasoning is inverted by those who, in the opposite direction, contend that at the moment of death each person undertakes a self-judgment. This would not be an encounter with a God who judges from without, but the moment of definitive maturation with respect to one's own truth, which impels man to place himself in the state that befits him. Each man, within his conscience—which is sacred—shapes his own identity for eternity1090.

### 9.1.4.2. Biblical, Patristic, and Scholastic Indications

Before responding to these arguments, it is necessary to examine the data of Revelation. The texts concerning full retribution after death, such as the parable of poor Lazarus or the episode of the good thief, attest that the definitive situation of each person is clarified immediately after death. A judgment of a personal character occurring at the moment of death may also be discerned in the parable of the talents (cf. Mt 25:14-30). The text of 2 Corinthians 5:10 ("For we must all appear before the judgment seat of Christ, so that each one may receive good or evil, according to what he has done in the body"), together with Hebrews 9:27 ("it is appointed for men to die once, and after that comes judgment") and others besides, refer to individual actions, without specifying the precise moment of the judgment.

Although during the patristic era there were doubts regarding immediate retribution, it must be recognised that the doctrine of the particular judgment is found in authors who doubted the former, such as Augustine, who believes that "souls are judged as soon as they depart from their bodies, before they come to that judgment by which they must be judged when their bodies have already been restored"1091.

Thomas teaches that, beyond the final judgment:

> there is also another judgment of God, according to which each person after death will be retributed as regards the soul according to his merit: for the righteous, released from their bodies, remain with Christ, as Saint Paul says, while departed sinners go to hell. One must not think, however, that this division takes place without the judgment of God, or that this judgment does not belong to the power of Christ1092.

The Angelic Doctor offers several arguments to justify the existence of this judgment. In the first place, he states that man's retribution is twofold: the one according to the soul at the moment of death, the other in the future re-assumption of bodies. Whereas the first occurs individually because each person dies alone, the second will take place simultaneously for all, because all will rise at the same time. Every retribution requires a judgment, and "therefore there must be two judgments: the first, particular, to assign to individual souls punishment or reward; and the second, universal, in which there will be assigned to all simultaneously, both in respect of body and of soul, what they have merited"1093.

Another argument, proceeding from the distinction between divine creative action and divine governing providence, affirms that two judgments must be distinguished:

> The first corresponds to the work of governance, which cannot be exercised without a judgment. And by this judgment each one is judged individually for his own actions, not only as regards himself, but also in relation to the governance of the universe: wherefore individual reward is deferred for the good of others, as the Epistle to the Hebrews notes (11:39), and the punishments of one are ordered to the advantage of others. It is therefore necessary that there be also a certain universal judgment, which corresponds instead to the first production of all things in being: so that just as then all beings proceeded immediately from God, so the world may receive its ultimate completion, each person receiving at the end what is personally due to him1094.

The third argument is grounded upon the distinction between the personal and social dimensions of the human being, which requires a twofold dimension of divine judgment:

> Every man is at once a single person and part of the whole human race. Therefore he must undergo two judgments. The first individual, which will occur after death, when each "will receive the recompense of the works done while in the body" (2 Cor 5:10): though not totally, since he will be recompensed not in the body, but only in the soul. The second judgment must concern him instead inasmuch as he is part of the whole human race [...]. Therefore when the universal judgment of the entire human race takes place through the total separation of the good from the wicked, each person will consequently be subjected to judgment. Nevertheless, God will not judge "twice the same thing", since he will not inflict two punishments for a single sin; but the punishment that before the judgment had not been completely inflicted will instead be so in the final judgment, with the torment of the wicked both in body and in soul1095.

### 9.1.4.3. Particular and General Judgment: Self-Judgment?

Thomas's arguments serve to clarify the doctrine of the particular judgment in the face of the objections raised. We are not confronted with an unnecessary duplication of judgment, nor with an ethical individualism that ignores the social dimension. We are simply confronted with the distinctive character of human nature, which possesses both a personal and a social (and hence ecclesial) dimension, both of which come into play at the moment of the highest anthropological truth—the definitive encounter with God in judgment.

The objection of duplication does not stand, inasmuch as judgment, rather than being a mere legalistic verdict, is the manifestation of truth. Rather than asserting that we are judged twice, it must be emphasised that, whereas immediately after death there is a manifestation according to the truth of one's own conscience, at the end of time that same revelation will take place before all creation.

> Every man exists within himself and at the same time outside himself; each exists simultaneously in others; what happens to the individual has repercussions upon all humanity, and what happens in humanity happens to the individual. Hence "Body of Christ" signifies that all men form a single organism, and that consequently the destiny of the whole is the personal destiny of each individual. Although in death, with the conclusion of his earthly activity, the life of man is decided in that he is now judged and his destiny is fulfilled, nevertheless his definitive place can be assigned to him only when the whole organism has been perfected and the entirety of history has been lived and suffered to the very end1096.

The notion of self-judgment seeks to highlight the importance of the person's conscience in determining his destiny, as would seem to be deduced from certain texts of Augustine and Thomas that speak of an active role of the subject in judgment1097. The aim is to avoid an extrinsic view of judgment—namely, that it be perceived as imposed by God from without, lacking any correspondence with the inner state of the conscience.

To avoid arbitrariness in God, however, the concept of judgment is attenuated when it is reduced to a purely subjective personal decision. Conscience is the proximate and ultimate subjective norm of morality, but it is not supreme1098, because it has its source and foundation in God; only the Creator, who is Truth itself, can judge with justice. It is not man who is the remunerator; God will retribute according to the truth of the conscience, and therefore there will be no arbitrariness, because God alone knows good and evil. The theory of self-judgment fails to see sin as an offence against God and salvation as God's gift; instead, it conceives human fulfilment in a reductive, autonomous, individualistic, and non-dialogical manner. "The particular judgment and the universal or final judgment, despite all possible distinctions, are again two aspects or phases, ultimately homogeneous, of a single process"1099.

### 9.1.5. The Presence of Death in Life

"*Media vita in morte sumus*. In the midst of life we are in death" (*Antiphona pro peccatis*). There is no need to experience extreme situations to grasp that the prospect of death forms part of our daily life, as this Gregorian chant observes.

> The phenomenon of death reveals [...] the objective condition of man during his life [...]. In this "anticipation" of death, man is able to perceive the fundamental situation of his life: through his own fault, he lives in death. Death, therefore, has power over life, and in this sense it is an ever-present reality. "Spiritual" death and "physical" death constitute, inseparably, the reality of the life of sin1100.

This awareness opens the way to the only perspective that offers a certain hope of victory.

> In accepting death, which is ever present throughout the whole of existence, man matures for true life, for eternal life [...]. Just as man's dying cannot be confined to the mere moment of clinical death, so too participation in the martyrdom of Jesus does not begin only at the moment when someone is executed for his sake. Its fundamental form [...] consists in the daily readiness to subordinate the advantages of one's own existence to faith, truth, and justice [...]. Martyrdom with Christ, as the subordination of the "I" to truth, is identified with the movement of love1101.

This holds true in a pre-eminent way when Christians bear witness to their faith with their lives, as occurred in the beginning and continues today.

> The Stoic, indeed, departs this life without passion, whereas the Christian martyr dies inflamed with a holy passion for the cause of Christ, because he knows he is part of the great drama of salvation [...]. [Stephen] sees Christ, victor over death [...]. In this certitude, that the death through which he must pass has already been conquered by Christ, who likewise passed through it, he undergoes stoning1102.

Whoever lives with this spirit confronts the final moments with joy and good humour. When Saint John of the Cross was close to death, and was asked whether he desired anything, he replied: "Some asparagus, if there were any"1103. "A child of God fears neither life nor death, because the foundation of his spiritual life is the sense of divine filiation [...]. You and I must act and live and die as lovers, and thus we shall 'live' eternally"1104.

The Christian vision of death profoundly influences the understanding of suffering:

> Christian faith is directed towards life. It believes in the God of the living. Its goal is life; wherefore it affirms life in all its phases as the gift and reflection of God, who is himself life. It confirms the "yes" to life right down into the darkness of suffering. Even under these conditions, life remains a gift of God; even under these conditions, new possibilities of being and new meanings open up to man. This is why Christian faith does not admit the concept of a "life unworthy of being lived". Wherever there is life, it remains in all its shades a gift of God1105.

The desire to abolish suffering:

> would be equivalent to contempt for love and therefore to the devaluation of man himself [...]. The man who is unwilling to accept suffering and pain refuses life itself. Flight from pain is flight from life [...]. Only eternal life is the fitting counterpart to the problem of man's existence and death in this world1106.

### 9.2. Purification After Death

When we cited the definition of *Benedictus Deus*, we focused upon full retribution and the immediate vision of God, but we omitted that part of the text which specifies the reference to the "saints in whom there was nothing to purify when they died, and neither will there be in the future when they die, or if there was or will be in them something to be purified, once they have been purified after their death" (DS 1000).

The formula acknowledges that he who has received a favourable sentence may not yet be in a condition to welcome the reward immediately. This opens the space for reflection upon an intermediate state in which "purifications and healings are also possible, which bring the soul to maturity for communion with God" (SS 45). If holiness belongs essentially to God, and we human beings, called by Him to share in his holiness, remain in this life marked by sin, it is logical to conclude that the transitory state of purification *post mortem* will be the condition of the vast majority of people1107.

The understanding of the intermediate state has not been uniform throughout history; on the one hand, there exists a diversity of conceptions between the Christian East and West; on the other hand, the vicissitudes of the late Middle Ages proved decisive for the rejection of purgatory by the Protestant Reformers1108.

Appealing to the fact that the definition of the dogma took place in the late medieval period, critics have raised the objection that this doctrine constitutes an "invention" of the Church, brought about by altered social conditions (the rise of the bourgeoisie), as well as aimed at augmenting clerical power over consciences and raising revenue through indulgences1109. From there to regarding purgatory as "an alimentary question"1110—a matter of the sustenance of the clergy—is but a single step. The question of indulgences provoked a fierce reaction in Luther because of the manner in which they were preached: notorious is the case of that German Dominican who urged contributions to the financing of the new Saint Peter's Basilica by declaring: "As soon as the coin clinks in the coffer, immediately a soul flies out (of purgatory). It is certain that with the clinking of the coin in the coffer gains and avarice can increase, whereas the suffrage of the Church rests on the decision of God alone"1111.

We can draw from this a positive aspect, which is the social and ecclesial dimension of this doctrine, translated into the centuries-old practice of praying for the dead, grounded upon the reality of the communion of saints across the three states of the Church: "Until the Lord shall come in his majesty [...] some of his disciples are pilgrims on earth, some have finished this life and are being purified, and others are in glory" (LG 49; cf. CCC 954).

We shall begin with the biblical testimony, in order then to analyse the historical events that led both to the definition of the dogma in dialogue with a different Eastern understanding of purification, and to the denial of this same dogma by the Reformers. Only then shall we offer reflections on certain theological aspects, concluding with a connection to the present life.

### 9.2.1. Otherworldly Purification in Scripture

### 9.2.1.1. The Biblical Context

There are not many biblical texts that speak explicitly of a state of purification after death. Nevertheless, that of purgatory is a doctrine coherent with three truths amply attested in Scripture: the holiness of God, who admits no imperfection into his presence; the sinful, not fully purified condition in which so many find themselves at the end of life; and the unity of the Church, which provides a mysterious solidarity that allows for the purification of its sinful members1112.

The holiness of the God of the Bible is bound up with the call to holiness addressed to his people, and entails the necessity of a condition of purity in order to draw near to Him. In the Old Testament, the concept of ritual purity is prominent and is linked to that of interior purity. In the New Testament, the promise of the vision of God arouses the hope of the believer, who "purifies himself as he [God] is pure" (1 Jn 3:3). Jesus unites both in the sixth Beatitude: "Blessed are the pure in heart, for they shall see God" (Mt 5:8).

Man senses the need to be purified because he is conscious of the power of sin in his life. This awareness is unmistakable in the Old Testament: "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me [...]; Behold, I was brought forth in iniquity, and in sin did my mother conceive me" (Ps 51 [50]:3, 5). In the New Testament, Paul expresses this reality in a pressing and existential manner:

> For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do. Now if I do what I do not want, it is no longer I that do it, but sin which dwells within me. So I find it to be a law that when I want to do right, evil lies close at hand [...]. So then, I of myself serve the law of God with my mind, but with my flesh I serve the law of sin (Rom 7:19-21, 25).

Since sin is constantly lying in wait in this present life, it appears that purification after death will be necessary.

Pauline teaching on the Church as the Body of Christ subsequently blossomed into the doctrine of the communion of saints1113:

> God has so adjusted the body [...] that there may be no discord in the body, but that the members may have the same care for one another. If one member suffers, all suffer together; if one member is honoured, all rejoice together. Now you are the body of Christ and individually members of it (1 Cor 12:24-27).

This communion entails the fact that the sufferings of one can serve for the good of others: "In my flesh I complete what is lacking in Christ's afflictions for the sake of his body, that is, the Church" (Col 1:24). Hence, the solidarity of the body makes possible participation in the purification of its members, which takes place through prayer for others, living or deceased, within the communion of the Church.

### 9.2.1.2. The Old and New Testament Texts

As regards the Old Testament, the clearest text is found in the Second Book of Maccabees, which recounts how, in gathering up the bodies of the fallen after battle:

> they found under the tunic of each one sacred tokens of the idols of Jamnia, which the law forbids the Jews to wear. And it became clear to all that this was why these men had fallen. So they all blessed the ways of the Lord, the righteous Judge, who reveals the things that are hidden; and they turned to prayer, beseeching that the sin which had been committed might be wholly blotted out. And the noble Judas exhorted the people to keep themselves free from sin, for they had seen with their own eyes what had happened on account of the sin of those who had fallen. He also took up a collection, man by man, to the amount of nearly two thousand drachmas of silver, and sent it to Jerusalem to provide for a sin offering. In doing this he acted very well and honourably, taking account of the resurrection (2 Macc 12:40-43).

The text speaks of the prayers of the people and the suffrages offered by Judas on behalf of those soldiers who, although they died fighting in defence of the chosen people, had sought the assistance of pagan deities, and thus sinned by not trusting completely in God. The fact that they died protecting Israel gives rise to the hope that their sin was not unpardonable, but could be expiated after death. They had not died in obstinate rebellion against God, but with sentiments of piety, while at the same time there remained within them something from which they needed to be freed. The sacrifices offered to obtain their purification are judged a noble deed, an expression of faith in the future resurrection.

One must not seek here an explicit mention of purgatory as a place, but rather grasp the value of prayer for the dead, which is the foundation of the development of ecclesial doctrine in East and West, as Ephrem and Augustine attest1114. The text is cited in reference to purgatory in several documents of the recent Magisterium (cf. LG 50, CCC 1032, SS 48).

The most characteristic New Testament text on the subject is found in the First Letter to the Corinthians, where Paul, employing the metaphor of a building, affirms:

> For no other foundation can any one lay than that which is laid, which is Jesus Christ. Now if any one builds on the foundation with gold, silver, precious stones, wood, hay, stubble—each man's work will become manifest; for the Day will disclose it, because it will be revealed with fire, and the fire will test what sort of work each one has done. If the work which any man has built on the foundation survives, he will receive a reward. If any man's work is burned up, he will suffer loss, though he himself will be saved, but only as through fire (1 Cor 3:11-15).

Paul is speaking of those who have built upon the foundation which is Christ, and not of those who have rejected that foundation. The text has an eschatological character, inasmuch as the Day to which it refers is that of judgment, wherein the test of "fire" will manifest the worth of what each has built. It speaks of an immediate reward for those whose work survives the test, whereas there will be punishment for those whose work is burned up. This punishment will not prevent salvation, which will arrive through "fire", that is, not without pain and anguish1115.

The most debated element of the text is the understanding of the fire, especially after Origen proposed the universal salvation of all through the fire of purification. The rejection of this position led in the East to a refusal to associate fire with post-mortem purification. A solution was subsequently found in the idea of the "intelligent fire" (*ignis sapiens*), in the sense that in Scripture the action of fire can be differentiated, as in Daniel 3:22-50, where the fire does nothing to the three young men while slaying the Chaldeans. The diverse action of fire was used as an argument by several Western Fathers (including Ambrose and Caesarius of Arles), and was not unknown in the East (Aphrahat in Syria), even though there the concern to oppose any confusion between purgatory and hell weighed heavily1116. The diversity of effects of the *ignis sapiens*, also termed *arbiter ignis*, paves the way for a Christological exegesis of the fire.

Tertullian sees an intimation of post-mortem purification in Jesus' counsel to settle with one's accuser lest one be cast into prison: "You will never get out till you have paid the last penny" (Mt 5:26). The eschatological interpretation of the passage would derive from the fact that the Greek word for "prison" (*phylake*) was the same term used to designate Hades (*hades*).

> The text means that the time between death and the resurrection is the time of imprisonment, in which the soul is offered the opportunity to "pay the last penny" and thus be freed for the resurrection: the stay in Hades here receives a new theological rationale, "which turns the interim into a purgatory necessary for all"1117.

We thus enter into interpretations that require us to turn our attention to the Fathers.

### 9.2.2. The Dogmatic Definition of Post-Mortem Purification in Dialogue with the Easterners

Two aspects correspond to the two texts considered: the value of prayer for the dead (treated in the Second Book of Maccabees) and the understanding of their post-mortem condition (described by Paul in the First Letter to the Corinthians). The two aspects are connected, inasmuch as the former is prompted by the latter. Whereas regarding the first element there is unanimity of understanding in the Western and Eastern traditions, the same is not true of the second. Their trajectories diverged, leading eventually to an effort at reunion through the dogmatic definitions of the medieval councils. Let us clarify the fundamental elements of this history.

### 9.2.2.1. Common and Divergent Elements in the Development of the Understanding of Post-Mortem Purification in West and East

In the West, the narrative of the passion of Saint Perpetua was attributed to Tertullian, recounting how her prayers bettered the condition in the hereafter of her young brother Dinocrates, who had died in early childhood. Regardless of the precise historical value to be ascribed to this work, it attests "how essential elements of the doctrine of purgatory developed from the ancient conception of the Jewish-Christian tradition, centring upon the idea of a suffering of the departed in the hereafter remediable by means of prayer"1118. The transition towards purgatory proper is found in Tertullian's treatise on the soul from his Montanist period. Purgatory is there understood as the prison that all must endure until the judgment (cf. Mt 5:25-26)1119.

Following Tertullian, Cyprian maintained a definitive salvation for the departed, especially the martyrs, immediately after death, while also affirming the existence of a definitive hell. For him, the problem was posed by weak Christians, the so-called *lapsi*, who had lacked courage in times of persecution yet subsequently desired to be reconciled with the Church. The text of Matthew 5:25-26 offered him the occasion to envision a possible continuation of ecclesial penance in the hereafter; thus Cyprian granted the *lapsi*, unlike the rigorists, re-admission into the ecclesial community. "With this interpretation of purification in the other world, the fundamental thought of the Western doctrine of purgatory is now clearly formulated"1120.

While the Western vision developed in continuity with Judaism, Clement of Alexandria, in the East, engaged in dialogue with the Gnostics and with Platonic and Stoic philosophy. The Greek concept of education (*paideia*) served him as a foundation for understanding the Christian's existence. Clement believed that the elect would not suffer the fire spoken of in 1 Corinthians 3:10-15, but that the remainder of humanity would: thus evil would be destroyed and men educated unto the good. There is thus effected a synthesis between the Gnostic idea of life as a process of purification and the purifying fire: the soul is transformed into a "body" (*soma*) of ever greater pneumatic perfection, so that in the transfigured subject soul and body merge. By this means, Clement explains life as a process of increasing spiritualisation, culminating in the resurrection of the dead, as well as the ecclesial character of purification, inasmuch as in all its phases the process of purification is always a process of mutual assistance.

Setting out from different paths, East and West coincide on two points: the value of ecclesial penance after death and the possibility of mutual assistance across the boundaries between this world and the next1121.

Clement's vision was developed, with certain modifications, by Origen, and was supported by others as well, including Gregory of Nazianzus. This proximity to the Origenist doctrinal sphere led to this opinion being rejected along with the idea of universal reconciliation (*apokatastasis*). This rejection took place decisively with John Chrysostom, who repudiated the interpretation of 1 Corinthians 3:10-15 in terms of Origenist apokatastasis, which in the meantime had become linked with the purifying fire. Chrysostom became the founder of the doctrine still taught in the Eastern Church:

> All will have to pass through an intermediate stage between death and resurrection; yet Hades contains "differentiated degrees of beatitude and misery" [...]. The Saints intercede on behalf of their brethren still living on earth and are invoked by them to intercede; while in their turn the living can obtain "rest and refreshment" for the souls abiding in Hades, by means of the Eucharist, prayers, and almsgiving—nevertheless, the "tribulation" which ought to be removed by such deeds is not interpreted as a suffering for the purpose of purification1122.

In Eastern theology the result was twofold: on the one hand, the existence of purgatory became linked to the delay of full retribution after death, generating confusion between purgatory and the *refrigerium interim*. On the other hand, purgatory was dissociated from purifying fire1123, because fire was seen as a necessary correlate of apokatastasis.

Prayer for the dead remained the undisputed common element1124, in particular the Eucharistic celebration, as Chrysostom's text indicates. In the Western sphere, this practice attained widespread diffusion through Gregory the Great, who directed that thirty Masses be offered for the monk Justus, so that he might be delivered from the torments of the fire1125. This is the origin of the so-called "Gregorian Masses", the number of which seems to have a remote foundation in the days of mourning for Moses (cf. Deut 34:7-8).

If prayers are of no avail for those who already enjoy the vision of God, nor for the damned, it follows that there are departed souls who can be aided in their purification1126. While the East avoided speaking of fire, the West encountered no difficulty in accepting the notion of a purifying fire1127. This notion passed into medieval theology, which, by virtue of the realism attributed to the fire, tended to localise it, and thus began to employ the substantive noun "purgatory"1128.

### 9.2.2.2. The Medieval Conciliar Pronouncements

The rejection of the realism of the Latin interpretation of purgatorial fire, which had led to its physical localisation, was the point of division in the colloquy held in 1231 or 1232 between a Latin friar and an Eastern bishop. To the latter, the doctrine of the fire of purgatory appeared identical to Origen's conception of a temporary hell1129. A controversy thus arose in which the Greeks accused the Latins of Origenism:

> Following Origen, the Latins also assign an end to hell, proposing I know not what purgatory in place of its torment. Entering this purgatory, those who have sinned—so they say—expiate their punishments until the last day. And this was maintained by none of the saints, for it destroys the words of the Lord, which establish that the punishments of hell are eternal just as life is eternal1130.

The Easterners had no difficulty in admitting an intermediate state for the souls of those who die with venial sins. Furthermore, they accepted the Church's practice of praying for them, given that prayers move God to forgive the debts of sinners.

In the reunion efforts with the Greeks at the Council of Lyons II (1274) and Ferrara-Florence (1439), the doctrine of purgatory became an obligatory point of reference. The profession of faith submitted to Michael Palaeologus at Lyons states:

> If those who are truly repentant have departed in charity before having satisfied with worthy fruits of penance for things done or things omitted: their souls are cleansed after death, as Brother John has clearly explained to us, with cleansing and purifying penalties; and for the relief of such penalties the suffrages of the living faithful profit them, that is to say, the sacrifices of Masses, prayers, almsgiving, and other works of piety (DS 856).

The Bull *Laetentur caeli* (1439) of the Council of Florence, in its endeavour to restore unity with the Greeks, solemnly defined:

> We define that the souls of those who were truly penitent and died in the love of God before having satisfied for sins and omissions with worthy fruits of penance are cleansed after death by purgatorial penalties (*poenis purgatoriis*); and that, for the relief of these penalties, the suffrages of the living faithful are profitable to them, namely, the sacrifice of the Mass, prayers, almsgiving, and other practices of piety, which the faithful are accustomed to offer for other faithful, according to the customs of the Church (DS 1304).

In both pronouncements, mention of fire and of physical location is carefully avoided. "The expression 'purgatory' is alien to the official doctrinal texts"1131, which speak of a "state" rather than a "place". "The dogma properly so called is, on the question of purgatory, of a great discretion"1132. One must therefore discard the opinion according to which purgatory emerged in the medieval era for economic and sociological reasons linked to the rise of the middle class and the consolidation of legalism. It represents, instead, an ancient conviction of faith. The Church had not formally defined it earlier because it had never previously been called into question. The divergence in its understanding led to the common dogmatic definition.

This does not preclude the fact that, in the course of history, erroneous or exaggerated representations developed, as well as abuses regarding indulgences, which were linked to purgatory. These factors, alongside the paucity of explicit texts in the Bible, explain the denial of the doctrine that took place in the sixteenth century within the Protestant Reformation.

### 9.2.3. The Denial of Purgatory by the Reformers and the Council of Trent

We have referred to the excesses in the preaching of indulgences, which aroused the indignation of the Reformers. Luther did not initially deny purgatory nor intercessory prayers for the departed; but "as soon as he clearly realised the import of his own doctrine of justification, he recognised the necessity of allowing the spirit of denial to reign here as well"1133. In the Leipzig Disputation of 1519, he stated that the existence of purgatory cannot be proved from Scripture, given that he rejected the canonicity of the Books of Maccabees. Encouraged also by Zwingli1134, in 1530 he attacked the existence of purgatory in his *Widerruf vom Fegefeuer*. This stance hardened in the Smalcald Articles (1537–1538), where he dismissed it as a diabolical delusion (*mera diaboli larva*)1135. Calvin concurred in the repudiation of this doctrine and in the critique of the practice of praying for the dead1136.

The principal motive was the understanding of justification realised by faith alone (*sola fide*): purgatory "contradicts the fundamental article that Christ alone, and not human works, can come to the aid of souls"1137. In Luther's perspective, salvation proceeds exclusively from God, whereas condemnation derives from one's own sins. Men can contribute neither to their own salvation nor to that of others. Justification does not effect an interior renewal that entails purification, but an external imputation of the righteousness of Christ1138. This paradoxical conception of man, who is considered simultaneously justified and a sinner (*simul iustus et peccator*), leaves unresolved the question of how "a sinfulness so deeply rooted, however much forgiven, can in the end be totally eradicated by the Spirit"1139. "Here we find ourselves confronted with the tragic contradiction that weighs upon Protestantism: the idea that one cannot safeguard the sovereign efficacy of God unless one asserts his exclusive efficacy"1140. It must be affirmed instead that we can cooperate with God, because we are created in his image, and this is essential for ecclesiology1141.

The Council of Trent reaffirmed in 1563 the earlier dogmatic definitions on purgatory:

> The Catholic Church, instructed by the Holy Spirit, in accordance with the Sacred Scriptures and the ancient Tradition of the Fathers, has taught in sacred councils, and most recently in this ecumenical council, that there is a purgatory, and that the souls there detained are helped by the suffrages of the faithful, and especially by the acceptable sacrifice of the altar (DS 1820).

Trent then added an exhortation to bishops, instructing them to:

> strive diligently that the sound doctrine of purgatory, handed down by the holy Fathers and sacred councils, be believed, held, taught, and everywhere preached,

in such wise that:

> the suffrages of the living faithful—namely, the sacrifices of Masses, prayers, almsgiving, and other works of piety which the faithful are accustomed to perform for the other departed faithful—be performed with piety and devotion according to the custom of the Church; and that those things which are due on their behalf from the foundations of testators or for any other cause be discharged by priests, ministers of the Church, and others whose duty it is, not perfunctorily and carelessly, but diligently and accurately (DS 1820).

While "the rejection of the doctrine, obscured by abuses, is repelled", at the same time "the protest of the Reformers against current practice and its abuses is welcomed and translated into a mandate for reform"1142.

The dogmatic foundation for the doctrine of purgatory is found in the decree on Justification, which was the subject of Session VI, held in 1547. In one of the final canons of the decree, the Council rejected the view of anyone who:

> asserts that to every repentant sinner after the grace of justification has been received, guilt is so remitted and the debt of eternal punishment so blotted out that there remains no debt of temporal punishment to be discharged either in this world or in the next in purgatory, before the entrance to the kingdom of heaven can be opened (DS 1580).

This implies that justification is not merely an external covering, but is interior to man, and therefore limited or imperfect, so that it can coexist not only with venial sins, but also with temporal punishment—namely, that which remains in man of sins already forgiven as to guilt, and which must be expiated during earthly life or after death before entering heaven1143.

In summary, in relation to the Eastern Churches, post-mortem purification is not a source of substantial dogmatic disagreement between Catholics and Orthodox. As regards dialogue between Catholics and Protestants, the efforts made in recent decades in the colloquies on justification have opened paths for future doctrinal reconciliation, which at present does not yet exist1144.

### 9.2.4. Theological Aspects of Purification After Death

In light of the history of dogma, it is essential to distinguish between the suffering of purgatory and that of hell: whereas the former is ordered towards salvation, and is therefore purifying and temporal, the latter lies outside salvation and is definitive. Both punishments stand in relation to sin, and this allows us to observe points of contact between them, which must not be exaggerated lest one fall into the confusions we have already addressed.

We must examine two questions that emerged during our historical exposition. First, the concept of temporal punishment, for which we shall draw upon the reflection of Saint Thomas; second, the Christological and relational dimension of post-mortem purification, with the assistance of the thought of J. Ratzinger. This deepening will lead us to develop certain spiritual aspects of the doctrine.

### 9.2.4.1. The Doctrine of Purgatory According to Thomas Aquinas

Thomas speaks of the existence of purgatory in the context of immediate retribution after death and the vision of God. "The rational creature, indeed, cannot be elevated to that vision unless it is entirely purified [...]. For of divine wisdom it is written that 'nothing defiled gains entrance into her' [...]. Now, the soul is defiled by sin"1145. Although Penance, like the other sacraments, serves this purpose, it can happen that "such purification is not completely accomplished in this life, but someone remains thereafter a debtor of punishment"1146. It becomes "necessary that souls be purified after this life, before attaining the final reward"1147, by means of sufferings analogous to the satisfactory penalties that would have been required in this life. "Therefore the souls of the good, who still have something to expiate in this world, experience a delay in obtaining the reward, until they have undergone the purifying punishments. This is the reason why we admit purgatory"1148.

Thomas views post-mortem purification as an anticipation of the vision of God, linking it both to divine holiness and to our sinful condition, which requires a purgative process that, if not fully accomplished in this life, must be completed in the hereafter, causing pain inasmuch as the reward of the vision of God is deferred. This is why one can speak of a "pain of delay" (*poena dilationis*), which cannot be equated with the "pain of loss" (*poena damni*) of hell, because its profound motive is the love of God1149. The same holds true for the purifying sufferings of the soul, which are the consequence of the *conversio ad bonum creatum* characteristic of sin, but are not comparable to the "pain of sense" (*poena sensus*) of damnation1150. Reflection upon the purifying fire bears witness to this1151. In any case, Thomas considers it reasonable to think of a "bodily" suffering of the soul1152.

How is the dynamic of post-mortem purification of the remnants of sin—that is, of temporal punishment—explained? With a doctrine which, as we have seen, would be adopted by the Council of Trent1153, Thomas evaluates the active and passive dimensions of purification and, within the latter, the role of other Christians and of the Church in general.

Regarding the active character, the Angelic Doctor considers that the punishment of purgatory may be termed voluntary in the sense that the soul realises it cannot reach its end without accepting it1154. The passive character of this acceptance is congruent not only with the state of the soul without the body, but also with the fact that after death the time for meriting has ended. Concerning this latter point, Thomas observes that it applies to the essential core of retribution, but not necessarily to its accidental dimensions; "thus in purgatory there can be a meritorious act as regards the remission of venial guilt"1155. This occurs "by means of an act of charity towards God that opposes the venial sins committed in this life", which, however, "does not merit the acquittal or diminution of the punishment as in this life"1156, given that the deceased can no longer merit.

Here the communion of saints intervenes through the ecclesial prayer for the departed. Precisely because what remains is not guilt, but punishment, and the capacity to satisfy for sins terminates with this life, it is possible that what the dead can no longer do, the living may perform in their stead, by virtue of the communion uniting all members of the Body of Christ1157. "The purification of the soul through the punishments of purgatory is nothing other than the expiation of the debt of punishment that impedes the reception of glory; and since the punishment that one undergoes can expiate the debt of another, as has been shown, nothing prevents one from being purified through the satisfaction offered by another"1158.

With this reflection, the fears that might arise on the Eastern side regarding a view that would ascribe an active expiatory capacity in the other life are dispelled. In the wake of Aquinas's reflection1159, the concept of "satispassion" (*satispassio*) was proposed—a purification received passively through the Church1160. Indeed, the rejection of purgatory is closely bound up with the understanding of justification and the role that the Church can play in its realisation.

### 9.2.4.2. The Christological and Relational Interpretation of Purgatory in J. Ratzinger in the Contemporary Context

Ratzinger considers that purgatory, in relation to sin, signifies:

> suffering to the very end for the legacy left behind, yet already in the certitude of being definitively accepted, though also in the unspeakable torment caused by the delay of the presence of the Beloved1161.

This language captures the best of the twentieth-century theological renewal—namely, the Christological perspective1162, according to which purgatory "is that necessary process of the spiritual transformation of man, which empowers him to be close to Christ, close to God, and to be united with the whole *Communio sanctorum*"1163. The Christological dimension illumines the two aspects previously considered: the state of the soul being purified, and hence the fire; and the participation of the whole Church in this purification, and hence prayer for the dead.

Regarding the first aspect, Ratzinger engages with the exegesis of those who, by identifying the purifying fire (cf. 1 Cor 3:10-15) with Christ the Judge, thought they had disposed of the doctrine of purgatory. On the contrary, "purgatory becomes a specifically Christian concept if it is understood in a Christological sense, namely that the Lord himself is the judging fire, who transforms man and conforms him to his glorified Body (cf. Rom 8:29; Phil 3:21)"1164. Since the *eschatos* is Christ himself, the Judge, and man does not lose his temporal condition, "it is impossible to distinguish between the Judge of the last day and the Judge who judges immediately after death"; with death, man enters his definitive destiny, plunging into the eschatological fire, whose "transforming moment" eludes earthly measures of time yet is not eternal, but a passage. Ratzinger adopts Balthasar's proposal that "the encounter with the Lord is this transformation, the fire that smelts him into that form free of dross which can become a vessel of eternal joy"1165. Those who read the paragraphs of *Spe Salvi* on this theme (nos. 46–47) can now grasp their origin. The Christological understanding of purification as a pain of love makes it possible to perceive the compatibility of joy and suffering in this condition1166.

"Is it possible for someone else to participate in the profoundly personal process of the encounter with Christ, of the transformation of an 'I' in the fire of his proximity?"1167. We come to the second aspect, concerning prayer for the dead, which links the relationality of our being to its Christological foundation. The connection of purgatory with ecclesial penance excludes any individualistic view of salvation, in the recognition that:

> neither is man's being a closed monad, for both in love and in hatred man is in relation with others; his personal being is thus present in others either as guilt or as grace. Man is never merely himself; or rather, he is himself only in others, with others, and through others1168.

The encyclical *Spe Salvi* describes how the relationality of the human being signifies a communion of love that transcends the frontiers of the beyond, implying that the pursuit of personal salvation includes responsibility for that of others:

> No one lives alone. No one sins alone. No one is saved alone. The lives of others continually enter into mine: in what I think, say, do, and achieve. And conversely, my life enters into the lives of others, in evil as in good. So my intercession for another is not something alien to him, something external, not even after death. In the interconnectedness of being, my gratitude to him, my prayer for him, can signify a small stage of his purification (SS 48).

In the contemporary context, some authors have suggested that purgatory is not an inflicted punishment, but a personal process of self-maturation1169. There is certainly a personal responsibility in one's own purification, but it would be insufficient to conceive it as one's own self-contained work. Holiness is not self-purification, but identification with Christ within his Body, and the assistance that can be received from other members of the Church is never superfluous. This does not detract from the fact that it is indeed more a process of maturation than of penal satisfaction1170. The most characteristic contribution of the Second Vatican Council on this theme concerns the prayer of the Church for the dead, to which it adds, as the Council of Trent did before it, an exhortation to avoid abuses, excesses, or defects (cf. LG 50–51).

### 9.2.4.3. Purgatory and Mercy: The Perspective of Spirituality

Reflections upon the Christological and relational dimensions of purgatory lead us to consider certain aspects of the spiritual experience of the saints and of the Christian people. In the interplay between theology and spirituality there occurs a mutual enrichment, and at times spirituality offers theology a more luminous grasp of the truths of faith.

Beginning with the relational aspect, prayers for the dead are an expression of the communion of saints, and this gave rise over time to the consolidation of devotion to the holy souls in purgatory1171. The living pray for the dead, but the souls who have departed this life can also assist those still on pilgrimage with their prayers. This conviction is found in theologians and saints of every era1172. Whether this assistance is rendered while they are being purified or only after their purification is a debated question. Saint Thomas is of the view that it occurs only afterwards: "The souls in purgatory, although superior to us by reason of their impeccability, are nevertheless inferior by reason of the pains they suffer. Therefore they are in a condition not of being able to pray, but rather of needing prayers"1173. Alphonsus Liguori makes a distinction in order to overcome this position without contradicting it:

> It is one thing not to be in a state of praying, and another not to be able to pray. It is true that those holy souls are not in a state of praying, because (as Saint Thomas says), being in suffering, they are inferior to us and rather in need of our prayers; nevertheless, in that state they can indeed pray, because they are souls that are friends of God1174.

As regards the Christological aspect, Catherine of Genoa, in her *Treatise on Purgatory*, presents it as an interior fire, born of perceiving "the goodness of God, the infinite distance of one's own life from this goodness, and a burning fire within oneself. And this is the fire that purifies, the interior fire of purgatory"1175. The Saint speaks of purgatory as an antechamber of heaven1176, with a description harmonious with Christocentrism, which views suffering as proceeding from love and purgatory as the last of God's mercies1177. The pain is exceedingly intense, but even greater is the consolation of knowing oneself saved. The suffering and the joy have the same root: the proximity of God's love, which purifies and comforts. "If it is true that the souls in Purgatory enjoy immense contentment, they suffer at the same time an immense pain, and the one does not impede the other"1178.

Catherine links the vision of purgatory with the present life understood as a permanent task of purification from sin1179. Spiritual authors evaluate this doctrine in light of the dynamic of purification that the saints experience in this life:

> This purification occurs in few souls in such a powerful way, and only in those whom the Lord wishes to raise to the highest degree of union [...]. And thus, this purification appears similar to that of purgatory; for, just as there spirits are purified so as to be able to see God in the clear beatific vision, so too, in its own way, here souls are purified so as to be able to be united to Him by love in this life1180.

It is insufficient to maintain that purification is instantaneous merely because it coincides with death itself1181. Traditional theology speaks of a duration and an intensity of purgatory, which will depend upon the personal situation of each individual. Thomas distinguished between the objective gravity of sins and the depth of their rootedness in the will1182. The liturgical practice of praying for the dead over a prolonged period of time suggests that instantaneous purification is untenable. Yet the time of purgatory is not comparable to earthly time: "With the Lord one day is as a thousand years, and a thousand years as one day" (2 Pet 3:8).

### 9.2.5. Purification as an Ongoing Task of the Christian Life

> Wisdom and prudence abound upon your lips when three things are present: confession of one's own sin, thanksgiving and the song of praise, and finally the word that edifies. For "with the heart man believes and is justified, and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation" (Rom 10:10). And again: "the righteous man is the first to accuse himself" (Prov 18:17); in the middle of his discourse he must magnify the Lord, and in the third part, if his wisdom is still abundant, he must edify his neighbour1183.

Thanksgiving, the petition for forgiveness, and the resolution of good works included in the plea for help represent three dimensions corresponding to what we have described as the scriptural context of purgatory, inasmuch as they can be linked, respectively, to the holiness of God, our own sinfulness, and the communion of saints.

The same three dimensions are encapsulated in what was a habitual prayer of Blessed Álvaro del Portillo, which can serve as a guide for the Christian tradition of the examination of conscience: "Thank you, forgive me, help me more!" (*Grazie, perdono, aiutami di più!*). To these words Pope Francis referred1184 when recommending to all Christians, as we have already recalled, the traditional practice of the daily examination of conscience (cf. GE 169).

We may conclude by making our own the words of Saint Jerome:

> We must leave this to the wisdom of the one God, who measures out not only mercy but also punishments, and knows whom he must judge, when, and for how long. Let us say only what befits human frailty: "O Lord, rebuke me not in your anger, nor chasten me in your wrath!" (Ps 6:1). And just as we believe that the torments of the devil and of all the godless apostates who said in their heart, "There is no God" (Ps 14 [13]:1) are eternal, so we hold that for sinners and the ungodly who are Christians, whose works must be tested and purified with fire, the sentence of the Judge will be balanced and tempered with mercy1185.
