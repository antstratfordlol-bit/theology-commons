## Chapter 10 Resurrectio Praesupponit Immortalitatem {#sanz-alfa-omega.10}

In the preceding chapter it was taken for granted that, after death, some dimension of the person remains, enabling them to receive full retribution and to be purified, if necessary, before enjoying the vision of God. The Protestant denial of purgatory, grounded in salvation as the exclusive work of God, proceeds by maintaining that, since salvation consists in resurrection, man presents himself before God without merits or possessions, without even an immortal soul1186.

It is not easy to maintain the balance between the diverse demands of Revelation regarding the relationship between immortality and resurrection1187. In a famous lecture, Cullmann posed the question: does the Christian faith profess the immortality of the soul or the resurrection of the dead? Setting the two affirmations in contrast, he opted for the second, denouncing the fact that for too long people had sacrificed 'chapter 15 of the First Epistle to the Corinthians to the Phaedo' by prioritizing a personal longing over God's revelation, thereby succumbing to the accusation of the opponents of Christianity, 'who never cease repeating that the faith of Christians is nothing more than the projection of their own desires'1188. This sparked an intense debate in both Protestant and Catholic circles.

We shall investigate, in the first place, the belief in immortality from the biblical perspective. We shall then proceed to the historical-theological standpoint in dialogue with philosophy, guided by St Thomas. We will thus be equipped to analyse contemporary proposals, beginning with the position of Ratzinger, whose thought on this topic underwent an evolution, before moving on to the exposition and evaluation of the various theories that have arisen within Protestant and Catholic spheres, with the help of the most recent ecclesiastical pronouncements. All this will lead us to a final reflection capable of justifying the title of this chapter.

### 10.1. Immortality or Resurrection in the NT?

Cullmann seeks to emphasise the priority that faith in the resurrection enjoys in the NT, which possesses such specificity that it must not be blended with Greek philosophy concerning the immortality of the human soul1189. Christianity is founded on Christ and on the history of salvation that reaches its fulfilment in Him. 'For the first Christians the soul is not immortal in itself, but becomes so solely through the Resurrection of Jesus Christ, “the firstborn from the dead”, and through faith in him'1190. Since the Resurrection signifies the inauguration of the new creation, which has already begun but has not yet been manifested in fullness, one must await the end of time: 'the transformation of the physical body into a resurrection body will take place only at the moment when the whole creation is created anew by the Holy Spirit'1191. This 'is not a transition to the hereafter, as for the soul according to the Greek belief in the immortality of the soul. The resurrection of the body is a transition from the present age to the age to come'1192.

Cullmann is aware of the fact that, if immortality is discarded in favour of resurrection at the end of time, one still needs to explain man's intermediate state between death and resurrection, which is an undeniable reality in the NT. Without evading this difficulty, Cullmann maintains that, even though 'death extends to the whole man, body and soul'1193, there is a form of survival of the inner man, who persists after death—to use Pauline terminology—in a state of sleep, by virtue of communion with Christ wrought by the Holy Spirit, who continues to dwell in him1194. 'The man without the physical body […], the inner man stripped of the body is no longer alone, no longer leads that shadowy existence which was the only one expected by the Jews […]. The Spirit is a gift that is not lost in dying. The dead Christian possesses the Spirit, even though he still sleeps'1195.

These expressions imply a concession to the very idea that is being so vigorously combated:

> Within certain limits we do indeed approach the Greek doctrine, in the sense that the inner man, transformed, vivified by the Holy Spirit from beforehand (Rom 6:3), continues to live, thus transformed, alongside Christ in the state of sleep […]. Here we glimpse at least a certain analogy with the immortality of the soul1196.

Cullmann immediately feels obliged to distance himself:

> And yet the difference remains radical: the state of the dead remains an imperfect state, of nakedness, as St Paul says, of sleep, of awaiting the resurrection of all creation, the resurrection of the body […]. If the dead, even in that state, already live alongside Christ, this is not related to the essence, to the nature of the soul, but is the consequence of a divine intervention that has acted from without through the death and resurrection of Christ, through the Holy Spirit who has raised the inner man1197.

Pending further comments on this approach, let us hold fast to three points: the priority of the resurrection over immortality, the necessary affirmation of an intermediate state, and a certain survival of the human being in the hereafter wrought by God. Are these elements incompatible with the Catholic affirmation of the immortality of the soul? Must one necessarily say that an unacceptable concession was made in the dialogue with Greek philosophy?

### 10.2. The Immortality of the Soul in Relation to the Resurrection: Tradition and St Thomas

> Ask a Christian, whether Protestant or Catholic, intellectual or not, what the New Testament teaches about the individual fate of man after death, and, with very few exceptions, you will always get the same answer: the immortality of the soul. Yet this opinion, widespread though it may be, is one of the greatest misunderstandings concerning Christianity1198.

Adopting the synthetic principle of *et et* leads us to agree with Cullmann concerning the priority of the resurrection over immortality1199, and distances us from two extremes: the one he denounced, namely the neglect of the resurrection in favour of immortality; and the one he himself fails to avoid, namely the denial of immortality in favour of the resurrection.

The Creeds of faith confess the resurrection of the dead without saying anything about the survival of the soul after death. Certain biblical texts seem to move in this direction, both because they speak of the death of the body and the soul, and because they affirm that God alone is immortal1200. To such texts can be opposed the sapiential affirmation of the immortality in which God created man, as well as other texts that can be read in this sense1201. Moreover, an expression such as 'salvation of the soul', which might appear to be the Scholastic residue of an individualistic view from which one ought to break free, is frequently used in the Church… because it is biblical!1202.

It is necessary to reaffirm the inadequacy of an exclusive affirmation of the immortality of the soul as the horizon of human and Christian hope. Augustine asked himself: 'And when you attain knowledge of your immortality, will your search cease? — It will be a great achievement, but for me it is still too little'1203. Ambrose, in the funeral oration for his deceased brother, was even more explicit: 'Immortality is a burden rather than an advantage without the intervention of grace'1204.

Thomas was conscious of the inadequacy of affirming the immortality of the soul as the definitive horizon for man in the hereafter, for the simple reason that this would exclude the body, which is an integral part of the human being, indispensable for his salvation and for his ultimate happiness.

> Man naturally desires his own salvation; now, since the soul constitutes a part of man's body, it is not the whole man, and my soul is not I; therefore, although the soul achieves salvation in the other life, nevertheless neither I nor any man does. And furthermore, since man naturally desires salvation also of the body, natural desire would be frustrated1205.

This statement is of great interest for understanding the novelty of Christianity not only in the face of Greek philosophy, but also in comparison with modern thought which, in certain respects, returned to dualism by proposing immortality in various forms as the destiny of man, especially in Hegelian idealism. Unlike the Greeks, who perceive immortality as a natural property of the spirit, Hegel conceives it as an acquisition of the subject in history. In idealism, the spirit has life in itself without reference to a creation1206, but as a divine expression, so that immortality is decisive for understanding man as a subject. Man is not immortal originally; in order to gain his own individuality, man had to commit the 'original sin' of the free exercise of thought, with whose birth nature dies and history, culture, the historical salvation of man, and immortality as a conquest are generated1207.

Thomas Aquinas departs from both ancient and modern forms of 'aggregative dualism' and teaches that, at death, it is not the body or the soul that dies, but man, constituted by both. There is no dimension of his being that is left untouched by death1208. This implies admitting the existence and the problematic nature of the state of the soul separated from the body, which is an imperfect situation; indeed, 'to be without the body is against nature for it [the soul]'1209. The person is composed of soul and body, and therefore if the soul is only a part of his being, 'it cannot be said that the separated soul is the person'1210, even though it is a personal reality.

Separation from the body is not natural for the soul, which exists as *forma corporis*; this means that such separation occurs accidentally (*per accidens*), and therefore one can envisage a subsequent reunification, which cannot take place naturally—and this not by defect of the soul, but of the body. Of great importance is the affirmation according to which the soul separated from the body retains a natural aptitude for union, and hence it would be possible for this to happen supernaturally1211. According to Thomas's anthropology, death and corruption are present in man by virtue of his materiality, whereas by reason of his spiritual form immortality would be more fitting for him. If the body, despite being composed of diverse elements, were incorruptible, it would be suited to the incorruptibility of its soul. It possesses a certain natural aptitude for immortality, but in order to attain it, it requires a gift superior to its natural capacities. This explanation, which may be termed integrative dualism, is from a theological point of view in agreement with the doctrine of the original state of man; from a philosophical point of view, it brings to light a new theme for thought, that of the connection—evoked by this paradox—between immortality and resurrection: whereas immortality is not natural (it is not so for the body), death is for us contrary to nature (contrary to the nature of the soul, which is in itself incorruptible)1212.

From the theological point of view, the corruption of the body, which occurs with death, is a consequence of sin and therefore not willed by the Creator, who had originally bestowed immortality. Even the state of the soul separated from the body must be regarded as the result of sin, as an imperfect, 'violent' situation, and therefore one to be overcome. Thomas states that, even if objectively the soul is fulfilled by the gift of the vision of God, subjectively it is not, because an essential desire remains unsatisfied: that of union with its body1213.

> There is in the soul a natural desire to be united to the body. Hence there cannot be the perfect rest of the will unless the soul is reunited once again with its own body […]. In order, therefore, that the soul reach its end completely, it is necessary that it be perfect in its nature: and this cannot be unless it is united to the body […]. That which is *per accidens* and contrary to nature cannot endure forever. Now, the separation of the soul from the body is necessarily accidental and contrary to nature, if the union of the soul to the body is naturally *per se*: therefore the soul cannot be perpetually separated from its body1214.

Following this approach, one cannot guarantee, from a philosophical point of view, that the separated soul possesses actuality of consciousness, but one cannot deny that, by a gift of divine omnipotence, a spiritual entity endowed with consciousness subsists, even without the body, with the marks of personality. This is what theology postulates when speaking of the particular judgment and retribution after death; this presupposes an extra-natural gift, compatible with the affirmation of a natural immortality of the soul1215.

If the unity of the human being must be regarded as an indispensable principle, it is reasonable to admit the necessity of the resurrection of the body to guarantee the immortality of the soul in the right order. 'If one denies the resurrection of the body, it is not easy—indeed, it is very difficult—to maintain the immortality of the soul'1216.

### 10.3. Contemporary Issues in Intermediate Eschatology

### 10.3.1. The Ratzingerian Understanding of the Immortality of the Soul

We do not know whether Cullmann was able to become acquainted in detail with the position of Thomas Aquinas; perhaps it would have surprised him to discover more elements in common than he might have imagined. The salvation-historical conception of the Resurrection in the Bible, which Cullmann believes to be opposed to the Greek metaphysical horizon of the immortality of the soul, can be connected with philosophy, as the Aquinas demonstrates. Along these lines, in contemporary theology we find an example of the compatibility between the metaphysical and the salvation-historical element within the eschatological horizon in the thought of Ratzinger, whose thinking on the theme of immortality underwent an interesting evolution.

In his widely circulated *Introduction to Christianity* (1968), the German professor felt more at ease with biblical explanations than with philosophical categories, considering the question of the resurrection and immortality from the salvation-historical and christological perspective, and less close to Scholasticism, as shown by his rejection of the concept of the 'separated soul', or his tendency to underline the continuity between nature and grace1217. When, a few years later (1977), he published his textbook on *Eschatology*, Ratzinger had found the way to connect the relational and dialogical conception of immortality with traditional theology and its affirmations regarding the intermediate state and the subsistence of the soul1218. Let us follow his reflection in detail, from Scripture to ecclesial pronouncements in the history of theology, up to the formulation of his own proposal.

The people of Israel shared with neighbouring peoples faith in the survival of the dead, expressed in the concept of *sheol*. Little by little, the certainty matured that communion with God is stronger than the destruction of the body, and thus the conviction became distinctive to Israel that 'being-with-Him' is the way man can overcome *sheol*1219. The expression in Wisdom that 'the souls of the righteous are in the hand of God' (Wis 3:1) confirms this, and does not constitute a triumph of Hellenism over Hebraism1220.

After analysing various NT texts on the intermediate state, Ratzinger observes that, although through faith in the Risen One the 'intermediate stage' and the resurrection are linked with greater coherence, they remain distinct. The sole model is the christology of the resurrection, and only starting from it must reason use all its capabilities to clarify the anthropological premises and consequences1221.

Within the variety of Pauline texts, it remains the case for him that '“waking” and “sleeping”, that is, earthly “living” or “being dead”, is not the decisive distinction; rather, the boundary line is drawn between “living-with-him” and being separated from him'1222. This leads to a reassessment of the weight given by Cullmann to the image of sleep, because in the NT those who have died in Christ live1223. Here Greek 'dualism' does not replace Jewish 'monism', but 'the Jewish heritage is Christianised with the utmost naturalness'1224. There was no felt need to make pronouncements on the immortality of the soul for the simple reason that it was taken as self-evident that the dead awaited the resurrection.

Since the christological principle did not determine all anthropological consequences, it was possible in a later period to explain more adequately the significance of the underlying thought. 'The Middle Ages answers the question of what happens between death and resurrection with the immortality of the soul'1225. This led to the definition in the Bull *Apostolici regiminis* of the Fifth Lateran Council (1513), against the contemporary versions of Aristotelianism that regarded the soul as mortal and collective:

> We condemn and reprobate all who assert that the intellective soul is mortal, or that it is only one in all men, and those who cast doubts on this truth; for it is not only truly, of itself and essentially, the form of the human body […] but it is also immortal, and, given the multitude of bodies into which it is individually infused, it can be, must be, and is multiplied (DS 1440).

The fact that the conciliar definition was directed against certain interpretations of Aristotle makes it difficult to maintain, as Luther did, that we are dealing with an exaggerated concession to philosophy, or, as has been repeated in recent decades, that Greek dualism of body and soul is being imposed upon the Church's formulas.

Christianity was faced with the task of developing an anthropology that, on the one hand, recognised man as the work of God, created and willed by God as a whole, but on the other hand distinguished in this man between what is perishable and what endures […]. It was necessary to accept the inseparable unity between body and *psyche* taught by Aristotle, while at the same time avoiding the interpretation of the *psyche* in the sense of an 'entelechy', that is, as bound to matter just as matter is stamped by the *psyche* […]. This anthropology would find its definitive form only in Thomas Aquinas. In reality, Thomas, by resolving the problem of the essence of the soul, like Aristotle, with the formula *anima forma corporis* (the soul is the 'form' of the body), radically transforms Aristotelianism1226.

The conciliar formula endorsed the Thomistic expression as suitable for conveying the mystery of the soul. On the basis of Aristotle, a Christian concept was arrived at, an

> affirmation that is of extreme importance: in man, spirit is so much one with the body that one can in all right apply to it the term 'form'. And conversely: the form of this body is such as to be spirit, and as such makes man a person […] The soul belongs to the body as 'form', but that which is the 'form' of the body is at the same time spirit, makes man a person, and unlocks immortality for him1227.

If Christianity transformed the Greek notion of the soul, its immortality was consequently also transfigured. This change does not imply renouncing its own proper specificity, because 'if immortality is determined solely on the basis of christology, if it is considered only as grace, one falls into supernaturalism and loses its common rationality'1228. One must appeal to the special creaturely character of man, crafted in such a way that, by its very nature, it entails indestructibility.

> This is what Thomas refers to when he says that immortality belongs to man by nature. At the foundation of this statement of his, we consistently find his concept of creation, which states that such a nature can be communicated only by the Creator, but that man becomes the owner of this gift, becoming a participant in what has been conferred upon him1229.

The man who does not live according to God, but who, asserting his own autonomy and substituting himself for God, closes himself in upon himself, obtains a shadowy life (*sheol*), which is not true life. 'Yet this does not mean that man can of himself revoke or annul God's creation […]. What he obtains in this way is therefore not the annihilation of being, but a being in contradiction with himself'1230. Here we touch the boundary between creation and christology.

> Only at this point is the properly christological affirmation grafted on. The Christian event means that God cancels this self-contradiction, without destroying human freedom by an arbitrary act from without. In the life and death of Christ it becomes manifest that God himself goes down into *sheol*, establishing communication in the place of incommunicability1231.

Ratzinger thus formulates his dialogical understanding: 'Although it is a gift of creation, immortality is not simply a natural fact'1232. Creatureliness implies a constitutive relation to God, the only One who possesses immortality as the correlation of Trinitarian love; this relation renders the soul of man immortal1233. Just as the doctrine of creation and christology are linked, so too are the immortality of the soul and the resurrection, in their distinction and articulation. There is no trace here of Greek dualism, but rather the assertion of a duality indispensable to the Christian faith1234.

### 10.3.2. Discussion of Some Contemporary Theories in Protestant Theology

The denial of purgatory was bound to lead, sooner or later in Protestant theology, to questioning the immortality of the soul as an illegitimate importation from Platonic thought. The centrality for Christian faith of the final resurrection is affirmed, which will take place, together with the judgment, at the coming of Christ. If death and resurrection do not coincide, what happens to the human individual in the meantime? Does anything of man remain after death?

To these questions three different answers have been given1235. The first, and simplest, asserts: 'With death we are completely lost; body and soul both disappear. Death is the collapse of man into a bottomless pit […] it is an exit into nothingness'1236. Resurrection in this case would be a new creation of the whole man, and therefore there would be no intermediate eschatology, and the idea of the immortality of the soul should be rejected. This is the theory of 'total death' (*Ganztod*), first introduced by C. Stange and championed by P. Althaus, E. Jüngel, and others. Given its radical nature, this explanation did not gain widespread support among Protestant theologians. Among the reasons for this rejection, it may be pointed out that

> a resurrection set over against integral death, without any element of continuity, slips into the realm of the miraculous and can no longer be sustained by any coherent anthropological doctrine […]; nowhere is death conceived as integral death in the strictest sense. Everywhere some kind of existence is presupposed1237.

A second, more articulated theory was proposed by K. Barth and E. Brunner and can be described as atemporalism. These authors maintain that death takes man out of time1238, so that each person experiences the resurrection as something occurring at the moment of death, which, by removing man from time, eliminates any temporal distance with respect to the Parousia. 'The day of our resurrection is the same for all and is not separated from the day of death by any interval of centuries; therefore this time interval exists only here, but not there, in the presence of God, in which a hundred years are like a day'1239. There is no room for an intermediate eschatology; this entails a considerable devaluation of history, prompting one to ask whether a place still remains for the Parousia and the judgment as historical events1240. As we shall see shortly, some Catholic theologians have echoed this theory.

Cullmann criticised these two solutions, which fail to respect the teaching of the NT, which obliges one to confront the problem of the intermediate state without evading it, as do those who assert that after death nothing of man would remain, or those who maintain that immediately after death the resurrection of the individual takes place. In virtue of the principle of the 'already and not yet', one must seek to offer theological explanations that do not cancel out the problem.

Cullmann agrees with the first theory in affirming that 'the resurrection of the body is a new creative act that embraces the universe' and that 'it cannot supervene at the moment of individual death, but only at the end of time'1241. This leads him to diverge from Barth's theory, 'who attributes to the Apostle Paul the idea that the transformation of the physical body occurs for each person at the moment of his death, as though the dead were outside time. According to the New Testament, they are still in time'1242.

This last affirmation obliges one to reject the theory of total death and to seek to grasp the *how* of human existence after death. Using the Pauline image of sleep, Cullmann proposes, in line with Luther1243, a third theory (already mentioned above), which maintains the survival of the inner man in a state of sleep, by virtue of the power of the Holy Spirit, who never abandons man, convinced that in the NT 'this intermediate time for the dead as for the living is a fact difficult to dispute'1244.

Cullmann's position represents a praiseworthy attempt within Protestantism to uphold the intermediate state. Nevertheless, his rejection of the immortality of the soul in favour of the resurrection raises problems, not only in view of a dialogue with the sciences that seeks to explain how the resurrection is possible, but also at the theological level: 'What happens in the meantime? A sleeping soul in the line of Luther? But if there is no soul! How can one speak of resurrection if there is no *who* to be awakened, but only created anew out of nothing?'1245.

The first two theories may be synthesised as follows: after death nothing remains and everything will be given at the end (total death); or after death everything is given immediately and therefore everything remains (atemporalism). In the first case, the contrast between immortality and resurrection ends with the elimination of the former in favour of the latter; in the second case, however, all difference between the two vanishes, because they are identified within the subject. Both solutions converge in the obliteration of the intermediate state and hence in asserting, against the vast majority of texts in the Christian tradition, a one-way, single-phase eschatology.

Cullmann seeks to recover this aspect by tentatively upholding a two-phase eschatology with an intermediate state, which nonetheless remains empty of ontological content. The assertion of the state of sleep to avoid any aspect of positive immortality of the human being fails to take into account that already from the patristic era the affirmation of faith concerns the *de facto* survival between death and resurrection of a conscious element in man, without specifying whether it is a natural gift or one of grace1246. It is no coincidence that some recent Protestant theologians have begun to acknowledge that there are no theological reasons to deny the immortality of the soul1247. It is not necessary to conceive it as an exclusive possession of man (in a Platonic or idealist sense), but it can be grasped in its character as a gift of God's creative action. This would open the way to accepting the realities associated with the intermediate state in Catholic ecclesiology, liturgy, and spirituality, which speak of the communion of saints, their intercession, prayer for the dead, purgatory, etc.

Protestant theology has a constitutive tendency to consider the human person primarily within the context of salvation, leaving aside the dimensions of his creaturely condition. This occurs in the theory of *Ganztod*, where resurrection is conceived as a new creation *ex nihilo* and therefore death sweeps away the created being of man, who paradoxically continues to exist only as a memory in the mind of God. But the theory of survival in the Holy Spirit also poses problems when we consider the fate of the damned, in whom there is no gratificatory presence of the Spirit. Only two positions appear possible, both problematic: 'Either all are saved by the power of the Holy Spirit, a position endorsed by several authors, or the condemned are annihilated, a position adopted by other authors who share Cullmann's view'1248.

### 10.3.3. Discussion of the Theory of Resurrection in Death in Catholic Theology1249

From a Catholic point of view, the prospect of a long interlude between death and the Parousia at the end of time may provoke perplexity: what meaning can it have, on God's part, for the subject to remain for a long time as a 'separated soul', in an incomplete and unnatural state? One might question the true scope of the dogmatic definition of full retribution immediately after death. If to this is added the debate on the Assumption of Mary (the dogma was defined in 1950), interpreted by some as making people aware of something that holds true for everyone, it is understandable that the idea of resurrection in death should have attracted interest in Catholic theology from the 1960s onwards.

The proposal was elaborated by G. Lohfink from the biblical side, and by G. Greshake from the dogmatic side1250. Lohfink challenges the devaluation of history present in the position of Barth and Brunner, and maintains that between time and eternity there is a transfigured time (*aevum*), in which the contemporaneity between God's eternity and the history of the human being occurs, and which arrives with death, followed 'immediately by the resurrection of the dead'1251. Since with death one exits time to enter eternity, like Our Lady who is already risen, then this actually happens with everyone, so it was argued on the Catholic side. The proximity of eschatology, typical of consistent eschatologism, enters here to say that each person is near the end because with death the Parousia and resurrection arrive for everyone, inasmuch as we experience the delay from our vantage point within history, but once entered into eternity everything coincides1252.

Greshake rejects the 'conception of an intermediate condition of the soul in the absence of the body', 'a soul freed from the body and still waiting for its body to be awakened on the Day of Judgment'1253. This would be an abstract, Platonic idea of the soul, devoid of history. What passes from this world towards God is a person with his history; therefore, only a resurrection occurring 'in the death of the unique and complete man'1254 can be considered an adequate articulation of Christian hope. Greshake recognises the problem this theory poses regarding the materiality of the risen body. He argues that resurrection must not be conceived in a physicalist manner, as a final event concerning bones and skin, but appeals to the Teilhardian concept of 'spiritualisation', which 'creates a form of matter different from that known to us on the basis of the objective world knowable by the senses'1255. Corporeality and the world are not to be understood as sheer physicality, and therefore in death they are not abandoned, but 'spiritualised and brought safely into absolute perfection, in restoring and reuniting the world in and with God'1256.

These ideas sparked a lively debate in Catholic theology and prompted the intervention of the Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith in 1979. Before considering this document, we can summarise the reasons for the inadequacy of this theory under three headings.

The first and fundamental reason is christological. If our resurrection is a participation in that of Christ, we must look to his mystery to understand our own reality. Jesus was not raised instantly, but 'on the third day', a crucial phrase in the formulation of the faith1257. Those who advocate resurrection in death must seek alternative interpretations for the third day and for the sign of the empty tomb. The Church does not celebrate the Resurrection of Christ on Good Friday, but on Easter Sunday, while Holy Saturday possesses profound meaning as an aliturgical day of Jesus' remaining in death. For the Church, the realism of historical events (Incarnation, Resurrection, Parousia) is essential, grounding the subjective faith of individuals. The fact that Jesus remained in death, and then rose on the third day, constitutes an undeniable foundation for speaking of an intermediate eschatology. Neither in Him nor in Christians do death and resurrection coincide.

Linked to the christological reason is the mariological one. This theory entails a reinterpretation of the dogma of the Assumption, maintaining that Our Lady's condition is not substantially different from that of the rest of humanity. Like her, everyone rises at the moment of death. Several authors have pointed out in one way or another that this explanation fails to respect the fact that Mary did not deserve to suffer the corruption of death because she was immaculately conceived. The purpose of the dogmatic definition was to affirm that what was accomplished in Mary possesses a singular character in comparison with the situation of the rest of humanity.

The most pressing reason is anthropological. This theory is a veiled return to Platonism and its view of immortality.

> In this model the body is abandoned to death, while at the same time a survival of man is affirmed. Consequently, the refutation of the concept of the soul loses its credibility, since it implicitly admits the existence of a personal 'reality' separated from the body, which is precisely what the concept of the soul was intended to express1258.

The relationship between time and eternity and the introduction of the *aevum* raise questions.

> Is it logically possible to locate man, who has lived the decisive period of his existence in time, within the structure of pure timelessness? Can, therefore, an eternity that has a beginning be eternity? Is not something that has a beginning necessarily non-eternal, temporal? But how can one deny that the resurrection of man has a beginning?1259

Whoever wishes to overcome Platonism by opting for resurrection finds himself with a timeless and dematerialised idea of resurrection, which is in reality a disguised immortality1260.

> An eternally unreciprocated and therefore completely static parallelism between the material and the spiritual world contradicts the *raison d'être* of history, contradicts God's creation, and contradicts the word of the Bible. For this reason, without wishing to diminish the merits of Greshake's book, we must reject the statement: 'Matter “in itself” […] cannot be fulfilled'. This would mean, despite all assurances to the contrary, a division of creation and hence an ultimate dualism, in which the whole realm of matter would be excluded from the intention of creation1261.

### 10.3.4. Recent Magisterial Interventions

The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith felt the duty to intervene in 1979 with the Letter on Certain Questions Concerning Eschatology (*Recentiores episcoporum synodi*)1262, with the purpose of reiterating several fundamental points of the dogmatic teaching of the Church concerning eschatology, mindful of the importance of the final article of the Creed, without which the life of Christians remains devoid of hope. The faith of ordinary people is shaken not only by the doubts that naturally arise for everyone concerning these themes, but also when these doubts are reinforced by the dissemination among the faithful of untested theological theories, partly owing to their overly specialised language1263.

Seeking to respect, on the one hand, the legitimate freedom of theologians and, on the other, the obligation to recall what is essential to the faith, there is brought to everyone's consideration: 'The teaching which the Church proposes in the name of Christ, especially concerning what occurs between the death of the Christian and the universal resurrection'1264. This is summarised in seven points, which constitute not only a response to recent theories, but a synthesis of all eschatology, which we reproduce below without commentary:

1) The Church believes (cf. Creed) in the resurrection of the dead.

2) The Church understands this resurrection as referring to the whole man; for the elect this is nothing other than the extension to men of the resurrection of Christ himself.

3) The Church affirms the survival and subsistence, after death, of a spiritual element, which is endowed with consciousness and will, so that the human 'I' subsists. To designate such an element, the Church employs the word 'soul', consecrated by the usage of Sacred Scripture and Tradition. Without ignoring the fact that this term takes on different meanings in the Bible, she nevertheless holds that no serious reason exists for rejecting it, and considers, moreover, that a verbal instrument to sustain the faith of Christians is absolutely indispensable.

4) The Church excludes any form of thought or expression that would render absurd or unintelligible her prayer, her funeral rites, and her cult of the dead, realities that constitute, in their substance, true theological loci (*loci theologici*).

5) The Church, in conformity with Sacred Scripture, awaits 'the glorious manifestation of our Lord Jesus Christ' (DV 4), which she considers, moreover, as distinct and deferred with respect to the situation that is proper to men immediately after death.

6) The Church, in her teaching on the fate of man after death, excludes any explanation that would deprive the Assumption of Mary of its meaning in what is unique to it, namely the fact that the bodily glorification of the Virgin is the anticipation of the glorification reserved for all the other elect.

7) The Church, in faithful adherence to the New Testament and to Tradition, believes in the happiness of the just, who will one day be with Christ. She believes that eternal punishment awaits the sinner, who will be deprived of the vision of God, just as she believes in the repercussion of this punishment throughout his whole being. Finally, as regards the elect, she believes in an eventual purification preliminary to the vision of God, which is, nevertheless, entirely distinct from the punishment of the damned. This is what the Church intends when speaking of Hell and Purgatory1265.

The document concludes by urging that one avoid both the excesses of imaginative depictions of the hereafter and the emptying of real content from biblical imagery. What is at stake is grasping the relationship of continuity and discontinuity between the hereafter and the present life, as we have repeated from the outset1266. A few years later (1990), the International Theological Commission published a document on *Some Current Questions Concerning Eschatology*, explaining in greater detail the Church's eschatological teaching1267.

### 10.4. Final Reflections

Faith in the resurrection has as its presupposition the immortality of the soul1268. The resurrection is the only concrete form in which the desire for immortality finds its meaning. This does not imply, *pace* Cullmann, a surrender to Greek philosophy. Christian faith assumes, without obliterating them, the deepest yearnings of the human being, imparting to them an unexpected and superior reality. The balance between the metaphysical and the salvation-historical dimension, characteristic of Christian thought, can be summarised in the classical axiom *gratia supponit naturam*, which we rephrase as *resurrectio praesupponit immortalitatem*1269.

From the beginning we have sought to explain the relationship between creation and redemption, between the first and the Second Adam, between metaphysics, the history of salvation, and eschatology, between nature, grace, and glory. Continuity opens onto novelty, as promise onto fulfilment, and apophaticism onto hope, which leads the Christian to live already here below savouring the goods he does not yet fully enjoy. This makes him ready for martyrdom, which translates into the joyful and daily gift of self to God and to others.

Intermediate eschatology, in its relationship with universal eschatology, becomes the guarantee of balance between dimensions that are always in tension: between present and future, between the individual and collective aspect of our existence. Neither individualistic nor collectivistic reductions of the *eschaton* are adequate, nor are reductions of eschatology to the moment of death or to the future Parousia. Eschatology speaks of death, the particular judgment, purgatory, heaven, and hell, but also of the Parousia, the general judgment, the new creation, and the resurrection of the body. In some subjects the individual and present dimension seems more evident, while in others it is the collective and future dimension that occupies centre stage. In all of them both dimensions coexist, in a continuity and at the same time discontinuity with what is human, finding integration only in Christ. Ultimately, the mystery of Christ enables the union of these diverse dimensions, inasmuch as it signifies a positive affirmation of the order of creation which, without being cancelled out, is elevated within the divine plan of salvation and redemption.

CONCLUDING REFLECTIONS ON ESCHATOLOGY

> God is the ultimate reality of the creature. Inasmuch as He is gained, He is heaven; inasmuch as He is lost, He is hell; inasmuch as He examines us, He is judgment; inasmuch as He purifies us, He is purgatory […]. Yet He is so as turned towards the world, that is, in his Son Jesus Christ, who is the revelatory aspect of God and therefore the compendium of the 'last things'1270.

Thus can be summarised the Trinitarian christocentrism that has guided us. If Christ is the Alpha and the Omega of the divine design, He teaches us that the Father is the true Alpha and Omega, source and goal, of the Trinity and of the entire economy1271.

This means

> that the body of man, his history too, which is a human history, and his works and actions as well, unfolded across space and time, will be assumed into the intimacy of God […]. He who is with the Father in eternity is not only the Word, but the God-man, Christ Jesus […] truly risen, he has remained man and as such has entered into eternity […]. God willed and created man in such a way that this would be possible; and God is such as to will it and make it possible. He is not the God of philosophy, or of the devotion of the spirit alone, but is wholly Other, unknown to man and revealed only in Christ. And unknown too was the mystery of man, which Christ alone has revealed1272.

In the face of false criticisms levelled against Christianity as a doctrine that 'belittles man, despises the body, discredits the world, relegates the believer to a spiritual and religious isolation, removing him from works and actions', we have sought to show that 'never as in the Christian message is such greatness attributed to man; no other doctrine takes the world so seriously; and never as through Christ are created things, which exist in temporality, raised up with such resoluteness towards God'1273.

On several occasions we have described, without explicitly thematising it, the fact that the Christian economy is a dynamic of promise and fulfilment. Unlike what so often happens with men, God is trustworthy because He is not a God of unfulfilled promises, but of fulfilled promises, as the history of salvation attests; and for this very reason, our hope that one day his promises concerning the future will be fulfilled is a well-founded hope:

> God has appointed the time for his promises, and has appointed the time for fulfilling what He had promised. The time of promises was that which extends from the prophets down to John the Baptist; that, on the other hand, which proceeds from then onwards until the end is the time of the fulfilment of the promises […]. God has promised us eternal salvation, blessed and unending life with the angels, an incorruptible inheritance, everlasting glory, the joy of beholding his face, his holy dwelling place in heaven, and, with the resurrection from the dead, assurance without any further fear of dying. All this constitutes, so to speak, his final promise, towards which every effort of ours is already directed and where, once we have arrived, we shall have nothing left to seek or to demand […]. What God was promising seemed incredible to men, announcing that from this very state of mortality, corruption, abjection, and weakness, from this ash and dust, men would become equal to the angels of God; therefore, He not only composed the Scripture with men so that they might believe, but also established a Mediator as a guarantee of his faithfulness, choosing not some prince, or an angel, or even an archangel, but his only-begotten Son […]. It was necessary, therefore, to know the only Son of God, who was to come among men, to assume man and to become man through the assumed nature: he was to die, rise again, ascend into heaven, sit at the right hand of the Father, and fulfil among the nations all that He had promised; after the fulfilment of these promises, he would also fulfil that of his return, to require an account of what He had given, to separate the 'vessels of wrath' from the 'vessels of mercy', to render to the wicked the threatened punishment and to the just the promised reward. All this, then, had to be prophesied, had to be foretold, had to be signified as destined to come, so that, arriving suddenly, it might not cause terror, but rather be accepted in faith and awaited1274.

FINAL REFLECTION

At the beginning of this book, we referred to the M which, together with the A and the Ω, is concealed within the Cross according to Rabanus Maurus, to signify that the beginning, the middle, and the end of all things are embraced by Christ1275. Having dealt with both the Alpha and the Omega, we can discover a new meaning for that M which stands out 'by the cross of Jesus' (Jn 19:25).

The Risen Christ is the new 'bridge' uniting man with God, but for Him to become man the concurrence of a mediation was required: 'The kingdom of God, since Jesus is a man—and a Jew!—does not come solely from above and from without; it is essentially also the fruit of the earth. Of Mary? Certainly. But in her of the whole holy people, which, as a true people, really has a mission in this world'1276. Mary 'represents the Church, Bride of the new Adam'1277; indeed, 'Christ, Bridegroom of the Church, would not have entered into Paradise in fullness if, in Mary, the Church had not already been present there', and 'Paradise would not be restored even now if the new Eve did not share, even now, the privilege of the new Adam'1278.

In the scene of the presentation of Jesus in the Temple (cf. Lk 2:22–40), there is found, so to speak, an iconic answer to the three radical questions we posed at the beginning of our treatise concerning our origin, our destiny, and our identity. Indeed, as Pope Francis has recalled on several occasions, at the moment of the presentation in the Temple a fruitful dialogue takes place between past, present, and future, between origin, identity, and destiny, between the different generations—represented by the Child Jesus, the elderly Simeon and Anna, and the parents Joseph and Mary1279. Past, present, and future meet in harmony, since the elderly share the wisdom of the memory of the past, which is paramount for safeguarding the present identity of the family and of the people, which in turn constitutes the foundation of hope for the future that lies in the hands of the young.

In the mystery of Mary we find the connection between protology, christology, and eschatology. On the one hand, she is all-fair (*tota pulchra*)1280, coming from God as an unblemished creature. On the other hand, Mary journeyed towards God and has now reached Him in a glorious manner. Thus, 'the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption receive that character of full luminousness which the Church recognised in them by defining them as an integral part of the faith, within the perspective of the theology of the New Adam'1281. Finally, Mary identified herself with the image of God who is the Word, to the point of not only cultivating Him within herself, but also conceiving Him and bringing Him forth into the world. Mary, therefore, shares in the Alpha as immaculate and in the Omega as assumed, and also participates in the mediation of Christ (M) as Virgin (welcoming the Word in all purity) and Mother (receiving it, she gives it and pours it forth upon the world). The absence of sin makes possible in her what seems contradictory for any other person: to be simultaneously virgin and mother, an anthropological fulfilment within christological centrality.

The union of opposites that occurs in Christ (the union of divine and human nature), or rather, the full realisation of what we have called the synthetic principle, is shared in a sublime manner by her who is so closely associated with the mystery of the Second Adam that we may call her, with the Fathers of the Church, the new Eve1282; indeed, we greet her with the word *Ave*, which since the Middle Ages has been seen in its antithetical parallelism with *Eva*, precisely because in her a new creation has begun1283. Mary offers to the Church and to the world not the fruit of the tree of perdition, but the fruit of her womb, which in the Eucharist becomes tree and wellspring of new life, and hence of happiness and joy: 'Who ever departed from her sick, or sorrowful, or empty of heavenly mysteries? Who did not return home joyful and glad after having obtained from the Mother of the Lord, Mary, what he desired?'1284.
