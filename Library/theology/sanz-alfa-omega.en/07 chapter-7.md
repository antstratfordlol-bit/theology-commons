PART II
INTRODUCTION TO ESCHATOLOGY

‘Man cannot be understood solely on the basis of his origin or of an isolated fragment that we call the present; rather, he is referred to his future. Only his future makes completely manifest who he is’632. The ‘Deuteronomic dimension of faith’ (cf. EG 13; EG 142 and 288) implies approaching the past through the memory of the people, reflecting upon the wonderful works that the Lord has performed; yet the Israelite does not halt at the past, but turns his gaze towards the future of God's promises, with an attitude of hope. The theocentric nature of the question of origin, identity, and destiny acquires a new light in Christ, who is ‘the same yesterday and today and for ever’ (Heb 13:8), ‘the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End’ (Rev 22:13); in Him is realized ‘the mystery hidden for ages and generations, but now made manifest to his saints [...] Christ in you, the hope of glory’ (Col 1:26–27; cf. Eph 3:9; Rom 16:25–26).
If in protology we looked towards the ‘first things’, we now direct our gaze towards the ‘last things’ (*tà éschata*)633, with apophatic and vigilant humility, as Jesus counsels when He declares that neither He nor much less His listeners know the day and the hour, but only the Father (cf. Mt 24:36), and therefore that one must watch (cf. Mt 25:13). Only God can reveal through His Spirit His secrets: ‘What no eye has seen, nor ear heard, nor the heart of man conceived, what God has prepared for those who love him’ (1 Cor 2:9).
If in the truths of origins there is a connection with human rationality, so too with regard to eschatological truths must we be ‘always prepared to make a defence to any one who calls you to account for the hope that is in you’ (1 Pet 3:15). This attitude is especially timely in a world that, over recent centuries, has undergone a process of secularisation of the Christian *eschaton*634. If in the past the influence of the Christian vision of man’s destiny informed art and literature (from Dante's *Divine Comedy* to Michelangelo's *Last Judgment*), today we witness the distortion of the divine promise into human demands, accompanied by the disillusionment of seeing the expectations of a better world unfulfilled (cf. SS 16–23). In the recent past, Marxism was an exemplar of this phenomenon; today transhumanism is in vogue, which aims to achieve three goals: super-longevity, super-intelligence, and super-wellbeing635. It is thought that the gifts of paradise can be recovered without awaiting the end of time, as the natural outcome of our own progress.

The study of the last things (*novissima*) can be articulated in various ways636. We shall follow the scheme that begins from the Parousia of Christ, which includes the resurrection of men and of the cosmos in view of judgment (cf. Chapter VII), proceeding to the final states that depend on the free response of the creature: heaven and hell (cf. Chapter VIII); followed by the intermediate situation between the end of individual human lives and the *eschaton*: death, purgatory (cf. Chapter IX). The final chapter (cf. Chapter X) relates the human desire for immortality to the Christian promise of resurrection, seeking to justify the necessity—stemming from revelation and harmonious with reason—of speaking not only of a universal eschatology and a consummated eschatology, but also of an intermediate eschatology637.
The synthetic principle that guides our reflection, applied to the study of eschatological realities, is expressed in the necessary conjunction of dimensions that ought not to be sundered: body and spirit, individual and society, continuity (identity) and discontinuity (newness); in short, the integration between the present and future dimensions of eschatology, the ‘already and not yet’, to use the terminology of Oscar Cullmann638, which has been assimilated into Catholic theology.

This expression refers to the central reality of Christianity, the Resurrection of Christ: ‘If Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain’ (1 Cor 15:14). The paschal mystery of Jesus' death and resurrection, the beginning of the new creation, is an already present reality but not yet fully manifest: ‘He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life, and I will raise him up at the last day’ (Jn 6:54). This promise constitutes the core of Christian eschatology: an eternal life already begun, but not yet brought to consummation.
Here lies rooted the characteristic tension in Christianity between the horizontality of present life, marked by a transience that fails to satisfy fully the longings of the human heart, and the verticality of divine irruption into history with a new life that presents itself as fulfilling plenitude (*fascinans*), yet at the same time inspires a holy awe (*tremendum*)639. The conjunction between the horizontal and the vertical is enacted in the Cross, whose power extends from the origins unto the end, as indicated by the representation of the evocative Sator magic square, found in many medieval churches, with an origin reaching back to the early Christians640. The letters (A, M, and Ω) indicate that Jesus embraces the beginning, the middle, and the end of all things641. The Cross is the new tree of life, to which we have access in the Sacrament of the Eucharist642.

## Chapter 7 Universal Eschatology: Parousia, Resurrection, Palingenesis, Judgment {#sanz-alfa-omega.7}

PAROUSIA, RESURRECTION, PALINGENESIS, JUDGMENT
The memory of the past intertwines in the present with the hope of the future. From the conviction that no hope exists without memory, a pessimistic vision of the end of the world can arise. ‘Our world has reached a critical stage. Children no longer listen to their parents. The end of the world cannot be far off’643. In these words of an ancient Egyptian inscription, the future end of the cosmos is linked to the defective dominion that man exercises over it, as though future catastrophic events of a cosmic nature could somehow be provoked by the human being. Gregory the Great warned:
> For we see in our own days, even more than the texts declare, nation rising against nation and distress encompassing the whole earth. You are well aware of the frequent reports arriving from other parts of the world concerning earthquakes that strike numerous cities. We are afflicted by ceaseless pestilences. We do not yet see openly extraordinary phenomena in the sun, moon, or stars, but from certain alterations of the atmosphere we can already infer that they are not far off644.

Christian faith shares with other worldviews the conviction of the future end of the world, but it does not view this reality solely in its cosmological or anthropological aspects; rather, it considers it in its primordial theological and Christological dimension: *‘Alpha et omega / ipse Christus dominus / venit, venturus / iudicare homines’*645. The Lord Jesus, who created the world and came in the fullness of time to live His paschal mystery, will one day return in glory for judgment. Here lies the root of Christian hope, which has prompted the cry from the very beginning: ‘*Marana tha!* Come, Lord Jesus!’ (1 Cor 16:22); ‘He who testifies to these things says, “Surely I am coming soon.” Amen. Come, Lord Jesus’ (Rev 22:20).
The distinctive mark of Christianity with respect to other visions is that the final events, even if they will not be devoid of drama, are viewed as objects of faith, and therefore of hope. The end of the world is not merely the outcome of the ambivalence of human action or the blind forces of nature; rather, according to the Bible, it is first and foremost an event of grace, the fruit of the action of God the Creator and Redeemer. In her various Creeds, the Church confesses her faith in Christ's coming, the *parousia*646.
In this chapter we shall begin from this event (7.1), which entails a universal participation in the Resurrection, both on the part of human beings (anthropological dimension: 7.2) and of the world (cosmological dimension: 7.3), concluding by returning to the centrality of the Christological dimension in the universal judgment (7.4).

### 7.1. The Second Coming of Jesus in Glory

‘We herald not one coming only of Christ, but a second also, far more glorious than the former. For the former had a degree of patient endurance, but the latter shall bring the crown of a divine kingdom’647.
The dynamic of the ‘already and not yet’ is manifested in the event of Christ's return. He has already come, but His Kingdom and His victory have not yet been fully manifested648. He must return to unveil His glory definitively. The Greek verb *apokalyptō* means to remove the veil, bearing a sense kindred to the verb *epiphainō*, to show oneself, to appear649. Both are employed in the NT: the former gives its name to the final biblical book (the Apocalypse), whilst the latter is associated with the events of Christ's birth (Epiphany). What was manifested to a few in the first coming will be made manifest to all in the second.
‘For what is our hope or joy or crown of boasting before our Lord Jesus at his coming? Is it not you?’ (1 Thess 2:19). In the earliest writing of the NT, the word ‘coming’ translates the Greek noun *parousia*, from the verb *pareimi*, which means ‘to be present’650. In the secular usage of the Hellenistic era, it designated the solemn arrival of a sovereign at a city, accompanied by a magnificent procession. In the NT this term customarily designates the coming of Christ in glory, with power and majesty, to judge mankind. That this is an event destined to occur in the future is recalled by Saint Peter, who connects ‘the power and coming of our Lord Jesus Christ’ with the experience he lived together with James and John at the Transfiguration, which had made them ‘eyewitnesses of his majesty’ and hearers of the Father's voice, as he testifies: ‘we heard this voice borne from heaven, for we were with him on the holy mountain’ (2 Pet 1:16–18).
If Peter and Paul are so convinced, it is because Jesus spoke of it on various occasions, both in the so-called eschatological discourses and in the parables. In Matthew's eschatological discourse, after describing the cosmological aspects of the event, Jesus announces that ‘then will appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven, and then all the tribes of the earth will mourn, and they will see the Son of man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory’ (Mt 24:30). At the beginning of His description of the judgment, He confirms: ‘When the Son of man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on his glorious throne’ (Mt 25:31). Before the Sanhedrin, when asked whether He was the Christ, He replied: ‘I am; and you will see the Son of man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven’ (Mk 14:62), thereby applying to Himself the prophecy: ‘behold, with the clouds of heaven there came one like a son of man, and he came to the Ancient of Days and was presented before him. And to him was given dominion and glory and kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him’ (Dan 7:13–14). With regard to the parables, Jesus had spoken of His future advent under the imagery of the harvesting of the weeds: ‘so will it be at the close of the age. The Son of man will send his angels, and they will gather out of his kingdom all causes of sin and all evildoers’ (Mt 13:40–41).
There is, therefore, a lucid New Testament foundation for the formulation of the Church's faith in the return of Christ: ‘from thence He shall come to judge’ (Apostles' Creed: DS 30), ‘and He shall come again with glory’ (Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed: DS 150). In recent times, objections have been raised against the realism of this faith; after examining them, we shall reflect theologically on the gradual character of the revelation of this mystery and on the delay of this event; this entails investigating its relationship with our present, both through an analysis of the ‘signs’ of the Parousia and through the consideration of its sacramental presence. We shall treat all these matters in orderly sequence in this section.

### 7.1.1. Objections to the Realism of the Parousia: Some Recent Theories

The realism of the dynamic between present and future, between ‘already and not yet’, entails an equilibrium that may experience instability, which in turn can arise from the different dimensions of the question—anthropological, cosmological, and exegetical.
One might question the propriety of describing the final coming of Jesus as a ‘return’, given that, in order to return, one must first depart. It seems obvious that only one who has been absent can return. ‘In the strict sense, Christ has not departed; the Resurrection did not inaugurate a Christological vacuum in history, nor is the Parousia a kind of homecoming of an expatriate. On the contrary, faith confesses a real and active presence of Christ in the world’651. The very same Lord who ascended into heaven is He who said to His apostles: ‘I am with you always, to the close of the age’ (Mt 28:20). ‘To be with’ denotes being present, returning to the etymology of *parousia*, that is, presence; rather than the return of one who departed and is therefore absent, it is the consummation of a presence already at work652.
If the Lord is present, yet unseen, one might think that His presence is purely spiritual, perceptible only subjectively. This can lead to the view, from an anthropological perspective, that the Parousia spoken of in the NT is a ‘private’ encounter with Christ taking place in the human heart. Certain theories of a Platonic-individualist cast have ended by ‘privatising’ the *eschaton*653. According to this anthropological reductionism, advocated by certain followers of the German Protestant theologian A. Ritschl, the end of the world is simply the end of the individual person, namely death. In our treatment of intermediate eschatology we shall refer to the implications of this concentration of eschatology at the moment of death.
At the opposite extreme of this individualist vision lies the Hegelian exaltation of the public and collective dimension of the consummation of history, which would consist in the final reconciliation of the finite and the infinite, occurring not outside history but within it, as the fruit of a necessary ‘horizontal’ process. ‘The Church appears as the ideal form of the Kingdom, whose actual reality is the State’654. The exterior dimension of the *eschaton* is recovered, but its newness as a ‘vertical’ irruption of the divine into history is lost. ‘Hegel, however, never thematicised the reconciliation wrought by Christ in its tension towards future fulfilment; rather, he considered it as already concluded, to the point that in the Christian religion the present of the *eschaton* demands merely to be realised in the world’655.

Both conceptions coincide in rendering the Parousia irrelevant as a future event. When belief in the final transformation of the cosmos appeared discredited, certain authors from the natural sciences argued, by virtue of the principle of entropy, that an end of the universe cannot be dismissed *a priori*, whose expansion seems to lead us towards a Big Crunch, an event symmetrical to the Big Bang that gave it birth656. These hypotheses are far from being established as robust, and they must not be confused with what Revelation teaches657.
How are the biblical texts regarding the end of the universe to be interpreted? In recent decades, certain exegetical hypotheses have succumbed to the influence of the theories just outlined, adopting two presuppositions: that the NT speaks of the dimension of the present as the definitive moment; and that eschatological realities concern the interiority of the subject, not the material cosmos. Although opposed in their manner of reading the words of Jesus, these theories converge in denying the cosmic character of His coming658.
Consistent eschatology (*konsequente Eschatologie*, cf. J. Weiss, A. Schweitzer, and others) underscored the essentially eschatological character of Jesus' message, who preached the ‘idea of universal ethical perfection’659 based on conversion and rupture with the present worldly order, because He ‘presaged with certainty the proximity of the Kingdom of God’660, an expectation that was not fulfilled and consequently provoked in Him a profound crisis. Notwithstanding the apostolic experience of the Master's resurrection, that kingdom failed to materialize for them either; hence the Christian message must continually confront the perception that eschatology appears increasingly remote and less credible. The dilemma is that, since this is the essential dimension of the Gospel, the Gospel itself would have to be regarded as a well-intentioned deception.
In contrast to this reading, realised eschatology, proposed by Charles Dodd661, contends that the kingdom has already arrived and has been fully realised in the life, words, death, and resurrection of Jesus, as evidenced by the presentist language of John662. The apocalyptic elements found in the NT are considered contingent insertions intended to underscore divine transcendence; reading them realistically represents an erroneous exegesis to which the community of believers is constantly prone. Through this approach, an effort was made to rescue the credibility of Jesus, which had been called into question by consistent eschatology.
In line with the presentism of realised eschatology lies the supra-temporalism of R. Bultmann663, who considers relevant for faith solely the present moment in which the believer's conscience confesses the sovereignty of God and can awaken to recognize within it the possibility of the eschatological instant. For Bultmann, to be a Christian means to ‘exist eschatologically’664; here the term ‘eschatological’ has shed every temporal component. Following Heideggerian existentialism, Bultmann argues that man comes to be in decision, that he does not posit himself, but ‘becomes possible in the moment of encounter with the Thou. Against this backdrop, the significance of Christ can be explained by the fact that He Himself is the “eschatological event”’665; this ‘means breaking the subject-object dichotomy, evading time; it means the end of time, radical abstraction from the world’666. The cost of identifying eschatology with the act of self-giving is exorbitant: ‘Christianity is placed outside reality [...]. A faith that is no longer capable of entering into conflict with history (or at any rate with experienceable reality) has nothing left to say to it’667.
The positions just outlined stand in mutual opposition: in the first case the future aspect of eschatology is underscored, whilst in the other two the present aspect dominates. Yet all converge in denying the Parousia as an end of the world in a real sense, a consequence of polarising towards one aspect at the expense of the other—not only regarding the temporal dynamic, but also regarding the conjunction of interior and exterior elements, or the individual dimension versus the collective. In these theories, one seems forced to choose one aspect over the other, with no possibility of integration.

### 7.1.2. The Gradual Character of the Revelation of the Parousia in the New Testament

Matters standing thus, the best response remains Cullmann's motto: ‘Already and not yet’. One is not forced to make an exclusive choice between present and future, between individual and collective, between interior and exterior, between the Jesus who preaches the Kingdom and the Jesus who founds the Church. Reality is neither univocal nor equivocal, but analogical; this implies that it is neither wholly present nor wholly future, but that there exists a gradualness, a compatibility between both dimensions668.
In certain texts of the Gospel according to Matthew, Jesus might seem to confirm His conviction of an immediate end with the coming of the Son of man (cf. Mt 10:23; 16:28; 24:34). Since such an end did not arrive, it might appear that consistent eschatology is correct (Jesus was simply mistaken) or realised eschatology is right (the apostles interpolated the texts for their own purposes).
A sound principle of biblical interpretation dictates that individual texts must be interpreted in light of the whole669. In the Gospel of Matthew there are other passages that seem to exclude such immediacy. In the first place, those speaking of the institution of the Church (cf. Mt 16:18 and 18:15–35) and of her missionary nature (cf. Mt 28:16–20), which would be unintelligible had Jesus envisaged an immediate end. Furthermore, there are several of the Lord's parables, both in the context of His eschatological discourse (the faithful and unfaithful servants: cf. Mt 24:45–51; the wise and foolish virgins: cf. Mt 25:1–13; the parable of the talents: cf. Mt 25:14–30) and in chapter thirteen, which contains a collection of them (the mustard seed: cf. Mt 13:31–32; the leaven: cf. Mt 13:33; and earlier, the wheat and the tares: cf. Mt 13:24–30). In all these instances, it is implied that a considerable interval of time is required for the arrival of the Kingdom. If one adds that the passages cited earlier regarding the imminence of the end were interpreted by various Fathers and Doctors as referring not so much to the Parousia as to other decisive moments in salvation history (the profanation of the Temple and the destruction of Jerusalem in 70 AD)670, one may conclude that we are confronted with a reality that unfolds gradually through history, and which, considering the tenor of other texts, also entails an engagement of human action in its realization: ‘you will not see me again, until you say, “Blessed is he who comes in the name of the Lord”’ (Mt 23:39).
The writings of Paul and John bear witness to this articulation of the present and future aspects of the New Testament conception of the Parousia. It has been noted that Paul, in his early epistles, fortified by faith in the Resurrection of Christ, speaks openly of His future coming (cf. 1 Thess and 2 Thess), whereas in his later letters he focuses on the reality of union with Christ in faith (cf. Phil 1:21–26). John emphasizes that eternal life is a gift of God that we have already received (cf. Jn 3:36; 5:24; 6:47), yet he looks forward to a resurrection promised in the future (cf. Jn 6:54).

### 7.1.3. The Fulfilment and Delay of the Day of the Lord

‘This Jesus, who was taken up from you into heaven, will come in the same way as you saw him go into heaven’ (Acts 1:11). The words of the angels after the Ascension announce the return of Jesus in continuity with the prophetic revelation concerning the Day of the Lord (cf. Is 13:6–9; Ezek 30:3; Amos 5:18–20; Zeph 1:14), which in the NT thus becomes the ‘day of our Lord Jesus’ (2 Cor 1:14; cf. 1 Thess 5:2; 2 Thess 2:2; 2 Pet 3:10). Whereas in the OT the characteristics of this event center upon the power of God acting against the enemies of His people, in the NT the accent falls not only on power and glory, but also on the ultimate purpose of this coming, namely judgment, of which we shall speak below.
It is inadvisable to attempt to picture the *how* of the Parousia671. Biblical language regarding the end (like that regarding the beginning) is prophetic and symbolic. Nevertheless, both the Ascension and the apparitions of the Risen Lord, as earlier His Transfiguration, constitute points of reference, linked to events that transpired within our history, which intimate the visible and universal character of the final event, even while we remain ignorant of the particulars. If all things were created in Him, through Him, and for Him, it is natural to conceive the *that* of the Parousia as the complete fulfilment of Christ's relationship with all creation. The Parousia has been called the Easter of creation672: ‘When all things are subjected to him, then the Son himself will also be subjected to him who put all things under him, that God may be everything to every one’ (1 Cor 15:28).
Among the early Christians there existed a sincere expectation of the Lord Jesus' swift return. Faith in His promise, coupled with yearning and the desire to be reunited with Him—especially after experiencing persecutions and earthly trials—stimulated the conviction of its imminence. Saint Paul had to address the issue in his letters to the Thessalonians, in which he indicates that the waiting must be prolonged, exhorting them to live according to the Spirit in watchful expectation, not to be ‘quickly shaken in mind or excited, either by spirit or by word, or by letter purporting to be from us, to the effect that the day of the Lord has come’ (2 Thess 2:2).
The conviction of the proximity of the end of the world persisted during the early centuries of the patristic era673, in the writings of Justin and Irenaeus. The opinion circulated that the world was destined to endure for six thousand years; according to the calculations of the time, the world was already drawing near its end, as Cyprian and Lactantius attest. In the third century, with the intensification of persecutions, a popular expectation of an imminent exhaustion of the cosmos spread, linked to the Stoic concept of the *senectus mundi*, related in part to the decline of Roman civilization. Centuries later, Pope Gregory the Great was likewise convinced of the proximity of the end.
Christianity developed and grew within the experiential reality of the delay of the Parousia, which was assimilated in a natural, peaceful manner, without precipitating crises of faith674. This does not mean that at certain moments there was not a heightened awareness of the possibility that the world was ending, as occurred around the year 1000 with the phenomenon of millenarianism675, which resurfaced in various guises at the turn of the millennium in 2000. In reality, no human generation lacks the experience of the fragility and contingency of the world and of its own existence, manifested also by cosmic and human calamities.

### 7.1.4. The Signs of the Parousia

Closely connected with what has just been said is the doctrine concerning the signs of the Parousia676. Although there is currently no strict uniformity in the manner of enumerating them, theology generally speaks of three principal signs: the universal preaching of the Gospel, the conversion of Israel, and the manifestation of the Antichrist, to which are added the general apostasy and cosmic upheavals677.
Regarding the first, namely the universal preaching, it refers to the words of Jesus in Matthew's eschatological discourse: ‘And this gospel of the kingdom will be preached throughout the whole world, as a testimony to all nations; and then the end will come’ (Mt 24:14). It is a sign of a missionary nature, pointing to the charge entrusted by the Lord to the Church: ‘Go into all the world and preach the gospel to the whole creation’ (Mk 16:15). It is unnecessary to apply this prophecy to every single individual, as it more probably pertains to peoples and nations.
Related to this is the second sign, attested by the Epistle to the Romans:
> Lest you be wise in your own conceits, I want you to understand this mystery, brethren: a hardening has come upon part of Israel, until the full number of the Gentiles come in, and so all Israel will be saved; as it is written, ‘The Deliverer will come from Zion, he will banish ungodliness from Jacob’; ‘and this will be my covenant with them when I take away their sins’ (Rom 11:25–27).

The universal proclamation of the Gospel is linked to the salvation of Israel by virtue of a precise theological reason: the fidelity of God to the covenant established with the chosen people, which ‘has never been revoked’678, for ‘the gifts and the call of God are irrevocable’ (Rom 11:29). This is the foundation of prayer for ‘our elder brothers’679.
The mission of the Church is not different from that of her Master, who encountered an opposition that led Him to death. Since ‘a disciple is not above his teacher’ (Mt 10:24), and because Jesus foretold it (cf. Jn 15:20), the mission of the Church continually meets not merely circumstantial difficulties of understanding, but genuine opposition. The NT speaks both of the lawless one and of the Antichrist. Paul teaches that before the end ‘the rebellion comes first, and the man of lawlessness is revealed, the son of perdition, who opposes and exalts himself against every so-called god or object of worship’ (2 Thess 2:3–4), described also as the ‘lawless one’ whose coming ‘is by the activity of Satan with all power and with pretended signs and wonders, and with all wicked deception for those who are to perish, because they refused to love the truth and so be saved’ (2 Thess 2:9–10).
John uses another designation for this figure, both in the singular and in the plural, as a sign that we are in the last hour: ‘as you have heard that antichrist is coming, so now many antichrists have come’ (1 Jn 2:18). His diabolical nature is disclosed when his falsehood appears: ‘Who is the liar but he who denies that Jesus is the Christ? This is the antichrist, he who denies the Father and the Son’ (1 Jn 2:22), deceiving and seducing men so that they do not acknowledge the Christ (cf. 1 Jn 4:3; 2 Jn 7). In the Apocalypse, John employs the imagery of the two beasts that rise from the sea, working wonders and dominating the world for a time before being vanquished (cf. Rev 13:1–8, 11–18; 15:2). This signifies the persecutions that Christians must endure, not only in the beginning, but in every age, including our own. Christianity reflects in history the life of Jesus, and therefore, as with the Master, the final passage for the Church will be especially sorrowful680.
The ultimate meaning of these signs is a summons to vigilance681. We do not know when they may be considered fulfilled, nor the degree of intensity of their realization, nor even, once they might be deemed complete, ‘how much space of time will still elapse between their arrival and the second coming of Christ’682. ‘But of that day and hour no one knows, not even the angels of heaven, nor the Son, but the Father only’ (Mt 24:36). This ignorance has been interpreted within the Church as an opportunity to live in a spirit of missionary conversion and vigilance, with a deep sense of the patience of God and gratitude on our part.
The Christian is called to live each day as though it were the last. In this, he adopts a wisdom derived from pagan thought, purified of all hedonistic elements683. The fact that ‘in a certain sense the predictions of Christ are fulfilled in every generation [...]; every generation can conjecture that the precursor signs have been accomplished in its own time’684 demonstrates both the perception of imminence and the gradual nature of the revelation of the end, in that conjunction of the ‘already and not yet’ characteristic of Christian eschatology. The last things are not a matter concerning the future alone, but are also operative in the present, illuminating it with their light. Beholding the example of Christ, we can learn to ‘live every instant with a vibration of eternity’685.

### 7.1.5. The Sacramental Presence of the Parousia, Hope of the Church

Christ's promise to give eternal life in the present to those who eat His flesh whilst awaiting the future resurrection has given rise to diverse reflections on the link between this life and the next. Without denying the drama of the final hour, the coming of the Lord is a cause for joyful hope, because it is the fulfilment of life. The paschal mystery includes the Passion and death, but is ordained to the Resurrection. Paul, in words later adopted by the liturgy, exhorted us to ‘live sober, upright, and godly lives in this world, awaiting our blessed hope, the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior Jesus Christ’ (Tit 2:12–13). The Apocalypse concludes with the figure of the Church as the Bride awaiting the arrival of the Bridegroom: ‘The Spirit and the Bride say, “Come” [...]. He who testifies to these things says, “Surely I am coming soon.” Amen. Come, Lord Jesus’ (Rev 22:17, 20).
The second coming of Jesus was perceived in the early Church as a desirable reality, intimately bound to the acceptance of Christ's message and love for Him already in this life. For those who do not accept Christ, the prospect of His return may be a source of dread: ‘Let us not resist His first coming, so that we need not dread His second [...]. If He finds you prepared, it will be no harm to you that He came without your knowing’686.
Between the first Advent and the last, there is the *adventus medius*687. In line with Johannine present eschatology, and without abandoning the expectation of the definitive coming, Bernard shows that the intermediate time is not an empty void. Within it occurs the ‘middle coming’, an anticipatory presence that forms part of Christian existence, whose modes are manifold: ‘The Lord comes through His word; He comes in the sacraments, especially in the Most Holy Eucharist; He enters into my life through words or events’688.
We arrive here at the nexus between the Parousia and the liturgy of the Church, for Christian hope is fundamentally a liturgical hope. ‘The Parousia constitutes the fullness of the liturgy’ and ‘the liturgy is the Parousia in our midst’689. The interweaving of present and future that characterizes Christianity allows us to affirm that ‘every Eucharist is Parousia, the arrival of the Lord, and yet every Eucharist intensifies the longing that He reveal His hidden splendour [...]. Jesus, as crucified, continues to be He who departs, yet He is simultaneously He who, as pierced with outstretched hands, continues to come’690.
The Parousia becomes
> the obligation to live the liturgy as a feast of hope in expectation of the Lord of the cosmos, the Christ [...]. In His cross, the Lord has gone ‘to prepare a place for us in the Father's house’ (Jn 14:2f); in the liturgy, the Church, so to speak, accompanying Him, must in turn prepare dwellings for Him in the world [...]. Vigilance is transformed into the concrete task of rendering the liturgy reality, in expectation that the Lord Himself will finally bestow upon it that ultimate reality which for now can be sought only in images691.

Since the liturgy is the work of God, but also our work, it expresses the cooperation of the Christian with the arrival of the Lord, according to the words of Peter: ‘waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God’ (2 Pet 3:12). Commenting on the text of the third secret of Fatima, Ratzinger reiterated that
> the future is by no means immutably fixed [...]; the vision in reality occurs only to summon freedom onto the stage and to direct it in a positive direction. The purpose of the vision is therefore not to show a film of an irremediably predetermined future. Its purpose is precisely the opposite: to mobilize the forces of change for the good692.

The maternal hand that deflected the bullet shows that ‘there is no immutable destiny, that faith and prayer are powers that can influence history’693. The last things, like the first, are so close to the mystery of God that they remain largely elusive to our grasp, yet accessible in some measure through symbolic language and the power of faith and prayer.
### 7.2. The Resurrection of the Dead

> For the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a cry of command, with the archangel’s call, and with the sound of the trumpet of God. And the dead in Christ will rise first; then we who are alive, who are left, shall be caught up together with them in the clouds to meet the Lord in the air; and so we shall always be with the Lord (1 Thess 4:16–18).

The immediate effect of the second coming of Jesus is the resurrection of the dead. This is possible because ‘Christ has been raised from the dead, the first fruits of those who have fallen asleep’ (1 Cor 15:20). Here we touch upon the very foundation of faith: ‘Now if Christ is preached as raised from the dead, how can some of you say that there is no resurrection of the dead? But if there is no resurrection of the dead, then Christ has not been raised; if Christ has not been raised, then our preaching is in vain and your faith is in vain’ (1 Cor 15:12–14).
The Incarnation and the Resurrection, central events of Christianity, refer directly to the positive value of matter within God's design, manifesting the unity of the plan of creation and salvation694. The return of Christ does not have as its goal a spiritualisation of the created order that would imply the destruction of its material dimension, but rather reveals the eternal vocation of matter. Faith in the resurrection is intrinsically bound to faith in the Creator. The Gnostic notion of the intrinsic malice of the bodily element is profoundly anti-Christian. ‘The work of Heaven alone was material; the making of a material world. The work of Hell is entirely spiritual’695.
The affirmation of the goodness and permanent endurance of corporeality stood in sharp contrast to the prevailing worldviews at the inception of Christianity, as demonstrated by the episode of Paul at the Areopagus. While this was unthinkable for the philosophy of that era, traces of belief in a resurrection could be discerned in certain Egyptian or Persian religious rituals. There was also the Orphic and Pythagorean doctrine of the transmigration of souls (*metempsychosis*), which reappeared in diverse guises throughout history as the doctrine of reincarnation. Although it shares with the Christian vision the affirmation of corporeality's participation in human destiny, the differences between them are manifest: for us, the resurrection is not a multi-stage process in which the body is merely accidental to the spirit; the body is rather a gift of God bestowed once and for all, in continuity with our created being.
Certain Fathers of the Church adopted the image of the phoenix rising from its own ashes as a symbol of the risen Christ, purifying it of pagan accretions and applying it to the specificity of Christian faith. It served as a way of demonstrating that faith is not alien to the cosmos, and that the power of God in raising the dead does not contradict the laws of nature, but confers upon them a new and definitive horizon of life696:
> When it leaves the holy places out of a desire for rebirth, it directs its flight towards that part of the world where death holds sway [...]. It then constructs its nest, which is also its tomb: for it dies in order to live, and yet gives itself life [...]. It is indeed itself, yet not the same, for it is both the same and not itself, attaining eternal life through the efficacy of death697.

This image could not fail to evoke the memory of the dust from which man was taken and to which, following sin, he must return; and simultaneously, the memory of the Resurrection of Jesus, which introduced into creation a momentous transformation because, starting from that dust, from death, one can now be reborn to new life.
We shall examine, first, the biblical specificity of faith in the resurrection. Secondly, we shall analyse the philosophical difficulties and possibilities inherent in this doctrine. Once the former are resolved, the latter will open onto a theological reflection seeking to elucidate both the newness and the continuity of the risen body in relation to the earthly body. Finally, we shall address the influence of hope in the resurrection upon our present life.

### 7.2.1. The Teaching of the Old and New Testaments

When Jesus seeks to comfort Martha after the death of her brother Lazarus, He refers to faith in the resurrection, which His interlocutor affirms: ‘Jesus said to her, “Your brother will rise again.” Martha said to him, “I know that he will rise again in the resurrection at the last day”’ (Jn 11:23–24). This seems to indicate that throughout the history of Israel there developed and became established, alongside conviction concerning the Day of YHWH at the end of the world, the notion of a personal resurrection. Nevertheless, the Gospel of Mark recounts that, descending from the mountain of the Transfiguration, when Jesus commanded them ‘to tell no one what they had seen, until the Son of man should have risen from the dead’, Peter, James, and John ‘kept the matter to themselves, questioning what the rising from the dead meant’ (Mk 9:9–10).
One may therefore ask to what extent faith in a universal resurrection at the end of time was clear in the OT and, if so, how widely it was shared by all698. Regarding the first aspect, the answer is that only at the close of the Old Testament period, in late apocalyptic texts, do we find an explicit affirmation of this doctrine. In the Book of Daniel, composed around 165 BC at the time of the persecution of the Jews by the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes, it is affirmed with reference to the end times: ‘And many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt’ (Dan 12:2).
‘This is the most explicit formulation of faith in the resurrection found in the Old Testament’699. The reference to those sleeping ‘in the dust of the earth’ discloses the manner in which the people of Israel conceived the afterlife. Various passages describe the survival in the netherworld (*Sheol*) of the dead (*rephaim*). The word *Sheol*, of debated etymology700, is customarily translated as ‘underworld’ or ‘abode of the dead’, not in the sense of what we call hell, but in relation to the depths of the earth (cf. Deut 32:22), a ‘land of gloom and chaos, where light is as darkness’ (Job 10:22), whither the dead descend (cf. Num 16:30; 1 Kgs 2:6; Prov 1:12; 7:27), the righteous and the wicked alike (cf. Ps 89:48), to endure a shadowy existence: ‘there is no work or thought or knowledge or wisdom in Sheol, to which you are going’ (Eccles 9:10). There the dead retain a certain consciousness, not being wholly consigned to oblivion (cf. Job 26:5; Is 14:9–17; Ezek 32:21). They were neither mere corpses nor fully active spirits. ‘Therefore, that after death the *rephaim* survive in Sheol in a shadowy existence, which is nonetheless not annihilation, belongs to the most ancient conceptions of Israel concerning the afterlife’701; this implies a belief in the immortality of the soul, which will become fully manifest in the Book of Wisdom.
In the verse from Daniel cited above, it is said that the dead shall awake. Such an awakening is the fruit of God's action, as explicitly stated elsewhere: ‘The Lord kills and brings to life; he brings down to Sheol and raises up’ (1 Sam 2:6). But the true novelty of the text is that the resurrection will occur under different modalities according to the deeds of individual persons. Israel was convinced of its endurance as a people by virtue of God's fidelity to the covenant—hence the importance of offspring as a sign of divine blessing. This endurance was not understood as immobility; on the contrary, their history of falls and restorations is described by certain prophetic texts, especially of the post-exilic era, in terms of death and resurrection. There was an idea of resurrection conceived not in a personal but in a collective sense, linked to the response given as a covenant people. The Books of Kings narrate miraculous resuscitations performed by Elijah and Elisha, but in a context marked by the transitory and earthly, not by the definitive702.
In line with Daniel, and contemporary with it, stands the Second Book of Maccabees, which recounts the persecution under Antiochus Epiphanes. One of the seven Maccabean brothers, about to undergo martyrdom, professes his faith and hope in the God who raises up and who rewards or punishes: ‘I got these from Heaven, and because of his laws I disdain them, and from him I hope to get them back again [...]. One cannot but choose to die at the hands of men and to cherish the hope that God gives of being raised again by him. But for you there will be no resurrection to life!’ (2 Macc 7:11, 14). Here it is underscored that the resurrection will encompass all, and that there will be different outcomes according to one's fidelity to God in action.
In this passage an element emerges explicitly that in Daniel remained only implicit: that the resurrection will occur by virtue of divine power, in perfect continuity with Israel's faith in God the Creator, whose dominion extends even to Sheol, the realm of the dead, because He is able to bring back up from thence (cf. 1 Sam 2:6; Ps 16:10; 49:15), being the ‘living God’ (1 Sam 17:26, 36), the God who created life in its manifold manifestations, and into whose design death did not enter: God ‘did not make death, and he does not delight in the death of the living. For he created all things that they might exist’ (Wis 1:13–14). ‘Communion with God is stronger than the destruction of the body’703.
The nexus between faith in the Creator and hope in the resurrection is manifested in the exhortation of the mother of the Maccabean brothers:
> I do not know how you came into being in my womb. It was not I who gave you life and breath, nor I who set in order the elements within each of you. Therefore the Creator of the world, who shaped the beginning of man and devised the origin of all things, will in his mercy give life and breath back to you again, since you now forget yourselves for the sake of his laws [...]. I beseech you, my child, to look at the heaven and the earth and see everything that is in them, and recognize that God did not make them out of things that existed. Thus also mankind comes into being. Do not fear this butcher, but prove worthy of your brothers. Accept death, so that in God’s mercy I may get you back again with your brothers (2 Macc 7:22–23, 28–29).

The existence of such a text might lead one to assume that, by the time of the NT, the doctrine of the resurrection of the dead ought to have been universally clear; yet this was not the case, as can be deduced not only from Peter's perplexity regarding the meaning of the phrase ‘rising from the dead’704. A more decisive piece of evidence is the controversy between the Sadducees and the Pharisees. The former denied the existence of angels and the reality of the final resurrection, whereas the latter affirmed both. When the Sadducees approached Jesus, contriving the hypothetical case of a woman married successively to seven brothers (cf. Mk 12:18–27) and inquiring whose wife she would be in the resurrection, the Lord's reply was instructive. He began by correcting a distorted conception of the afterlife, as if in the resurrection the future world were merely a replica of this one with its material necessities, as the Pharisees taught. Wishing to emphasize discontinuity, Jesus said that ‘when they rise from the dead, they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven’ (Mk 12:25). With these words, Christ taught the Pharisees to adopt a more adequate vision of the world to come. Immediately thereafter, the Master addressed the Sadducees: ‘And as for the dead being raised, have you not read in the book of Moses, in the passage about the bush, how God said to him, “I am the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob”? He is not God of the dead, but of the living; you are quite wrong’ (Mk 12:26–27). Jesus argued by citing Exodus, belonging to the Torah which the Sadducees accepted, thereby linking once again faith in the resurrection to faith in the God of the living:
> It would be to make God a God of the dead and hence to subvert the Old Testament concept of God, were one to declare dead those who belong to Him who is life [...]. Faith in the resurrection forms part of faith in God Himself [...]. To belong to Him, to be called by Him, means to find oneself in eternal life705.

In His disputes with the Jews, Jesus united the themes of the God of life, judgment, and resurrection, explicitly citing Daniel:
> For as the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself, and has given him authority to execute judgment, because he is the Son of man. Do not marvel at this; for the hour is coming when all who are in the tombs will hear his voice and come forth, those who have done good, to the resurrection of life, and those who have done evil, to the resurrection of judgment (Jn 5:26–29).

Jesus' preaching on the resurrection stands in continuity with the OT. A further step is seen in the miraculous resuscitations worked by Jesus, which express His dominion over life and death: the daughter of Jairus (cf. Mk 5:21–43), the son of the widow of Nain (cf. Lk 7:11–17), and Lazarus (cf. Jn 11:1–44). These resuscitations are not yet the definitive manifestation, since they did not prevent the subsequent death of these persons. The significance of these signs was to prepare and, in some measure, anticipate the truth of the words spoken to Martha: ‘I am the resurrection and the life; he who believes in me, though he die, yet shall he live’ (Jn 11:25). Jesus reveals Himself as the personal fulfilment of the promise of resurrection, which would be accomplished in His paschal mystery706.
The novelty lies not in Jesus sharing the expectation of resurrection, but in His identifying Himself with it, passing from proclamation to reality: *‘Surrexit Dominus vere, alleluia!* The Lord has risen indeed’ (Lk 24:34). Jesus is He who on Easter Day rises to die no more, inaugurating the new creation, a new way of being human. ‘Christ has been raised from the dead, the first fruits of those who have fallen asleep’ (1 Cor 15:20). His disciples can participate in this mystery through Baptism, whereby ‘we were buried therefore with him by baptism into death, so that as Christ was raised from the dead by the glory of the Father, we too might walk in newness of life’ (Rom 6:4; cf. 1 Pet 3:21). The disciple no longer belongs to himself, but to the Lord, whose Spirit makes His dwelling in him: ‘If the Spirit of him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised Christ Jesus from the dead will give life to your mortal bodies also through his Spirit who dwells in you’ (Rom 8:11), and this indwelling means that ‘your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit within you’ (1 Cor 6:19).
In the apparitions of the Risen Lord there is an element worthy of note: we refer not to the empty tomb, which underscores continuity between the Crucified and the Risen One, but to the difficulty that His visible recognition provoked in the witnesses. Initially, several experience difficulty in identifying Him, as happens to Mary weeping in the garden (cf. Jn 20:14–16), Thomas who doubts (cf. Jn 20:24–28), Peter on the shore of the lake (cf. Jn 21:3–7), and the disciples on the road to Emmaus (cf. Lk 24:13–32). Then that mysterious figure performs a gesture familiar to the interlocutor (letting His voice be heard, showing His wounds, preparing the fire, breaking the bread), facilitating recognition and eliciting confessions of faith, among which that of Thomas is paradigmatic: ‘My Lord and my God!’ (Jn 20:28).
Why do they not recognize Him at first, yet do so afterwards? Here we stand before the mystery of the irruption of the *eschaton* (that which is ultimate), indeed of the *eschatos* (He who is the Last), into our history, with the tension characteristic of the ‘already and not yet’, of present and future, continuity and discontinuity, identity and newness. Jesus is Himself, yet He is no longer the same. Through Baptism, the Christian participates in this mystery: ‘it is no longer I who live, but Christ who lives in me’ (Gal 2:20). ‘I, yet not I: this is the formula of Christian existence grounded in Baptism, the formula of resurrection within time’707. This paradox leads us to a theological reflection on the nature of risen bodies, to demonstrate the appropriateness of which we must first address several difficulties.

### 7.2.2. The Philosophical Difficulties and Possibilities of This Doctrine

Saint Paul had to confront on numerous occasions the perplexity that faith in the resurrection aroused, both within Israel and among the Gentiles. Before the Sanhedrin, after being arrested in Jerusalem, Paul defended himself shrewdly: ‘Brethren, I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees; with respect to the hope and the resurrection of the dead I am on trial’ (Acts 23:6). These words ignited a fierce dispute between both factions, ‘for the Sadducees say that there is no resurrection, nor angel, nor spirit; but the Pharisees acknowledge them all’ (Acts 23:8). Transferred to Caesarea before Governor Felix and the high priest Ananias, Paul professed his hope ‘that there will be a resurrection of both the just and the unjust’ (Acts 24:15); indeed, ‘it is with respect to the resurrection of the dead that I am on trial before you this day’ (Acts 24:21). After two years' imprisonment, in the presence of the new governor Festus, King Agrippa wished to hear Paul's defence: ‘And now I stand here on trial for hope in the promise made by God to our fathers [...]. And for this hope I am accused by Jews, O king! Why is it thought incredible by any of you that God raises the dead?’ (Acts 26:6, 8). Having recounted his conversion, he returned to the core point, saying ‘nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would come to pass: that the Christ must suffer, and that, by being the first to rise from the dead, he would proclaim light both to the people and to the Gentiles’ (Acts 26:22–23). These words elicited diverse reactions: while ‘Festus said with a loud voice, “Paul, you are mad; your great learning is turning you mad”’ (Acts 26:24), King Agrippa remarked: ‘In a short time you think to make me a Christian!’ (Acts 26:28).
In the Areopagus address, after securing the attention of his hearers by starting from the Creator, Paul concluded by announcing the day appointed by God ‘on which he will judge the world in righteousness by a man whom he has appointed, and of this he has given assurance to all men by raising him from the dead’ (Acts 17:31). The response was far from positive: ‘Now when they heard of the resurrection of the dead, some mocked; but others said, “We will hear you again about this”’ (Acts 17:32). This occurred because ‘those who believed in the immortality of the soul were bound to encounter far greater difficulty than others in accepting the Christian doctrine of the resurrection’708.
The Fathers of the Church likewise experienced the extent to which pagans derided faith in the resurrection: ‘On no other subject does the Christian faith face attacks so violent, obstinate, tenacious, and fierce as regarding the resurrection of the flesh’709. For this reason, since it constituted the very core of Christian faith, they taught it from the outset, composing dedicated treatises on the resurrection710.
The contrast with the spirit of the age was stark711. On the one hand, it seemed to affront common sense, for one could not conceive that corruptible matter was destined for glory712. On the other hand, in the dominant philosophy—Neoplatonism—matter was conceived as alien to spirit, regarded as its prison, an evil principle from which the soul had to be liberated. Like the Gnostics, Neoplatonists insisted on what they deemed man's true salvation: the immortality of the soul alone.
In the Christian vision of the resurrection, there was no opposition between the two affirmations:
> Many pagan philosophers have debated this widely, leaving written in numerous books that the human soul is immortal. Yet when it comes to the problem of the resurrection of the flesh, they do not hesitate, but openly oppose it. And so vehement is their contradiction that they maintain it to be utterly impossible that this earthly flesh should ascend into heaven713.

Arguments had to be formulated to defend the reasonableness of faith in the resurrection. Gregory the Great noted how many ‘doubt the resurrection, and seeing that in the graves the flesh rots and the bones turn to dust, they cannot believe that bones and flesh could ever be reconstituted from those remains’. He responded by directing contemplation to the omnipotence of God who created all things: ‘What wonder is it if He who created all things from nothing should reconstitute man from dust? It is a far more wondrous deed to have brought heaven and earth out of nothing than to restore a man to life from dust’714. This is a summons to place trust in the infinite power of God the Creator, which does not contradict the dynamic of nature created by Him, but brings it to perfection.
Christianity teaches that ‘matter and the human body acquire a role and dignity beyond all expectation. In the light of the final resurrection, matter, though created and corruptible, is revealed to possess a genuine vocation to eternity’715. Adopting the Aristotelian doctrine of man's substantial unity, which conceives the soul as the form of the body (*anima forma corporis*), Saint Thomas demonstrates the philosophical horizons opened by the resurrection, simultaneously combating the Manichaean tendencies emerging in the medieval era. He maintains that the state of a soul separated from its body is not only unnatural, but even ‘contrary to nature’ (*contra naturam*)716, in so far as its natural activity is to inform the body. This means that the Christian doctrine of the resurrection, affirming the future reunion of soul and body, is ‘natural’ in its finality, even though brought about solely by divine power transcending nature: ‘The resurrection is natural with respect to its end, in as much as it is natural for the soul to be united to the body; yet its active principle is not natural, but will be caused solely by the power of God’717. Commenting on the Angelic Doctor's position, it has been written:
> If one takes as a principle the unity of man, one must conclude in favour of the reasonable necessity of the resurrection of the body to guarantee the immortality of the soul in its proper order. Thomas says that if the resurrection is denied, it is very difficult to maintain the immortality of the soul. For it is evident that the soul is united to the body naturally, whereas it is separated from it contrary to its nature. Thus, without the body, the soul is imperfect718.

In modern philosophy, a progressive ‘Platonic’ retreat occurred towards the *res cogitans* at the expense of the *res extensa*. An exaggerated focus on human subjectivity led philosophers, even while remaining Christian, to view spiritualism as more conformable to reason. Kant asserted:
> Under this latter premise (that of spiritualism), reason can have no interest in dragging into eternity a body which (if personality rests on physical identity), however purified it may be, must always consist of the same matter that constitutes the basis of its organism, and for which, moreover, during his lifetime, man never harboured any great affection719.

This implies a perilous bifurcation between the sphere of eschatological salvation, reduced to the interior realm, and the sphere of matter, body, and, by extension, the visible and social dimension of human life.
Along this trajectory one might speak of a purely symbolic resurrection, as in Bultmann, for whom the resurrection is individual and existential, yet disconnected from the physical world; through faith, Christians are already raised from the dead, already saved through faith in Christ720.
The proposal of a real future resurrection continues to engage contemporary man. Happiness is that desire necessary yet impossible for man within the horizon of worldly experience, which, according to Kant, God might render possible in a distinct, trans-mundane realm. The founder of modern ethics, autonomous from religion, leaves experience *ad portas spei*, at the threshold of hope721.
Christianity represents a challenge to philosophy, in so far as it affirms that ‘a concrete, historical, and real person, belonging to common human experience, has already died and has already been raised by God without waiting for the general resurrection anticipated at the end of history’722. It is likewise a challenge to religion, because the hallmark of Christianity in distinction to Judaism and Islam consists in the fact that, whereas the latter place the resurrection at the end of time and the judgment at the center of faith, thereby becoming religions of the future, Christianity distinguishes the future Parousia from its center—the event that has already transpired in the midst of our experience and history: the Resurrection of Christ. While the others are eschatological religions, Christianity is fundamentally Christological723.
Opening oneself to the resurrection entails expanding the mind to the possibility of a divine action without precedent in mundane experience which, through the persistence of the individual self beyond death, represents a surplus of reality724. Saint Thomas speaks of the Resurrection of Jesus as exemplary cause and also as efficient cause, that it might be a font of hope. ‘What is necessary but impossible for man becomes a real possibility if the Risen One acts not only as *exemplum* but also as *sacramentum*, producing Himself the transforming conditions that render such an exemplar materially achievable’725.
Christianity offers ‘the possibility of transforming experience into hope’726. ‘Faith in the resurrection of the dead and hope in eternal life open our eyes to the ultimate meaning of our existence [...]. Stripped of the light of faith, the entire universe ends up enclosed within a tomb without future, without hope’727. The tomb of Christ has been opened, meaning that the risen Jesus ‘is not the same, but He is Himself’728. We conclude by analysing this paradox.

### 7.2.3. The Risen Body: Between Identity and Newness with Respect to the Earthly Body

### 7.2.3.1. The Newness of the Risen Body

‘Our commonwealth is in heaven, and from it we await a Savior, the Lord Jesus Christ, who will change our lowly body to be like his glorious body, by the power which enables him even to subject all things to himself’ (Phil 3:20–21). This and other NT texts (cf. Rom 6:5–9; 8:10–17; Lk 24:36–39; Jn 20:19–28) indicate that the resurrection involves a discontinuity with the present condition: neither the resurrection of Jesus nor that promised to us is a return to earthly temporality, but a new, glorious condition, a life that dies no more (cf. Mt 13:43; Lk 20:36; Rev 7:16–17; 21:4f).
Paradigmatic is chapter 15 of the First Epistle to the Corinthians, wherein
> Paul decisively opposes the prevailing Jewish conception, which understood the risen body as absolutely identical to the earthly body and the world of the resurrection as the simple continuation of the earthly world [...]. For him, corporeality exists not only in the Adamic sense, that is, as an ‘animated body’ (*sōma psychikon*), but also in the Christological sense, on the model of the resurrection of Jesus Christ, as a corporeality proceeding from the Holy Spirit. Materialistic realism is countered not by spiritualism, but by a pneumatic realism729.

A challenge to naturalism is also found in John: on the one hand, the Evangelist records the words of Jesus: ‘my flesh is food indeed, and my blood is drink indeed. He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood abides in me, and I in him’ (Jn 6:55–56); on the other hand, he reports words teaching its true meaning: ‘It is the Spirit that gives life, the flesh is of no avail’ (Jn 6:63). ‘The “flesh” of Christ is “Spirit”, but the Spirit of Christ is “flesh”: only in this tension shines forth the singular and new realism of the Risen One, transcending all naturalisms and spiritualisms’730.
It is arduous to describe the state of risen bodies, but it would be still more so had we not the indications furnished by the Gospels regarding the apparitions of the risen Jesus, together with Paul's reflections in 1 Corinthians 15. On this basis, Thomas Aquinas identified certain qualities indicating the specificity and newness of risen bodies compared to our earthly state.
Although risen bodies possess the same nature as the earthly body, ‘their dispositions will be diverse; primarily because all bodies of the risen, both of the good and of the wicked, will be incorruptible’731, as Saint Paul asserts: ‘For this perishable nature must put on the imperishable, and this mortal nature must put on immortality’ (1 Cor 15:53). The body, corruptible in itself, is rendered incorruptible by divine power, which establishes the soul as sovereign over the body regarding its vivifying function. The consequence of this incorruptibility is that the risen human being becomes immortal732.
This characteristic is shared by the bodies of the damned, for their souls and bodies are good by nature in as much as created; hence Paul's statement applies also to them: ‘the dead will be raised imperishable’ (1 Cor 15:52)733. Aquinas illustrates here the equilibrium between Christological and metaphysical dimensions, in relation to the distinction between nature and person. We shall rise to die no more because the cause of our resurrection is the Resurrection of Jesus, who overcame sin and with it death, living forever. The effects differ according to whether one considers human nature or the human person: ‘The Son of God assumed human nature to restore it. Therefore, whatever constitutes a defect of nature will be restored in all, and so all will return from death to life. Conversely, defects of the person will be remedied only in those who have adhered to Christ’734. Just as Adam's sin had consequences for the human nature he transmitted, all the more does the Resurrection of Christ, the Last Adam, have consequences for human nature, which will rise at the end of time in every individual.
Hence one understands why, according to Aquinas, after the resurrection there will be no human generation: generation, as Aristotle noted, serves the perpetuation of the species, participating in God's perpetuity.
> Now it is evident that the resurrection is not ordained to the perpetuation of the species, since this could be attained through generation. Therefore, it must be ordained to the perpetuation of the individual. But not according to the soul alone, for this the soul already possessed prior to the resurrection. Consequently, it must be according to the composite. Therefore, the man who rises will live in perpetuity735.

Vast differences will nonetheless prevail: glorious bodies will possess specific properties arising from the soul's elevation to the vision of God, overflowing onto the subordinate body by divine power not only in being, but also in operation736. This is manifested in four qualities: clarity (*claritas*), agility (*agilitas*), impassibility (*impassibilitas*), and subtilty or spirituality (*subtilitas*).
Clarity (*claritas*) is the refulgence in the body of the light of the soul enjoying the beatific vision of God, according to Paul's words: ‘It is sown in dishonour, it is raised in glory’ (1 Cor 15:43), and Jesus' proclamation: ‘Then the righteous will shine like the sun in the kingdom of their Father’ (Mt 13:43)737.
The soul enjoying the vision of God experiences the fulfilment of its every desire; and as the body is moved by the desire of the soul, the glorious body will obey the spirit in all things; hence the risen bodies of the blessed will be agile. Aquinas finds the biblical basis in the continuation of the Pauline verse: ‘It is sown in weakness, it is raised in power’ (1 Cor 15:43), as well as in Wisdom: ‘they will run like sparks through the stubble’ (Wis 3:7)738.
The body perfected by the soul, and in proportion to it, will be immune to all suffering and corruption, both in act and in potency: in act, because no defect or decay will exist in the risen; in potency, because they will be unable to undergo any harm. Thus they will enjoy the gift of impassibility, expressed by Saint Paul: ‘It is sown in corruption, it is raised in incorruption’ (1 Cor 15:42)739.
The final quality summarizes the preceding ones, describing the glorious body as a spiritual body. It is a body perfectly submissive to the soul, participating in its characteristics as far as possible, since a being is more perfect in nature the more perfectly matter is subordinated to form. Saint Paul writes: ‘It is sown a physical body, it is raised a spiritual body’ (1 Cor 15:44), not because the body becomes spirit, but because it is entirely subject to spirit. Closely linked with this is the subtilty (*subtilitas*) of the glorious body, enabling spatial flexibility740. Scheeben observes that while the Fathers spoke of the subtilty of Christ's glorious body—by virtue of which He passed through closed doors, yet could immediately be touched—Aquinas understands by subtilty essentially a spiritualisation of the body741.
The bodies of the condemned, while preserving incorruptibility, will exhibit contrary qualities. They will not be spiritual bodies, for not having submitted to the spirit, their souls will be carnal. They will lack agility (a quality tied to docility to the spirit), being heavy and opaque, manifesting the soul's rebellion against God. They will remain passible—even more so than in their earthly condition—enduring sensible affliction reflecting the frustration of their natural desire for happiness. They will be dark and gloomy, reflecting the absence of divine light in their souls. In short, the bodies of the damned will rise without glory742.

### 7.2.3.2. Identity Between the Risen Body and the Earthly Body

The preceding considerations must not be interpreted as if the ‘spiritual body’ (1 Cor 15:44) implied a substantial mutation of corporeality into pure spirit. Our resurrection will conform to that of Jesus Christ (cf. Phil 3:20–21), who possessed a tangible body, as He Himself reminded the astonished apostles who thought they saw a spirit (cf. Lk 24:39). Risen man will possess a tangible body composed of flesh and bones; otherwise, ‘he would be man only in an equivocal sense’743. It is impossible for the human body to be converted into a spiritual substance.
Christian faith affirms not only that the risen body is genuinely bodily, but that it is the *same* body, that there exists an identity between the body in this life and the body in eternal life. In the early Church's professions of faith, alongside *resurrectionem mortuorum*, the formula *carnis resurrectionem* spread to underscore this continuity744. Julian of Toledo taught that ‘we must give no credence to those who prate that it is not this body that will rise, but another, intangible one. Following the truth expressed by Sacred Scripture, each one will rise receiving the body in which he now lives’745.
Continuity is evidenced by the fact that one rises as male or female. In the state of glory, the sexual condition of the subject is not lost746, nor are the organs of nutrition eliminated, even though neither function (nutrition and reproduction) will be operative747.
Aquinas appears to locate the identity between the earthly and risen body in the continuity of the immortal soul: ‘Since therefore the soul will then be specifically the same, it is evident that it will have specifically identical matter. Therefore, after the resurrection the body will be specifically identical to what it was before’748. It was already clear in his time that the numerical identity of the body cannot reside in the material continuity of its component particles, known to change throughout life. What preserves the identity of the individual from birth to death is the form749:
> For a man to rise numerically identical, it is not required that he resume all that existed materially in him throughout the course of his life; but that he assume as much as suffices to attain due stature; and above all it seems that what was most thoroughly informed by the form to constitute the human species must be reassumed750.

Through this solution, Aquinas distanced himself from those who saw in the risen body an idealized, ethereal body in a Platonic sense—a view held by Origen, which the Church never accepted751. The reception of Aristotle permitted a reformulated realism through the doctrine of the soul as *forma corporis*: ‘Both—the soul and the body—are realities only when referred to each other’752. Ratzinger highlighted two corollaries: first, that ‘the soul can never wholly renounce its relation to matter’; and second, that ‘precisely because corporeality belongs so inseparably to human being, the identity of corporeality is determined not by matter, but by the soul’753. If pressed with strict rigor, this Thomistic view might struggle to explain physical continuity.
While Aquinas, ‘profoundly impressed by the consequences of his thesis, moderated it’, shortly after him Durandus of Saint-Pourçain (c. 1275–1334) dared to ground ‘the identity of the resurrection body exclusively upon the identity of the soul’754. This position, maintaining a purely formal identity, fails to take ‘sufficient account of the eschatological implications of the liturgical practice of venerating bodily relics of the saints (for all liturgy is celebrated in anticipation of the Parousia), and the dogma of the Assumption of Our Lady into heaven’755. Notwithstanding this inadequacy, the core insight remains valid: ‘the unity between body and soul according to the intention of creation; an unity implying, on the one hand, the inseparable ordination of the soul to matter, yet meaning, on the other hand, that bodily identity is to be conceived as determined not by matter, but by the person, by the soul’756.
Since the hylomorphic schema applied to the human being does not completely resolve the dilemma—identity cannot be grounded exclusively either in matter or in form—how are we to explain the continuity and identity between the risen body and the earthly body? Aquinas provides a clue when addressing the age at which we shall rise: ‘All must rise at the age of Christ, which is youth, because the perfection of nature is attained only therein. Infancy has not yet reached perfection of nature through development; and old age, through decay, has already departed from it’757. The foundation is the Pauline text envisioning our arrival ‘to mature manhood, to the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ’ (Eph 4:13). The Angelic Doctor specifies: ‘Old age is venerable not for the conditions of the body, which are defective, but for the wisdom of the soul [...]. Hence in the elect, through the fullness of divine wisdom found in them, the venerable character of old age will remain, but its defects will be eradicated’758.
This opens the hylomorphic schema onto a distinct horizon, kindred to the Augustinian conception of memory, wherein man ‘finds past, present, and future gathered in a singular manner’, so that memory ‘manifests both man's unique bond with time and his superiority over time’759. ‘Time is not in man merely a physical property remaining external to him, but shapes him in knowing, loving, declining, and maturing, within his very humanity’760; hence, rather than physical time, there exists for us an anthropological time that might be termed ‘memory-time’761. This indestructible relationship of human life with history, and therefore with its sociality, is rooted in God's perpetual relationship with human history, finding its ultimate ground in the Incarnation762.
The fact that the risen Jesus retains the wounds of His Passion discloses His identity, as He said in the Upper Room to doubting Thomas: ‘Put your finger here, and see my hands; and put out your hand, and place it in my side; do not be faithless, but believing’ (Jn 20:27). The glory of the Risen Lord is not incompatible with assuming the history of His Passion763. This vision accords with the Fourth Lateran Council on the resurrection: ‘all will rise with their own bodies which they now bear, so that they may receive according to their works, whether good or evil: the latter, perpetual punishment with the devil; the former, eternal glory with Christ’ (DS 801). According to the Apocalypse, those who die in the Lord ‘may rest from their labours, for their deeds follow them!’ (Rev 14:13).
These reflections allow us to affirm that man rises with his history:
> ‘Body’ is not merely form present in space; it also has a history. From birth to death it assumes countless forms. Which of them is the true one? That of the child, the mature adult, or the elderly person? There can be but one answer: each is essential [...]. The ‘body’ of man is thus in truth an infinite number of forms, all of which must be present in the risen body [...]. ‘Resurrection’ means therefore that not only form rises, but also history; not only substance, but the life of man764.

### 7.2.4. Hope in the Resurrection and Present Life

The Church has never endorsed a synthesis that reduces the ‘scandal’ of the resurrection of the flesh to the demands of human speculation. This refusal of every rationalising reduction follows from conviction of God's fidelity towards His entire creation, revealed in the healing ministry of Jesus as fidelity to the body, to the world created by God765. Matter will belong to spirit in a new and definitive manner, and spirit will be at one with matter. When such unity of creation is established, God will be ‘all in all’ (1 Cor 15:28)766.
This transformation will not occur only at the end. The formula ‘already and not yet’ confirms the link between the final resurrection and the present working of the Spirit in the believer, whose power
> takes possession from now on of the inner man in so decisive a manner as to renew him [...]. The transformation of the physical body, destined for corruption, into a spiritual body will occur only at the end of time [...]. Body and soul [...] can and must be liberated by the life-giving power of the Holy Spirit [...]; both soul and body are liberated from the power of death, which is the flesh767.

This liberation, accomplished in the Resurrection of Jesus as the inception of the new creation, is anticipated through the Holy Spirit in the Eucharistic breaking of bread, to such an extent that ‘if the Lord's Supper were eaten by all members of the community in a completely worthy manner, union with Christ's risen body would act from now on upon our human bodies to such a degree that neither sickness nor death would exist any longer! (1 Cor 11:28–30)’768. These affirmations invite the consideration that the resurrection will encompass not only human beings, but that in some manner the cosmos will also participate in it.
### 7.3. The New Heavens and the New Earth

We began from the Christological dimension of eschatology—the appearance at the end of time of the Son of God—which possesses an immediate anthropological implication, in as much as the Second Adam, first fruits of the new creation, extends His risen condition to all men. In this condition, according to biblical testimony, material reality must also share: ‘Since the world was in some measure made for man, it is fitting that when man is glorified in the body, other bodies should also be transformed into a superior state, so that the universe may become a more suitable and delightful dwelling’769.
The cosmic dimension of the Parousia means that, as Christ died and rose, and as men die and will rise at the end of time, so the world will undergo its own process of death and resurrection, of ruin and renewal, bringing forth at the end a new heaven and a new earth770.
This is the logical consequence of the realism of which we have spoken: Christian faith does not pertain solely to individual conscience in its relation to God, but embraces all dimensions of creation; nor does it speak of the end as a merely natural process, but as proceeding from a superior principle, which is God771. It is neither naturalism nor spiritualism, but pneumatic realism.
Such an affirmation of faith must confront the challenges of reason in two contexts. On the one hand, it must show itself compatible not only with what philosophical inquiry can reasonably argue, but also with what natural science indicates regarding the end of the universe, just as in the protological section regarding the origin of the cosmos. On the other hand, since the cosmos is shot through with its relation to man, and since the temporal dimension is not merely chronological but historical by virtue of its relation to anthropology, the question arises—in analogy with what was said of the risen body—as to what degree of continuity and discontinuity the new creation will bear with respect to the present one, and consequently what real value and influence human activity exercises in the coming of the Kingdom. If we spoke earlier of the resurrection of a life that has a history, we now ask how much of what was accomplished in this world as genuine human progress will abide in the Kingdom of God.
In both instances, the inquiry seeks to ascertain to what extent the new creation may be an effect or development of principles inherent in the first creation, or whether it represents a radical newness, the fruit of divine intervention. We begin with biblical affirmations before addressing the two questions raised, concluding by returning to the Christological foundation that leads us to conceive the renewal of the cosmos as the recapitulation of all things in Christ.

### 7.3.1. Biblical Affirmations

The unpredictable event of the Parousia will be accompanied by natural catastrophes and cosmic upheavals:
> For as the lightning comes from the east and shines as far as the west, so will be the coming of the Son of man. Wherever the body is, there the eagles will be gathered together. Immediately after the tribulation of those days the sun will be darkened, and the moon will not give its light, and the stars will fall from heaven, and the powers of the heavens will be shaken; then will appear the sign of the Son of man in heaven, and then all the tribes of the earth will mourn, and they will see the Son of man coming on the clouds of heaven with power and great glory (Mt 24:27–30).

Saint Peter, who heard the words of Jesus, provides an analogous description in his Second Epistle:
> But the day of the Lord will come like a thief, and then the heavens will pass away with a loud noise, and the elements will be dissolved with fire, and the earth and the works that are upon it will be burned up. Since all these things are thus to be dissolved, what sort of persons ought you to be in lives of holiness and godliness, waiting for and hastening the coming of the day of God, because of which the heavens will be kindled and dissolved, and the elements will melt with fire! But according to his promise we wait for new heavens and a new earth in which righteousness dwells (2 Pet 3:10–13).

The cosmos will be engaged in this process of death and resurrection. There will be a final destruction as the condition for the arrival of new heavens and a new earth, in relation to the execution of divine justice, as Jesus foretold: ‘Truly, I say to you, in the new world, when the Son of man shall sit on his glorious throne, you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel’ (Mt 19:28).
This theme was not unknown to the OT; it is the fulfilment of what was prophesied by Isaiah regarding the latter days: ‘For behold, I create new heavens and a new earth; and the former things shall not be remembered or come into mind. But be glad and rejoice for ever in that which I create; for behold, I create Jerusalem a rejoicing, and her people a joy’ (Is 65:17–18). The reference point of the new world is the renewal of the holy city of Israel, with depictions broadening the horizon to the whole of creation, evoking paradisiacal times: ‘The wolf and the lamb shall feed together, the lion shall eat straw like the ox; and dust shall be the serpent’s food. They shall not hurt or destroy in all my holy mountain’ (Is 65:25; cf. the Messianic prophecy of Is 11:5–9).
These themes reappear in the Apocalypse:
> Then I saw a new heaven and a new earth; for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away, and the sea was no more. And I saw the holy city, new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God, prepared as a bride adorned for her husband; and I heard a loud voice from the throne saying, ‘Behold, the dwelling of God is with men. He will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself will be with them; he will wipe away every tear from their eyes, and death shall be no more, neither shall there be mourning nor crying nor pain any more, for the former things have passed away.’ And he who sat upon the throne said, ‘Behold, I make all things new’ (Rev 21:1–5).

The dramatic aspects of the event are a Christological sign: just as Jesus through His Passion entered into glory, so too we and the cosmos:
> I consider that the sufferings of this present time are not worth comparing with the glory that is to be revealed to us. For the creation waits with eager longing for the revealing of the sons of God; for the creation was subjected to futility, not of its own will but by the will of him who subjected it in hope; because the creation itself will be set free from its bondage to decay and obtain the glorious liberty of the children of God. We know that the whole creation has been groaning in travail together until now; and not only the creation, but we ourselves, who have the first fruits of the Spirit, groan inwardly as we wait for adoption as sons, the redemption of our bodies (Rom 8:18–23).

### 7.3.2. Contemporary Challenges in Light of Evolution

The primary question is whether the biblical texts cited must be interpreted in a real sense or whether they admit of, or indeed require, a purely symbolic reading. The denial of the cosmological dimension of the *eschaton* transgresses the bounds of an exegesis that restricts itself to historical-critical methodology, resting instead upon a systematic dogmatic claim—in this instance, the thesis that ‘matter “in itself” [...] is never completely finished’772. Nothing prevents one from conceiving the contrary; hence we cannot harbour the illusion that exegesis alone can resolve the question.
In Aquinas' perspective, human reason stands in a situation analogous to that concerning the origin of the universe: it is incapable of affirming or denying *a priori* that there was an absolute beginning or that there will be a temporal end of the world; it can attain knowledge of the Creator not only as the origin of all things, but also as He who conserves creatures in being, guiding them to their end. Pannenberg pressed further, maintaining the compatibility of the end of the universe with the contemporary scientific worldview, and that the cosmological picture emerging from modern science leads one to view the future as the locus from which the cosmic process is grounded from its inception. Theologically, this expresses the insight that eschatology is constitutive of the world, that only in the Omega does the whole become intelligible773.
Observation of creatures points in two almost antithetical directions: on the one hand, the continual emergence of novelty, the capacity of creatures for renewal and development; on the other hand, the realization that in individuals, cycles of existence—particularly in living beings—are finite: they are born, grow, reproduce, and pass away; and that, as science informs us, this applies to the universe as a whole, which exhibits an unmistakable entropic trajectory. Confronted with the impasse to which this antagonism seems to lead774:
> only a principle proceeding from outside can open the way to new affirmations. The Christian message anticipates both realities simultaneously: corruption as the intrinsic course of the cosmos, and perfection wrought by the new power arriving from without, which is named Christ [...] from whom every created thing proceeds, and who, coming therefore ‘from outside’, can bring to fulfilment the innermost essence of the cosmos775.

We must consider the synthesis between evolutionary thought and Christian faith proposed by Pierre Teilhard de Chardin, which provoked diverse reactions in the Catholic world; a priest and palaeontologist, he did not regard himself as a professional philosopher or theologian776. In his writings, Teilhard sought a synthesis between science and faith, as evident in his *Hymn to Matter*: ‘Blessed be you, harsh matter, barren soil, stubborn rock: you who yield only to violence, you who force us to work if we would eat [...]. Blessed be you, universal matter, immeasurable duration, boundless ether, triple abyss of stars and atoms and generations, you who by overflowing and dissolving our narrow standards reveal to us the dimensions of God’777. The French Jesuit fused without sufficient distinction the cosmic and salvific dimensions of the unified divine plan in Christ: ‘I believe that the Universe is an Evolution. I believe that Evolution proceeds towards Spirit. I believe that Spirit [in Man] is completed in a Personal. I believe that the supreme Personal is the Universal Christ’778.
The arrival of the new heavens and new earth is the culmination of the evolutionary process, the consummation of an Incarnation not yet completed because we ourselves contribute to its completion:
> Through our individual efforts of spiritualisation, the World slowly accumulates, starting from all matter, that which will constitute the heavenly Jerusalem, that is, the New Earth [...]. The Incarnation will be finished only when the proportion of chosen substance enclosed in each object—substance spiritualised a first time in our souls in Jesus—shall have attained the definitive Centre of its fulfilment [...]. In virtue of the inter-connection Matter-Soul-Christ, whatever we do, we bring back to God a particle of the being He desires. With every work of ours, we labour, atomically yet authentically, to build the Pleroma, and thus we collaborate a little in the consummation of Christ779.

The consummation of the world, of man, and of Christ are identified:
> The greater Man becomes and the more united, conscious, and master of his power Humanity shows itself, the more beautiful Creation will be and the more perfect its adoration, all the more will Christ find, through mystical extensions, a Body worthy of Resurrection [...]. To desire the Parousia, we need only let the very heart of the Earth beat within us, Christianising it780.

It is no longer necessary to speak of an original sin at the dawn of history, because in a creation advancing towards plenitude, evil is a necessary element of the journey to arrive there781.
Certain Catholic philosophers, such as Maritain and Gilson, subjected the French Jesuit's formulation to severe critique. Despite his assurances to the contrary, the shadow of pantheism hovered over his thought782. De Lubac, his theologian confrere, came to his defence. Others highlighted both the positive contributions and the problematic ambiguities. Daniélou praised his
> effort to think Christianity taking into account the perspectives opened by evolutionism. Even when this or that position appears disputable, one must recognise that his work has been a leaven, hidden yet effective, whose influence on contemporary theology will be considerable. And the broad contours of his schema, according to which history rises progressively from the sphere of life to that of thought, and from thought to that of Christ—a schema echoing the patristic visions—remains a permanent acquisition [...]. This optimistic perspective must be complemented by another, that of original sin, which constitutes an equally irreducible demand of Christian thought783.

Echoing this observation, Ratzinger cautioned:
> In an evolutionary hypothesis of the world (to which in theology corresponds a certain ‘Teilhardism’), there is evidently no room for any ‘original sin’ [...]. To accept this vision means, however, to invert the very structure of Christianity: Christ is transferred from the past to the future; redemption simply means marching towards the future as a necessary evolution towards the better784.

In Teilhard de Chardin's vision, Christ's character as the ‘Alpha’ (and not only ‘Omega’) of creation appears compromised, as does the ‘already’ of eschatology alongside the ‘not yet’. This debate helps us understand, on the one hand, that the question of the new creation is not alien to cosmic history; on the other hand, it reveals that its accomplishment within the Christocentric vision of our faith entails genuine newness and discontinuity, in so far as it proceeds from a principle that, while not extrinsic to the world, arrives from beyond and transcends it. The foundational distinction between Creator and creation will not vanish even when, as Saint Paul declares, God will be ‘all in all’ (1 Cor 15:28).

### 7.3.3. Debates on the Relationship Between Human Progress and the Kingdom of God

The tension between present condition and future hope manifests itself acutely in the question of human progress. From the second half of the 1940s, in harmony with the renewal of Catholic theology, scholars began elaborating a theology of earthly realities785 to overcome an excessively defensive stance that had ended by enclosing Christian hope within an individualist expectation of the salvation of the soul786.
This phenomenon had already transpired in Protestant thought. Bultmann's proposal of a religiosity lived in an atemporal encounter with Christ in inwardness ‘had stripped faith of all content and severed the inquiry into the meaning of the world and history’787. A turning point occurred in 1964 with J. Moltmann's *Theology of Hope*, wherein Christian time *par excellence* is eschatological and future, maintaining as a structuring element of Christianity the contradiction between the present world and faith, between present and future. Connected with this, a political theology emerged within Catholicism (cf. J.B. Metz), aiming to ‘practise Christianity as a transformation of the world according to the criteria of hope’788, which swiftly evolved into liberation theology. The objections inevitably raised against these attempts crystallize in the charge of transmuting eschatology into political utopia, distorting both and fostering pseudo-messianisms. ‘The distinction between eschatology and politics represents one of the foundational tasks of Christian theology’789. One must never forget the firmness with which Jesus opposed the Zealots.
At the root of these ideas lay the dialogue established in those years between Christianity and Marxism, grounded in Hegelian thought. These attempts bear affinity to certain forms of millenarianism occurring throughout Christian history, which sought to apply human programming to God's revelation and salvation, as happened prominently with Joachim of Fiore's theory of historical stages. Such phenomena suffer from a ‘defective synthesis between religion and rationality’790. The Church has constantly rejected diverse configurations of millenarianism, refusing to accept that ‘history can achieve its own perfection within history’, since this ‘ignores both man’s constant openness and the perpetual possibility of failure in his freedom’. From this perspective, faith in Christ's return ‘preserves man, precisely by this content, from his own dehumanisation’, opening him to the certainty that the world will be saved by Love791.
The Second Vatican Council succeeded in overcoming the dispute between the so-called ‘incarnationist’ and ‘eschatological’ theologians, though discussions continued in subsequent debates over political and liberation theology792. The incarnationists, among whom were numbered G. Thils and M.-D. Chenu, maintained that an incarnate faith entails responsibility for building the Kingdom in this world, viewing favourably the social and political dimensions of Christian commitment. The eschatologists, J. Daniélou and L. Bouyer among others, argued that the fullness of salvation must not be conceived in total continuity with mundane dynamics793. Two conciliar passages, one from *Lumen Gentium* and the other from *Gaudium et Spes*, constitute, in their complementarity, a model of equilibrium between Christian engagement in the world and eschatological expectation.
The first text opens the chapter on the eschatological character of the Church, expressing the harmony between her present (‘already’) and future (‘not yet’) dimensions:
> The Church, to which we are all called in Christ Jesus, and in which we acquire sanctity through the grace of God, will attain her full perfection only in the glory of heaven, when there will come the time of the restoration of all things (Acts 3:21). At that time the human race as well as the entire world, which is intimately related to man and attains to its end through him, will be perfectly reestablished in Christ (cf. Eph 1:10; Col 1:20). Christ, having been lifted up from the earth, has drawn all to Himself (cf. Jn 12:32). Rising from the dead, He sent His life-giving Spirit upon His disciples and through Him has established His Body which is the Church as the universal sacrament of salvation. Therefore the promised restoration which we are awaiting has already begun in Christ, is carried forward in the sending of the Holy Spirit, and through Him continues in the Church in which we learn through faith the meaning of our earthly life, while we bring to completion with hope for future goods the work entrusted to us by the Father in the world, and thus work out our salvation (cf. Phil 2:12). Already the final age of the world has come upon us (cf. 1 Cor 10:11) and the renovation of the world is irrevocably decreed and is already in some real way anticipated in this world: for the Church already on this earth is endowed with a sanctity which is real although imperfect [...]. While we are at home in the body we are away from the Lord (cf. 2 Cor 5:6) and having the first fruits of the Spirit we groan within ourselves (cf. Rom 8:23) and we desire to be with Christ (cf. Phil 1:23) (LG 48).

The second passage concludes the chapter devoted to human activity, focusing on the value it assumes within the eschatological horizon:
> We do not know the time for the consummation of the earth and of humanity (Acts 1:7), nor do we know how all things will be transformed. As a matter of fact, the figure of this world, which has been deformed by sin, is passing away (1 Cor 7:31; St Irenaeus, *Adv. Haer.*, 5, 36, 1). But we are taught that God is preparing a new dwelling place and a new earth where justice will abide (2 Cor 5:2; 2 Pet 3:13), and whose blessedness will answer and surpass all the longings for peace which spring up in the human heart (1 Cor 2:9; Rev 21:4–5). Then, with death overcome, the sons of God will be raised up in Christ, and what was sown in weakness and corruption will be clothed with incorruptibility (1 Cor 15:42, 53). Enduring with its fruits will be charity (1 Cor 13:8; 3:14), and all that creation which God made on man's account will be unchained from the bondage of vanity (Rom 8:19–21). Therefore, while we are warned that it profits a man nothing if he gain the whole world and lose himself (Lk 9:25), the expectation of a new earth must not weaken but rather stimulate our concern for cultivating this one. For here grows the body of a new human family, a body which even now is able to give some kind of foreshadowing of the new age. Earthly progress must be carefully distinguished from the growth of Christ’s kingdom. Nevertheless, to the extent that the former can contribute to the better ordering of human society, it is of vital concern to the Kingdom of God (GS 39).

### 7.3.4. The Fulfilment of the End of Creation: The Recapitulation of All Things in Christ

The reflections of the preceding sections have demonstrated once again the difficulty of preserving equilibrium between aspects held in tension: present and future, continuity and discontinuity; ultimately, the natural and supernatural dimensions of the Christian mystery. We must return to the assertion examined in protology regarding the glory of God as the end of creation. We asked whether the world was made for God or for man, whether in the end everything must bend to divine action or whether there remains room for the autonomy of human agency. The answer is Christological: God made the world for Christ, who is true God and true man; hence there is no mutually exclusive dilemma between divine and human action.
Daniélou showed that ‘the union of the two natures in the person of Christ is properly the realization of eschatology’794. In Him are fulfilled the realities announced by the OT for the end of time, manifested in a twofold direction: transcendent eschatology, namely the announcement that YHWH will accomplish wondrous deeds at the end of time; and historical messianism, the coming of a human figure of the lineage of Israel, compared to whom kings and prophets are but pale foreshadowings795. ‘The core of the NT is the proclamation that these two trajectories converge in Jesus’796. Eschatology leads towards Christology:
> The *eschaton* is not merely a *peras*, a simple chronological end, but truly a *telos*, a goal, which is the fulfilment of a development [...]. The terminus of this development is no longer an event, but a Person. And thus we have passed from the *eschaton* to the *eschatos*. The very Person of the incarnate Word is the goal of the divine design797.

This leads us to consider the eschatological consummation of the world as the recapitulation of all things in Christ798.
The manifestation of the glorified Christ at the end of time as the new creation signifies that the divine purpose will be accomplished: ‘to unite all things in him, things in heaven and things on earth’ (Eph 1:10), so that the glorified Church will be ‘the fulness of him who fills all in all’ (Eph 1:23), because the glorified Christ will fill all things (cf. Eph 4:10) and, through Him and in Him, God will be all in all (cf. 1 Cor 15:28).
Aquinas holds that Ephesians 1:10 refers not only to angels and men, but to all things: the final destiny of the universe depends on the final destiny of man: ‘since all things were created for man, for this reason it is said that all things are recapitulated’799.
The glorification of all creation in Christ will be a supernatural transformation of the cosmos, a new creation. ‘He is not only the *archē* and the *telos*, the Alpha and the Omega; He is also, from another perspective, *telos-archē*, the end of one world and the beginning of another, the hinge of history’800. This conception
> underscores the unity of the divine plan for the world and man, the original supernatural ordination of the natural order [...]. This does not imply that nature ‘demands’ the supernatural, nor that the ‘new creation’ is the terminus of an immanent development or evolution of the first creation; between them there is discontinuity, though not total, since both are accomplished according to the sovereignly free divine design by Christ, in Christ, and for Christ, and because the new creation will not follow upon the annihilation of the first, but will be its glorification [...]. The definitive state of the world will not be an absolute novelty, but the recapitulation of all things in Christ, realizing fully that which was initially verified in the Incarnation801.

Because sin intervened, damaging not only man but all creation, final consummation and glorification will entail a purification not only for men, but also for the cosmos802, as suggested by biblical passages speaking of the final conflagration803, which is in close relation to the judgment804, to which we now turn.
### 7.4. The Universal Judgment

The study of universal eschatology, having begun from the Christological dimension (the return of Christ) and proceeded through its anthropological (resurrection of the flesh) and cosmological (new heavens and new earth) extensions, concludes by returning to its point of departure to indicate its ultimate finality: Christ ‘from thence shall come to judge the living and the dead’ (DS 30); ‘and He shall come again, with glory, to judge the living and the dead; and His kingdom shall have no end’ (DS 150).
Artistic depictions, such as the celebrated fresco in the Sistine Chapel, might give the impression that the Church seeks to instill fear in human hearts, compelling them to conversion solely to escape divine condemnation. Christ would appear as a severe figure, remote from the merciful countenance shown by the Saviour during His earthly life.
Yet the prospect of judgment answers man's profound yearning for justice and truth. Our daily experience demonstrates that in this world justice is not always done, and that falsehood often seems to triumph over truth. Human societies are organized to administer justice, yet cannot guarantee complete success. Socrates remarked to Polus that he preferred to suffer injustice rather than commit it, because he who commits injustice becomes unjust, whereas he who suffers it does not805. In this Platonic dialogue is expressed ‘a premonition of the just judgment which in large measure remains true and salutary for the Christian as well’ (SS 44)806.
The judge is, as etymology recalls, ‘he who declares what is right’ (*ius dicere*), who recognizes what is just, what is due to each, the truth of reality. Aristotelian-Thomistic epistemology reaffirmed that truth does not reside in the simple apprehension of concepts, but in the judgment, which ‘entails an “ontological commitment”, a pronouncement regarding the reality of things; hence in principle it must be either true or false’807.
To declare what is just, the foremost condition is to be just. In the face of injustices and falsehoods, the human being rebels against those whose duty it is to prevent them: concretely, human authorities; but more broadly, the lament turns towards that God who, holding all things under His sway, permits so many shameful situations. The temptation is to take justice into one's own hands, with the attendant risk that life degenerates into a struggle for raw power, since no mere mortal possesses the authority to judge history. Theodor W. Adorno, a thinker of the Frankfurt School of Marxist orientation, acknowledged that true justice would be possible only under the hypothesis—alien to him—of the resurrection of the flesh808.
The hope of Christians is that God will establish justice, not in a spirit of vengeance, but to re-establish truth and love. This conviction is shared with other religions: God will judge at the end to render to each according to his works. The Church has always held that to be saved one must believe that God exists and that He rewards: ‘For whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him’ (Heb 11:6; cf. LG 16). ‘The question of justice constitutes the essential argument, or in any case the strongest argument, in favour of faith in eternal life’ (SS 43).
Meanwhile, we live the tension of the ‘already and not yet’ of the restoration of justice. The Christian lives in expectation of the judgment, which will occur first at the moment of death, as we shall examine later, and subsequently in a universal manner at the end of time, while striving to abide in the presence of that God who, throughout this life, ever grants pardon. Let us examine these questions in orderly sequence.

### 7.4.1. Judgment in Scripture

### 7.4.1.1. The Just God and Judge of the Old Testament

God's judgment upon man appears in the opening pages of Genesis, after our first parents ate of the tree of the knowledge of good and evil through the serpent's deceit. God acts coherently with what He had forewarned. Despite the punishment, in God's merciful judgment the vindication of truth is not opposed to the love He continues to offer to sinful creatures, as demonstrated by the promise of a Saviour (cf. Gen 3:15) and, subsequently, the protection of Cain after his fratricide (cf. Gen 4:15).
God can judge because He brought all things into being and knows the truth of all reality; indeed, He is the Truth from which all proceeds. He is just, as the OT proclaims: ‘Rise up, O judge of the earth; render to the proud their deserts! [...]. For the Lord will not abandon his people; he will not forsake his heritage; for justice will return to the righteous, and all the upright in heart will follow it’ (Ps 94:2, 14–15)809.
In creating man, God made him righteous, establishing him in a state of original justice. Human transgression of God's command takes the form of an injustice: man refuses to give God what is His due, breaking the order to become like God himself (cf. Gen 3:5). The continuation of the narrative vindicates Socrates: he who commits injustice becomes unjust, whereas God, who suffers injustice, is in no way diminished in His fidelity to His plan of love towards mankind.
Primordial history repeats itself in the history of salvation: God makes promises tied to the observance of the covenant, and the people wander away from the Lord. The prophets remind the people of their covenant duty and of divine judgment in punishment for disobedience: ‘The Lord has taken his place to contend, he stands to judge his people’ (Is 3:13). A generic confidence in the Lord who had delivered them in the past merely because they were His people was insufficient. Good works consonant with such faith were required. The prophet underscores the link between Judge and salvation: ‘For the Lord is our judge, the Lord is our ruler, the Lord is our king; he will save us’ (Is 33:22). The figure of the judges also carried this meaning, exercising a role of liberation and salvation for the people: ‘Whenever the Lord raised up judges for them, the Lord was with the judge, and he saved them from the hand of their enemies all the days of the judge; for the Lord was moved to pity by their groaning because of those who afflicted and oppressed them’ (Judg 2:18).
Wisdom literature praises those who remain faithful to God amidst calamities or injustices, such as Job; the righteous are a small yet potent remnant in their fidelity to God, not fearing judgment: ‘The Lord judges the peoples; judge me, O Lord, according to my righteousness and according to the integrity that is in me. O let the evil of the wicked come to an end, but establish the righteous, you who try the minds and hearts, you righteous God!’ (Ps 7:8–9); ‘Moreover I saw under the sun that in the place of justice, even there was wickedness, and in the place of righteousness, even there was wickedness. I said in my heart, God will judge the righteous and the wicked, for he has appointed a time for every matter, and for every work’ (Eccles 3:16–17).
The explicit articulation of the eschatological dimension of judgment occurs in prophetic texts addressing, after the exile, the theme of the Day of the Lord810. Zephaniah speaks of that day in terms of destruction (cf. Zeph 1:2), bearing the character of sacrifice and purification (‘the day of the Lord is at hand; the Lord has prepared a sacrifice and consecrated his guests’: Zeph 1:7), because its purpose is to establish justice (‘At that time I will search Jerusalem with lamps, and I will punish the men who are thickening upon their lees, those who say in their hearts, “The Lord will not do good, nor will he do ill”’: Zeph 1:12). It is therefore a *dies irae*, a ‘day of wrath’ (Zeph 1:15), yet constitutes a summons to conversion and righteousness: ‘Seek the Lord, all you humble of the land, who do his commands; seek righteousness, seek humility; perhaps you may be hidden on the day of the wrath of the Lord’ (Zeph 2:3).
The prophecy of Joel broadens the reference of judgment to all nations, specifying the locus of its execution and describing the final cosmic events:
> For behold, in those days and at that time, when I restore the fortunes of Judah and Jerusalem, I will gather all the nations and bring them down to the valley of Jehoshaphat, and I will enter into judgment with them there, on account of my people and my heritage Israel, because they have scattered them among the nations, and have divided up my land [...]. Let the nations stir themselves up, and come up to the valley of Jehoshaphat; for there I will sit to judge all the nations round about [...]. Multitudes, multitudes, in the valley of decision! For the day of the Lord is near in the valley of decision811. The sun and the moon are darkened, and the stars withdraw their shining. And the Lord roars from Zion, and utters his voice from Jerusalem, and the heavens and the earth shake. But the Lord is a refuge to his people, a stronghold to the people of Israel (Joel 3:1–2, 12, 14–16 [Heb. 4:1–2, 12, 14–16]).

Here the way is paved for the apocalyptic movement that would consolidate in Israel, conceiving the judgment of God no longer as a gradual historical process, but as a definitive, public, future, and universal event through which He will triumph over evil. The perspective shifts from one focused on membership in the chosen people to a universal criterion based on the deeds of every person belonging to every nation. God will judge not according to external affiliation, but according to works proceeding from the heart, the inner sanctuary of man, where belonging to God is decided812.

### 7.4.1.2. The New Testament: ‘He Is the One Appointed by God to Be Judge of the Living and the Dead’ (Acts 10:42)

Confronted with a request for arbitration concerning an inheritance, Jesus observed: ‘Man, who made me a judge or divider over you?’ (Lk 12:14). It seems that Jesus did not wish to judge, much less be judged: ‘are you angry with me because on the sabbath I made a man's whole body well? Do not judge by appearances, but judge with right judgment’ (Jn 7:23–24). Yet He Himself had to endure a monstrously unjust trial that led to His condemnation to death (cf. Jn 18:28–40).
Jesus appears at times to speak paradoxically: ‘You judge according to the flesh, I judge no one. Yet even if I do judge, my judgment is true, for it is not I alone that judge, but I and he who sent me’ (Jn 8:15–16). This passage allows us to articulate the transition to the newness of the NT: God alone judges, and precisely for this reason Jesus participates in the judgment, because He is the Son whom the Father has sent for this purpose:
> The Father judges no one, but has given all judgment to the Son [...]. For as the Father has life in himself, so he has granted the Son also to have life in himself, and has given him authority to execute judgment, because he is the Son of man [...]. I can do nothing on my own authority; as I hear, I judge; and my judgment is just, because I seek not my own will but the will of him who sent me (Jn 5:22, 26–27, 30).

Judgment coincides with the person of Jesus, who declares Himself to be the truth, as well as the way and the life (cf. Jn 14:6). His life and His words are judgment for the world: ‘I did not come to judge the world but to save the world. He who rejects me and does not receive my sayings has a judge; the word that I have spoken will be his judge on the last day’ (Jn 12:47–48). Jesus' judgment must be understood in the light of His mission, which is salvation: ‘For God sent the Son into the world, not to condemn the world, but that the world might be saved through him’ (Jn 3:17).
The coming of Jesus into the world is the inception of judgment: ‘For judgment I came into this world, that those who do not see may see, and that those who see may become blind’ (Jn 9:39; cf. Jn 12:31–32). Judgment is confrontation with truth, the light that Jesus brought because He is identified with it (cf. Jn 3:19), which will be manifested in fullness on the last day. This confrontation will not be painless: ‘For we know him who said, “Vengeance is mine, I will repay.” And again, “The Lord will judge his people.” It is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God’ (Heb 10:30–31; cf. 1 Pet 4:17–19).
The NT takes up the Day of the Lord, bringing it to Christological fullness813: ‘For the Son of man is to come with his angels in the glory of his Father, and then he will repay every man for what he has done’ (Mt 16:27); ‘When the Son of man comes in his glory, and all the angels with him, then he will sit on his glorious throne. Before him will be gathered all the nations, and he will separate them one from another as a shepherd separates the sheep from the goats, and he will place the sheep at his right hand, but the goats at the left’ (Mt 25:31–33). What follows specifies the object of the judgment: ‘Truly, I say to you, as you did it to one of the least of these my brethren, you did it to me’ (Mt 25:40); ‘Truly, I say to you, as you did it not to one of the least of these, you did it not to me’ (Mt 25:45). Love of neighbour is identified with love for Jesus, and seeking God's will translates into concrete deeds, even if not necessarily those one deems decisive:
> Not every one who says to me, ‘Lord, Lord,’ shall enter the kingdom of heaven, but he who does the will of my Father who is in heaven. On that day many will say to me, ‘Lord, Lord, did we not prophesy in your name, and cast out demons in your name, and do many mighty works in your name?’ And then will I declare to them, ‘I never knew you; depart from me, you evildoers’ (Mt 7:21–23)814.

Paul recalls the judgment of God as retribution for works, relativizing the judgments of men:
> Therefore you have no excuse, O man, whoever you are, when you judge another; for in passing judgment upon him you condemn yourself, because you, the judge, are doing the very same things. We know that the judgment of God rightly falls upon those who do such things [...]. But by your hard and impenitent heart you are storing up wrath for yourself on the day of wrath when God's righteous judgment will be revealed. For he will render to every man according to his works (Rom 2:1–2, 5–6; cf. Rom 14:10–12; 1 Cor 4:4–5; 1 Cor 5:12–13; 2 Thess 1:5–10).

The Apocalypse refers to eschatological judgment under the imagery of the harvest (cf. Rev 14:14–16), already present in the parable of the wheat and the tares (cf. Mt 13:24–43) and in the Baptist's preaching (cf. Mt 3:12); and also under the imagery of the book of life: ‘And the books were opened. Also another book was opened, which is the book of life. And the dead were judged by what was written in the books, by what they had done [...]. And if any one’s name was not found written in the book of life, he was thrown into the lake of fire’ (Rev 20:12, 15).
Other texts enrich our perspective regarding the subject of judgment. There exists an intimate nexus between judgment and the Holy Spirit, who ‘will convince the world of sin and of righteousness and of judgment: of sin, because they do not believe in me; of righteousness, because I go to the Father, and you will see me no more; of judgment, because the ruler of this world is judged’ (Jn 16:8–11). This is connected with the bond between judgment and truth: ‘When the Spirit of truth comes, he will guide you into all the truth’ (Jn 16:13).
The parable of the tares speaks of the role of the angels: ‘The Son of man will send his angels, and they will gather out of his kingdom all causes of sin and all evildoers’ (Mt 13:41). They participate as witnesses of what takes place: ‘every one who acknowledges me before men, the Son of man also will acknowledge before the angels of God; but he who denies me before men will be denied before the angels of God’ (Lk 12:8–9).
If judgment is according to works, those who sought to identify themselves with the truth will exercise an active role: ‘in the new world, when the Son of man shall sit on his glorious throne, you who have followed me will also sit on twelve thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel’ (Mt 19:28). The role of the apostles in judgment is greater than that of the angels; indeed, the judgment of the saints will include celestial beings: ‘Do you not know that the saints will judge the world? And if the world is to be judged by you, are you incompetent to try trivial cases? Do you not know that we are to judge angels? How much more, matters pertaining to this life!’ (1 Cor 6:2–3)815.

### 7.4.2. Brief Theological Reflection on Judgment

In Scripture, God alone is able to judge, because judgment is inseparable from truth. God, who is Truth, the sole Creator and Lord of all, is uniquely suited to such a task816. God the Father has committed this task to the Son, sent into the world for the salvation of mankind. Judgment began with His life and witness to the truth, but will find at the end of time, at His return, its full expression as the re-establishment of truth beyond all falsehoods and corruptions occurring in history through the instigation of the evil one and the sins of those who follow him. It will be the definitive moment, the revelation of the truth of each person before God. We shall deepen several dimensions of judgment essential for its understanding.

### 7.4.2.1. Jesus Judges in Truth and According to Charity as God and as Man

New Testament passages, particularly in the Gospel of John, link the charge of judging conferred by the Father upon Jesus with His divine status as Son. ‘If the divine power of judging belongs to Christ, it is a sign that He—the Son of man—is true God, because to God alone belongs judgment’817. ‘Judgment is simply truth itself, its unveiling [...]. God is Truth, Truth is God, it is “person”. A truth that judges, that is definitive, can exist only if it possesses a divine character; God is judge in as much as He Himself is Truth [...]. Hence in and through Christ, God is the criterion of truth for man’818.
Being the Creator of our freedom and conscience, the Lord from whom no thought is hidden, He alone is capable of knowing the innermost truth of our life:
> The truth of man, which in the judgment becomes definitive, is that truth which in the course of his life and his ways became the fundamental orientation of his existence. But it belongs exclusively to the power of God to judge what is the ultimate orientation within the totality of choices that built an entire life819.

This corrects
> that all-too-human manner of conceiving God's judgment, viewed merely as cold justice, if not outright vengeance. In reality this expression, of clear biblical pedigree, appears as the ultimate link of God's love: God judges because He loves and in view of love. The judgment that the Father entrusts to Christ is according to the measure of the Father's love and our freedom820.

The authority to judge belongs to Him who in His humanity died and rose for us; for justice to be visibly displayed, it is necessary that Jesus appear in His humanity821. In the Gospels it is stated that ‘Christ exercises this power not only as God-the-Son, but also as Man; He exercises it—and pronounces sentences—in the name of solidarity with every man who receives from others either good or evil [...]. Christ identifies Himself precisely with this neighbour’822.
The criterion of charity becomes also one of faith and truth, for it is a matter of knowing how to recognize Jesus: ‘every one who acknowledges me before men, the Son of man also will acknowledge before the angels of God’ (Lk 12:8)823. This contrasts with the harrowing experience of an infamous and counterfeit trial in which Jesus was not only unrecognised, but rejected, to the point of being condemned to death in the most unjust manner824. In the dialogue with Pilate, the question of truth emerges (cf. Jn 18:37–38). At the hour of judgment, what matters is each one's relation to the truth. Jesus, as man, ends His days stripped of all things, and thus the truth that accompanies Him shines resplendent. Pilate thought he possessed everything yet had nothing, because he neither knew nor wished to know what truth is.
> To Christ as man the power to judge was conferred as a reward for the humility displayed in His Passion [...]. It is therefore just that all men should be present at the judgment, to behold His exaltation in human nature, according to which He ‘has been appointed by God to be judge of the living and the dead’ (Acts 10:42)825.

> It belongs to Christ to judge in so far as He has dominion over men. He is our Lord not only by creation, since ‘the Lord is God; it is he that made us, and we are his’ (Ps 100:3), but also by redemption, which is attributed to Him in His human nature, according to the words of St Paul (Rom 14:9): ‘For to this end Christ died and lived again, that he might be Lord both of the dead and of the living’ [...]. Since therefore the final judgment is ordained to introducing some into the Kingdom [of heaven] and excluding others, it is fitting that Christ Himself should preside over that judgment in His human nature, through which entrance into the Kingdom is obtained by the benefit of redemption826.

He will therefore judge in His human nature, yet in virtue of His divinity827.

### 7.4.2.2. Judgment and Salvation

‘The Truth that judges man took upon itself the initiative of saving him’828. Christ ‘is first and foremost He who teaches the way of salvation, not the accuser of the guilty’829. ‘The divine power of judging was bound to the mission of Christ as Saviour’, and so ‘judging itself belongs to the work of salvation, to the order of salvation: it is a definitive salvific act’. ‘The order of justice was inscribed, from the beginning, within the order of grace. The final judgment must be the definitive reaffirmation of this bond’830.
In consequence of the new relationship of judgment to Christology, a purification of the concept of God occurs in the NT, consisting in affirming that ‘Christ condemns no one; He is pure salvation, and whoever adheres to Him enters the realm of salvation and grace [...]. In Christ's word, which is the offer of salvation, it will become evident that he who is lost has drawn by his own hand the boundary separating him from salvation’831.
This explains why
> the measure of judgment will be cooperation with the gift received from God: cooperation with grace or rejection of it [...]. One must therefore say that, before this Light which is God revealed in Christ, before such Truth, in a certain sense one's own works judge each person [...]. Unbelief properly means: rejecting the salvation offered to man in Christ832.

Drawing upon the reference to conscience in Romans 2:15 and the imagery of the opening of the books in Revelation 20:12, Saint Thomas teaches that ‘in the general judgment [...] the consciences of individuals will be like books wherein their deeds are recorded, and from which judgment will proceed’, distinguishing between the books designating the lives of the saints, wherein God reveals His commandments, and the book of life, which indicates the consciences of individuals, speaking of it ‘in the singular because through the single power of God all will recall the deeds they performed’833.
The role of the saints in judgment attests the ecclesial dimension of this truth of faith:
> The encounter with Christ occurs in the encounter with His own, in the encounter with His Body; hence our destiny, our truth, precisely when understood in the theological and Christological sense, depends upon the relationship we established with His Body and with His suffering members; in this regard the “Saints” are “judges”834.

Since it is possible to perform an act merely by consenting with him who performs it, those ‘who approve the sentence of Christ the Judge may in this sense be said to judge. And this kind of judgment belongs to all the elect’835. The attribution of judgment to the elect can be understood in the sense that ‘they incarnate the decrees of divine justice, according to which men will be judged’836.
Aquinas asserts that those who dedicate themselves entirely to the service of God ‘will be saved, but not judged’837. ‘The office of judge is accorded to a saint because his heart will be imbued with all divine truth, so as to manifest it to others. Now, in the advance towards perfection, the first renunciation encountered is that of external riches’. Thus,
> the faculty of judging is specially attributed to poverty [...] since voluntary poverty belongs to those who, disdaining all things of the world, devote themselves exclusively to Christ. Therefore in them nothing is found that could deflect them from justice. Consequently they are rendered apt to judge838.

### 7.4.2.3. Judgment and Hope

The prospect of judgment has always shaped Christians, providing the criterion for ordering present life, arousing conscience, and eliciting hope in God's justice. In the architectural design of sacred buildings, ‘it became customary to depict on the eastern side the Lord returning as King—the image of hope—and on the western side the Final Judgment as the image of responsibility for our lives’ (SS 41).
God assumed ‘the figure of the suffering one who shares the condition of man abandoned by God’, which
> has become certainty-hope: God exists, and God can create justice in a way that we cannot conceive, yet which through faith we can intuit [...]. For this reason, faith in the Final Judgment is first and foremost hope—that hope whose necessity has become evident precisely in the upheavals of recent centuries [...]. Only in connection with the impossibility that the injustice of history should be the final word does the necessity of Christ’s return and of new life become fully convincing (SS 43).

> Protest against God in the name of justice is of no avail. A world without God is a world without hope (Eph 2:12). God alone can create justice. And faith gives us the certainty: He does so. The image of the Final Judgment is not primarily an image of terror, but an image of hope; for us perhaps even the decisive image of hope. But is it not also an image of fear? I would say: it is an image that evokes responsibility. An image, therefore, of that fear of which Saint Hilary says that all our fear has its place in love (*Tractate on the Psalms*, 127:1–3). God is justice and creates justice. This is our consolation and our hope. But in His justice there is also grace [...]. Grace does not exclude justice. It does not change wrong into right. It is not a sponge that wipes everything away so that whatever someone has done on earth ends up being of equal value (SS 44).

### 7.4.3. The Presence of Judgment in the Christian Life

The perspective of judgment manifests the ‘already and not yet’ of salvation in sacramental life. If the Parousia is linked to the Eucharist, judgment is sacramentally present in Reconciliation. ‘In this sacrament, the sinner, placing himself before the merciful judgment of God, anticipates in a certain way the judgment to which he will be subjected at the end of his earthly life’ (CCC 1470)839. The judicial form of the sacrament anticipates the final one. Confession of sins by the penitent requires an examination of conscience. Conscience participates in judgment when one contemplates one's life and actions in the light of God.
> Discernment [...] is an instrument of struggle for following the Lord better. We always need it: to be able to recognize God's timings and His grace, not to waste the inspirations of the Lord, not to let His invitation to grow fall by the wayside [...]. Therefore, I ask all Christians not to fail to make every day, in dialogue with the Lord who loves us, a sincere examination of conscience (GE 169).

This evokes Paul's admonition: ‘But if we judged ourselves truly, we should not be judged. But when we are judged by the Lord, we are chastened so that we may not be condemned along with the world’ (1 Cor 11:31–32). For one accustomed to living in converse with the Lord, He ‘will not be a Judge—in the severe sense of the word—but simply Jesus’840.
Christians who strive to live thus are the manifestation of God's judgment in the present world. In continuity with the prophets, the saints do not fear the judgment of earthly potentates, because they live in the sight of the sole Judge. Their lives are a reminder to reckon with the only definitive judgment. The saints are submissive to God's sole authority and free to adhere to truth. As Francis teaches:
> The strength of the testimony of the saints lies in living the Beatitudes and the rule of conduct of the final judgment. They are few words, simple, yet practical and valid for all, because Christianity is made above all to be practised, and if it is also an object of reflection, this has value only when it helps us live the Gospel in daily life (GE 109).

The study of universal eschatology concludes by affirming that ‘faith in the return of Christ is simultaneously the certainty that in the end it will be Truth that judges and Love that triumphs’841. We cannot guarantee *a priori* that every human being will accept that victory, but we must maintain open the two possible outcomes of judgment, which are the acceptance or the rejection of salvation. Thus opens the study of consummated eschatology.
