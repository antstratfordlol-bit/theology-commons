## Chapter 1 Creation, Between Metaphysics and Theology {#sanz-alfa-omega.1}

Paul’s preaching at the Areopagus in Athens was an episode that apparently ended in a remarkable failure: "We will hear you again about this" (Acts 17:32). This reaction was provoked in his hearers when Paul began to speak of the resurrection of the dead; but up to that moment their attention had remained steadfast. Seeking a point of contact with the religiosity of the Greeks, Paul had set out from a reference to the altar bearing the inscription to an unknown God, pointing out that "what therefore you worship as unknown, this I proclaim to you. The God who made the world and everything in it, being Lord of heaven and earth..." (Acts 17:23–24).

The episode is instructive for several reasons. Paul’s discourse to the pagans did not begin with the proclamation of Christ, but with God the Creator[^28]. The truth of creation presents itself as a point of contact between faith in the risen Jesus and human reason, which, in its journey of seeking God, can find His traces in the world (cf. Wis 13:1–9; Rom 1:18–20). If the Christian message is not accepted, discourse on creation can remain in abstraction, without any real influence upon people's lives. Despite the existence of diverse accounts of origins in the religious cultures of different peoples, the specificity of the notion of creation was grasped only with Judaeo-Christian revelation; within this tradition, creation is often the starting point of prayer, as in the early Christian community: "They lifted their voices together to God and said, 'Sovereign Lord, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them...'" (Acts 4:24).

We divide the present chapter into three parts. In the first, we address how the Christian conviction that creation is simultaneously a truth of faith and of reason has been articulated, with special reference to the synthesis of Thomas Aquinas. Secondly, we approach the question from the perspective of contemporary theology, in terms of the relationship between metaphysics and salvation history, bearing in mind the reflections of J. Ratzinger. The thought of both masters will converge, in the third and final point, upon a principle that distinguishes Christianity from other worldviews, particularly Gnosticism, and which may be termed "creatural optimism".

### 1.1. Creation, Faith, and Reason According to St Thomas

In his book on the figure of the Angelic Doctor, G.K. Chesterton proposed calling him "Thomas of the Creator". Several scholars have echoed this proposal, because it expresses a fundamental characteristic of the thought of the Angelic Doctor[^29].

The perspective of creation was already central among the great Fathers of the Church. Irenaeus of Lyons makes the doctrine of creation, and hence the positive view of the material world, the "hallmark" of the Christian faith against the Gnostic heresy[^30]: "And this is the rule of our faith, the foundation of the building, and the stability of our conduct: God the Father, uncreated, boundless, invisible, one God, the Creator of all things; this is the first article of our faith"[^31].

If Irenaeus’s work has the merit of being the first compendium of Christian doctrine, with the passage of time the dialogue between faith and reason, so characteristic of the Fathers, reached its summit with Augustine, who affirms, in his commentary on Genesis, that "not only does the Catholic faith command us to believe, but reason also teaches us with solid arguments that the matter of all natures could not have existed except by the agency of God, the primary author and creator"[^32].

St Thomas made this perspective his own when, from the very beginning of his teaching career, he taught that *creationem esse, non tantum fides tenet, sed etiam ratio demonstrat*[^33] ("that there is creation is not only held by faith, but reason also demonstrates it"). Creation is not only believed by faith, but is also demonstrated by reason. If one bears in mind that the distinction between supernatural faith and natural reason lies at the foundation of scholastic methodology[^34], the assertion seems paradoxical: how can a truth of faith be rationally demonstrated?

One must understand that the context had altered since the time of the Fathers. Whilst the Fathers, "distinguishing themselves from Neo-Platonic philosophies in which religion and philosophy were inextricably intertwined", presented "the Christian faith as the true philosophy", when the universities arose "those religions no longer existed in the West, but only Christianity; and thus it became necessary to emphasise in a new way the proper responsibility of reason, which is not absorbed by faith". Therefore, "it is the historical merit of St Thomas Aquinas—in contrast to the different response of the Fathers on account of their historical context—to have brought to light the autonomy of philosophy, and with it the proper right and responsibility of reason enquiring on the basis of its own powers"[^35].

For St Thomas, the central mysteries of Christianity, the Trinity and the Incarnation, revealed in salvation history, lie beyond the cognitive capacity of human reason: "It is impossible to arrive at the knowledge of the Trinity of the divine Persons by natural reason alone"[^36]. Proceeding from this premise, and once Revelation has occurred, reason can penetrate divine reality through an enquiry capable of deepening our knowledge of the central mystery of the Christian faith, as occurred with the Augustinian psychological analogy regarding the Trinitarian processions.

This bears witness to an effective intelligibility of the mystery, which, whilst strictly supernatural, presents elements that can be penetrated by natural reason, enabling it to welcome what would otherwise remain incomprehensible to man[^37]. This reasoning holds good both for mysteries supernatural in themselves (*quoad substantiam*) and for those supernatural as to the manner in which they are revealed (*quoad modum*), but which can be known through the natural light of reason, such as the existence of God, in connection with the affirmation of the Creator[^38].

St Thomas gives primacy of place to the theological dimension of the mystery of creation[^39], whose deepest meaning is linked to the mystery of the Trinitarian life and the economy of salvation. On the one hand, Thomas deepened the theological understanding of creation through the Trinitarian principle of creation, according to which the procession of creatures rests upon the eternal processions[^40]. Thus, knowledge of the divine Persons is necessary for a correct understanding of creation: that God created all things through the Word safeguards against the error of positing necessity in the creative act; by stating that divine action is grounded in love, any hypothesis of a divine necessity to create the world, or of any extrinsic cause conditioning Him, is eliminated: the sole source of the creative act is love and the intention to communicate His goodness[^41].

On the other hand, Thomas assesses the historical-salvific aspect when he affirms that Christian faith in creation presupposes a temporal beginning[^42]. This understanding distinguishes the revealed idea of creation from other, partially similar philosophical views; in it there is no subordination of the revealed mystery to human categories[^43].

Another aspect shaping Thomas’s thought on creation is his fidelity to Scripture, within the apologetic and evangelising perspective of his theological teaching. In commenting upon the Pauline expression according to which God has manifested Himself to the Gentiles, Thomas clarifies that knowledge of God is not restricted to those who have received an interior illumination from Him, but is accessible to all who are capable of perceiving His presence in the "book of nature"[^44]. Faith in biblical Revelation leads to confidence in man’s capacity to ascend from creatures to the Creator.

Far removed from any separation between faith and reason, as well as from their illicit confusion, Thomas’s orientation may be described as one of circularity[^45]. The truth of creation manifests the distinction and harmony between faith and reason: being a prerogative of faith, a mystery in the fullest sense, it can without contradiction be understood as a reality partly accessible to human comprehension. The doctrine of creation represents a starting point for the Christian proclamation from its origins, inasmuch as it articulates the answer to one of the fundamental questions that accompany the history of mankind.

Thomas’s thought provides an exemplar of the encounter between biblical Revelation and philosophical reflection. He is convinced that the question of the radical dependence of the world upon God with respect to being had already been raised by ancient philosophers, albeit imperfectly; the aid of Christian Revelation transformed it into a genuine doctrine of creation. The truth of *creatio ex nihilo* needed to be revealed in order to articulate in fact its intrinsic rationality[^46].

The Angelic Doctor’s view was not common in his day. Several authors, including Maimonides and Albert the Great, regarded creation strictly from the standpoint of revelation. Thomas proposes a distinction: on the one hand, *creatio ex nihilo*, which is rationally demonstrable; on the other, the temporal origin of all things, which is accessible only by means of Revelation, because it is impossible to demonstrate that the world had a temporal beginning.

The Angelic Doctor pointed out that there is no contradiction between the first principle of the philosophers and the divine attributes of the Christian God[^47]. Confronted with the identification of the first principle with reality (pantheism), and with their stark opposition (Gnosticism), he shows how the doctrine of creation best respects rationality: the first principle communicates being to things whilst remaining transcendent with respect to them. This is the very conclusion of the Bible, yet the philosophical legitimacy of this conclusion does not derive from faith, but from the fact that creation, understood as the relation between God and finite beings, is a truth accessible to reason.

A unilateral stance regarding creation as an exclusively biblical doctrine would obscure the concept of creation and the capacity of human reason to grasp it[^48]. Thomas’s epistemology distances itself from a dialectical manner (in the Hegelian sense) of presenting questions, favouring integration over an "either/or"; accordingly, in line with the main Christian tradition, the Angelic Doctor does not set faith and reason in opposition, but rather, while distinguishing them, unites them: *et fides et ratio*.

There is no sort of cult of Aristotle or of philosophy here[^49], but rather the recognition of the real dimension of things, leading to the judgement that "false is the opinion of those who say that it is entirely indifferent, with respect to the truth of faith, what one thinks about creation, provided one holds a right opinion about God: for an error concerning creation engenders a false idea of God"[^50].

Even though Revelation presents God as the Creator of all things, and thus affirms the point of arrival, philosophical enquiries are by no means rendered superfluous. The fact that God identifies Himself with Being (cf. Exod 3:14) stimulates metaphysical enquiry, which by its very nature is directed towards an investigation into the origin of being[^51].

Thomas is conscious of the fact that Revelation surpasses human capacities of comprehension; he knows that Christ crucified is "a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Cor 1:23), but he also knows that Revelation opened a horizon previously unimaginable. The affirmation of God the Creator addresses a question that man, with his limitations, had already posed in his search for truth. Here the universal truth-claim of Christianity and the question that has always nestled in the human heart meet. The truth of creation occupies a privileged place within the dialogue between Christian faith and philosophy, precisely because it establishes communication among God, the world, and man. An evangelical openness and a dialogical orientation lead Thomas to stretch reason to the utmost of its capacities, whilst respecting the boundaries established by divine primacy (faith) and the autonomy of the created order (reason).

### 1.2. Metaphysics and Salvation History in the Perspective of J. Ratzinger

### 1.2.1. The Status of the Doctrine of Creation in Recent Decades

One may ask whether this perspective of Thomas is capable of illuminating the contemporary situation of the theology of creation. In order to answer, let us first attempt to describe our present context.

Benedict XVI considered that "the renewal of the doctrine of Creation and a new understanding of the inseparability of Creation and Redemption is of paramount importance". This is due to the fact that "in recent decades, the doctrine of Creation had almost disappeared from theology; it was virtually imperceptible". Yet "if God has nothing to say in Creation, if He is simply relegated to a sector of history, how can He truly embrace the whole of our life? How will He truly be able to bring salvation for man in his entirety and for the world in its totality?"[^52]

What had occurred in theology over the preceding decades that caused the doctrine of creation almost to vanish?[^53] In 1936 Gerhard von Rad, the German Lutheran Old Testament exegete, wrote that the faith of Israel is essentially historical-salvific, because it concerns the salvific interventions of God in history. The Creator and creation would therefore not be a substantial theme in the Bible, but something secondary: a projection onto the origins of humanity and the cosmos of Israel's foundational experience of faith. After him, the idea was frequently repeated that Israel did not find God in the natural order, but in His extraordinary interventions, and that Israel’s primary experience was not creation but salvation. This is the "soteriological understanding" of faith in creation.

A few years later, in 1945, Karl Barth, the Swiss Calvinist dogmatic theologian, published the third volume of his *Church Dogmatics*, devoting considerable space to the doctrine of creation. There he formulated a bipolar axiom that has enjoyed great fortunes over the years: "Creation is the external basis of the covenant; the covenant is the internal basis of creation". From all eternity God ordained the human being to become His partner in a covenantal project, and this has creation as its presupposition. The consequence of this is that scientific and philosophical reflection is deemed irrelevant to faith in creation, which must therefore cease acting as a "court of the Gentiles" and enter with both feet into the "temple", which is theology[^54].

From the middle of the twentieth century, this theological understanding of the mystery of creation was received with enthusiasm by Catholic theology, which was in search of a new method more in keeping with the biblical and patristic heritage and less dependent upon an apologetics focused on philosophical presuppositions. As a fruit of the dialogue with Barth[^55], a Christocentric understanding of the mystery of creation developed within Catholic theology, rediscovering New Testament texts that, by means of specific prepositions ("in him", "through him", "for him"), express the role of Christ in the work of creation[^56].

Whereas previously focus had fallen upon the rational aspects of the doctrine of creation, theology now moved towards absorbing it entirely within its own domain. This offered undeniable advantages, insofar as it avoided conflict with scientific worldviews, as well as the numerous problems posed by the interpretation of the opening chapters of Genesis. Yet by centring everything upon the anthropological and Christological dimension, other dimensions were relegated to the background. The theology of creation, which up to that point had constituted a specific treatise, was absorbed into the nascent theological anthropology. The 1960s and 1970s thus witnessed an eclipse of creation.

This state of affairs changed due to several factors. One of these was the ascendancy, within biblical scholarship, of the theses of the German Protestant exegete Claus Westermann, who championed an interpretation divergent from that of von Rad: creation in the Bible is not dependent upon historical-salvific narratives, but coincides with the popular accounts of the origins of the cosmos and of man characteristic of the cultures neighbouring Israel. Creation, therefore, did not belong to their specific faith, but was a presupposition of their thinking. The history of Israel takes place upon the common ground of the universal history of peoples. "Both in the Old and in the New Testament, discourse concerning God’s creative activity possesses its own intrinsic cohesion and meaning; its origin and history are distinct from those concerning the theme of the activity of the Redeemer"[^57]. To this must be added that von Rad himself, towards the end of his life, published a work on Wisdom in Israel in which he corrected his exclusively soteriological approach to creation.

Thus, also owing to other factors (not least the ecological question), from the 1980s onwards reflection on creation experienced a new, albeit gradual, revival, and appears to have returned to a point of balance, as evidenced by the reflections of J. Ratzinger / Benedict XVI. On the one hand, he embraced Barth’s formula on creation and covenant, to the extent of employing it in an Easter Vigil homily[^58]. On the other hand, he indicated the importance of reopening today the so-called "court of the Gentiles", because the question of God the Creator is meaningful for every human being, including non-believers[^59].

### 1.2.2. Harmony Between Metaphysical and Historical-Salvific Thought

To the question of which themes ought to be emphasised in preaching to the contemporary world, Benedict XVI replied that "we must return to God the Creator, to God who is creative reason, and then find Christ, who is the living Face of God". A reciprocity operates here: "On the one hand, the encounter with Jesus, with this human, historical, real figure, helps me gradually to know God; and, on the other hand, knowing God helps me to understand the greatness of the mystery of Christ, who is the Face of God"[^60]. The order indicated—first God the Creator and then Christ—is not accidental. Years earlier, Ratzinger had warned that "the decline of metaphysics is bound up with the decline of the doctrine of creation"[^61]. This was not only because he perceived such a surrender in certain theological proposals that hypothesised a non-omnipotent God in order to exonerate Him from the dramatic catastrophes of the twentieth century[^62], but also because, as a result of the theological discussions of the 1950s in dialogue with various Protestant currents, he had become convinced that the identity of Catholic theology lies in the simultaneous affirmation of both metaphysical and historical-salvific thought[^63].

The concept of salvation history, central to the renewal of twentieth-century Catholic theology, stems from Protestantism, within which the term *Heilsgeschichte* was first coined, subsequently translated in Catholic circles as *historia salutis*[^64]. Nevertheless, reflection on salvation history "appears in the history of modern theology as the Protestant antithesis to the ontological approach of Catholic theology"[^65], which was accused of having corrupted the purity of biblical faith with modes of thought alien to the scriptural message.

At the origin of this critique lies Luther’s negative experience of history. While medieval theology rested upon the continuity of Christianity with the world and history, Luther placed the accent upon their discontinuity. Thus arose the antitheses characteristic of his cast of mind, such as the opposition to ontology, the philosophical expression of continuity and hence—in his view—a scholastic corruption derived from Hellenism. The affirmation of an identity and continuity of being capable of embracing the differences of history disappeared.

This perspective persisted in twentieth-century Protestant theological currents, which initially conceived salvation history in opposition to metaphysics, whilst subsequently a new paradigm emerged characterised by the contraposition between salvation history and eschatology. The historical-salvific model (cf. Barth, Cullmann) described "faith as salvation purely as an historical event, rejecting categories of essence and resolving the problem of the mediation of history with respect to essence by suppressing the latter and declaring that history alone possesses consistence"[^66]. Later, proceeding from the primacy of the word over the event, Bultmann instead conceived salvation as something realised within the subject in an atemporal manner, something that must be purged of every historical remnant[^67]. The contraposition between metaphysics and salvation history thus shifted into an opposition between salvation history and eschatology. Confronting this impasse, Pannenberg attempted a reunification between event and word, identifying revelation, history, and reality in an ontological-eschatological vision in which it is difficult not to see a surrender of theology to idealism[^68].

How can one assimilate the positive elements of these approaches whilst simultaneously recovering the balance between the terms of these antitheses? One must go to the fundamental question: what does it mean to be a Christian? Whereas for Cullmann (the historical-salvific model) faith is the insertion of the Christian into the salvation history of God with mankind, for Bultmann (the existential-eschatological model) faith consists in an existence lived in a radically eschatological sense.

Faced with this alternative, the Catholic response starts from the *regula fidei*, the centre of which is the confession of the divinity of Christ, implying an ontological affirmation: "Jesus the man is God". Inasmuch as that "is" presupposes the event of the Incarnation, it includes history[^69]. The same holds true for the New Testament confession of faith ("Jesus is Lord"): an ontological affirmation founded upon an historical event, the Resurrection, which transcends history and introduces eschatology into it.

Catholic thought upholds the harmony between history and being, moving beyond the subjectivist reduction of Luther, which was radicalised by existential theology: a reduction that denies the existence of an *in-itself* outside its existential *for-me* interpretation, and ends by conceiving salvation strictly within the bounds of a pure, non-objectifiable subjectivity. The actuality of God’s action is not reduced to the past, but is secured by acknowledging the metaphysical dimension: "that 'is' which faith immediately formulated to express it: Jesus is the Christ [...]. God 'is' man: only in this formula is the paschal reality finally embraced with all the consequences it entails, thereby becoming, from a point in transient history, the very axis of history"[^70].

The synthetic principle expressed in the axiom *gratia supponit naturam* could be reformulated in the light of these considerations: *historia salutis supponit metaphysicam*, or, using the terms of Barth's formula, *foedus supponit creationem*.

### 1.3. Creatural Optimism vs (Neo-)Gnosticism: Towards a Recovery of the Notion of Creation in the Contemporary Context

### 1.3.1. The Optimism of Thomas and Ratzinger’s Critique of Gnosticism

Chesterton’s proposal regarding the name "Thomas of the Creator" helped Ratzinger understand more clearly that "the Creator and creation are the core of his [Thomas's] theological thought"[^71]. "Nobody can begin to understand the Thomist philosophy, or indeed the Catholic philosophy, who does not realise that the primary and essential part of it is the praise of life, the praise of Being, the praise of God as the Creator of the World"[^72].

Thomas would not have had the courage to uphold the positivity of sensible natural reality and of human corporeality without the thought of the Incarnation of God[^73]. Whoever believes that the Logos was united in Christ with human nature cannot deny the goodness of matter. Ratzinger affirms that "the conviction that being in its totality is created by God entails creatural optimism; it implies the joyful certainty that being is good to its very depths; it denotes the 'yes' to matter, which is willed by God no less than spirit"[^74].

Among the causes of the contemporary oblivion of creation, both in philosophy and in theology, Ratzinger highlights Gnosticism, which is primarily not an ontology (or an asceticism), but a vital experience that can be described as a rupture of agreement with being—a rupture giving rise to an existential malaise marked by a passionate desire for suffering. For the Gnostic, the world is a prison from which he longs to escape, and for this purpose knowledge (*gnosis*) is required. One imagines that freedom can be attained as soon as one succeeds in breaking free from the Creator and from creation. In this sort of metaphysical protest against creation, there is an urge to unshackle oneself from one’s biological condition, whether in the contraposition of the sexes or in libertinism.

This Manichaean and Gnostic mentality constitutes an ever-present danger for the Christian faith, because the rejection of being is made to rest upon the theology of the Cross[^75]. In the religiosity of man’s liberation from an adversarial God, characteristic of modernity, something analogous occurs: the apparent radicalisation of Christian elements can lead to the anti-Christian[^76].

The contemporary eclipse of creation bears the Gnostic hallmarks of rejecting the dependence proper to the being of the creature. Dependence, however, does not take the form of servitude, but rather of love, which alone can transform dependence into freedom. Against this Christian "entitative" humility of recognising being stands contempt towards it, the dissatisfaction with existence that leads to a desire for redemption which becomes self-redemption, because it is the fruit of self-creation. Rather than pitting redemption against creation, Christianity affirms that only if creation is good is man redeemable. Only if the Redeemer is also the Creator can He truly be the Redeemer[^77].

### 1.3.2. Towards a Recovery of Creation in Harmony with Pope Francis

We have already recalled the thought that the Pope Emeritus confided to the Pontiff: "Your Holiness, this is the era of sin against God the Creator!"[^78] A few months earlier, Francis spoke of two forms of persecution at the present time: the one, explicit, for confessing Christ; the other, by contrast, presenting itself as "polite" and modern, persecutes "man not for confessing the name of Christ, but for wishing to possess and manifest the values of a child of God. It is therefore a persecution against God the Creator in the person of His children"; by imposing "laws against the dignity of the child of God, they persecute them and go against God the Creator: it is the great apostasy"[^79].

Pope Francis considers it necessary to recover those dimensions of the Christian idea of creation that can be shared by all. In our Western societies we witness a succession of laws and large-scale decisions that exalt the founding value of our coexistence, freedom, while calling into question the permanence of certain values tied to the root that unites us all: human nature, whose eminent dignity (which includes the gift of freedom) is grounded in its being created by God in His image and likeness.

What pertains to the realm of religion and of God, in our secular societies, is relegated to the private sphere so as to respect the autonomy of the political and social order. The values that Western tradition had received from the Judaeo-Christian heritage seem stripped of any possibility of exerting a public influence, let alone of being imposed as "dogmas"[^80]. Freedom remains as the sole principle, understood as the capacity to dispose arbitrarily: the more this capacity is expanded (over life, death, one's own identity), the more worthy an existence is presumed to be. A freedom no longer perceived as a gift, but as a conquest that can expand indefinitely until it becomes omnipotent. One must no longer conform to principles established by the Creator; rather, man must become the creator of himself, of his circumstances, and of his happiness. It may be said that we are witnessing the sacrifice of truth upon the altar of freedom[^81].

In his last book, published shortly before his death, John Paul II analysed the current situation, pointing out the root of the post-Enlightenment ideologies that have caused such destruction and dehumanisation:

> God as Creator was rejected, and therefore as the source of the determination of what is good and what is evil. There was a rejection of what constitutes us most deeply as human beings, that is, the notion of human nature as a "given reality", and in its place was put a "product of thought" freely formed and freely alterable according to circumstances.[^82]

The Christian proclamation is a call to "passionately love the world"[^83], to manifest "the original optimistic vision of creation, the 'love for the world' that beats at the heart of Christianity"[^84]. Gnostic Manichaeism (which separates the Creator from the Redeemer) and crypto-theological idealism (which ends by confounding creation and redemption) are temptations that a Christian overcomes by proclaiming the "Gospel of creation"[^85].

The preceding reflections demonstrate the fittingness of understanding the world and man as creatures of God. Since the Enlightenment, we find ourselves in a cultural milieu highly sensitive to autonomy understood in a radical fashion, which ends by perceiving God the Creator as a rival to man. The doctrine of creation, which speaks of dependence upon God, is seen as a threat to freedom. Here lies the deepest meaning of sin: man does not wish to be a creature; he does not wish to be dependent.

Is not the growing awareness of being members of the same human family, the consciousness of journeying together towards the same destiny, and the multiplicity of relationships at every level—facilitated by technological progress—an invitation to deepen the relationality of human life and to value dependence (love, solidarity, fellowship)? These aspects are reinforced by a metaphysics of creation understood as a relation of dependence, which helps overcome both the denial of freedom and its unwarranted exaltation: human freedom is created, not absolute; it exists in co-dependence with truth and the good. The dream of freedom as sheer arbitrary power reflects a distorted image of man and, even prior to that, of the Creator[^86].

The notion of creation underpins the intelligibility of reality. In identifying the Son Jesus Christ with the *Logos*, Christianity made a fundamental option for dialogue with reason[^87]. This is a reason conscious of its ever-open possibilities and of its created character, since it discovers that it did not bestow existence upon itself, nor can it dispose entirely of its own future. It is a reason open to that which transcends it, to primal Reason. Paradoxically, a reason turned inwards upon itself, closed in upon itself, believing that it can find within itself the answer to its deepest questions, ends by pronouncing the meaninglessness of existence, and consequently the lack of intelligibility of reality. The Christian religion affirms that acknowledging dependence upon the Creator is the source of wisdom and freedom, of joy and confidence.

These considerations will be explored more deeply in Chapter 3. First, however, we must delve into what Revelation tells us concerning God the Creator, and this is the task of Chapter 2.
