# Reconciling Creational and Baptismal Filiation: An Integrative Account of Divine Filiation

**Christof Betschart, O.C.D.**

The divine-human relationship can be expressed in various ways: through the biblical language of spousal union, the concept of divine friendship, or the model of a filial relationship. In this Festschrift for Paul O’Callaghan, my esteemed colleague in the Pontifical Academy of Theology, I will follow his filial approach to theological anthropology, focusing on the relationship between God and humanity.[^1]

In the life of the Church, this filial relationship is celebrated sacramentally in baptism. The rite alludes to the newness of filial life at several points, for example, in the blessing of the baptismal water: “Most merciful Father, from the font of Baptism, you have made the new life of your children well up within us.”[^2] Another instance is in the introduction to the Lord’s Prayer: “these children, reborn through Baptism, are now called children of God, for so indeed they are.”[^3] According to these and other texts, a person becomes a child of God through baptism. There is no mention of a divine filiation that would precede baptism—that is, of being a child of God by creation. Prior to baptism, the terms _child_ and

Within this perspective, however, how can we say with Pope Francis and in line with the Second Vatican Council that we are _fratelli tutti_, which implies that God is the Father of all humanity? At first glance, an opposition seems to exist between divine filiation through baptism and a divine filiation that extends to all humans as God’s creatures. This opposition would then present an exclusive alternative (_aut-aut_) requiring that we choose between a creational approach and a baptismal approach. Contrary to this view, I will argue in favor of both a creational filiation and a grace-related filiation. More importantly, I will explain how these two types of filiation are related. In other words, as stated in the subtitle, I will present an integrative account of divine filiation.

I will begin by outlining two extreme positions. Then, I will present two possible solutions proposed in post-conciliar Catholic theological anthropology to understand the relationship between creational and baptismal filiation. Finally, I will offer my own proposal. It considers divine filiation by analogy with human filiation and, more specifically, starts from the simple yet fundamental distinction between _being_ a child of one’s parents and _becoming_ a child through a filial relationship that develops over time.

## The Two Extreme Positions

I will begin by presenting and critiquing two extreme positions. The first, which should be rejected, is the view sometimes found in baptismal catechesis that becoming a child of God through adoptive filiation is an absolute novelty, excluding any prior form of divine filiation. This interpretation exalts adoptive filiation by minimizing or denying what might precede it. The language of _becoming_ can be misleading, as it often implies that a person becomes something they were not previously. For example, if someone becomes a priest, it means he was not a priest before. While this seems obvious, the statement is not entirely true even in the

While Paul certainly insists on the novelty of υἱοθεσία (adoptive filiation), he does not do so in a way that excludes every other form of filiation. This is especially noticeable in the three occurrences of the term in Romans 8–9. Paul first states that we “have received a spirit of adoptive filiation [πνεῦμα υἱοθεσίας]” (Rom 8:15).[^4] He then refers to a filiation yet to be realized, writing that we “groan inwardly while we wait for adoptive filiation [υἱοθεσίαν ἀπεκδεχόμενοι]” (Rom 8:23). Finally, in chapter 9, he speaks of Israel possessing “adoptive filiation” as its primary privilege (Rom 9:4). Thus, it is clear that adoptive filiation is seen in a dynamic perspective with reference to the past of the chosen people (Rom 9:4), the present of the Church (Rom 8:15), and its eschatological future (Rom 8:23).

Moreover, denying any divine filiation before the coming of Christ contradicts several Old Testament texts that describe the people of Israel or their king as God’s sons. In addition, a few texts speak of God’s fatherhood over all people, such as Deuteronomy’s question, “Is not he [YHWH] your father, who created you, who made you and established you?” (Deut 32:6), or the prophet Malachi’s exclamation, “Have we not all one father? Has not one God created us?” (Mal 2:10). In other words, a biblical tradition exists that affirms a close connection between God’s creation of humanity and divine filiation.

A classic proponent of this view is Thomas Aquinas, who taught that divine filiation by creation is a prerequisite for adoptive filiation. Similarly, O’Callaghan chooses not to call human beings “children of God” directly, but he rightly focuses on God’s action and grace, which “enters into a profound and meaningful symbiosis with the human being and all its faculties at an ontological and existential level.”[^28] In this perspective, every human being created in the image and likeness of God “has a clear filial connotation,”[^29] precisely because God’s fatherhood is not only soteriological but also, “at a deeper level, creational.”[^30]

In my view, the reason for this terminological caution in the teaching of the Church and in O’Callaghan’s theological anthropology is the risk of confusing creation with salvation, and nature with grace. One way to avoid this confusion is to distinguish between a theocentric creational filiation and a Christocentric adoptive filiation. In this view, the divine filiation of all human beings as God’s creatures is not denied; rather, this filiation refers directly to God and not yet to Christ. The relationship with Christ is established only by grace, particularly in baptism, which, through the Spirit, makes us sons and daughters in the Son of the Father.

The key distinction between the two forms of filiation is their point of reference: the former refers to God, the latter to Christ. The advantage of this solution is its avoidance of confusion. Creational filiation can be understood as a generic, filial dimension of the Creator-creature relationship, one that does not necessarily engage Trinitarian theology. Adoptive filiation, in contrast, consists in the specific novelty of belonging to Christ through the work of the Spirit. This distinction has obvious pedagogical value,

## An Alternative Proposal

I will now explain why, in my view, this distinction is ultimately unsatisfactory. In other words, the distinction between theocentric and Christocentric perspectives fails to illuminate our filial reality. I contend that it is preferable to consider both creational and adoptive filiation in reference to Christ. This perspective on filiation then opens a path to connect the topic with its pneumatological and ecclesiological corollaries.

One of the major issues of twentieth-century theological anthropology is the relationship between Christology and anthropology. Prioritizing Christology over anthropology denotes a position that sees Christ not only as the crown of anthropology but as its constant correlate, from beginning to end. In this sense, it is not possible to develop a protology without reference to Christ. As Paul writes in a key passage for the debate between Barth and Bultmann, Adam is “a type [τύπος] of the one who was to come” (Rom 5:14). While a full exploration of Barth’s influence on twentieth-century theology is beyond the scope of this article, I will summarize the passage’s consequence as articulated by Luis Ladaria: “It is not Adam who explains Christ, but Christ who explains Adam. Therefore, only through Christ can we know what the human being is.”[^31] This perspective does not mean that anthropology should be reduced to Christology—a reduction that can be seen in some of Barth’s passages—but that

If humans are created as sons and daughters in the Son of God, how should the expression _filii in Filio_, used in _Gaudium et Spes_ §22, regarding life in the Spirit, be interpreted? Are we in danger of falling into the very confusion we wanted to avoid? I believe not, provided a distinction is made between _being_ a child of God and _becoming_ one. The same distinction applies to the image of God: there is a difference between _being_ and _becoming_ the image of God in the Image that is Christ. Catholic theology assumes that it is possible to _be_ in the image of God by virtue of creation, and to _become_ more fully that image by grace.

To demonstrate this, I will start from human experience, since scriptural language about our divine filiation is based on this very experience and derives its intelligibility from it analogically.[^32] The key to this analogy is the distinction between _being_ a child by birth and _becoming_ one through a filial relationship built with one’s parents over time. Generation is, or should be, the beginning of a long process leading to an actualized filial relationship. ### Rupture (Not Destruction) and Reconciliation of Filiation

If the Father’s gratuitous commitment is known to us through the saving missions of the Son and the Spirit as his universal will for salvation (1 Tim 2:4), the question of realizing this “adoption” arises dramatically for the children themselves. Human experience shows that a child can resist the filial relationship, either because of parental behavior, other external factors, or simply a desire for independence. Such resistance goes against a constitutive element of being human: “Filiality, as an anthropological category, constitutes a definition of our own humanity. It signifies that no human being is his own source.”[^34] Human beings are not self-founded but are marked by incompleteness and thus incapable of being self-sufficient. This applies _a fortiori_ to divine filiation by creation—that is, to the fact that all human beings are sons or daughters of God. How do we deal with this original bond and dependence? The answer this article is concerned with lies at the level of human history, viewed as a filial relationship with the Father. From this perspective, salvation history reveals a rupture in the filial relationship—a rupture that involves all of humanity and that theology, especially since Augustine, has called original sin.

It is possible to interpret this sin from a filial perspective, as François-Xavier Durrwell proposes, grounding his view in creational filiation: “There would be no sin if the bond of paternity and filiation did not first exist between God and human beings. In every sin, the human being disregards God’s paternity or even opposes himself to it.”[^35] This sin is precisely the rejection of the original incompleteness and dependence associated with filiation, and the attempt to found oneself, which consequently implies the

### From the Relational Aspect to the Ontological Aspect

The proposal just presented rests on an analogy with human filiation, taking as its starting point the distinction between _being_ a child—that is, being irrevocably generated by one’s parents— and the (often dramatic) _becoming_ that occurs within the filial relationship. Some might object that my relational approach fails to account for the real, ontological transformation of the person that takes place in baptism. I reply that while I have emphasized the history of the filial relationship, this focus does not exclude—but rather demands—an ontological effect. Here, I echo the words of Luis Ladaria in his theological anthropology: “It is not a matter of opposing the ‘dialogical’ to the ‘ontological,’ but of seeing the two dimensions in their mutual relationship, in which the first, in my view, has a certain priority: the fact that God wants to make us for himself.”[^38]

In other words, the _becoming_ of the filial relationship necessarily has an impact on the _being_ of the person. In the patristic tradition, for example, the theme of filialization has often been closely linked with divinization, and thus, ultimately, with true humanization.[^39] If by creation we are already children in the Son, the question remains as to how the grace-filled _becoming_ of children in the Son transforms our very being. The way forward, in my view, is through a reflection on participation in the Son’s love. ## Conclusion

In this contribution, I have sought to develop an integrative approach of _et-et_ (both-and), rather than an exclusionary one of _aut-aut_ (either-or). It is neither tenable to claim that no form of filiation exists before baptism, nor is it sufficient to say that baptism only manifests a pre-existing creational filiation. In place of these extremes, what is needed is a theological anthropology capable of articulating the relationship between creation and salvation, nature and grace, _being_ children of God by creation and _becoming_ children of God through the work of the Spirit. Ultimately, we can only _become_ children of God in baptism because, in a profound sense, we _are_ already his children. We may be rebellious children, yet we remain children nonetheless. And it is only because we are children that we can be called to live and experience a true filial relationship in love.
