## Preface to the Second Edition {#sertillanges-intellectual.preface-to-the-second-ed}
This short work, reissued now, has already had several reprints. It dates back to 1920. I hadn’t reread it yet. I asked myself, as I cast a new look at it with a fifteen year older experience, if I would recognize my thoughts in it. I found it entirely, except for some nuances that I will not fail to adjust in the revision that I now begin. The reason for this is that these pages, in truth, have no date. They came from the deepest part of me. They had already lived inside me for a quarter of a century when they came to light. I wrote them as one who expresses his essential convictions and lets the heart speak.

What gives me confidence that they are beneficial is, without a doubt, their great dissemination; but above all, it is the testimony of innumerable letters, some thanking me for the technical assistance I offered to the workers of the spirit, others for the warmth they said they communicated to young or virile souls, most for what seemed to the reader the most precious of revelations: that of the spiritual atmosphere suitable for the blossoming of the thinker, their elevation, their progress, their inspiration, their work.

That is indeed the main thing. The spirit rules everything. It is he who begins, does, perseveres, and concludes. As he presides over each acquisition, each creation, he directs the most secret and demanding work that the worker performs on himself throughout his entire journey.

I believe that I will not tire the reader by insisting once more on the whole of the vocation of a thinker or speaker, writer or apostle. And in fact, that is the fundamental question; it is also the background question, and therefore the secret of success.

You want to produce an intellectual work? Start by creating within yourself a zone of silence, a habit of withdrawal, a will of dispossession, of detachment, that makes you entirely available for this work; acquire this state of soul, free from the weight of desire and one’s own will, which is the state of grace of the intellectual. Without this, you will not do anything; in any case, you will not do anything of value.

The intellectual is not a son of himself; he is a son of the Idea, of eternal Truth, of the creating and animating Word immanent to his creation. When he thinks well, the thinker follows the paths of God; he does not follow his own chimera. When he investigates and struggles in the effort of the search, he is Jacob wrestling with the angel and “strong against God.”

Is it not natural, under these conditions, for the called man to deliberately abandon and forget the profane man? To reject everything concerning him: his superficiality, his unconsciousness, his laziness, his earthly ambitions, his proud and sensual desires, the inconsistency of his will or the disorderly impatience of his desires, his complacencies and antipathies, his bitter moods or his conformity, the innumerable retinue of impediments that obstruct the path of truth and hinder its conquest?

The fear of God is the beginning of wisdom, says Scripture. This filial fear is essentially nothing but fear of oneself. In the intellectual domain, we can say that it is an attention free from any lower concerns and a constantly cautious fidelity to not decline. An intellectual must always be in a good disposition to think, that is, to receive a part of the truth that flows in the world and that has been prepared for him, in this or that curve of this river, by Providence. The Spirit passes and does not turn back. Blessed is he who is prepared not to lose, and even to provoke and take advantage of the miraculous encounter!

Every intellectual work begins with ecstasy; only afterwards does the talent of the arranger, the technique of connections, of relationships, of construction come into play. Now, what is ecstasy if not an exit away from oneself, a forgetting to live in oneself so that one may live in thought and in the heart the object of our unrest?

Also, memory participates in this gift. There is a low memory, the memory of a parrot, not of an inventor; it causes obstruction, closing the paths of thought with words and ready formulas. But there is an open memory in all directions and in a state of perpetual discovery. In its content, there is nothing “ready;” its acquisitions are seeds of the future; its oracles are promises. Now, this memory is also ecstatic; it operates in contact with the sources of inspiration; it does not take pleasure at all in itself; what it contains is still intuition, under the name of remembrance, and the self in which it lodges surrenders itself, through it, to the exhilarating Truth as much as it does in investigation.

What is true in acquisitions and achievements was already true in the call, at the beginning of the trajectory. After the hesitations of frequently anguished and perplexed adolescence, it was necessary to reach the discovery of oneself, the perception of that secret impulse that aims, in us, at some distant result that consciousness ignores. Do you think this is simple? “Listening to oneself” is a formula equivalent to: listening to God. And in the creative thought that inhabits our true being and our self in its authentic form. Now, this truth of our eternity, which dominates our present and augurs our future, is only revealed to us in the silence of our soul, the silence of empty thoughts that lead to childish and dissipating “amusement;” silence of the attractive noises that disordered passions are always producing.

The vocation demands consent, which, in a unique effort to leave oneself, listens and consents.

It will also be so for the choice of means of realization, the definition of its way of life, its relationships, the organization of its time, the sharing between contemplation and action, between general and special culture, between work and rest, between necessary concessions and implacable intransigence, between concentration that strengthens and enriching expansions, between solitude and contact with geniuses, with people who share their interests, with nature and with social life, etc., etc. All of this also can only be judged with wisdom in ecstasy, alongside the eternal Truth, far from the self that covets and falls in love.

Finally, the offering of the results and their measurement, decided up there, will demand the same virtue of acceptance, the same disinterest, the same peace before a Will that is not ours. We come as far as we can, and our power needs to be evaluated, not to underestimate, on one hand, or, conversely, to exceed in pretensions and in a vain inflation. Where does this judgment come from, if not from a gaze faithful to impersonal truth and from submission to its verdict, even when it costs us an effort or a secret disappointment?

The great men seem to us bold; deep down, they obey more than others. A sovereign voice instructs them. And because an instinct coming from it activates them to take, always courageously and sometimes with great humility, the place that posterity will later give them, daring actions and risking inventions often at odds with their environment, even being the target of their sarcasms. They are not afraid because, as isolated as they may seem, they do not feel alone. They have in their favor He who decides everything in the end. They sense their future empire.

We, who undoubtedly must conceive a humility of another kind, must nevertheless be inspired by the same peaks. It’s the altitude which judges the smallness. Those who do not have the feeling of greatness easily exalt themselves or abase themselves, and sometimes do both. It is to avoid thinking about the giant beetle that the ant finds the flea very small, and it is to avoid feeling the wind of the peaks that the walker lingers languidly on the slopes of the mountain. Always conscious of the immensity of truth and the meagerness of our resources, we will not undertake what is beyond our power, and we will go to the limit of our power. We will be happy, then, with what is given to us according to our measure.

It’s not just about pure measurement. The sense of observation is that both weak work and pretentious work are always bad work. A life that aims too high or stays too low is a disoriented life. A tree may have mediocre or magnificent branches and blossoms: it neither claims nor constrains them; its vegetal soul expands through the action of general nature and environmental influences. Our general nature is eternal thought; from it, we drink with the forces it emanates itself and with the help it gives us: there must be harmony between what we receive as a gift—including courage—and what we can expect as a result.

There would be much to say about this fundamental disposition for a destiny wholly dedicated to thinking life! I mentioned before the resistances and the misunderstandings that reach the great ones; but they also reach the small ones: how to resist them without a pure adherence to the true and without forgetting oneself? When we displease the world, it takes revenge; and when it is pleased, the world also takes revenge, corrupting. The only recourse is to work away from it, as indifferent to its judgment as willing to be useful to it. Perhaps it is best that it rejects you and thus forces you to turn inward, to grow internally, to control yourself, to deepen. These benefits are favorable to our higher disinterest, that is, to our interest in the only necessary thing.

Perhaps are we exposed, in relation to others, to the temptations of detraction, envy, unjustifiable criticisms, quarrels? We must then remember that such dispositions, disturbing the spirits, injure eternal truth and are incompatible with its worship.

We should observe in this respect that detraction, under a certain aspect, is more apparent than real, and is not without value for the formation of the current opinion. We often deceive ourselves about the way masters speak of each other. They criticize harshly, but they know very well each other’s worth, and they make their criticisms without taking that into account.

Common progress always requires peace and mutual aid and is immensely hindered by narrow conduct. Faced with someone’s superiority, there is only one honorable attitude: love it, and it will thus become our own joy, our own fortune.

A different fortune might tempt us: that which comes from external success, which today, truth be told, is quite rare, when it comes to a true intellectual. The public, as a whole, is vulgar and only loves what is vulgar. Edgar Allan Poe’s publishers said they had to pay him less than others because he wrote better than them. I knew a painter to whom a dealer said, “You should take some lessons.” —? . . . —“Yes, t0 learn not to paint so well.” The man who devotes himself to what is perfect does not understand this language; he does not consent at any price, in any form, to be a devotee of what Baudelaire called “zoocracy.” But . . . what if this dedication were to wither?

Even disregarding the judgment of the ignorant, are we not exposed, in our innermost selves, to the foolish judgments of vanity and instinctive juvenility? “Never remain silent, never hide from yourself what can be thought against your own thoughts,” wrote Nietzsche. Here it is no longer a matter of the incompetent and the curious, but of our own testimony in a vigilant and upright state. How often we would like to equivocate, to deceive ourselves fallaciously, to unduly prefer ourselves! Severity towards oneself, so favorable to the righteousness of thoughts and their preservation against the thousand risks of investigation, is heroism. How does one declare oneself guilty and love one’s own condemnation, without the wild love for the one who judges?

This can be corrected, indeed, by an uncompromising fidelity to our deep convictions, to the intangible intuitions that underlie our efforts and our criticism. One does not build on nothing, and the craftsman’s touch does not affect the foundational principles. What has been acquired and examined must be preserved from unjustified revisions and scruples. It is the love for truth itself that demands it; it is the disinterestedness that in ourselves is interested in what transcends us and yet has chosen to dwell in our consciousness. Such evaluations are delicate but necessary. For nothing in this world should the lofty certainties, on which all the work of intelligence rests, be shaken.

It is even necessary to defend oneself, in the name of the same fidelity, from this excellent which was rightly called the enemy of the good. It happens that, by broadening the field of research, it is weakened, and also that, by deepening it beyond certain limits, the spirit becomes disturbed and all that is achieved is to remain perplexed. The star that we fixate upon with too much ardor and insistence may, for that very reason, flicker more and more and end up disappearing from the sky.

From this it does not follow that we should not delve into, much less neglect, this broad culture that is a condition of depth in any domain; I warn of the danger of excess, and I point out that a pure fidelity to truth, without personal passion, without frenzy, is what characterizes this depth.

There is yet another defense against precipitation in judgments and in the elaboration of works. No one is blinded, when they love the truth, by a brilliant idea adorned with banalities. One does not accomplish a work at this price. It also happens to the mediocre to encounter an idea, like a pure diamond or a pearl. What is difficult is to polish the idea, and above all to embed it in a jewel of truth that will be the true creation.

“Among the hasty readers of a work,” said Ramon Fernandez in a witty formula, “I would gladly include the author of this work.” Very well! But where does this negligent haste come from, which absolves in advance a reader less interested and less responsible? It will be avoided if we devote ourselves more deeply to the truth alone.

One should not also approach a particular theme that one wants to develop without having explored its antecedents and its relationships. Being multiple for a long time is a condition for being one with richness. The unity of the beginning is empty. One feels this when one worships the high and mysterious truth. If the individual does not then use everything he has learned, there is in what he says a secret resonance, and trust rewards this fullness. It is a great secret to know how to make an idea radiate thanks to the layers that underlie it in a twilight night. And it is another secret to preserve, despite this radiation, its power of convergence.

Does failure threaten us, or is it experienced? Then it’s time to take refuge in the immutable, unconditional worship that inspired the work. “My brain has become a refuge for me,” wrote Charles Bonnet. Above the brain is the one to whom it is consecrated, and the refuge, then, is particularly secure. Even at the cost of pain, creation is a joy, and, more than creation, the veneration for the idea that it proceeds.

Furthermore, as Foch observed, “and with the remnants, one wins battles.” You failed at this, which prepares you to win in that, to win, simply, what is guaranteed for anyone who has worth and strives.

I want to point out one last effect of the high submission I have just praised. It limits our pretensions, not only personal, but human. Not everything can be achieved through reason. Its ultimate realization, according to Pascal, consists of recognizing its limits. And it only does so when it has surrendered to its first law, which is not its own truth, considered as property or conquest, but the impersonal and eternal Truth.

Here there is no limitation to honor, by the simple fact of having renounced presumption. Mystery rewards. The faith that supersedes investigation lifts the spirit to expanses it would never know by itself, and the illumination of its own terrain is the prize of one whom distant stars compel to lift their eyes to the sky. Reason only seeks one world; faith gives it immensity.

I don’t want to prolong this speech. It will necessarily be found again, for its purpose is to indicate where the whole is.

From all this, I defended the rights with an insufficiency of which I am fully aware and for which I apologize. I hope that my suggestions about him, feeble as they may be, contribute to leading to him better panegyrists and more ardent servants.

A. D. Sertillanges, O. P.,

December 1934.
