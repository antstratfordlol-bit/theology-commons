## Chapter 5. The Field of Work {#sertillanges-intellectual.5}
### 1. Comparative Science {#sertillanges-intellectual.5.1}
One cannot give very specific advice on what it is advisable to learn, much less on the dosage of the elements that should be included in a work plan. Saint Thomas makes no mention of this in the sixteen precepts. In fact, it is a question related to personal vocation, in close dependence on the desired objective. However, some indications are possible, and giving them can serve as a starting point for profitable reflections.

We won’t address the subject from its origin; we speak to people who already have basic education and who propose to organize or complete deep studies. At this level, Father Gratry’s interesting observations on comparative science are suitable for the subject. One might think that the development of this theme in the Sources[36](part0018.xhtml#a1HM) is outdated; but its foundations remain valid and deserve serious meditation from young intellectuals.

We understand by comparative science the widening of specialties through the approximation of all disciplines connected to them, and then the relation of these specialties and their set with general philosophy and theology.

It is not wise, it is not fruitful, even for those who dedicate themselves to a very determined specialty, to close themselves in it too early. It would be putting blinders on oneself. No science suffices unto itself; no discipline considered in isolation has sufficient light for its own paths. Separated, it shrinks, withers, weakens, and at the first opportunity, loses its way.

A partial culture is always indigent and precarious. The spirit continually resents this; it lacks a certain freedom of movement, a certain security of vision, which paralyzes its gestures. A “dry fruit” is one who knows nothing, but also one who has shrunk and dried up for having prematurely confined themselves to an exclusive terrain.

One can affirm without paradox that deep immersion in any science leads to other sciences, sciences into poetry, poetry and sciences into morality, then into politics and into religion itself, in what it has of human. Everything is in everything, and solitary confinement is only possible through abstraction. To abstract is not to lie, says the proverb: abstrahere non est mentiri; but on the condition that the abstraction that distinguishes, that methodically isolates, that concentrates its light on a point, does not want to separate from what it studies everything that is more or less directly related to it. Thus, isolating its object from communications falsifies it, for its relations are part of itself.

Can one study a timepiece without considering its neighboring parts? Can one study an organ without worrying about the body? Nor can one advance in physics or chemistry without mathematics, in astronomy without mechanics and without geology, in morals without psychology, in psychology without the natural sciences, in nothing without history. Everything is related; the lights intersect, and an intelligent treatise of each science makes more or less allusions to all the others.

Therefore, if you want to pave the way to have a spirit open, clear, truly vigorous, mistrust, from the beginning, of specialty. Establish your foundations according to the height you intend to reach; broaden the diameter of the excavation according to the depth it must have. Furthermore, understand that knowledge is neither a tower nor a well, but a human dwelling. A specialist, if not a man, will be a bureaucrat; his splendid ignorance makes him a stranger among men; he is maladjusted, abnormal, and foolish. The Catholic intellectual cannot copy this model. Belonging to the human race by vocation, he wants above all to be a man; he will walk with a firm step, with a base of support, and not skipping on tiptoes.

Our wise men sought to probe the night in every sense; our scientists plunged their hands into it to reach the stars; this noble effort leaves no true thinker indifferent. To follow to a certain point the explorations of each investigator is your obligation, which in the end will result in a tenfold capacity for your own investigations. When you return to the special, having experienced much culture, broadened your gaze, reached the feeling of deep connections, you will be a very different man from the one who is confined to a narrow discipline.

All science, cultivated in isolation, is not only imperfect, but it offers dangers that all sensible men have recognized. Mathematics, in isolation, distort judgment, habituating it to a rigor that no other science allows, much less does real life allow. Physics and chemistry obsess with their complexity and give no breadth to the spirit. Physiology leads to materialism, astronomy to wandering, geology turns you into a sniffer dog, literature empties you, philosophy inflates you, theology leads to false sublimity and to doctoral pride. It is necessary to pass from one spirit to another to correct one through the other; it is necessary to ally cultures to avoid ruining the soil.

And don’t think that taking this comparative study to a certain point will overload and delay you from starting a special study. You won’t overload yourself, because the insights gained from comparison will make everything lighter; acquiring greater breadth, your spirit will be more able to absorb without becoming heavier.

When one reaches the center of ideas, everything becomes easier, and what better way to reach the center than to experience different paths, since all, like the rays of a circle, give the feeling of convergence and a common intersection?

I know a linguist who learns a new language in fifteen days. Why? Because he knows many others. With a single glance, he captures the spirit of the new language, its fundamental characteristics, its entire constitution. Sciences are diverse languages in which the ineffable nature is stuttered by men with difficulty; deciphering several of them favors each one, for deep down they form a unity.

Furthermore, the powerful instinct and enthusiasm awakened in every well-endowed man by this journey through the sciences, by the exploration of these magnificent domains, as if he were visiting successively the fjords of Norway, the Golden Horn, the hypogeums of Egypt, the pampas of America, and the Chinese palaces, this ardor somewhat epic, which infects a strong intelligence upon contact with great minds, communicates to study a certain verve and marvelous facilities.

A rabbi who was accused of overburdening the Law replied: “When a bucket is full of nuts, we can still put several measures of olive oil in it;” this man had zeal, which, for spiritual capacity, corresponds to the heat that expands bodies. A cup in the Sun has more capacity than the shadow. A spirit exalted by the spectacle of nature, stretched by it like a rainbow, becomes capable of absorbing without fatigue, with joy, knowledge that would exhaust the sad scholar of only one science.

The greatest men have always shown themselves more or less universal; excellent in a certain field, they were in others at least curious, often specialists, and sometimes even masters. You could not confine in a single culture men like Aristotle, Bacon, Leonardo da Vinci, Leibniz, or Goethe. Henri Poincaré, in the Academy of Sciences, amazed his colleagues from all areas with his brilliant intuitions; consulting him was equivalent to placing oneself immediately in the center of knowledge, where there are no more diverse sciences.

You don’t have such aspirations? Very well! But, to each of us, what the great ones accomplished remains a fruitful indication. Make a broad plan, which little by little will be reduced in terms of the time devoted to each secondary study, never in relation to the breadth of vision and spirit of work.

Choose your advisers well. Only one in a thousand, for the whole, others for each field, if necessary. Divide your time; order the succession of cultures: do not leave that to chance.

In every matter, go straight to the essentials; do not let yourself be obstructed by minutiae: it is not through minutiae that we approach a science; often it is through detail, but the characteristic detail, that is, once again, through the fundamental.

Incidentally, you will not be able to orient yourself in all of this before you have absorbed what we still need to say.

Just as no particular science is sufficient, neither is the whole of the sciences sufficient without the queen of sciences, philosophy,[37](part0018.xhtml#a1FK) nor the whole of human knowledge without the wisdom that comes from divine science: theology.

Father Gratry expressed on this point capital truths, and St. Thomas, with even greater depth, marked the place, the role of these two queens of a double kingdom.[38](part0018.xhtml#a1HS) The sciences, without philosophy, lose their majesty and become disoriented. The sciences and philosophy, without theology, lose even more majesty, for the crown they repudiate is a heavenly crown, and they become more irreversibly disoriented, for the Earth without the Sky finds neither the trajectory of its translation nor the influences that make it fruitful.

Today, with philosophy in decline, the sciences degrade and fragment themselves; today, with theology ignored, philosophy is sterile, concludes nothing, exercises a compassless critique, and also, compassless, studies history; often sectarian and destructive; and sometimes understanding and welcoming, but never providing a foundation, never truly enlightening; it does not teach. And for its masters, who have the double misfortune of being ignorant and unaware of their ignorance, theology belongs to another world.

And, with certainty, another world that theology has as its object; but the other world contains this one, encompasses it in all senses, backwards, forwards, and upwards, and it is not surprising that it can illuminate it.

If the Catholic intellectual belongs to his time, he can do nothing better than to give his contribution to restore the order that we lack. What our time does not have, from the doctrinal point of view, is not the quantity of knowledge, but the harmony of knowledge, a harmony that is only obtained by considering the first principles.[39](part0018.xhtml#a1CX)

The order of the spirit must correspond to the order of things, and since the spirit is truly instructed only by investigating causality, the order of the spirit must correspond to the order of causes. If there is, therefore, a first Being and a first Cause, that is where knowledge is ultimately completed and clarified. First in philosophy, through reason; then in theology, using the light that comes from above, the person who belongs to truth must center their investigation on that which is the starting point, rule, and ultimate purpose in the highest degree, on that which is everything to everything, and also to everyone.

One only achieves order, in any kind of objects or disciplines, at the moment when the principles, hierarchically arranged up to the first principle, fulfill their role as principles, as commanders, as in an army, as in a well-ordered house, as in a people. Today, the first principles have been repudiated, and knowledge has scattered. We only have fragments, magnificent patches, but not garments; magnificent chapters, and not a complete book, not a Bible.

The bibles of knowledge were formerly the summas: we no longer have summas, and none of us has the capacity to write one. Everything is chaotic. But if a collective summa is premature, at least every man who thinks and truly desires to know can try to compose his personal summa, that is, to put an order in his knowledge by relating them to the principles of that order, that is, philosophizing, and crowning his philosophy with a summary but profound theology.

Christian scientists, from the beginning to the end of the seventeenth century, were all theologians, and scientists, Christian or not, until the nineteenth century were all philosophers. Then, knowledge debased itself; it gained in surface and decreased in height, therefore also in depth, since the third dimension has two corresponding senses. May the conscious Catholic of this aberration and its consequences not succumb to it; being an intellectual or pretending to be one, may he aim for complete intellectuality; may he give himself all these dimensions.

“Theology,” said Father Gratry, “has inserted into the tree of knowledge a divine graft, thanks to which this tree can bear other fruits.” Nothing is taken away from its sap; on the contrary, it receives a glorious current. As a result of this new impulse given to knowledge, this appeal of human achievements to celestial collaboration, all knowledge is vivified, and all disciplines expanded. The unity of faith gives to intellectual work the character of immense cooperation. It is the collective work of men who are united in God. And so Christian science, as it is, and even more so when the sum of modern times is written, must necessarily surpass in scope and inspiration all the monuments of Antiquity and neo-paganism. Encyclopedias resemble it no more than Babel does cathedrals.

Those who seek the truth should not ignore such a treasure. I hope that the next generation, guided by the current one, which so notably surpasses the previous one, will frankly and without human respects approach the science of sciences, the song of songs of knowledge, the inspiring theology, and the only one that is decisively conclusive. You would find in her both the maturity and the elevation, the powerful and calm lyricism that constitutes the complete life of the spirit.

It’s not as difficult as one might think to delve into theology, and it’s not a very long study, at the level at which it needs to be acquired. Making it a specialty would be another matter. Dedicate four hours a week over the five or six years that constitute a formation—it will be sufficient; then it will be enough to keep it alive.

But above all, do not trust false masters. Begin with Saint Thomas Aquinas. Study the Summa, not without first learning the content of the faith. Have at hand the Catechism of the Council of Trent, which is in itself a wonderful summary of theology.[40](part0018.xhtml#a1GS) Absorb that manual completely and then continue with Saint Thomas, day after day, the rational development of divine science. The text will initially seem dry, obscure; then, little by little, powerful lights will shine; overcoming the initial difficulties, you will have new victories as a reward; you will learn the language spoken in that region, you will move around it as in your own home, realizing that this house is a sublime dwelling.

Study it, of course, in Latin! The translations of the Summa are often betrayals; and they are always insufficient. Whoever considers a hindrance the small effort to absorb a language that a mediocre spirit learns in two months does not deserve us to worry about their formation.[41](part0018.xhtml#a1DV) We speak to the fervent: let them, eager to enter the “wine cellar,” take the trouble to find their key.

Some introductory work that makes you sense the thought of St. Thomas and serves as an appetizer would be useful. Do not dwell on it; but take this hand that reaches out to the beginning of the journey.[42](part0018.xhtml#a1HN)

On the other hand, a professor with an open and well-informed spirit would initially be very helpful; I almost said: an indispensable help. He could introduce you to the special vocabulary of Thomism, avoid hesitations and misunderstandings, clarify a text through another text, indicate paths, and preserve you from missteps. However, as I am sure of the harm that clumsy friends can do, of the discouragement and the kind of scandal that their foolish comments cause, I affirm to you: prefer solitude to limited assistance. Strive to crack the nut; it will hurt your hands, but it will yield, and Saint Thomas himself will instruct his disciple.

To do so, carefully consult, when studying each article, the different passages to which the editions refer you; consult the Index tertius,[43](part0018.xhtml#a1CU) this imperfect treasure, but still a treasure; compare; make the documents complement each other, comment on each other, and write your own article. Excellent exercise, which will give your mind flexibility, vigor, accuracy, hatred of sophisms and inaccuracies, breadth, and, at the same time, a progressive accumulation of clear, deep, well-connected notions, always related to your first principles and constituting, by their interrelation, a powerful synthesis.

### 2. Thomism, Ideal Frame of Knowledge {#sertillanges-intellectual.5.2}
What I have just said naturally leads me to explain my thoughts on Thomism considered as the framework of a comparative science.

There’s no way to contest the usefulness of having as soon as possible, ideally from the beginning, a set of guiding ideas that form a single body, and capable, like a magnet, of attracting and subordinating all our knowledge. Those who do not have it resemble, in the intellectual universe, the traveler who easily falls into skepticism when visiting so many different civilizations and opposing doctrines.

This madness is one of the great misfortunes of these times. To withdraw from it, thanks to the intellectual balance provided by a secure doctrine, is an incomparable benefit. Now, Thomism is, from this point of view, sovereign. At this point, I hope there will be many protests. I already heard them in 1920; I must anticipate them again today. Therefore, I believe it is useful to say to those who are willing to trust me a little: the further I advance, the more certain I am that this is where the future lies for our Catholic intellects, for their own worth, and even more, and above all, for their adaptation to the current times. Whatever the advocates of current trends may say, the weight of a doctrine and its novelty are distinct things. Genius has no date. Particularly when it comes to eternal things, wisdom consists in addressing those who, no matter when, have managed to delve more deeply into the heart of eternity.

Furthermore, I point out here a stumbling block. Fascinated by the glory of Saint Thomas, many approach him with enthusiasm: “Let’s go there!” And after two or three pages, they become disillusioned. Because, without suspecting it, they hoped to find in his work, instead of heavy gold bars, the fashionable jewels, and naturally they are disappointed. But it is a mistake to approach the masterpieces of thought, both of art and of nature, comparing them with the vague and falsely grand idea one has of them. They do not fit into that frame. On the other hand, their solid perfections exist, and it is foolish to deprive oneself of them because they do not correspond to what was expected, because they do not adapt to what was expected.

I insist, then, on saying to the young Catholics who read me: study Saint Thomas, he is the man for these times. We can say that he was created seven centuries ago to quench the thirst we have today. Compared to the muddy water they serve us, he is a crystalline source. After the first difficulties of an archaic exposition have been overcome by a vigorous effort, he reassures his spirit, installs it in full clarity, and offers it a flexible and strong framework for its further acquisitions.

Thomism is a synthesis. Therefore, it is not a complete science; but complete science can make use of it as an almost miraculous power of coordination and elevation. If a Pope can say of the work of Saint Thomas examined in detail “quot articuli, tot miracula,”[44](part0018.xhtml#a1CA) with even more reason we can say that the whole is a prodigy.

Study this system, appreciate its characteristics, judge its master ideas, then its order, then the fertility of its descending genealogy, the breadth of perspective, or, better saying, the vital capacity of each notion in relation to the facts and the accessory notions that can nourish it; you will see with astonishment that no partial set can be compared to it as an attractive force before the whole, that no seed has a greater power to absorb and channelize the substances of the earth.

Thomism is such a well-chosen spiritual viewpoint, so distanced from all the extremes where the abysses open, so central in relation to the peaks, that one is led to it from all branches of knowledge, and from it one can walk, without breaks in the journey, in all directions of thought and experience.

Other systems oppose neighboring systems; this reconciles them under a higher light, meditating on what deceived them and seeking to do justice to all they possess of truth. Other systems have been challenged by facts; this one meets them, encompasses them, interprets them, classifies them, and consecrates them in their fair measure.

No metaphysics offers the natural sciences more reliable principles of ordering and interpretation; no rational psychology is in a better relationship with what experimental psychology and its related sciences have discovered; no cosmology is more flexible and welcoming to the discoveries that have disconcerted so many ancient fantasies; no morality serves better the progress of human consciousness and institutions.

I can’t even attempt to prove here minimally the reason for these assertions; I hope everyone experiences it for themselves, it’s a matter of trust. And the Catholic’s trust should not naturally be placed in the one who received the mission and grace to guide from above the movement of their spirit?

The Church believes today, as from the very beginning, that Thomism is a salvific ark, capable of keeping spirits afloat amidst the flood of doctrines. It does not confuse it with faith, nor with science in all its breadth; it knows that it is fallible and that it participated, in what is transitory theory, in the errors of its time; but it esteems that its structure corresponds in its entirety to the constitution of reality and intelligence, and it notes that science and faith can converge in it, for it has itself settled between them like a castle at a crossroads.

Nothing can be imposed in such a domain; but I say to those who intend to devote themselves to comparative science, that is, to those who have the purpose of approaching the particular sciences, philosophy, and theology as a single and unique study: turn to yourselves; try to find in your heart a sufficient faith in its secular guide to not refuse it a free filial adherence. If you succeed, your fidelity will be rewarded; you will rise to an unknown level, both from proud solipsism and from modernity without an eternal foundation.

### 3. Expertise {#sertillanges-intellectual.5.3}
Let us now complete what we have just said about comparative science, so that it is not thought that we propose under this denomination an encyclopedic science. The more one knows, within certain conditions, the better; however, when these conditions cannot be safeguarded—and today more than ever—, the encyclopedic spirit becomes an enemy of science.

Science is more in the depths than on the surface. Science is knowledge through causes, and causes are deep like roots. We must always sacrifice extension for penetration, for the simple reason that extension is nothing in itself, and penetration, introducing us to the center of facts, provides us with the substance of what was sought in an endless investigation.

We have just suggested a certain extension, but in favor of the depth itself and for the sake of formation; once formation is obtained and the deepening of its possibilities ensured, then it is necessary to dig, and only specialization can allow it.

Often what is indispensable at the beginning becomes harmful later on. This harm can manifest itself here in many ways and lead by various paths to the decline of the spirit.

Firstly, everyone has their capacities, their resources, their internal and external difficulties, and we must ask ourselves if it would be wise to equally cultivate what we were made for and what is more or less beyond our reach. Overcoming a difficulty is very good; it’s necessary; but intellectual life should not be permanent acrobatics. It’s extremely important to work with joy, and therefore with relative ease, and therefore in the same direction as one’s own abilities. It is necessary, advancing first through various paths, to discover oneself and, once aware of one’s special vocation, to fix oneself in it.

Furthermore, a danger lurks for spirits that spread themselves too thin: being content with little. Satisfied with their explorations across all fields, they no longer exert themselves; their progress, initially very rapid, is like that of will-o’-the-wisps upon the earth. No energy sustains itself for long if not stimulated by increasing difficulty and maintained by a growing interest in arduous excavation. Examining the whole, judged in its relations and unity in the light of fundamental principles, it is urgent, if one does not want to remain stagnant, to dedicate oneself to a precise, limited task, proportionate to one’s strengths, and to devote oneself to it wholeheartedly.

Our previous propositions find here their reciprocity. We said: it is necessary to walk through diverse paths to have the feeling of encounters; it is necessary to approach the earth with breadth to descend into the depths. Having done this, if what one now wants is to dig towards the center, the apparent narrowness will be beneficial for the whole space, the bottom of the excavation will reveal the entire sky. When one knows something deeply, however little one knows about everything else, that remainder benefits in its entirety from that journey into the depths. All abysses resemble each other, and all foundations communicate.

Furthermore, assuming that one approaches with the same enduring energy all branches of knowledge, soon an impossible task will arise. What to do? Whoever wishes to be legion will forget to be someone; wanting to be a giant, one diminishes as a man.

Each one, in life, has his work; he must apply himself to it courageously and leave to others what Providence reserves for others. It is necessary to avoid specialization when the purpose is to become a cultivated man, and, concerning the hero of these pages, a superior man; but it is necessary to return to specialization when it comes to being a man performing a function and proposing a useful yield. In other words, it is necessary to understand everything, but to end up achieving something.

### 4. The Necessary Sacrifices {#sertillanges-intellectual.5.4}
See, from this, the obligation that each person must decide, in due time, to make the necessary sacrifices. It is very painful to say to oneself: by taking this path, I abandon a thousand others. Everything is interesting; everything could be useful; everything attracts and seduces the generous spirit; but there is death, there are the needs of the spirit and of things: one must submit and be content, regarding what time and wisdom steal from us, with a look of sympathy that will also be a homage to the truth.

Don’t be ashamed to ignore what you could only know by paying the price of dispersion. Recognize it with humility, for it is a sign of our limits; but our limits, once accepted, become part of our virtue; a great dignity comes from it: that of the man who lives under his own law and fulfills his role. We are little, but we are part of a whole, and that is our honor. What we do not do, we also do: God does it, our brothers do it, and we are with them in the unity of love.

Do not think, therefore, that everything is possible for you. Measure yourself, measure your task; after some inevitable attempts, learn to limit yourself without rigidity; maintain, amidst readings and the necessity of small tasks, the benefit of initial cultivation, contact with vastness, but as for the essential of your time and your strength, concentrate. The half-scientist is not the one who only knows half of things, but the one who only knows them halfway. Know what you have decided to know; only glance at the rest. What does not belong to your own vocation, leave it to God—He will take care of it. Do not be a deserter of yourself, for wanting to replace everyone.
