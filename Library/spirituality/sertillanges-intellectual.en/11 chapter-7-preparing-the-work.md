## Chapter 7. Preparing the Work {#sertillanges-intellectual.7}
### A. Reading {#sertillanges-intellectual.7.a-reading}
1. LIGHT READING

To work means to learn and to produce: in both cases, a long preparation is necessary. Producing is a result, and to learn in difficult and complex matters, it is necessary to go through what is simple and easy—“By the streams, and not directly, is how we reach the sea,” says Saint Thomas.

Well, reading is the universal means of learning, and it is the immediate or distant preparation for all production.

We never think in isolation; we think in society, in immense collaboration; we work with the workers of the past and the present. The entire intellectual world can be compared, thanks to reading, to an editorial room or a business office: each one finds beside them the initiation, the help, the control, the teaching, the encouragement they need.

To know how to read and to utilize readings, therefore, is a primordial necessity for the man of studies, and may God forbid that routine unawareness allows us to forget about it!

The first rule is this: read little. In 1921, in the newspaper Le Temps, Paul Souday, who apparently had some reason to avenge himself on me, took this precept, “read lightly,” to try to find in it a spirit of ignorance. The reader knows how much such criticism is worth. Paul Souday, of course, knew no less.

I do not advise reducing readings indiscriminately: everything that precedes protests against that interpretation. We want to form a broad spirit, practice comparative science, keep the horizon open in front of us: this cannot be done without a lot of reading. But much and little only oppose each other on the same ground. Absolutely, much is needed, because the work is vast; relatively, little, given the deluge of writings with which today even the smallest specialty crowds libraries and souls.

What I proscribe is the passion for reading, the compulsion, the intoxication by an excess of spiritual food, the disguised laziness that prefers easy companionship to effort.

The “passion” for reading, which many honor as a precious intellectual quality, is actually a fetish; it differs in no way from the other passions that devour the soul, bring disturbances to it, throw and interweave confused currents in it, and exhaust its forces.

We must read intelligently, and not passionately. Let’s go to books as the housewife goes to the market, already having the menus of the day defined according to the laws of hygiene and prudent economy. Your spirit in the market is not the same as it will be at night, at the cinema. It’s not about dreaming, getting excited, but about governing a home and giving it the best possible life.

Disordered reading dulls the mind, does not nourish it; gradually renders it incapable of reflection and concentration, and therefore of production; externalizes it from within, if I may put it that way, and makes it a slave to its mental images, to that ebb and flow of which it becomes an ardent spectator. Such exaltation is an alibi; it strips intelligence and makes it only able to follow the thoughts of others and surrender to the current of words, of presentations, of chapters, of volumes.

The small constant excitements thus provoked destroy energies, like a constant vibration damages steel. We should not expect any true work from the voracious reader, after having overloaded the eyes and the meninges; these, spiritually, in a state of headache, while the sensible worker, maintaining self-control, calm and free, only reads what he intends to retain, only retains what should serve him, organizes his brain and does not stupefy it with an absurd burden.

Set aside some time to go out, read the book of nature, breathe fresh air, relax. After the chosen activity, organize the chosen fun, instead of surrendering to an automatism that only has its matter intellectual, which in itself is as banal as slipping down a slope or climbing a mountain for no reason at all.

One talks about being “up to date,” and without a doubt an intellectual cannot ignore the human genre, nor above all lose interest in what is written in the world of their specialty; but be careful that “current events” do not exhaust all your capacities for work and, instead of moving it forward, paralyze it. One only advances by rowing with one’s own arms; no current can lead you to what you intend to achieve. Make your own path, and do not embark on all directions.

The reduction should fall primarily on the less substantial and less serious readings. Poisoning oneself with novels, forget it. One, occasionally, to rest and not neglect some literary glory, is fine; but it is a concession, for most novels disturb and do not rest, they agitate and disorient thoughts.

Regarding newspapers, defend yourself from them with an energy that the constancy and indiscretion of their attacks have made indispensable. It is necessary to know what the newspapers contain. But they contain so little! And it is very easy to find out about it without indulging in endless sessions of lazy reading! In any case, there are more appropriate times for this foray into news than the work hour.

A great worker should content himself, it seems to me, with the weekly or bi-weekly chronicle of a magazine, and as for the rest, being attentive to what people comment on day-to-day, only resort to newspapers if they indicate a remarkable article or if something important happens.

I summarize what I have just stated by saying: never read when you can withdraw; read, except in moments of distraction, solely what relates to the objective you have determined, and read little, so as not to devour the silence.

2. CHOOSING

In these initial observations the principle of choices is already included. How much discernment do we need, said Nicole, regarding what serves as food for our spirit and will become the seed of our thoughts! For what we read today with indifference will arise when it is opportune, and will provide us, without even realizing it, with the thoughts that will be the source of our salvation or our perdition. God awakens good thoughts to save us; the devil awakens the bad thoughts whose seed he finds in us.[50](part0018.xhtml#a1EB)

We therefore must choose, which means two things: to choose the books, to choose within the books.

Choose the books. Do not believe in self-serving advertisements or enticing titles. Have faithful and expert advisors. Only drink from the sources. Frequent only the elite of thinkers. That’s not always possible in personal relationships but it’s easy in reading materials, and we must take advantage of that. Admire with all your heart what deserves to be admired, but do not lavish admiration. Despise shoddy works, which are probably poorly thought out.

Just read first-hand, where the master ideas shine. These are few in number. Books repeat themselves, dilute themselves, or contradict themselves, which is another way of repeating themselves. If we observe carefully, the findings of thought are rare; the old foundation, or rather, the permanent foundation is the best; and it is on it that we must rely to truly communicate with the intelligence of man, far from the babbling or quarrelsome little individualities. It was a fashion merchant (Miss Bertin) who found this formula: “Only that which has been forgotten is new.” Most writers are nothing but editors; that’s something, but what I need is authors.

So, you will read without prejudice what is well-written; give to what is current the part that belongs to it; it will be greater when it comes to information, positive notions that are developing or growing; you must belong to your time; you must not be “an archaic type.” But also, do not have the superstition of the new; love the eternal books, which speak the eternal truths.

Then you must choose in the books, where not everything has the same importance. But not with the attitude of a judge; be rather, towards the author, a brother in truth, a friend, and an inferior friend, for, at least under certain aspects, you take him as a guide. The book is an older brother; you must honor it, approach it without pride, listen to it without prejudices, endure its defects, seek the seed in the haystack. But you are a free man, you remain responsible: maintain sufficient reserve to protect your soul and, if necessary, defend it.

“The books are works of men,” Nicole also said, “and the corruption of man mixes with most of his actions, and as it consists of ignorance and concupiscence, almost all books suffer from these two defects.”[51](part0018.xhtml#a1EF) Therefore, filtering, to purify, is often necessary throughout a reading. To do so, trust in God and in the best within oneself, in the self that is a child of God and in whom an instinct for truth, a love for goodness will serve as a safeguard.

Remember, also, that in large part a book is worth what you are worth, and it is you yourself who makes it valuable. Leibniz used everything; St. Thomas took from the heretics and pagans of his time a multitude of thoughts, and none of them harmed him. A wise man finds intelligence in everything; a fool casts the shadow of his narrow and inert forehead on all sides. Choose the best you can, and make everything good, broad, open to truth, prudent and fruitful, because you yourself will have been so.

3. FOUR TYPES OF READING

To be even more precise, I distinguish four species of reading. One reads to form oneself and become someone; one reads with a task in mind; one reads in order to practice for work and for good; one reads to be distracted. There are background readings, readings of occasion, readings of encouragement or edification, and readings of relaxation.

All these species of readings must be done in the light of our observations; each one also presents its particular demands. Background readings require docility, occasional readings, mastery, stimulus readings, ardor, rest readings, freedom.

When one is in training and almost everything still needs to be acquired, it is not the time for initiatives. When it comes to a first formation, a collective culture, or when addressing a new discipline, or a problem neglected until then, the consulted authors for this purpose should be accredited rather than criticized, and followed in their own paths, and not used according to the reader’s ways. Acting too early harms acquisition; initially, it is wise to bend. “One must believe in their master,” says Saint Thomas, repeating Aristotle. He himself believed and did very well.

It is not about blindly surrendering; a noble spirit never imprisons; but just as one learns the art of command by obeying, so too is the mastery of thought achieved through discipline. An attitude of respect, trust, and provisional faith, while one does not yet possess all the norms for judgment, is such an evident necessity that only pedants and presumptuous individuals deny it. No one is infallible; but the student is far less so than the master, and if he refuses to submit, for every time he is right, there will be twenty times when he errs the truth and falls victim to appearances. On the contrary, belief is a relative passivity, granting the master something of what we owe to the truth, is favorable to it, and allows us to eventually utilize even the shortcomings and even the illusions of the doctor. We can only know what a man lacks if we know his wealth well.

Preliminarily, it is an elementary wisdom to choose, among a thousand, the guides in whom one wants to place such trust. The choice of a spiritual father is always serious. We recommend Saint Thomas because of his lofty doctrines; we cannot restrict ourselves to him; but three or four authors that one should know thoroughly for general culture, three or four others for specialization, and a similar number for each problem that arises, is sufficient. We will resort to other sources for information, not for formation, with a different attitude of mind.

It will in fact be reverse, under certain aspects; for those who inform themselves, who wish to utilize, are no longer in a state of pure receptivity; they already have their own idea, a plan; the consulted work is at their service. A dose of submission is always required; but it now refers to truth and no longer to the writer, and if it concerns the latter as well, it grants him a faith that may transform his conclusions, that may not hinder his own path. These questions of attitude are very important, for consulting in the same way as one studies is a waste of time, and studying with a spirit of consultation is equivalent to being one’s only master and losing the benefit of the training offered by an initiator.

One who reads to do a job has the spirit dominated by what he intends to accomplish; he does not dive into the river, draws water from it; he stays on its banks, keeps freedom of movement, reinforces his own idea with what he extracts from it, instead of fitting it into someone else’s idea, and comes out of the reading enriched, not impoverished, as would happen if the fascination of reading harmed the intention of usefulness that justified it.

Regarding stimulus readings, the choice, besides the general rules, should take into account everyone’s experience. What has already worked for you will probably work again. An influence can wear off in the long term, but at the beginning, it strengthens itself; habit revives it; a more intimate penetration installs it in us; the association of ideas and feelings connects certain soul states to a certain page that return when we come back to it.

Having your favorite authors, their stimulating pages, at hand in moments of intellectual or spiritual depression, always ready to inject their good sap into you, is an immense resource. I know people to whom the peroration of the Funeral Oration of the Great Conde[52](part0018.xhtml#a1HC) raised them up for many years every time their inspiration declined. Others, on a spiritual level, cannot resist Pascal’s Mystery of Jesus, a prayer by Saint Thomas, a certain chapter of The Imitation of Christ, or a particular parable. Let each one observe themselves, notice what revives them, keep these remedies for the diseases of the soul close at hand, and do not fear to tirelessly resort to the same cordial or antidote.

When it comes to resting, the importance of choice seems much smaller; and indeed it is, relatively; but don’t think it’s indifferent to distract oneself in this or that, when the goal is to restore the conditions suitable for what constitutes its reason for being. Certain readings do not give you sufficient rest; others rest you too much, impairing the introspection that should follow them; others may divert you, in the etymological sense of the word itself, that is, lead you away from your path.

I met someone who was resting from hard work with Zeller’s History of Greek Philosophy; it was a distraction, but insufficient. Others read spicy or fantastic stories that scatter; others give in to temptations that discourage their work and harm their soul. None of this is good. If books are servile, like the objects we use in our lives, especially those that have an accessory role, they should be at our service. Don’t sacrifice yourself for a trifle.

Many thinkers have found a relief and a habitual taste in travel and exploration narratives, in poetry, in artistic criticism, in reading comedies, in memoirs. Each one has their tastes and taste here is paramount. According to St. Thomas, only one thing gives true rest: joy; trying to distract oneself in boredom is a mistake.

Read what gives you pleasure, what doesn’t excite you too much, what doesn’t harm you in any way, and since, even when distracted, you are a consecrated, have the intelligence to read that which, beyond the restful usefulness, can be useful to you in another way, helping you to improve yourself, to adorn your spirit, to be a man.

4. CONTACT WITH GENIUSES

I want to speak particularly, as I attribute extreme importance to the conduct of the mind and life, about the use of great men. Contact with geniuses is one of the special graces that God grants to modest thinkers; we should prepare ourselves for it as we prepare for prayer with Scripture, as we gather ourselves and assume an attitude of respect when approaching a great personality or a saint.

We think very little about the privilege of this solidarity that multiplies the joy and the profit of living, that expands the world and makes our dwelling in it more noble and precious, that renews for each one of us the glory of being human, of having the spirit open to the same horizons as the greatest among us, of living in the heights and of building with our fellow men, with our inspirers, a society in God. “After the geniuses, come immediately those who know how to recognize their value,” said Teresa Brunswick, speaking of Beethoven.

From time to time, remembering those who shine with special brilliance in the firmament of intelligence and leafing through our titles of nobility, and this pride has the beauty and effectiveness of the pride that a son has of a distinguished father or a high lineage.

If you are a writer, do you not appreciate the benefit of having as predecessors Homer, Sophocles, Virgil, Dante, Shakespeare, Corneille, Racine, La Fontaine, Pascal? If you are a philosopher, would you renounce Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Saint Thomas Aquinas, Descartes, Leibniz, Kant, Maine de Biran, Bergson? Scientist, do you not know how much you owe to Archimedes, Euclid, again Aristotle, Galileo, Kepler, Lavoisier, Darwin, Claude Bernard, Pasteur? Religious man, think of the impoverishment of all souls if they did not have, besides Saint Paul, Saint Augustine, Saint Bernard, Saint Bonaventure, the author of The Imitation, Saint Catherine of Siena, Saint Teresa, Bossuet, Saint Francis de Sales, Newman.

The communion of saints is the support of mystical life; the banquet of the wise, eternalized by our worship and our assiduity, it’s the comfort of intellectual life. Cultivating admiration for illustrious thinkers and the consequent coexistence with them is the means not of becoming those whom you honor but of becoming who you are, and I repeat that this is the goal to aim for and achieve.

Contact with geniuses provides us, as an immediate benefit, with elevation; solely by their superiority, they already gratify us before even teaching us anything. They give us a tone; they accustom us to the air of heights. We walk in a region of low elevation: they immediately transport us to their atmosphere. In this world of lofty thoughts, the face of truth seems to unveil itself; beauty shines; the fact that we accompany and understand men with such clear vision shows us that we are ultimately of the same race, that the universal Soul is within us, the Soul of souls, the Spirit to which one would suffice to adapt to burst forth in divine speeches, since, at the origin of all inspiration, always prophetic, is “God, the first author of everything that is written!”[53](part0018.xhtml#a1C0)

When the genius speaks, we immediately perceive that he is simple; he expresses the man, and his echo resonates within us. And when he falls silent, can we not continue in the same tone and complete the interrupted phrase? Unfortunately, no! When he leaves us, we return to the previous helplessness, we babble; but we know that the true word exists, and our babblings already have a new accent.

Listen to some preludes by Bach. They tell us little: a short motif that is resumed, with insistent variations, with a relief as little accentuated as a Roty medal. But what a degree of inspiration! To what unknown world are we transported! To inhabit and move freely in it would be wonderful! But at least we can return to it in memory, and what benefit does this possibility of ascent bring us, which distances us from trivialities, tunes us and helps us to judge appropriately the puerile fireworks that commonly make up the feasts of the spirit!

When the genius then provides us with themes, offers us truths, explores mysterious regions for us, and sometimes, like a Thomas Aquinas or a Goethe, shows us centuries of culture concentrated in a single person: how much we owe him! “The human spirit cannot go very far,” wrote Rodin, “without this condition: that the individual’s thought unites with patience and in silence with the thought of generations.” The great thinker who sums up in himself the generations allows us, therefore, to go very far with his help; he gives us possession of domains he conquered and explored, that he sowed and cultivated. At the hour of harvest, he calls us.

The society of intelligences is always restricted: reading expands it; we cast upon the brilliant page a supplicating gaze that does not go unanswered; we are assisted; we are instructed; the work of God in exceptional spirits is for our benefit as much as for theirs; we are enriched by them; the giant carries the dwarf, and the ancestor leaves us an inheritance. Should we not take advantage of these riches? And we can: just attention and fidelity.

The genius totally renews us. And the gift par excellence of this visionary is to present to thought in a new light, at the core of a system of relations that, so to speak, recreates it, the reality that was already there, evident, and that we did not see.

All the infinity of thought is behind any fact; but we await the perspective to open; only genius advances, removes the veils and says to us: come! Science consists of seeing from within: genius sees from within; it frequents the innermost of beings, and thanks to it the very being speaks to us, instead of our weak and doubtful echoes.

The genius simplifies. Most great discoveries spring from sudden and dazzling concentrations. The great maxims are composed of multiple condensed experiences. The sublime stroke, in painting, in music, in architecture, in poetry, contains and unifies values that until then were scattered and indecisive.

A great man, by reflecting common humanity, reduces everything it has acquired to the essential, as Leonardo da Vinci synthesized in one moment the changing expressions of his model. The Egyptian line applied to everything: this is genius, and its rich simplicity composes our splendor.

The genius stimulates us and gives us confidence. The emotion it provokes is the spur of ardent initiatives, the revealer of vocations, and the remedy for restless shynesses. An impression of sublimity is to our soul as the sunrise. The wisdom that manifests in these heroes also extends its secret invitations to us, and what happiness to be able to say: it is also within me!

Perhaps it is not true that great men reflect only their century; but it is true that they reflect humanity, and every member of that humanity has his share of glory in them. The malevolent thinkers may speak as much as they want; they are as mistaken about humanity, by the mere existence of geniuses, as the Jews were about Jesus, when they said: “Can anything good come out of Nazareth?”[54](part0018.xhtml#a1CB) Yes, something good can come from this poor world, for from it came a Plato. A great man would be nothing if he were not, by his resources and by the use he makes of them, a son of Man. Now, the trunk that nourishes him has not lost its vigor; all who receive the same sap can always expect to grow and produce, just like them, immortal flowers.

Even the errors of the great can contribute to the benefit we seek in their company. We must defend ourselves from these errors; the strength of these men sometimes deviates; almost all of them have their shadows, like a mask with accentuated features; the exaggeration of a certain point of view, or any other outburst can lead them away from rectitude. However, there is not one of them who, despite their aberrations, does not show to a sensible spirit the eternal foundations of science and the secrets of life.

Your errors are not ordinary errors; they are excesses; the depth and acuity of vision are not absent in them; following them with caution, one can be sure to go very far, while preserving oneself at the same time from their false steps. “All things work together for good for those who love God,” says the Apostle;[55](part0018.xhtml#a1F1) for those who are seated in truth, everything can be useful. Having formed our spirit in the good school, keeping our frames of thought well-adjusted and firm, we can always expect to grow in contact with ingenious errors. In this danger, as long as we do not expose ourselves indiscriminately to it, there is also grace; a new dimension is revealed to us; a facet of the world is shown to us, perhaps with excessive exclusivity, but with power; the animation breathed into our spirit will remain in it; the depths demanded by the very resistance will strengthen us; we will be better formed, better protected, for having run these sublime risks without succumbing.

Saint Thomas, from whom I draw inspiration here, concludes from these observations that we owe our recognition even to those who tempted us thus, if by the occasion they gave us and by their ideas we have progressed in something. Directly, we are debtors only to the truth; but indirectly we owe those who erred the addition of formation that thanks to them Providence has given us.[56](part0018.xhtml#a1GZ)

Think about what the Church owes to heresies and philosophy to its great controversies. If it were not for Arius, Eutyches, Nestorius, Pelagius, Luther, Catholic dogma would not have been constituted. If Kant had not shaken the foundations of human knowledge, criteriology would still be in its infancy, and if Renan had not written about the origins of Christianity, the Catholic clergy would be far from the historical and exegetical formation it possesses today.

What is true collectively is also true individually. We must learn to think well especially in contact with the wise; but madness can also teach us; whoever escapes its contagion draws strength from it. “He who stumbles without falling, takes a bigger step.”

5. CONCILIATING INSTEAD OF OPPOSING

One essential condition for making good use of readings, whether they are current or ingenious, is to always seek to reconcile their authors instead of opposing them. The critical spirit has its applications; it may be necessary to dissect opinions and classify men; the method of contrast must then be used and requires only that we do not force it. But when it comes to education, personal use, or even doctrinal exposition, the procedure is different. What matters then are not the thoughts, but the truths, not the battles between men, but their work and what remains of it. In this case, insisting indefinitely on differences is vain; seeking points of contact is the fruitful investigation.

Saint Thomas gives us here an admirable example. He always strove to bring doctrines closer, to clarify them, and to complement one another. Aristotelian, he leans on Plato; without being Augustinian, he makes Augustine his constant nourishment; having declared Averroes a corrupter of Peripateticism, he does not fail to call him a sublime spirit (praeclarum ingenium) and cites him frequently. When he comments, he seeks to extract from the text its purest truth or its greatest richness, saying what we should see in it, charitably closing his eyes to what may be reproachable. No one is so far from those reviewers who only read to find printing errors.

Whoever wants to acquire, in coexistence with the authors, not attitudes of combat, but truth and perspicacity, must cultivate that spirit of accommodation and diligent collection, the spirit of the bee. Honey is made with different flowers. A procedure of exclusion, of summary elimination, and limited choice infinitely harms a formation, and reveals in the spirit tempted to practice it a stigma of ominous augury. “Every individual who is not a creator,” writes Goethe, “has a negative, narrow, exclusive taste, which ends up stripping the creative being of its energy and life.” Such a constituted intelligence is diminished; instead of looking at everything from a universal point of view, it ends up falling into the spirit of sectarianism and slander.

Not all the slanderers are on the doorstep; they are also found throughout the history of philosophy, of the sciences, and even of theology, and they have many imitators. Go higher. You who seek the truth, always willing to recognize its face, do not pit your servants against each other, nor these “incomplete angels,” partial geniuses that truth visited without making its abode in them.

Especially in relation to the greatest, it is a kind of profanation to assume a querulous attitude. Let us be saddened by their mistakes but let us not massacre them; let us build bridges, not dig ditches between their doctrines. There is great light in the discovery of relationships that secretly connect the most disparate ideas and systems. Devoting oneself to this work of reconstructing the integral truth amidst its deformations is much more fruitful than perpetual criticism.

Ultimately, if we know how to use them, great men allow us to communicate the same essential truths. I do not say that all of them proclaim them, but they all place us in perspectives that allow us to see these truths, lead us, or even inexorably impel us towards them. They seem to fight among themselves and divide science, disunite the human spirit; in reality, they converge. The columns of a temple rest on the slabs, move away, group themselves into distant beams; but they press the arches towards each other and, through numerous ribs, end up forming a single vault. Finding this shelter and taking refuge in it is what suits your vocation, you who seek not the noise, the clash of parties, the restraint or the factitious excitement of intelligence, but solely the truth.

6. APPROPRIATING AND LIVING

One last and crucial indication imposes itself in relation to readings. The reader must, although passive in a certain way to open oneself to truth and not hinder its empire, must also react to the reading to appropriate what is read and thus compose his soul. We read to think, we acquire riches to use them, we feed ourselves to live.

We have already condemned the eternal reader who little by little falls into a mechanical habit, into an erudite automatism that is no longer a true work. But it is not necessary to be a voracious reader to fall into this passivity. Many read as if they were knitting. Abandoned to a kind of indolence, their spirit watches the parade of ideas and remains inert,

Like a sleepy shepherd watches the water flow.[57](part0018.xhtml#a1GM)

The work, therefore, is life; life is an assimilation, and assimilation is a reaction of the living organism to food. It is not enough to harvest at the right time, gather the wheat into sheaves, and finally bake your bread; it is necessary to transform it into one’s own flesh, for it is only for this purpose that the magnificent grains serve.

Those who are always learning may never be instructed if they do not transform into their own substance what they have learned in their gentle companionships. Docility is virtuous and necessary, but not sufficient. “Obedience is the foundation of improvement,” said Auguste Comte, but it is not improvement. The genius that instructs us could say, like his Inspirer: “I came that they may have life, and have it abundantly.” [58](part0018.xhtml#a1JU) What was life in another might not be in us like a lamp extinguished?

No one can instruct us without our cooperation. Reading proposes truth to us: it is up to us to seize it. It is not the market vendor who nourishes my body. What I absorb must become my own being, which only I can do. “Through teaching,” Boethius wrote, “the spirit of man is merely incited to know.”[59](part0018.xhtml#a1FU) Saint Augustine had said before him: “A man is to teaching as a farmer is to the tree.”[60](part0018.xhtml#a1FV)

Saint Thomas, delving deeper into the subject, observes that the word or writing do not directly reach the spirit; their only role, through sounds and signs, is to give the soul a matter. The sound resonates; the light vibrates; our senses perceive and communicate the signal, and, by a reverse movement, this signal, which came from the idea, has the mission of provoking a similar idea. But in all this, the spirits do not unite; someone’s signs only indirectly come into contact with another, and what science does is not the system of signs proposed to us, but the work of our own reason on these signs.

In the background, the propositions of science that reach us remain as external to intelligence as the very things we seek to know; they have only the advantage of corresponding, as signs, to ideas already elaborated and arranged. This facilitates our thinking but does not replace it. Teaching only provides us with the means to act spiritually, as medicine offers our bodies the means of healing; but just as no remedy can act on an inert organism, no teaching achieves its purpose in a negligent spirit.

In reality, nature heals itself, and the spirit can only be illuminated by its own light—unless we assert: by the light of God infused in it, as the psalmist’s word: “Let the light of your face shine on us, O Lord!”[61](part0018.xhtml#a1BS) Finally, God is our only Master, and it is He who speaks to us within ourselves, and it is from His relationship with us that all instruction comes; from one man to another, thought is strictly incommunicable.[62](part0018.xhtml#a1BT)

This penetrating analysis has practical consequences. If the idea does not come to us from outside, if it is within ourselves that it must be born, let us strive so that the intellectual material provided by the book, so that these signs of a mute interlocutor truly elevate us to expressed thought and even beyond it, for an evocation, in an active spirit, should always provoke.

We only enter into the intimacy of geniuses if we partake in their inspiration; to merely listen to them from the outside is to condemn oneself to not hearing them. It is neither with the eyes nor with the ears that one hears a profound assertion, but with a soul that rises to the level of the one who uttered it, with an intelligence illuminated by the same light.

The source of knowledge is not in books; it is in reality and in thought. Books are signposts; the road is older, and no one can make the journey to truth for us. What a writer said is not the most important thing; what interests us is what is, and our spirit must aim not to repeat, but to understand, that is, to apprehend with it, that is, to absorb vitally, and finally to think for oneself. The assertion we hear must, following the author, perhaps thanks to him, but ultimately independently of him, compel the soul to reaffirm it for itself. We must recreate all science for our own use.

The main benefit of reading, indeed, at least that of great works, is not acquiring scattered truths, but the increase of our wisdom. Amiel, comparing the French spirit with the German spirit, used to say: “Germans put the wood on the fire; the French bring the flame.” The judgment is perhaps a little categorical, but there is no doubt that what matters most is the flame.

The outbreak of wisdom was the main objective of our education; it must also be the aim of the education we give ourselves. Without it, what we put into ourselves has no value at all; it would be the copy of a book, another book, as useless as the first when it was in the library. Also in ourselves, there are many volumes and great texts that we have not yet read.

What an abuse to live with geniuses and only take their formulas! And how this will become evident when one wants, when writing, to use them! Quickly this parroting will become clear, and it will be found that there is no one behind these formulas.

To truly use is to invent. Even when someone quotes literally, if the quoted passage is embedded in a discourse and occupies an exact place in it, if the discourse itself has a good level, if it belongs to the same lineage as the borrowing and absorbs it into its living unity, the writer is original and, so to speak, equals the master. Glorifying another, receives a similar glory. The quotation is for you as the word that the dictionary provides and that nevertheless you create, as the soul creates its body.

Saint Thomas cites thus, Bossuet also, Pascal also. And we, who dedicate ourselves to very humble tasks, must apply the same laws of the spirit. Truth is the ancestor of all men; wisdom invites all; we should not leave only to the greatest the monopoly of great quotations. Before geniuses, we are only children, but children who are heirs. What they give us is ours, belongs to eternity; they also received it. What was before them and is above us, what God prepared for all, that is what we must contemplate while they speak to us.

That is the price of originality, and if one day our wisdom grows, we will be able to create an original work in the good sense of the term. In a truly personal production, reading serves only to stimulate, to nourish our own being, and not our pages. There is in this a new sense of what I was saying earlier: to find in books what is not there, entries to penetrate into new domains.

If we can only arrive at common knowledge by ourselves, with much more reason will we only contribute our share of new thought by our own effort. When I read, I would like to find in the book a favorable starting point, and then abandon it, free myself from it with the feeling of a debt. I have the duty to be myself. Why repeat another? However little I may be, I know very well that God does not make any of His spirits in vain; not even natural things He makes in vain. I obey my Master by becoming free.

I live, I am not a reflection, and I desire a fruitful life. What does not engender is not: may my reading allow me to engender my thought, not resembling my inspirer, but myself!

This is, I believe, the final word on the matter of books. A book is a sign, a stimulant, an aid, a starter, not a substitute, nor a shackle. Thought should be our own. In reading, we should not go to our masters, but start from them. A work is a cradle, not a tomb. Physically, we are born young and die old; intellectually, due to our secular heritage, “we are born old; let us try to die young.” [63](part0018.xhtml#a1C7)

The true geniuses did not want to chain us, but to free us. If they wanted to enslave us, we should flee from them, defend ourselves from this invasion that annihilates so much the less we are able to fight with our own strengths. Let us emancipate our soul. The more thought proceeds from our innermost selves, from what in us is incommunicable, the more it will reflect humanity, and the more other men will recognize themselves in it. Human respect keeps us apart, and spontaneity brings us closer to humanity. Repetitions, whether open or disguised, soon become tedious. “When someone speaks only of what they have read,” said Schopenhauer, “they are not read by anyone.”

We work, in short, for us and for truth, for us and for God. Our model is creative thought. Geniuses are a shadow of it. To be a shadow of a shadow is a degradation for whoever, small or great, is an incomparable, unprecedented, and unique spiritual fact in this world.

The man is multiple and we are one of his examples; God is in all: let us know how to honor the man in us and thus respect God.

### B. The Organization of Memory {#sertillanges-intellectual.7.b-the-organization-of-me}
1. WHAT MUST BE KEPT

Acquiring through reading would be useless, and reflecting would be impossible, if memory did not retain and present to us in due time what is useful for our work and for the work of the spirit.

Several geniuses enjoyed a prodigious memory; to others, it was lacking; most had a median memory and supplemented it in various ways. We cannot classify masters by this gift; it is indeed true, however, that under equal conditions, a broad and strong memory is a precious resource.

Let’s not conclude from this that we should exercise memory without discernment and overload it with the greatest possible number of notions, facts, images, texts. Saint Thomas seems to affirm it, when he wrote in the sixteen precepts: “Everything that is possible, deposit in the treasure of your spirit, as one who seeks to fill a vessel.” But we must grant to this maxim the benefit of an understood. One must retain everything that is possible, under the condition that it be useful, as it is under the same reservation that we must read everything that is possible.

We’ve already warned the intellectual about the abuse of readings; what we’ve said about it largely applies here, since memorizing and preserving acquisitions from which we cannot separate what is advantageous from what is harmful.

All the masters tell us that overloading memory is detrimental to personal thinking and attention. The spirit drowns in the mass of its possessions; what remains unused fills and paralyzes it; the dead weight oppresses the living being, excess food poisons it; many pretentious scholars of false and inert spirit, many “living libraries,” “walking dictionaries,” are proof of this.

We do not live by our memory; we make use of it to live. Record only what can help you to conceive or to execute, which can be assimilated into your soul, correspond to your objectives, vivify your inspiration, and bolster your work. As for the rest, consign it to oblivion. And if now and then many things turn out to be useful that did not seem so, or are not commonly so, this is no reason to say: let us memorize everything indiscriminately. What you need, you can find again; paper retains it effortlessly. No one memorizes all train schedules on the pretext that they might board any of them.

Pascal said he believed he had never forgotten anything he wanted to remember: that is useful memory, as long as one wants to retain only what may be beneficial. When Saint Augustine defined happiness as “to desire only what is good and to have all that one desires,” he also defined good memory. Entrust to yours what is good, and ask God to grant you, if it is His will, the grace of Pascal, that of Saint Thomas, “in whom nothing perished,” or that of Mozart, who could recall in its entirety, upon first hearing, a solemn mass. But I repeat that such grace is not necessary and can be supplied without any essential harm. And why calculate its cost if we must manage what has been given to us, not what is lacking?

One fundamental rule consists in making memory enter the general current of intellectual life, in making it participate in its vocation. Memory must specialize itself as the mind does, to the same extent, with the same concentration regarding the principal and its developments.

There are things that everyone should know, which every Christian in particular should keep in mind; there are those that an intellectual cannot ignore; there are others that refer to a specialty through more or less close ties, and each will feel the need to retain them in accordance with the spirit of breadth or restriction that they dedicate to them; there are finally others that are the very specialty and without which one falls short of their task, which rightly is labeled as ignorance and culpable inertia.

What each one must seek to keep in full light in their head and available at the first request is what constitutes the basis of their work, what is known, for the same reason, by all eminent spirits of their profession. There is no possible negligence in this, and it must be fulfilled as quickly as possible. As for the rest, it will be acquired as it is required by a particular task, without much effort to record it definitively.

In both cases, we see that retention stems from a preconceived idea, as in reading; the difference is that a particular task is a momentary vocation and vocation is a lasting task, and memory must adapt to that.

Nicole suggests to the Christian that they “learn by heart various psalms and various sentences from Sacred Scripture, in order to sanctify the memory with these divine words.”[64](part0018.xhtml#a1DY) It is a way to consecrate our common heavenly vocation and to facilitate the effort for good. Very few understand such advice today. Someone, for example, memorizes passages from Virgil, Racine, Musset, but would be in trouble to recite a psalm, ignores their Angelus, their Salve Regina, their Te Deum, their Magnificat. This is evidently a disorder. What is united to our spirit by the bonds of memory acts to a greater extent; a Catholic should desire the maximum of this action when it comes to that which animates their faith. It would be very good to be able, from time to time, throughout a journey or on an occasion that prompts it, to say to oneself formulas full of Christian sap!

2. IN WHICH ORDER TO RETAIN

Once the quantity of content is regulated, we must consider its organization. A memory cannot be chaos. Science is knowledge through causes; all experience only counts for its connections, its groupings, and its hierarchies of values. Accumulating indiscriminately is to render everything unusable, and to condemn oneself to only being able to remember something by chance.

It is essential that an intellectual memory obtain the characteristics of intellectuality; now, this is not satisfied with disparate notions, without precise affinities. Always seek what relates one thing to another, what conditions this or that, and let it be this coordination, and not scattered fragments, that establish themselves in your memory. A well-made head is like a family tree, in which all branches are connected to the trunk and thus communicate with each other; kinship in all its degrees is clearly revealed in it, expressing a lineage in all its relations and as a whole.

And this means that one must relate everything to the essential, both in memory and in thought itself. The primordial, the fundamental, the simple, from which the complex emerges in tiers and through successive differences: that is what sustains memory, as well as science, making it effective at the moment it must intervene.

It’s no use acquiring myriad notions if the first notions, instead of finding enrichment in them thanks to their dependencies that memory manifests, clash against a solid block of them and thus see their ruinous loneliness aggravated. Fifty data points are no more valuable than one if they do not express this profound relationship; scattered, they are sterile and, like the fig tree in the Gospel, occupy the earth uselessly.

First and foremost, preserve—as we have already advised seeking before anything else—the master ideas; let them present themselves as the first call, ready to clarify everything that is offered, to maintain in their position, despite the new inputs, the old ideas, to develop themselves on the occasion of each progress, as the brain utilizes what the stomach receives, as the heart benefits from the exercise performed by the limbs.

A new thought acts retrospectively; a torch also illuminates backwards. Materials stored transfigure themselves when arranged under an idea. Everything then recreates itself within us and becomes animated with new life. But for this to happen, it’s necessary that the paths of light be open, that our thoughts be arranged and able to communicate with each other.

Having regulated its inner order, you will be almost automatically protected against overload, and you will find that two precepts that seem distinct are, so to speak, a single precept. The useless, which always finds a place in chaos, does not find it in an organization. Either something serves, or it has no place! It becomes ridiculous the effort of someone or something to enter a device where it has no function, for which it neither cooperates nor assists in anything. Every hierarchy does its own policing.

Being thus relieved and well structured, a spirit will be able to dedicate itself to its works with all its strength; it will go directly to what matters and will not linger on details that, for another, are indeed the main.

When Pasteur came south to attack and conquer in a short time the evil that threatened French sericulture, he did not know the habits of the silkworm; he casually informed himself about them with the great entomologist Henri Fabre. He was initially surprised by the apparent superficiality of the “Parisian;” but soon, seeing that Pasteur investigated in a deeper zone and worked on the very sources of life, he understood, and later would praise this genius simplicity.

In each subject there are some ideas that govern everything, which are their universal keys; there are also those that govern life, and it is before them that we must light, within our hearts, the lamp of the sanctuary.

The creative faculty depends largely on the wisdom and sobriety of memory. Focusing on the essential leaves all perspectives open to the outside, and the logic of what we acquire tends to extend into new acquisitions. Thoughts serve as bait for thoughts; water runs to the river; only the rich are lent to; “For to those who have, more will be given, and they will have an abundance,” declares the Gospel.[65](part0018.xhtml#a1JV) Each truth is the dawn of another; every possibility seeks to realize itself, and when the inner order opens up to the experience, it’s like a root penetrating the earth: its substance works, its filaments expand and absorb the sap; life grows because the adaptation of the living being to its environment is the only condition for its fertility, as initially was the condition of its substance.

The medium of science is the cosmos, which is itself organization, structure: it is necessary, and quite so, for the student to progress, to establish within himself, thanks to memory, a corresponding structure that allows him to adapt and thus act.

3. HOW TO RETAIN

It remains to be said how one obtains such qualified memory, and how to use it. It’s not a great secret, although it relates to the deepest conditions of our mental life.

It is essential that an intellectual memory obtain the characteristics of intellectuality; now, this is not satisfied with disparate notions, without precise affinities. Always seek what relates one thing to another, what conditions this or that, and let it be this coordination, and not scattered fragments, that establish themselves in your memory. A well-made head is like a family tree, in which all branches are connected to the trunk and thus communicate with each other; kinship in all its degrees is clearly revealed in it, expressing a lineage in all its relations and as a whole.

Saint Thomas proposes four rules: 1st. to order what one wants to retain; 2nd. to deeply apply the spirit to it; 3rd. to meditate on it frequently; 4th. when one wants to recall it, to take the chain of dependencies by its end, which will bring all the rest. [66](part0018.xhtml#a1FJ)

The importance of order has already been emphasized from another point of view; but to fix the memory, everyone can try it for themselves. A succession of words, names, ideas, or unrelated elements is very difficult to keep; these isolated elements do not articulate; each one is as if wandering and quickly escapes. On the contrary, a series is a body and is preserved. What is based on its reason for being and its natural society, what is in its own environment, is less likely to disperse. Only what exists is preserved, and for an element separated from connected elements, there is no fullness.

To retain, therefore, put your attention on the connections and reasons for things; analyze, seek the whys, observe the genealogy of events, successions, and dependencies, imitate mathematical order, where necessity starts from the axiom and reaches the furthest conclusions. To deeply understand, then apprehend and introduce into the mind, not just links upon links, but a whole chain, ensuring the adherence of the whole. Unity is strength.

The application of the spirit, recommended next, aims to press the mysterious chisel that traces within us the figure of words and things. The more ardent the attention, the deeper the chisel penetrates, and the better the strokes will resist the permanent flow that tends to replace ideas as death replaces beings. When reading or listening with the aim to apprehend, be completely concentrated and present; repeat as if speaking aloud what has been said to you; hammer the syllables. I speak figuratively, but sometimes applying this literally has its advantages. Become capable of, immediately after reading or hearing what you study, reproducing it to the precise extent it needs to be fixed. In the case of a book, do not abandon it without being able to summarize it, appreciate it. I add this last observation because the object that has provoked an active intervention on our part is much less elusive; it becomes part of ourselves.

After that, meditating as frequently as possible, and as much as the object that must be preserved from oblivion deserves, is a necessity consecutive to the first. Life erases the traces of life, and therefore it is advised to us to chisel intensely; the same reason invites us, when we realize that despite everything the traces weaken, to go over the chisel in the carvings, to use plenty of etching solution, that is, to constantly revive our useful thoughts and to ruminate on the facts that we wish to keep before our eyes.

The agitation of the spirit is contrary to this work, a tranquil life and the distancing from passions are as necessary for the proper use of memory as for all intellectual functions.

The ability to admire oneself, the youthfulness of the soul before nature and life also contribute to remembrance. We retain better what impresses us. For this reason, in addition to all others, the intellectual must cultivate the feeling of novelty, of the ever new, which is the starting point of vigorous impulses for fruitful creations or for investigation.

Finally, when it comes to retrieving the memory and reactivating old images, we are advised to rely once again on this reality of mutual dependencies between thoughts, between impressions, which served as the basis for the constitution of memory. Everything more or less links up in the brain, even if one does not want it to: desiring it with all one’s might and having actively formed the most natural connections possible between notions, we will reap the benefits of this work.

Therefore, do not seek randomly in a whole that was not formed randomly; proceed logically, using the logic of things as it imposes itself, or as it was initially determined, ascending and descending through established series, invoking the contiguity of ideas, circumstances, finally, bringing back the force to the focus of attention that attention had fixed and arranged according to its own laws.

And what St. Thomas calls pulling the chain, is the end of the chain that he advises us to grab is the one that is as if in the most immediate dependence on what we seek. For example: I remember having thought of a study plan; this plan escapes me; but I know that when I thought of it, I was in a certain place, or talking to a friend, or that it was related to a certain set of spiritual operations, to a certain aspect of my vocation, or even that the project had been inspired by a previous reading, or was required by some previous work. To recover the lost idea, I recall the impression of the place, the interaction with the friend, the ideological set, the role to fulfill, the book analyzed or the work done. From there, I explore its surroundings, and through various attempts, I try to find again what I know is connected to one of these pieces of data.

In summary, what matters for memory is less the number of its acquisitions than their quality, first, their order, second, and finally the ability to use it. Materials are never lacking to thought; it is thought that lacks them. To apprehend is nothing without intelligent assimilation, penetration, chaining, the progressive unity of a rich and regulated soul.

What is interesting is not the construction site, but the architecture, and above all the spirit of the inhabitant. Have lofty inspiration, burning attention, emotion in the face of truth, zeal for investigation, and you will have sufficient memories.

### C. The Notes {#sertillanges-intellectual.7.c-the-notes}
1. HOW TO TAKE NOTES

We are obliged to repeat ourselves frequently. If we do so even more when talking about reading, memory, and notes, it’s because they form, so to speak, a single point. It’s always about completing ourselves so that we can, in due time, accomplish our work.

We should read relatively little; we should retain even less, and nature, as for the rest, takes care of the work. The notes, which are a kind of external memory, a “memory on paper,” as Montaigne said, should be much fewer in number than the readings, but they can expand much more than memory, supplement it, and therefore relieve it and come to the aid of work in a measure difficult to determine.

If we had to rely on memory to keep intact and ready for use what we found or discovered throughout our life of studies, it would be a disgrace. Memory is unfaithful; it loses, flees, and does not obey our call. We refuse to overload it, to suffocate the spirit; we prefer the freedom of the soul to an indigestible wealth. The solution is the notebook or a file.

Furthermore, memory classifies in its own way, and we try to assist it; but its classifications are capricious and unstable. To find the necessary recollection at the right moment, one would need a self-control that no mortal possesses. Notebooks and files will then come to our rescue. We must organize our reserves, deposit our savings in a bank, where they will certainly not yield any profit, but will also be in a safe and available place. We will be the cashier.

The practices here are diverse; but there are some general laws that it will be good to remember, so that each one can be inspired by them.

We can distinguish two species of notes: one corresponding to distant preparation and the other to immediate preparation of the work. You read or meditate to shape yourself and feed your spirit; ideas present themselves that seem good to fix; facts, various indications that may be useful; you then note them down. On the other hand, when studying a specific subject, when carrying out a production, you seek to document yourself, read what exists on the subject, resort to all sources of information available to you, and reflect on your own account, always with the pen in hand.

The first category of notes has the characteristic of being somewhat fortuitous; only the limits of your specialty and your wisdom can reduce the dose of chance. As life is always complex, the fleeting spirit, and as we ourselves have already advised expansions, many random things are introduced into notes of this kind. On the contrary, when you take notes to produce, having this production a defined character, the notes also define themselves, closely follow the chosen subject, and form a more or less organic whole.

There is, for these two types of notes, common rules and specific rules.

In both cases, we should avoid excesses, an accumulation of materials in which we later wrap ourselves and which become unusable. Some have notebooks so crammed and so numerous that a kind of anticipated discouragement prevents them from even opening them. These alleged treasures cost a lot of time and effort, and yield nothing; a multitude of trifles clutter them; even those that are useful could remain advantageously in the volumes from which they were extracted, with a reference accompanied by a keyword in place of tedious pages.

Have notes taken with reflection, with sobriety. To avoid the surprises of the moment, the effect of passing worries, and also the enthusiasms caused by a brilliant expression, only file definitively after a certain time. With calmness, at a good distance, you will judge your harvest and only store the good grain.

In both cases, equally, one must note after a work of energetic spirit and with a personal sense. It’s about completing oneself, equipping oneself, arming oneself with a panoply suitable for one’s profile, according to the needs of the battle one intends to wage. The fact that something is beautiful and good, that it is theoretically precious, is not a reason to write it. Thank God, there are many beautiful things in books: that doesn’t mean you’re going to copy the national library! You don’t buy a coat because it’s beautiful, but because it’s useful, and a piece admired in an antique shop should remain there if its size or style does not suit the apartment to furnish.

Avoid caprice in everything. Just as reading is a form of nourishment, and memory is an enriching asset that becomes part of a person, notes are both a reserve of sustenance and personal. Readings, memories, notes—all of this should fulfill us and, therefore, resemble us, be of our species, of our role, of our vocation, correspond to our ends and to the form of the gestures through which we can and want to achieve them.

We know how a book of accounts informs us about its owner, their way of living, and the objectives they pursue: the record of notes, the ledger, should be thus close to the intellectual, to what they must and want to be; in it, their assets are calculated, at least in part, and this account must correspond to the owner, on one hand, and to the presumed expense, on the other. I reflect on myself in my works: I must also reflect on my means, if I have adapted them well to each other and to myself.

Even better, it would be desirable that, beyond the documents themselves, facts, texts, or statistics, the notes you take were not only adapted to you, but were yours, and this not only when they emanate from your reflections, but also when they come from a reading. Also, the reading must be reflected upon, and a loan can become ours to the point of no longer differing from a creation.

I read and, as I read, I write; but I write what I think in contact with another, more than the other’s thought, and my ideal is that this be so even when I transcribe literally, perceiving that I would not express the common thought better. The writer is the one who conceives, and I also conceive what I assimilate deeply, what I strive to penetrate, to understand in the fullest sense of the word, what I make mine: thus I also become the writer, and I keep the writing as my own wealth.

For notes taken from a distance, there is nothing more essential to consider. For immediate ones, aimed at work, we first must reinforce the application of our rules, and we have to add the following.

We said that the mode of annotation must be personal, that is, it must have a precise relationship with the writer: it is also necessary that it be in a rigorous relationship with the work to be carried out. You have a precise subject; think intensely about it; let your spirit outline, if necessary, a provisional plan according to which you will direct your readings and reflections, according to which you will also annotate this or that which will fulfill your needs. Claude Bernard declared that a scientific observation is an answer to a question posed by the mind, and that only what is sought is actually found. Similarly, intelligent reading is a possible response to the question posed in us by the subject to be treated; it is therefore necessary to read very carefully, just as we observe, at the exit of a station, the wave of travelers waiting for a friend.

May reading therefore be increasingly biased; may it take into account not only vocation and the person, but their current application. Such reading is a priori; now, an a priori is like a sieve that retains the desired grain and lets the others pass. Do not get distracted; do not linger; have only your goal in mind, without any consideration for the author’s, who may be something completely different. I dare say, despite the unpleasantness and contraindication this expression may have in almost all cases: put blinkers on to better concentrate on what currently demands all your attention.

Here are two slightly different procedures, which perhaps it is best to use alternately, according to the nature of the work.

You can establish a detailed plan and only document yourself afterwards. You can start with documentation, with reflections and readings that obviously presuppose some guidelines, but without a proper plan. You will then walk around the subject, considering all its aspects, conducting surveys that aim not to leave anything unexplored in it; ideas for a plan will come to you and you will jot them down, as Pascal did when he wrote above a fragment: “order;” you will keep the documents to be used without modifying them; you will fix the ideas that you should develop, of which you will only note, if they appear, the main characteristics; you will mention the right words, the interesting comparisons that arise; sometimes you will write a whole passage, not with the intention of giving it a definitive form, but because it came by itself and because inspiration is like grace, which passes and does not return anymore.

When you think you’ve exhausted the subject, I mean in relation to what you intend or expect, your work is ready; the construction site is full of materials, some of which are formless, others temporarily carved. We will talk soon about construction, but one can already see that here the plan will proceed from the materials themselves, not the materials from the plan.

This last procedure, which seems the least logical, and in fact it is, speaking abstractly, has the advantage of leaving you freer in your reflections and preliminary studies, of favoring your inspiration, of keeping you in joy, because you are not obliged to seek very distinctly, because you can go, come, defer, await the favorable disposition and do a viscous work, without mental coercion.

A work can thus be finished without having been begun; all its value is determined by its notes; the plan is found in them in a latent state, a drawer plan, as architects say, that is, in which various combinations are possible; but the material is already gathered, mastered, and you are sure that, once the plan is established, it will correspond to a real conception, to ideas that you possess, not to ideas that you still have to seek, that it will not therefore be an arbitrary scheme, a system of fields to fill in, often having, to put into it, nothing fertile, spontaneous, and therefore nothing alive.

The notes thus understood, study notes, notes of inspiration, cannot be made in “idle hours;” they are effective work, and we must reserve their research for what we call moments of fullness. The other notes, without escaping the obligation of effort, will sometimes have the character of a happy find, of a chance. However, the best ones will be those that a deep study impels you to gather and store as the wealth of a whole life.

2. HOW TO CLASSIFY YOUR NOTES

The notes taken, assuming that they are considered to be useful in the future, should be classified. In industry, the order is money, and how much money! In science, it’s about thought. It’s useless to jot down if you can’t find at the right moment what would then be like a buried treasure. Keeping track of your readings and reflections, collecting documents, is very good, but only if it allows you to flip through your favorite author whenever you want and also flip through yourself.

It is necessary to guard against a certain collector spirit that often takes hold of those who work with notes. They want to fill their notebooks or files; they are in a hurry to complete the gaps, and they stack texts as if filling a stamp album or postcard collection. This practice is deplorable; it is childish; there is a risk of becoming a maniac. Order is a necessity, but it is at our service, not us at its service. Insisting on accumulating, completing, and being distracted from producing and even learning; excessive concern with classification becomes more important than the use of what we gather; now, everything here must be subordinated to the good of the work.

How to classify your notes? Famous men adopted diverse systems. The best, in the end, is the one we experiment with, confront with our needs and intellectual habits, and consecrate through long practice.

The registration system in which notes are written or pasted in a row is quite defective, as it does not allow any classification, even with the resource of leaving blank spaces, whose measure cannot be determined. Different records for each subject correct this inconvenience a little, but they also do not allow for precise classification, besides being poorly suited for use when writing.

One can have folders made of cardboard with a title, and put the notes of a certain category in each one. A set of these folders, corresponding to a more general title, can be placed in a gap of a bookshelf, which will have affixed, if not the title itself, which perhaps one might prefer not to leave so boldly displayed, at least an order number that corresponds to an index that the worker will always have at hand.

But the most practical method of all for most jobs, it seems to me, is the method of cards. Take cards made of a sufficiently consistent paper, with a uniform size, which you will determine according to the average length of your notes. Nothing will prevent you from continuing a citation started on one card on a second card. Your cards will be accurately cut by the machine, a task that any bookbinder or printer will do in five minutes, and which specialized shops spare you by offering cards of all sizes, in various colors, as well as filing systems and other necessary accessories.

Furthermore, if your collection of notes is large, you will need binders, a piece of furniture with drawers of adequate size. You will also need specially marked cards or dividers to visibly indicate the categories, after marking each card in the corner or in the middle.

Having done this preparatory work, here is how you should proceed.

When taking a note during a reading, or reflecting on the work, or in bed, etc., do it on a card or, if you don’t have one at hand, on a smaller piece of paper than it, on one side only, in order to stick it later on a card. After writing it, classify it, unless you decide to wait for a while, as I advised above.

To classify presupposes that you have already adopted a carefully chosen classification method according to your work. I can only give general indications here. Each person should create, if necessary, a subject catalog, with divisions and subdivisions, on which they already have or intend to gather notes. A very ingenious system, called the decimal system, can be applied to all kinds of research; you can find a clear explanation of it in Dr. Chavigny’s interesting brochure on the Organization of Intellectual Work.[67](part0018.xhtml#a1HR)

If you want to avoid complications, which are indeed a serious inconvenience, make use of your own experience to establish a classification system. In this, it is necessary to be practical and not to amuse oneself by establishing a priori divisions, which would serve no purpose.

Following the catalog, if there is one, each division or subdivision with its letter or ordinal number, you can file your cards. Once they are organized, it will be easy to find them when it’s time to work.

3. HOW TO USE YOUR NOTES

We’ve reached the moment to utilize the documentation. You made your immediate harvest, notes taken in view precisely of the current work; you also have in reserve, not yet collected, old notes that relate more or less directly to it. Gather everything, referring, if necessary, to the catalog and the indications it provides you. Then, as we explained above, two paths open up.

If you have a detailed plan and have organized or researched your notes accordingly, number the successive articles of the plan; then number (lightly and in pencil if the notes will be used on other occasions) the cards that concern them; gather the cards containing the same number; put a paper clip on each group, classify the groups, and then write the paper by placing in front of you, successively, the content of each group.

If, on the contrary, you prepared your work without having formulated a plan, only with guidelines, it is now a matter of establishing the plan; you must extract it from the documentation itself. To do this, here’s how you can proceed. All the index cards are shuffled; take them one by one and write down on a sheet, in sequence, their content, using propositions as brief as possible. After exhausting your stock, you will have before you the ideas you have. Go through them, observing their relationships and dependencies; mentally extract the main ideas; place under them what concerns them; use for this a marginal numbering that can be corrected as many times as necessary. Little by little, light will be shed, and an order will be established in this confused mass.

After doing that, copy your propositions in the obtained order, following the numbering. If there are gaps in your plan, fill them: you will, if necessary, conduct supplementary research on the subject; mark with a corresponding number for each theme the index cards related to it; classify and group as we said earlier, and your writing will be prepared.
