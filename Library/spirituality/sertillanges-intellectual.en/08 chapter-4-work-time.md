## Chapter 4. Work Time {#sertillanges-intellectual.4}
### 1. Permanent Work {#sertillanges-intellectual.4.1}
We have already qualified intellectual labor in various ways; we now need to observe more closely its various conditions and above all the time that the thinker devotes to it.

The study has already been called a prayer to truth. Now, prayer, says the Gospel, should never be interrupted: “to pray always and not to lose heart.”[31](part0018.xhtml#a1CJ) I know that this text can be read broadly; it would mean then: do not let a day, a week, a long period pass without addressing oneself to God. But our doctors always avoided interpreting such great advice narrowly; they took it literally and extracted from it a profound doctrine.

Prayer is the expression of a desire; its value comes from our inner aspiration, from its content and its strength. Take away the desire, and the prayer dissolves; change it, and it modifies itself; strengthen or weaken its impulse, and the prayer takes flight or loses its wings. Conversely, suppress the expression preserving the desire, and the prayer, in many aspects, remains intact. Does a child who says nothing but fixes a burning gaze on a toy in a shop window, and then looks at the smiling mother, not formulate the most moving prayer? Even without seeing anything, the desire for the toy, innate in the child like the urge to act, is not for them a permanent prayer that they answer?

“To pray always” therefore amounts to saying: one must always desire eternal things, the things of time that favor them, the daily bread of every nature and every opportunity, life in all its splendors, earthly and celestial.

Apply this comment to the active clause of study and you will enter extremely valuable considerations. The thinker is renowned; however, he is a thinker in activity only for a few hours. Carlyle used to say, “I think no man of letters consecrated literature a fifth of his time.” Since most of his life passes at a medium or low level, it is necessary for the man of heights to descend and incline himself: what profit, if he manages not to bend completely!

Since prayer can last all the time, since it is a desire and desire remains, why couldn’t study last all the time, being also a desire and an appeal to truth?

The desire for knowledge defines our intelligence as a vital power. Instinctively, we want to know, as we want bread. If most men cling to more earthly desires, the thinker has this particularity: the desire to know absorbs him. Why not make this desire work, make it work, I say, constantly, like a stream in which turbines have been installed?

It is possible to do so, and psychology proves it, as well as experience. The brain works all the time; the turbines I demand exist, rotate, drive a gear system from which ideas emerge like sparks from a dynamo at its maximum output. Nervous processes are linked in a continuous series and do not stop, like the movements of the heart and the breath in our lungs. What is lacking to use this permanent life in favor of truth? Only discipline. The dynamos must be connected to the turbines, the turbines to the current; the desire to know must regularly activate, not occasionally, the conscious and unconscious cerebral functioning.

Most of our nervous activity is useless, because it is not captured. In fact, we cannot capture it all, because our power over it is relative and, if we force its output, we will break the machine; but what is possible is only desired by very few; habit has a great weight in this; if well assembled, this machine will act like a second nature, and it is here that our practical advice comes in.

“Everything you can,” says Saint Thomas to the man of studies, “seek to preserve in the ark of the spirit, as one who longs to fill a pitcher.” We will return to this comparison, which could generate misunderstandings; but here it is a matter of the care in acquiring, and not of the way to do it. What matters to the man of truth is to understand that truth is everywhere, and that he does not fail to take advantage of this continuous flow to activate his soul.

“Wisdom cries out in the street;” says the Bible; “in the squares she raises her voice. At the busiest corner she cries out; at the entrance of the city gates she speaks: “How long, O simple ones, will you love being simple? . . . Give heed to my reproof; I will pour out my thoughts to you. . . . I have called and you refused, have stretched out my hand and no one heeded.”[32](part0018.xhtml#a1HK) This vehement appeal of truth, when heeded, enlarges a spirit and enriches it more than many hours of labor. These remain necessary; but the light concentrated in them expands so much that it covers almost all of life; a current arises that illuminates the results of diffuse thought and returns to it to provide it with a habitual direction, and therefore fertility.

See what happens when you want to furnish an apartment. Until then, you didn’t think about furniture to the point that, walking through the streets of Paris, where, out of every four shops, one is an antique shop, you didn’t even see them; their shapes didn’t catch your attention; you were unaware of fashion trends, the chances of finding something interesting, the specialty of each block, prices, etc. On the contrary, when your spirit is awakened by desire, everything attracts you, everything holds you back; Paris becomes an immense magazine, and you discover in eight days what would take a lifetime to learn.

The truth is more widespread than furniture. It “cries out in the streets” and does not abandon us when we abandon it. Ideas are in the facts; they are also in conversations, in chance encounters, in spectacles, in visits and outings, in the most mundane readings. Everything contains treasures because everything is in everything, and a certain number of laws of life or nature govern everything else.

Newton would have discovered gravity if his attention to the real had not warned him and prepared him to realize that apples fall like worlds? The laws of gravity of the spirits, sociological, philosophical, moral, and artistic laws are also applicable to everything. A great thought can arise from any fact. In every contemplation, be it of a fly or a passing cloud, there is an opportunity for infinite reflections. Any ray of light can lead to the Sun; any open path is a corridor that leads to God.

Now, we could capture these riches if we were present. Looking at everything in a spirit of inspiration, we would see in everything lessons, prophecies of truth or confirmations, omens and consequences. But generally, we are not present, or we do not pay attention. “Everyone looks at what I look at,” said Lamennais to Saint-Malo, “in front of a stormy sea; but no one sees what I see.”

Create the habit of being present in this game of the material and moral universe. Learn to see; confront what is offered to you with your familiar or secret ideas. Do not see in a city only its houses, but human life and history. Let a museum not present you with pictures, but schools of art and life, conceptions of destiny and nature, successive or diverse orientations of technique, inspiring thought, and feelings. Let a workshop not only speak to you of iron and wood, but of the human condition, work, ancient and modern economy, and the relations between classes. Let travels teach you about humanity; let landscapes evoke to your eyes the great laws of the world; let the stars speak to you of immeasurable durations; let the pebbles of the road be for you the residue of the formation of the Earth; let the sight of a family awaken in you the vision of generations, and let a simple visit teach you about the highest conception of man. If you cannot see in this way, you will not become or will no longer be more than a banal spirit. A thinker is a filter through which the passage of truths leaves its best substance.

Learn to listen, and always listen, to whomever it may be. If it’s in the markets, as Malherbe wished, that one learns their own language, it’s also in the markets, or rather, in everyday life, that one learns the language of the spirit. A multitude of truths circulate in the simplest speeches. A single word heard with attention can be an oracle. A peasant is sometimes much wiser than a philosopher. All men unite when they delve deep into themselves; and if some profound impression, an instinctive or virtuous return to original simplicity, dispels conventions, the passions that normally divert us from ourselves or others, we always hear a divine discourse when a man speaks to us.

In each man is all of mankind, and a deep initiation can come to us from there. If you’re a novelist, don’t you feel what you can extract from it? The greatest of novelists is formed on the threshold of doors; the smallest, in the Sorbonne or in the salons. Only, instead of mingling, the great observer preserves himself, lives within himself, rises, and the humblest life appears to him as a grand spectacle.

Well, what the novelist seeks can serve everyone, for everyone needs that profound experience. The thinker is not truly a thinker if he does not find in the lightest external stimulus the occasion for an endless impulse. His character consists in keeping throughout his life the curiosity of childhood, the liveliness of his impressions, his tendency to see everything from the angle of mystery, his fortunate faculty of finding in everything fruitful surprises.

And be especially attentive when you have the happiness of conversing with someone who knows and thinks. How sad to see elite men so little useful to their acquaintances! They are practically identified with the simpletons; one takes from them what they have in common, not what is rare in them. There is a treasure in them, and one plays with the key instead of opening it. Sometimes one laughs at their lack of skill, at the little peculiarities of those who live in abstractions, and this is nothing more; but what is foolish is to take on an air of superiority and ignore the greatness within them.

The great values are too rare to be left unused like this. They benefit themselves, and everyone benefits from them without realizing it; but whoever realizes it can receive an instruction and an impulse that may determine an entire existence. Many saints, many leaders, explorers, scientists, artists, became what they were because they found an eminent personality and listened to the voice of a soul. The echoes of this silent call resonated in them until the end of their lives, and it was a cry that propelled them forward; an invisible current carried them. The word of a great man, like that of God, is often creative.

It is said that great men are only great after they die. Most do not recognize them. Perhaps someone who has the valor of a Descartes is sitting next to you, and you do not listen to him, do not question him, argue with a bellicose spirit, cut him off to say nonsense. And even if he does not have such stature, but is a great spirit, why do you let him hide or silently carry his wealth?

Observing and listening (I don’t speak of readings because we’ll come back to them), you will learn to reflect, make it your own, and adapt to your needs what you have acquired. Great discoveries are the result of reflection on facts common to all. Many have gone through something thousands of times without seeing anything, and one day, the genius observes the relationships between what we ignore and what was right in front of our faces minutes ago. What is science if not the slow and successive cure of our blindness? It is true that observation must be prepared by previous studies and solutions. Whoever seeks, finds. To those who have, more will be given. That’s why I spoke of a back-and-forth between inner and outer lights. The spirit must be in a perpetual disposition to reflect, and in a perpetual disposition to see, to hear, to catch in full flight, like the good hunter, the bird that appears.

Let’s be even more precise and say that this awakening of the spirit can be beneficial not only for culture in general, but also for our specialty, for our current study, for the work we are developing. Take your problems with you. The rented horse makes its journey and returns to the stable; the free steed always has its nostrils to the wind.

Since truth is everywhere and everything is related, why not study each question in contact with what is closest to it? Everything should nourish our specialty. Everything should testify for or against our theses. The universe is largely what we make of it. The painter sees forms, colors, movements, expressions everywhere; the architect, balance of masses; the musician perceives rhythms and sounds; the poet, occasions for metaphors; a thinker, ideas in action.

There’s no narrow particularism in that; it’s a matter of method. One can’t embrace everything. By reserving a space for free observation, we devote to a particular investigation the attention that remains to us, and “always thinking about it,” as Newton did, we gather the elements for a work.

Always keep your mind attentive: that’s the great secret. The spirit of man is a ruminant. The animal looks into the distance, chews slowly, gathers a tuft here, a leaf there, takes the whole field for itself, and also the horizon, composing with one its milk, with the other its obscure soul.

They teach us to live in the presence of God; can we not also live in the presence of truth? Truth is like the special divinity of the thinker. A certain particular truth or a certain object of study may always be before us. Would it be wise, would it be normal, to let the studious man remain in his study, and thus have two souls: that of the worker and that of the bon vivant who circulates? This dualism is not natural; it leads one to think that the pursuit of truth is for us a profession, and not a noble passion.

There is a time for everything, says the Bible,[33](part0018.xhtml#a1H1) and I agree that we cannot avoid division; but since, in fact, we think all the time, why not use that thinking to benefit what troubles us?

Will they say that such tension is incompatible with brain health and life conditions? Very well; but it’s also not about tension, not even, ordinarily, about the current will. I spoke of habit; let’s speak, if you want, of subconsciousness. Our spirit has the power to function without us, as long as we prepare its task a little and lightly trace the path of the channels in which its obscure currents will flow.

If the desire to know is well-rooted in you, if the passion for truth is burning, if your conscious attention is frequently turned to the facts of life that can feed this fire and satisfy that desire, you will make your spirit a predator in a perpetual hunt. This will no longer be costly; it will obey a new nature. It will think as easily in one specific direction as it once thought at random.

That direction is not, without a doubt, absolute, and excessive tension would be absurd; but is it convenient to refuse what we can using as an excuse what we cannot? This is an immense resource; you will employ it by establishing a little discipline in cerebral work that already occurs, but despite you and in an anarchic way. Structure this work, and your brain will also become an intellectual.

Doing so, one realizes that it is not at all tiring, and, on the contrary, it saves a lot of effort; the findings made like this, at the whim of the gaze, without having been sought, simply because we have decided and trained ourselves not to be blind, these inventions, often the happiest because they are spontaneous, encourage the scholar greatly; they keep him attentive and enthusiastic; he looks forward joyfully to the hour of retreat when he can consolidate and develop his acquisitions.

Many times, we will thus obtain the difficult chaining, the path we sought in vain before the desk, limited by a point of view from which we could not escape. What had no direct relation to the work led to something that was in the depths. Laborious science will thus be suddenly enlightened; we will know where we are going and soon expect a new surprise.

This procedure based on chance harmonizes with the cerebral contingencies and with the obscure work of the association of ideas. A multitude of laws come into play here, without there being a law that can be applied in this or that case, at this or that time, and all this happening independently of us—I mean, without an act of our will, only under the influence of the desire that exists in the soul of the thinker and that qualifies him, as play qualifies the child, as love qualifies the woman—, which does not require the overload that is imagined.

Does a woman tire, during a stroll, of noticing the tributes she receives, or does a young woman tire of seeking moments of joy, or does a boy tire of running and jumping? The spirit that seeks truth out of love, not out of obligation, out of a primarily instinctive tendency, then certainly cultivated, but lovingly, passionately, also will not tire. It plays, hunts, engages in a useful and intoxicating sport; it loves, and nothing is further from this than the distinct and voluntary effort of hours of concentration.

Thus the wise man walks through all times and in all paths with a spirit prepared for the acquisitions that the common people neglect. The darkest of occupations is for him the extension of the most sublime of them; his social visits are rich investigations, his strolls, explorations, his hearings and his silent responses are a dialogue that within himself truth holds with itself. In all places his inner universe confronts the other, his life with Life, his work with the incessant work of beings, and when he leaves the narrow space in which his study is concentrated, one has the impression not that he abandons truth, but that he opens the doors for the world to bring him all the truth that lives amidst its turmoil.

### 2. Night Work {#sertillanges-intellectual.4.2}
Father Gratry recommended with insistence that one should not exclude from permanent work the hours of lethargy and darkness. He wanted us to make the night work. This advice is based on psychology and experience.

Sleep is a relaxation; it is the abdication of conscious desire that no longer thinks about living, does not propose any objective, and is thus largely surrendered to the general nature. It is not a gratuitous symbol for man to sleep lying down, close to the earth, as if to say to nature: “Receive me; I have already fought enough against your powers; I have fought, standing, against your leveling determinism; to the equalization of forces, which is the law of this perishable world, I opposed the upheaval of life; I surrender now, until the time to resume the fight.”

Having life thus suspended, with the motor’s transmission belt having passed from individual freedom to the freedom of cosmic forces, a new operation emerges that has its own laws, which follows paths unknown to the awakened consciousness and realizes combinations strange to conscious wills and whims. Our inner forces group themselves; our thoughts organize; interrelations are produced; the energy abandoned by action is employed effortlessly. Knowing how to use this work without disturbing its rhythms is a new wealth for the thinker.

It’s not about watching; on the contrary, the sleepwalker is a bad worker; we have already postulated regarding this point obedience to general hygiene, which should, for the studious man, be even more demanding. But sleep itself is a worker, a collaborator of daytime work; it is possible to domesticate its forces, to use its laws, to take advantage of this filtering, this clarification that occurs in the abandonment of the night.

A cerebral work begun, an idea sketched out, an idea that an internal or external incident had prevented from fully emerging or finding its natural place, now develops and gears up: do not miss this opportunity; gather, before it plunges back into mental darkness, this clarity that can be of help to you.

How to seize it? On certain occasions, no special procedure is necessary. Upon awakening, we find already prepared and well recorded the collaboration of sleep. The work of wakefulness arises under a clearer light; a new path, a still virgin region opens before us; relationships of ideas, of facts, of expressions, a happy comparison, a luminous image, a whole passage, perhaps, or even an entire plan of realization will have emerged. The whole presents itself, quite clear; it will be enough to use, in due time, what Hypnos deigned to do for you.

But normally that’s not what happens. Nature isn’t at our beck and call; it follows its path; its river holds gold, but it’s up to us to gather and not let what its opulent currents carry be lost.

Frequently lights will shine during a few minutes of insomnia, or even for a second: it is necessary to fix them. Entrusting them to the relaxed brain is like imprinting them on water; there is a great chance that in the morning not even the trace of a vague incident will remain.

Then do something better. Have at hand a notepad or a box of index cards. Make a note without awakening too much, without turning on the light, if possible, and then return to your shadows. Thus, relieving the mind may even favor sleep instead of disturbing it. If you say, “I will remember, I want to remember,” that will is more an enemy of sleep than a quick scribble. Remember that sleep is a relaxation of wanting.

On other occasions, it will be in the morning, upon waking up, that the lights will come. You open your eyes, and it’s as if the inner gaze also opens, illuminating a new world. The Earth has turned; the skies of intelligence do not have the same aspect; new constellations shine. Look closely at this unprecedented spectacle and do not delay in outlining it in general lines; note its most expressive features, its contours, enough to expose the details when you have time to come back to it.

Every thinker experiences these moments of morning lucidity, sometimes surprising, we would even say miraculous. Complete treatises have come to light thus, after a long and painful series of intricate studies, in which the author had the feeling of being lost in a forest, without clearings or perspectives.

Inventions were made like this. Dispersed elements in the mind, old experiences or knowledge that apparently had no interest associated, and questions were suddenly solved, by the spontaneous organization of mental images that represented the idea of their solution.

Go quickly to the notebook when such a happiness presents itself. Seize it while the idea is present; extract, do not add anything on your own. Without any disturbing intervention, with a submissive attention to the nature of the work you are doing, gently pull the chain that has formed, map its links, the small accessory chains that come out of it, mark the proportions, the dependencies, without any concern for style—I refer to a studied style, because it may happen that there are precious elements of style in this spontaneous record.

When the source is dry and it seems like the current of new thoughts has been entirely extracted, stop writing, but do not cease to fix your eyes on its richness for some time: it may be that it still grows, that the current brings new links, that the little chains multiply and subdivide. All this is so precious that no portion should be lost. It is work saved for your day. The night, an excellent collaborator, gifted you without any effort on your part with a journey of twenty-four complete hours, of weeks, perhaps, that would be necessary to polish with an effort of will the luxurious jewel that it gave you.

However, the care of collecting is not enough. Sleep, which works on its own, works on preexisting material; it creates nothing; skilled in combining and simplifying, in completing, it can only operate on the data of experience and on daily work. It is necessary, therefore, to prepare its task. To rely on it is to rely first on oneself.

The monks have the habit, as ancient as piety itself, of placing, at the end of their day, like a seed, in the furrows of the night, their point of meditation; they hope to find, upon awakening, the seed already softened, penetrated by the moisture of the earth and perhaps germinated: it will grow more quickly in the soil of reflection and grace.

Without renouncing this practice, which deserved to be generalized among Christians, we can add to it the sowing of the night through labor. Human soil is rich: two seeds will coexist without conflicts. Take with you, when you go to sleep, and entrust to God and the soul the issue that concerns you, the idea that delays unfolding its potentialities, or even remains inert. Make no effort to delay sleep. On the contrary, rest in this thought: the universe works for me; determinism is the slave of freedom and, while I rest, its mill will turn; I can dispense with my effort: the heavens revolve, and by revolving they cause delicate gears to move in my brain that I might falsify; I sleep, nature watches, God watches, and tomorrow I will gather a little of His work.

In this calm disposition, he had fully relaxed, more than in the restlessness of a tomorrow with no help at all, especially more than in that return, so frequent at night, of the torments of the journey, torments that a semiconsciousness intensifies, that poison the night, and that in the morning will still be there to serve him his portion of bitterness.

Just as sweet and regular work harmonizes the day, the unconscious work of the night can pour peace into it and ward off digressions, exhausting and sinful madness, nightmares. Take a child gently by the hand: their turbulence calms down. Therefore, I am not advocating an overload, the confusion of day with night. No, sleep is necessary; restorative sleep is indispensable. But I tell you that the night, while night, can work by itself, that it “advises;” that sleep, while sleep, is a useful artisan; that rest, while rest, is also a force. And according to its own nature, and not by violence to its constitution, we must employ its aids. Rest is not death; it is life, and all life yields its fruit. Being able to reap it yourself, do not leave the fruits of sleep to the night birds.

### 3. Mornings and Nights {#sertillanges-intellectual.4.3}
Give extreme importance, both to the worker and to the religious, to the beginning and end of the days. We cannot prepare, watch over, conclude the hours of rest with an attentive soul if we leave to chance those that are contiguous to them.

The morning is sacred; in the morning, the renewed soul looks at life as from a curve from where it sees the whole road. Destiny is on the horizon; our task is resumed; and the moment to evaluate it once again and to confirm, by an expressed act, our triple vocation as men, as Christians, and as intellectuals.

“Philip, remember that you are a man.” This phrase that the Macedonian slave said to his master is spoken to us by day when, opening our eyes, it evokes the lights of the soul; “a man,” I say, not in general, but qualified for a precise task, a man who is there, before God, as a singular, original fact, and, however small it may be, the only one who can occupy his own place.

This man, when leaving the hours of unconsciousness, will he not, renewed and as if reborn, consider the whole of his life with a quick glance, mark the point where he stands, compose the journey that begins and walk resolutely, with the spirit enlightened for his new stage?

Such will be the combined effort of awakening, morning prayer, meditation, and above all the Mass, if there is the possibility of attending it or the joy of celebrating it.

Awakening must be sursum corda! Saying a Christian formula at this moment is an excellent practice; saying it out loud is even better, for, as psychologists know, our voice suggests to us and plays the role of a double for us. This is a “slave” that we cannot neglect; it has authority over the self, and the self, and its voice sounds with the strange authority of someone who is simultaneously himself and another.

We teach children to “give their hearts to God;” the intellectual, who is also a child in this, must also give his heart to truth, must remember that he is its servant, repudiate the enemies of truth who live within him, love, so that its external enemies return to it, and consent to the efforts that truth asks of him for the current journey.

Follows the prayer. Father Gratry advises the intellectual to say the prima,[34](part0018.xhtml#a1H0) with compline as a complement; nothing more beautiful, indeed, nothing more effective, more auspicious. Most liturgical prayers are masterpieces; they are as broad and sweet as the sunrise and sunset. Try it: you won’t be able to say anything else about them. All true life is there, all nature, and work will be thus prepared as if it were a journey begun in a bay flooded with sunlight.

Whatever your choice, the intellectual’s prayer should underline in passing what relates to his situation, draw from it a benefit, and thus compose the good purpose that Christian work accomplishes: an act of faith in the lofty truths that uphold science; an act of hope in divine aid to illuminate and sustain in virtues; an act of love for the One who is infinitely lovable and those whom our study seeks to bring closer to Him; an Our Father, in order to request along with bread the nourishment of intelligence; a Hail Mary, directed to the Woman clothed with the Sun, victorious against error and against evil. In these and other formulas, the intellectual finds himself again, evokes his task, and, without separating his specialty from Christian life as a whole, can benefit from what is provided for him and providentially deposited in the common treasury.

Meditation is so essential to the thinker that it is not necessary to return to its praise. We have already praised the spirit of prayer; where will it be better nourished than in these morning contemplations, in which the relaxed spirit, not yet taken by the cares of the day, conducted, elevated by the wings of prayer, easily ascends to those sources of truth that study captures with so much difficulty?

If you can attend the holy mass, if you can celebrate it, will you not be carried away by the vastness it contains? Will you not see, from the top of the Calvary raised again, from the top of the Cenacle where the last supper is renewed, humanity gathering around you, that humanity with which you cannot lose contact, that life that the words of the Savior illuminate, that indigence that His wealth helps and that you must help Him to help, enlighten, save, thus saving yourself?

The mass truly places you in a state of eternity, in the spirit of the universal Church, and in the “Ite missa est,” you will be ready to see a mission, a sending forth of your zeal to the nakedness of the ignorant and alienated Earth.

The morning imbued with all this dew, renewed and vivified by these spiritual breezes, cannot help but be fruitful; approach it with faith; proceed with courage; the journey will consume the light provisions of dawn; night will come before the clarities are exhausted, as the year ends leaving in the barns the seeds for the next year.

The night! How little we commonly know to sanctify it, appease it, shape it for a restorative sleep! How it is wasted and polluted, how it is disoriented!

About what men who live for pleasures make of her, let’s not insist: her case is not ours. But look at these serious people we call workers: merchants, industrialists, civil servants, big traders—I’m talking about most of them. When night comes, here they are “free from brakes” and, without thinking of anything else, surrendering their spirits to dissipation that supposedly relaxes, dining, smoking, playing, talking loudly, rushing to the theater or bars, yawning at the cinema and throwing themselves on the bed “rested.”

Refreshed, indeed, but like the violin whose strings have all been loosened. What difficulty, the next day, to tune them again!

I know industrialists who rest by reading Pascal, Montaigne, Ronsard, Racine. Accommodated in a good armchair, with good lighting, warmed, with the family in silence or conversing gently around them, they live, after having suffered. This moment is just for them; this is the moment of the man, after the specialist has struggled, with the head and the heart, against twenty obstacles.

An intellectual, if he does not have need of this compensation, has great need of this calm. His evening must be a withdrawal, his dinner a light meal, his amusement the easy arrangement of the work of that day and the preparation of the work of the following day. He needs his compline—I take them here in the figurative sense—those which complete and those which inaugurate; for every complement of a continuous work, as we require, is at the same time a beginning and an end. We close to reopen. The night is the organ of connection between the daytime fragments whose total makes up a life. In the morning, it is necessary to start living right away: we must prepare ourselves for it at night, and prepare for the sleep that will come to unify, in its own way, the conscious labors.

No matter what the passionate and interested illusion of those who think that man must preserve something of the bon-vivant; dissipation is not rest, it is exhaustion. Rest cannot consist in the dissemination of forces. Rest is a retreat away from effort, towards its sources; it is restoration, not senseless waste.

I know well that spending is sometimes acquiring: it’s then about sports, recreation, and we must demand, not just tolerate, this active relaxation. But that’s not the normal business of the night. For the night, there is a double rest, a spiritual one, and a physical one: rest in God and rest in maternal nature. Now, the first is given by prayer; as for the other, the rest of the body, since it precedes the more complete rest of sleep, must lead to this.

We must surrender ourselves, at night, to the gentle rhythms whose model is nocturnal breathing. Let the easy determinisms operate in us, let habits replace initiatives, let family companionship replace the effort of intense activity, in a word, let go of wanting, in a way, so that the renunciation of the night begins: this is wisdom. And wisdom will be recognized in the structure of this attenuated life, of this semi-activity that is calming down. The family will have its part in this; a gentle conversation will seal the union of souls; one speaks of the impressions received, of the projects conceived; visions, purposes are confirmed; solace is found for the decline of the day; harmony reigns, and a worthy vigil will be celebrated for the feast that each new day must be for the Christian.

A sleeping person often remains, without knowing it, in the position they were once in their mother’s womb. It is a symbol. Rest is a return to origins: origins of life, origins of strength, origins of inspiration; it is a renewal; the general gathering of the night has that significance. Now, revitalizing oneself cannot be to stir oneself up; it is to shelter oneself, to provide human sap, through peaceful concentration, a renewal of one’s vigor; it is to restore in us organic life and sacred life through good relaxation, prayer, silence, and sleep.

### 4. The Moments of Fulfillment {#sertillanges-intellectual.4.4}
We now arrive at what is no longer preparation, prolongation, utilitarian relaxation, rest aimed at work, but work itself and time dedicated to studious concentration, to full effort. Therefore, we will call these peaks of our intellectual life considered from the aspect of its duration “the moments of fulfillment.”

Most of this pamphlet aims to determine the use of this time; we must therefore treat here to structure it in itself, to adjust it, to preserve it, to protect the “inner cell” from the invasion that threatens it.

As moments of our life have very unequal value, and for each one of us the sharing of these values obeys different laws, we cannot establish an absolute rule; but we must insist on the theme. Study yourself, take into account what your life is, what it allows you, what favors or forbids, what it itself proposes for your moments of fervor.

They will take place in the morning, at night, a little in the morning and a little at night. Only you can decide, because only you know your obligations and your nature, on which the structure of your journeys depends.

When someone has only a few hours available and they can be freely arranged, it seems that the morning should be preferred. Sleep has repaired their strength; prayer has given them wings; peace reigns around them and the swarm of distractions has not yet begun. But, for some, there may be contraindications. If sleep is difficult, the morning period can be anxious and numb. Or else solitude is lacking; one then awaits the hours of isolation.

Be that as it may, once the choice is made, it is a matter of organizing the chosen moments and organizing oneself to fully explore them. Everything must be foreseen so that nothing hinders, dissipates, reduces, or weakens this precious time. Aim for its fullness; exclude distant preparations; make all useful arrangements; know what you intend to do, and how; gather the materials, notes, books; do not be disturbed by trifles.

Furthermore, so that this time is preserved and truly free, let its awakening be prompt, punctual; its nourishment, light. Avoid empty conversations, useless visits; limit correspondence to strictly necessary; just skim through the newspapers. These prescriptions, which we have already given for the safeguarding of all study life, apply especially to what is its center.

With everything thus arranged, everything foreseen, soon you will be ready for work; you will be able to dedicate yourself entirely to it, absorb yourself and carry it forward; your attention will not be distracted, your effort will not be divided. Above all, flee from work done halfway. Do not imitate those who spend a long time at the desk with loose attention. It is better to restrict the time and use it in depth, increasing its value, which is what matters.

Do something or do nothing. Whatever you decide to do, do it ardently, with diligence, so that the whole of your activity is a series of vigorous renewals. Half-hearted work, which is half rest, favors neither rest nor study.

Then invoke inspiration. If the goddess does not always respond, she is always sensitive to sincere efforts. It is not a matter of tensing too much, but of orienting oneself, aiming at the goal and removing from sight, like a shooter, everything that is not the target. Renew the spirit of prayer; be in a state of eternity, the heart submissive to truth, the spirit under the great laws, the imagination open like a wing, your whole being feeling above the silent stars, even during the day when they are not visible. Beneath your feet, down there, will be the noises of life; you will not even notice them, you will only hear the song of the spheres, which in the dream of Scipio symbolize the harmony of creative forces.

To open up to truth like this, to abstract from all else, and, if you permit me the expression, to buy a ticket to another world, that is the true work. That’s what we mean when we say that two hours a day are enough for a work. Obviously, it’s little, but if all conditions are fulfilled, that indeed suffices and is worth more than the alleged fifteen hours that so many chatterboxes boast about.

Certain executioners of work have even reached fabulous figures; their case is what we could call a happy monstrosity, unless it is a ruinous madness. Normal workers estimate that we can dedicate, continuously and truly fruitfully, from two to six hours. The main question is not there, it is in the use of time, it is in the spirit.

Whoever knows the value of time always has as much as they need; unable to increase it, they elevate it, and above all, they do not shorten it. Time has a density, like gold; a strong coin, well-minted and of pure alloy, is worth more than tin expanded by the blows of the hammer. Not all that glitters is gold. Many are content with appearances, with tumultuous vanities, they talk without stopping and never work.

Note that a session of deep work cannot be more uniform than intellectual life as a whole. Proportionally, it has the same phases as it; it intensifies little by little, sometimes painfully; it reaches its maximum, then declines. It is a complete cycle, with its fresh morning, its burning noon, its evening. We must be the Joshua of this afternoon so that the battle, always too short, can continue.

We will return to the conditions of this protected clarity; for now, I only point out this: to defend solitude with a rigor that does not condescend to anything. If you have duties, give them their due time; if you have friends, arrange timely meetings; if inconvenient people come looking for you, kindly close the door to them.

It is essential, during these sacred hours, not only that you are not disturbed, but that you ensure you will not be; that you have complete certainty of this, allowing you a fruitful tension. A luxury of severe precautions will never be too much. Let Cerberus be at your door. Every external demand resonates within and can steal precious discoveries from your spirit. “When half-gods go, the gods arrive.”[35](part0018.xhtml#a1CZ)

Note only that this complete solitude, the only favorable climate for work, should not be taken materially. A presence can double your quietness instead of dissipating it. If you have by your side an equally ardent worker, a friend absorbed in some thought or occupation harmonious with yours, a chosen soul that understands your work, that joins it, that supports your effort with a silent tenderness and with an ardor inflamed by yours: this is no longer distraction, it is an aid.

On certain days, in public libraries, we feel the seclusion penetrating and enveloping us from all sides like an atmosphere. A religious impression subdues us; we dare not distract ourselves, nor is it possible to decline. The more we have around us these worshipers who render true worship in spirit and in truth, the more alone we will be before the only true one, the easier and more delightful contemplation will be to us.

The home of a young couple, where in the husband’s office we find either the desk or the wife’s work basket, where love knows how to hover and remain silent, letting its wings float in the wind of noble dreams and inspiration, is also an image of work. In the unity of life that a Christian marriage inaugurates, there is room for unity of thought and for the solitude that is necessary for it. The closer kindred souls are together, the better defended they will be from what is external.

After being well understood and well prepared, solitude must be obstinately defended. One must not listen to anyone, not to indiscreet friends, nor to unconscious relatives, nor to passersby, not even to charity. Charity cannot be extended to everyone all the time. You belong to truth: your devotion is for it. Except for cases that are not even discussed, nothing should prevail over your vocation.

The time of a thinker, when he truly employs it, is, strictly speaking, a universal charity; we do not appreciate it otherwise. The man of truth belongs to the human race as much as truth itself: do not fear to be selfish by isolating yourself for the sake of this sublime and universal benefactor of men.

Know how to act in such a way that you are affectionately absolved by those who are being neglected and who sometimes feel hurt by it. Buy your solitude; pay for your freedoms using delicate gestures and kind favors. The ideal is that your withdrawal is more beneficial to others than direct interaction. Or at least that it is not too burdensome for them. Pay the necessary price, and let your relative independence be balanced by your absolute dependence when duties present themselves.
