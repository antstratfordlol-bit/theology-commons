## Chapter 9. The Worker and the Man {#sertillanges-intellectual.9}
### 1. Keeping Contact with Life {#sertillanges-intellectual.9.1}
After having asked so much and, apparently, forged so many chains, would it be an irony to turn to the intellectual one last time and say to him: keep your soul free?

What matters most in life are not the knowledges, but the character; and character would be threatened if the man were, so to speak, below his work, oppressed by the rock of Sisyphus. There is another science beyond that which resides in memory: the science of living. Study must be an act of life, must be profitable for life, must be imbued with life. Of the two species of spirits, those who strive to know something and those who try to be someone, the palm always goes to the latter. Everything in knowledge is a sketch; the finished work is the man.

Certainly, intellectualism contributes to the sovereignty of man; but it is not enough. Besides morality, which includes religious life, various developments must be considered. We have already mentioned society, practical activity: let us add living with nature, caring for the home, the arts, gatherings, a dose of poetry, the worship of words, intelligent sports, public demonstrations.

The measure of all this is difficult to pinpoint: I hope the reader finds here at least the spirit of these decisions. It’s a secure hint, for thought and practice, to know how to appreciate the relative value of things.

The purpose of the study is to allow the expansion of our being: we cannot allow it to narrow us down. If art is man added to nature, science is nature added to man: in both cases, it is necessary to save man.

Pascal refuses to esteem the specialist who is nothing more than a specialist; he does not want that, when a man is among other men, they remember his book. “It is a bad sign,” he says, and he does not refer only to that spirit of comparative science that we have described, but thinks of the harmony of the human person.

It is always necessary to expand what we are; the philosopher must be a little poet, and the poet, a little philosopher; the craftsman must be a poet and philosopher sometimes, and no one will reproach him for that. The writer needs to have some practical occupation, and the man of practice must know how to write. Every specialist is first and foremost a person, and the essence of the person is beyond everything that is thought, beyond everything that is done.

One cannot comprehend destiny as one comprehends a particular thing; we open ourselves to it “with the flower of the spirit,” as Zoroaster says. Particular objectives do not give life its worth, nor do acts give action its value, nor does talent equal a broad intuition in which all existence is encompassed; the work does not equal its creator. Everything that is detached from its broader relationships becomes harmful, and it is only in our general environment that our soul blossoms.

Those who only think about their work, work poorly; they diminish themselves; they acquire a professional temperament that will turn into a fixation. The spirit must remain open, maintain contact with humanity and with the world, so that with each study session it is capable of a new flight.

We’ve already cited this quote from a rabbi: “When a bucket is full of nuts, we can still pour several measures of olive oil into it;” we apply it to tasks that support each other instead of repelling each other. Let’s now call technical work as a whole the nuts, to which we can add, without overburdening it—on the contrary, lightening it—, the gentle intellectual life, noble leisure, nature, art.

The very technical work will benefit from it. It greatly benefits from society, friendship, external action, and I have already explained the reasons for this. Here I am only extending this notion, whose scope is general. Are a visit to the Louvre, the hearing of the Heroic Symphony or Oedipus Rex, a stroll in Versailles in a golden autumn, the simple sight of a sunset, a patriotic celebration at the Trocadero or in the grand amphitheater of the Sorbonne, the Olympic games, a mystery in Jumièges or at the Orange Theater, a beautiful sermon in Notre-Dame, strange to a specialty, whatever it may be?

One who did not see its connections with all creative manifestations would understand the thought very poorly. Nature renews everything, rejuvenates every mature mind, opens paths, and suggests approaches that abstraction ignores. The tree is a teacher, and the meadow is as full of ideas as it is of anemones or daisies; the sky brings inspirations with its clouds and its stars; the mountains anchor our thoughts with their mass, and lofty meditations follow the currents of the rivers.

I met someone who, observing a fast torrent on a mountain, irresistibly elevated the idea of worlds, imagining those masses rushing with the same speed, under the dominion of the same laws, dependent on the same forces, thanks to the same God from whom everything emerges and to whom everything returns. Returning to his work, he felt encouraged by the unique Force, permeated by that Presence that is found everywhere, and immersed his obscure action in the communion of beings.

But you, demolished spirit, dried-up heart, you think you’re wasting time if you follow the torrents or walk among the flock of stars. The universe fills man with its glory and you ignore it. The evening star grows bored in its dark case, it wishes to dwell in thought, and you refuse it shelter. You write, calculate, line up propositions, and elaborate theses, and see nothing.

Who doesn’t know that during a concert the intellectual can be seized by an impression of grandeur, of beauty, of power, which soon infiltrates his way of being, enriches his objects of study, colors his usual themes and later provides him with a more fruitful work session? Won’t he hastily scribble on the back of his program, the outline of a chapter or a speech, an idea to be developed, a vivid image? Harmony raised the pitch of his inspiration, and the rhythm, in which he was abducted like a passerby by an army on the march, led him to new paths.

In Saint-Sulpice, in the Chapel of the Angels, Delacroix would deliciously immerse himself in the sounds of the grand organ and the religious chants; he attributed to this harmony the extraordinary execution of his Jacob Wrestling and the Knight of Heliodorus.

The music has this preciousness for the intellectual: it determines nothing, and therefore creates no obstacles. It provides only states of soul, which each one will apply to a particular task according to their purpose. Rodin will make a statue, Corot a landscape, Gratry a burning page, Pasteur a more passionate and attentive investigation. Everything can lean on harmony and regenerate within it. Rhythm, progenitor of the world, is also the progenitor of genius, where the world is reflected. In the indistinct horizon of the dream, each one sees the image of their preference emerge and records its traits in their own language.

According to St. Thomas, personal circumstances and concrete circumstances are part of activities, contribute to integrating them, and communicate their characteristics. Would the action of thinking be an exception? Would it not be influenced by the imaginative, sensory, spiritual, and social environment that we provide it, so that it is no longer an isolated corner, but one of the voices of the orchestra?

We are very poor alone, in a workroom! And it is indeed true that we can bring the universe into it and populate it with God; but this settlement is only effective after a long experience whose elements are everywhere. How can I find God in my room if I have never been to church or been under the sky that “proclaims his handiwork?”[82](part0018.xhtml#a1F8) How can I write under the impression of nature and universal beauty if the great landscapes, the placid countryside, and the theaters of art have not educated me before?

Thus, it is necessary to expand work so as not to become a chained prisoner and not to reduce intellectuality to the role of a shackle. Work is a free act.

Therefore, you who intend to consecrate yourself to the vocation of study, do not turn your back, because of it, on the rest of life. Do not renounce anything concerning man. Maintain a balance in which the dominant weight does not try to drag all the others. Know how to make a thesis and admire a dawn, plunge into deep abstractions and, like the divine Master, play with children. Nowadays, the “togas of pedants” and the “pointy hats” mocked by Pascal are no longer used; but they persist, internalized, they are in souls: do not adorn yourself with them. Refuse to be a brain separated from the body and a man who has renounced his soul. Do not make work a monomania.

My intellectual is the man who possesses broad and varied knowledge that extends into a specialty known in depth; he is a friend of the arts and natural beauties; his spirit is the same in daily occupations and in meditation; he is identical before God, before his peers, and before his servant, carrying within himself a world of ideas and feelings that are not only inscribed in books and speeches, but propagate in conversation with friends and guide his life.

At the bottom, everything is united, and everything is the same thing. Intellectuality does not contain any dividing line. All our themes are doors to penetrate into the “secret garden,” in the “wine cellar” that is the end of ardent investigations. Thoughts and activities, realities and their reflections all have the same Father. Philosophy, art, travels, domestic cares, finances, poetry, and tennis can form alliances, and only contradict each other when there is disharmony.

The necessary, at every moment, is to be where one should be and do what matters. Everything comes together in the human-divine concert.

### 2. Knowing to Unwind {#sertillanges-intellectual.9.2}
Everyone must realize that expanding oneself as we require already is to rest. The best part of rest lies in those secondary modes of life that we mentioned. It is good, however, to praise more explicitly rest, the reverse of work, by which work qualifies in a certain way, revealing itself excessive, reasonable, subjected or not to the human rule that is confirmed in God’s Law.

Nothing in excess. Work, precisely because it is a duty, requires limits that keep it in its vigor, in its duration, and provide it, throughout life, with the greatest sum of effects of which it is capable.

Intemperance is a sin because it destroys us, and we are obliged to govern life well because we are obliged to live. Now, intemperance does not refer only to sensual joys; the subtlest, the noblest enthusiasms partake of its malice. To love truth at the expense of prudence, that is, of the truth of life, is an inconsistency. It proves that, despite assertions, what is loved is not the truth, but the pleasure it provides, the advantages it can bring to vanity, to pride, to ambition, like those passionate people of whom it is said that they love to love and that they love love, and not the object of that love.

Rest is a duty, like hygiene, which includes it, like the conservation of strength. “I want you to save yourself,” says Saint Augustine to his disciple.[83](part0018.xhtml#a1FY) The spirit does not tire, but the incarnated spirit tires; our capacity to think is proportional to a certain amount of action. Moreover, as the sensible is our connatural means, and as the small practical actions form the fabric of the life for which we prepare ourselves, to abandon this means to elevate oneself to what is abstract is quite tiring. Effort cannot be prolonged. It is necessary to return to nature and immerse oneself in it, end of remaking ourselves.[84](part0018.xhtml#a1FZ)

The being in contemplation is “heavier than air:” it remains aloft with a considerable expenditure of energy; in a short time, the fuel runs out, and it is necessary to “fill the tank” again.

We can admit without paradox these words of Bacon, which corroborate the data of physiology: “And it is lazy to study for a long time.” It is laziness directly because it is an impotence to overcome determinism, to use the brake. It is laziness indirectly because the refusal of rest is the implicit refusal of an effort that rest would allow, and that overload would compromise. But it is also laziness in a more hidden form. In fact, physiologically, rest is an enormous work. When it comes to thinking activity, the body’s inner genius undertakes a restoration that it intends to be complete. The so-called leisure is a transformation of energy.

In the theater, when the curtain closes, a whole army of operators rushes onto the stage, cleaning, repairing, modifying, thus preparing for the next act. Would the director who interrupts or hinders this hustle and bustle not be an enemy of the play, the author, the performers, the audience, or himself? The person overwhelmed with tasks thus opposes his own vocation, the one who called him, the collaborators of his intellectual work, his brothers who would benefit from it, to his own good.

The best way to relax would be, as far as possible, not to tire oneself out, that is, to balance work in such a way that one operation rests you from another. In medicine, often a harmful drug is combated by means of its antagonist. Tasks do not tire in the same way, nor at the same time. The foundryman who sweats by his furnace would rest by tying bundles in open fields, and the hay-baler distributing hay in the mangers.

We have already given indications in this regard. When we talk about the use of time, and then about constancy in work, we touch on the principle of task distribution. Not everything in intellectual life is exhausting concentration; there are preparations, parallel tasks, practical corollaries of thoughts and creations. Choosing books, selecting documents, gathering notes, classifying manuscripts, pasting slips in the margins, reviewing proofs, tidying up the desk, organizing the library, these are occupations, not exactly work. By organizing ourselves well, we can engage in work with discernment and, in the intervals, still perform many of these less tiring tasks, which are nevertheless indispensable and have a value of contemplation in themselves.

This distribution of work according to its cerebral demands will have a double advantage: it will avoid overload and give intense work all its purity. When we do not anticipate rest, the rest that we do not take takes us; it surreptitiously intersperses itself into work in the form of distractions, drowsiness, and needs that we must meet because we did not think of them in due time.

I am in full creative effort: behold, I lack a reference, the ink runs out in the inkwell, a classification of notes has not been made, a book or manuscript that I need is in another room, or buried in piles from which I will have to rescue it. An hour ago, I would have done all this playfully, with joy, thinking of the quiet session I would be preparing; now it disturbs me, cools my enthusiasm. If I omitted these preparations for the sake of a false work that my intemperance wanted to accomplish, then the disgrace is twofold. And here is my situation: neither true rest, nor true work. Disorder reigns.

Carefully avoid, as I’ve already said, when it comes to “moments of fulfillment,” work done halfway, which is rest done halfway and has no benefit. Work energetically, then rest, even if it is that relative rest that prepares, second or concludes the work.

The complete rest, on the other hand, will also be necessary; I understand by complete the momentary abandonment of any laborious concern, except for the “permanent work” of which we already know the softness and benefits.

St. Thomas explains that the true repose of the soul is joy, delightful action. The games, family conversations, friendship, family life, the pleasurable readings whose laws we have already exposed, the communion with nature, gentle art, a light manual labor, the intelligent stroll through a city, the not very strong nor very exciting spectacles, moderate sports: these are our means of relaxation.

Also in this there cannot be excess. Resting too long, besides consuming time, impairs dedication to a laborious life. It is very important that each one discovers the rhythm that will allow this maximum dedication allied to the minimum of fatigue. Working for an excessive amount of time and becoming exhausted; stopping too early and falling short of one’s own measure. Likewise, resting for an excessive amount of time destroys the effort achieved; resting too little does not restore one’s strength. Know yourself, and then moderate all things. In general, frequent and short rests, which relax without requiring starting from scratch, are the most favorable.

Ah, if we could work amidst nature, with the window open to a beautiful landscape, in a condition where, when fatigue comes, we can breathe for a few moments in a garden, or if an obstacle arises to thought, ask the opinion of the mountains, the assembly of trees or clouds, of the passing animals, instead of bitterly troubling ourselves, I bet the product of work would be doubled and would be much kinder, much more human.

We are as realistic when we walk through the fields, and at the same time our soul is so uplifted! The “categorical imperative” cannot have been thought of in a meadow, much less the supposedly moral arithmetic of a Bentham.

Young people who aspire to great heights and want to go far, stick to human reality! Preserve leisure; don’t exhaust yourselves; work calmly and with spiritual joy; be free. Use tricks with yourselves, if necessary: promise yourselves, in moments of effort, some pleasant relief whose image will already rejuvenate your thoughts, in the hope that it will restore your strength.

If you are in a group, welcome each other’s distractions. The man who never jokes, says St. Thomas, who neither accepts a joke nor favors another’s play or rest, is a brute, and is burdensome to his neighbor.[85](part0018.xhtml#a1F5) No one can live a whole day, said Aristotle, with a completely gloomy man.

### 3. Accepting the Trials {#sertillanges-intellectual.9.3}
This balance of work and restful joy is even more necessary because the trials of intellectual work are numerous. More than once, we have spoken of them. In matters of science, as in everything else, salvation is only achieved through the cross. Discontent with oneself, the delay of inspiration, the indifference of one’s environment, envy, misunderstandings, sarcasm, injustices, abandonment by superiors, defection of friends, everything can contribute, and everything has its time.

“Superiority is confronted with so many obstacles and sufferings,” wrote George Sand about Balzac, “that the man who patiently and sweetly accomplishes the mission of talent is a great man.” I know you don’t intend to apply this last expression to yourself; but if in one degree or another you become someone of value, prepare yourself to experience various trials: the ordeal of the ideal, which seems ever more distant as you advance; the trial of fools who understand nothing of what you say and are scandalized; the trial of the envious who consider you insolent for having surpassed their line of combat; the trial of the good who are shaken, suspect you, and abandon you; the trial of the mediocre who are legion, and whom you disturb by the silent assertion of a superior world. “If you belonged to the world,” declares the Savior, “the world would love you as its own. Because you do not belong to the world, . . . therefore the world hates you.” [86](part0018.xhtml#a1EC)

The amusements mentioned above as means of relaxation can also be helpful here. Everything that gives rest from work is equally apt to soothe suffering. However, resort above all to supernatural means, and among them to the supernaturalized work which is our only end.

Work cures the sufferings of work and those of the worker; it is an enemy of sorrows, diseases, and sins; it places us in a high region where the bitterness of existence and the weaknesses of the body find relief. The ardor it communicates, the sense it gives to energies expel boredom and free us from miserable worries.

Stay idle and feel your body: probably a multitude of vague discomforts will make themselves felt; work hard and you will think no more of it. We can say the same of the evils of the soul. When I ask myself: “What can I do against the worries and burdens that assail me at work?,” I find only one answer: work. How to comfort my heart when it doubts its work? Work. What is the means of resisting the enemies of effort and the envies of success? Work. Work is the remedy, work is the balm, work is the strength. Add to it silence, its companion, and prayer, its inspirer, enjoy good friendship, if God graces you with one, and you can overcome everything.

Work balances the soul; of inner unity. Allied with the love of God, which establishes the hierarchy of values, it orders all forces, and the soul stabilizes. Without this, the need for unity can only be satisfied with some inferiority complex or some passion, and our most diverse weaknesses will assume power.

It is not in vain that laziness is called the mother of all vices; it is also the mother of fainting and sorrow, or at least favors them. The feeling of victory that work generates fights this depression; the rhythmic use of forces raises its tone and regulates them, like the ardor of the team that rows singing.

The truth is also a defense; it fortifies us; it rejoices us; with it, we console ourselves from ourselves and from others; its discovery is a reward, its manifestation is a noble revenge in times of contradiction.

The worker is exposed, among other bitternesses, to what may be the most sensitive to the intellectual, if not to every man: criticism spares him not. When this criticism is superficial and unjust, he suffers, and tends to become irritated; and if it touches his weak point and points out in his productions or in his character the defects he would like to forget and hide from others, because he cannot overcome them, it is especially then that he feels hit.

What proper reply to make, and what attitude to take? The same as always. “To all criticism I have only one response to give,” Emerson said: “return to work.”[87](part0018.xhtml#a1GY) They also say that Saint Thomas, when attacked, which happened much more frequently than his posthumous triumph suggests, endeavored to strengthen his position, to clarify and explain his doctrine, and then fell silent. The “dumb ox of Sicily” was not going to let the gestures and cries of a children’s crusade divert him from his path.

To correct oneself and be silent, that is the great maxim; all who have practiced it have always risen greatly; they transformed the force used to overthrow them into a victorious impulse; with the stones thrown at them, they built their dwelling.

And it’s childish to try to defend one’s works, or to try to demonstrate their worth. Worth defends itself. The solar system does not arbitrate between Ptolemy and Copernicus. The truth is, and the works of truth participate in its being and its power. To flutter around them is to weaken oneself. Be silent; humble yourself before God; mistrust your judgment and correct your faults; then stand firm like a rock before the waves. The time and effort spent defending a work would be better used to accomplish another, and your peace is worth much more than trivial success.

“The true sage does not argue,” wrote Keyserling; “he does not defend himself. He either speaks or listens; he asserts or seeks to grasp meanings.”

When you receive criticism, instead of reacting externally or internally like a wounded animal, consider, as a man, the extent of what was said; be impersonal and integral. If the criticism is correct, aim to resist the truth, even if it comes from enmity, have the courage to accept it and the noble purpose of using the slander that God puts at your service. For evil itself is in the hands of God, and malicious criticism, being the sharpest, can be of greater benefit to you.

Having thus taken advantage of it, leave the rest to the Lord, who judges for you and will know how to do justice in his time. Do not listen anymore. “Nothing bad is said,” wrote Saint Augustine, “in front of someone who does not listen.” Envy is a tax on the income of glory, distinction, or work. Work, which is invulnerable in itself, demands its price from the worker. Let him pay and not complain. “Great souls suffer silently,” said Schiller.

When there is nothing to take advantage of in an attack, there is the benefit of withdrawing, of escaping unharmed, free from weakening and resentment, and therefore magnified, improved by the trial. True spiritual strength is exalted in persecution; it sometimes groans, but its groan is like that of all creatures who are “groaning in labor pains until now,”[88](part0018.xhtml#a1GD) as the Apostle says.

We said that intellectual life is a heroism: how could heroism not cost anything? Things are worth exactly in proportion to what they cost. For later, success; for later, praise, perhaps not that of men, but that of God and His court, who will make your conscience their prophet. The workers, your brothers, will also recognize it, despite their apparent defection. Among intellectuals, many small villainies are committed and sometimes great iniquities; but an undermined distinction still consecrates true values, even when they are forgotten by publicity.

If it is necessary to postpone equally your own usefulness—who knows, perhaps for a time when you will no longer be in this world—consent to it; posthumous honor is the most disinterested, and the posthumous usefulness sufficiently satisfies the true ends of your work. What do you desire? Glory? Profit? Then you are not a true intellectual. Truth? It is eternal. It is not necessary to make use of eternity.

The truth unveils itself little by little; those who bring it out of the shadows have no reason to demand a halo for it; they serve it, and this is enough, and to wield for just one moment the sword of heroes or carry the shield before them is their reward.

Doesn’t the work stand for itself? It is one of the crimes of these times to have depreciated it and replaced its beauty with the ugliness of cruel selfishness. Noble souls live a beautiful life and expect it to bear fruit as an addition. They work not only for the fruit, but for the work itself, so that their life may be pure, straight, and manly, similar to that of Jesus and ready to join with it. They also do not dwell on disappointments. Love does not fear disappointments, nor does hope, nor does faith with solid roots.

To work without apparent fruit, to sow and not reap, to swim and not reach the shore, to walk and only find endless spaces, none of this is a disappointment to those who believe, to those who hope, and it pleases those who love, because love proves itself much better when one works for the pleasure, the pleasure of the beloved and the pleasure of serving them.

### 4. Enjoying the Good Part {#sertillanges-intellectual.9.4}
Moreover, not everything in work is adversity; it has its joys, and it is a happiness when the disposition to work and rest after effort has its source in joy.

We should be joyful even in afflictions and contradictions, following the Apostle’s example: “I am overjoyed in all our affliction.” [89](part0018.xhtml#a1BY) Sadness and doubt kill inspiration, but they only kill it when we give in to them. Rising above them by Christian joy is to rekindle the dimmed flame.

“The weak think of the past,” wrote Maria Bashkirtseff, “the strong take revenge on it.” This is always possible, and to help us, God allows us to rest sometimes in a tranquil joy.

The feeling of the height of action gives the worker a soul at once concentrated and happy, as to the mountain climber of mountains and glaciers. The landscapes of ideas, more sublime than those of the Alps, excite their intoxication. “To see the order of the universe and the arrangements of divine Providence is an eminently delightful activity,” said St. Thomas Aquinas.[90](part0018.xhtml#a1BE)

According to the Angelic Doctor, contemplation begins in love and ends in joy: love for the object and love for knowledge as a vital act; joy of ideal possession and the ecstasy it provokes.[91](part0018.xhtml#a1BG)

The Christian intellectual chooses renunciation; but renunciation enriches him more than high opulence. He loses the world, and the world is given to him spiritually; he ascends to the throne from which the twelve tribes of Israel are judged. [92](part0018.xhtml#a1D6) The ideal becomes his personal reality, which replaces the other for him and absorbs its flaws in beauty. Stripped of everything in spirit and often in effective poverty, he is enriched by everything he abandoned or that abandoned him, for he secretly rediscovers the magnificent possession of it. Forgotten by himself in the most absorbing inner action, in the depths of that apparent sleep he could say, like the Bride: “I slept, but my heart was awake.”[93](part0018.xhtml#a1D7) “Upon my bed at night I sought him whom my soul loves; . . . I held him, and would not let him go.”[94](part0018.xhtml#a1D8)

When one is in the required conditions and the soul is entirely in the work, when one studies well, when one reads well, when one takes good notes, when one makes the unconscious work at night, the works one prepares are like the seed under the Sun, like the child that the mother brings into the world in pain, but so happy to have given birth to a man who no longer remembers his pains.[95](part0018.xhtml#a1GN)

The reward of a work is having done it; the reward of effort is the very magnification.

It’s amazing how the true intellectual seems to escape these sorrows caused by age that inflict upon so many men a premature death. He is young until the end. We would say he takes part in the eternal youth of truth. Having matured generally very early, he is still mature, neither sour nor rotten when eternity gathers him.

That admirable perpetuity is also that of the saints, and suggests to us that holiness and intellectuality are of the same essence. In fact, truth is the holiness of the spirit and preserves it, as holiness is the truth of life and tends to strengthen it for this world as well as for the other. There is no virtue without growth, without fruitfulness, without joy; likewise, there is no intellectual light without these same effects deriving from it. Wisdom is one, and it includes the double rule of thought and action.

### 5. Longing for the Fruits {#sertillanges-intellectual.9.5}
With this we reach the last words that it behooves us to address to the listener of this short and overly long theory of intellectual life. “If you follow this path,” says St. Thomas to his disciple, “you will produce in the vineyard of the Lord leaves, flowers, and useful fruits for all the time of your life. If you practice these pieces of advice, you may attain what you desire. Farewell.”

Is it not a noble farewell that promises to the worker and to the faithful the honor of truth, ensuring to those who fulfill the necessary conditions the results they desire?

You can’t promise anything to those who aren’t endowed. But, assuming the vocation, we have the right to say that culture isn’t primarily the child of genius; it’s born of work, of qualified, organized, and continuous work, as we seek to portray it.

The work fabricates for itself its own instrument. Like the blacksmith who forges his tools, it forms our character and gives us solidity, and consequently confidence.

This trust that is based on a law of its own belongs more to the work than to the worker; however, the worker must also have faith in himself. Is with him not the God who said: “For everyone who asks receives, and everyone who searches finds, and for everyone who knocks, the door will be opened?” [96](part0018.xhtml#a1EJ) We all have the Truth behind us, and it impels us through intelligence; we also have it in front of us and it calls us; above us, and it inspires us.

The soul is equal in all; the Spirit blows in all; the objective and deep aspirations are the same in all; what is different, besides courage, are the cerebral elements more or less free and active, more or less interconnected; now, we know that with our earthly and heavenly resources we can overcome many deficiencies. Light can infiltrate through gaps that we can widen; when it is present, by itself it expands and corroborates its kingdom.

We should not rely on ourselves; but on the God who lives in us no confidence is excessive. We can never have an excessively high idea of the self when it comes to the divine self.

Moreover, we also expect from our friends, our brothers, and those who initiated us into intellectual life a permanent contribution. We have geniuses on our side. Great men are not great only for themselves; they carry us; our trust relies on them. With their help, we can have a life as great as theirs, considering the disproportion of strengths.

The true intellectual should not fear sterility, uselessness; it is enough for a tree to be a tree to produce seeds. The results may come late, but they come; the soul pays; events pay. If we cannot match those we admire, we can always match ourselves, and it is necessary to repeat one last time: this is our only objective.

Every individual is unique: therefore, every fruit of the spirit is also unique. The unique is always precious, always necessary. We do not steal from God, and God’s success will be partly ours as well. This is what can console our inferiority and, if we produce, comfort us in the face of the deluge of books.

Of all that there is in you, and if you are faithful to yourself, if you are so until the end, be sure that you will achieve the perfection of your work—yours, that is to say, the one that God expects from you and that corresponds to his graces, both interior and exterior. At that moment, you should say to yourself that many works and many lives are more beautiful than yours, but you can add: “There is none more beautiful to me, and there is none equal to it.”

I add one more thing, which is one of our reasons for confidence. When fidelity is demanded of us, intense and well-regulated work does not exclude any failure; promises with this condition would be ludicrous. To err is human; but of all prescriptions, let us retain the essential, the habitual; and it is from this part that we are told: “Enough, it is indispensable.”

It would be desirable that our life were a flame without smoke and without slag, that nothing in it were lost, that nothing were impure. But that is impossible; however, the possible is still beautiful, and beautiful and tasty are its fruits.

Resolved to pay, inscribe in your heart, today, if you haven’t already, a firm resolution. I advise you to write it down in black and white, very legible, and to keep its terms always before you. When you go to work and after you have prayed, day after day, take the resolution again. You must take care to mention especially what is less natural and more necessary to you, as it is. When you need to, recite it aloud, so that more clearly from your word to yourself.
