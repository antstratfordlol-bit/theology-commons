## Chapter 1. The Intellectual Vocation {#sertillanges-intellectual.1}
### 1. The Intellectual Man is a Devoted One {#sertillanges-intellectual.1.1}
To speak of vocation is to refer to those who intend to make intellectual work their own life, both those who have all their time to dedicate to study, and those who, engaged in their professional occupations, reserve for themselves, as a happy supplement and reward, the deep development of the spirit.

I say deep to discard the idea of a superficial veneer. A vocation is not satisfied with vague readings and small scattered works. It requires penetration and continuity, a methodical effort that aims at a fullness that corresponds to the call of the Spirit and to the resources that He wanted to communicate to us.

This appeal should not be prejudged. To enter a path that cannot be treated with safety can only result in failures. Work is imposed on everyone, and after a burdensome initial formation, it would be foolish to let the spirit gradually fall back into the initial indigence; but one thing is the tranquil conservation of what we have acquired, another is the resumption from its foundations of an instruction that we recognize as provisional, that we consider only as a starting point.

This last one is the state of mind of a vocation. It demands a serious resolution. The life of studies is austere and imposes heavy obligations. It rewards, and greatly so, but it requires a dedication that few are capable of. The athletes of intelligence, like those of sports, must foresee deprivations, long trainings, and a sometimes superhuman tenacity. It is necessary to give oneself completely so that the truth gives itself. The truth only serves its slaves.

Such guidance should not be assumed before a deep and lengthy examination. Intellectual vocation is like all others; it is inscribed in our instincts, in our abilities, in some inner impulse that reason controls. Our dispositions are like the chemical properties that determine, for each body, the combinations that this body can achieve. They cannot be created. They come from Heaven and from natural generation. The whole question boils down to being docile to God and to oneself, after having heard these two voices.

Thus understood, Disraeli’s phrase, “Do what pleases you, as long as it truly pleases you,” is precious advice. The taste that is in correlation with deep tendencies and aptitudes is an excellent judge. If Saint Thomas can say that pleasure qualifies functions and can serve to classify men, he could conclude from this that pleasure can also reveal our vocations. It is enough that we dive into those depths where taste and spontaneous impulse meet with the gifts of God and His Providence.

Beyond the immense interest in the realization of oneself in fullness, the study of an intellectual vocation has a general interest to which no one can abstain.

The Christian humanity is composed of diverse personalities, and none of them abdicates itself without impoverishing the whole and without depriving the eternal Christ of a part of His kingdom. Christ reigns unfolding Himself. The entire life of one of His “members” is a qualified instant of His duration; every human and Christian existence is an incommunicable, unique existence and consequently necessary for the extension of His “spiritual body.” If you have been designated as a light-bearer, do not place under the bushel the small or large flame that they expect from you in the household of the Father. Love the truth and its life-giving fruits, for yourself and for others; devote to the study and its use the main part of your time and your heart.

All paths, except one, are bad for you, as they deviate from the direction in which your action is expected and required. Do not be unfaithful to God, to your brothers, and to yourself by rejecting a sacred calling.

This assumes that you come to intellectual life with disinterested purposes, not for ambition or foolish glory. The jingles of publicity only tempt frivolous spirits. Ambition, subjugating the eternal Truth, offends it. Is it not a sacrilege to play with the main questions about life and death, with mysterious nature, with God, forging a literary or philosophical destiny at the expense of the true or independent of the true? Such objectives, and above all the first, do not sustain a seeker; in a short time, the effort would wither, and vanity would end up contenting itself with emptiness, despising realities.

But this also assumes that you add, to the acceptance of the goal, the acceptance of its means, without which obedience to vocation would be nothing. Many would like to know! A vague aspiration directs the crowds to horizons that most admire from afar, like the gout patient or the dreamer look at the eternal snows. Obtaining without paying is a universal desire; but it is the desire of cowardly hearts and sick minds. The universe does not rush to the first call, and God’s light does not come to your lamp without a persistent plea from your soul.

You are a consecrated one: desire whatever you want, the truth; consent, for it, to mobilize yourself, to install yourself in your own domains, to organize yourself and, being inexperienced, to rely on the experience of others.

“If only the youth knew! . . .” It’s mostly the young who need this warning. Science is knowledge through causes; but in practice, as for its production, it is a creation through causes. We must know and adopt the causes of knowledge, and then realize them, and not postpone the care for the foundations for the moment of laying the roof.

In the first free years after studies, with the intellectual land recently plowed, with the seeds sown, what beautiful cultures could arise! And a time that will not return, a time that one will live later: as it was, so will the man, for there is no way to plant new roots later. Living superficially, his punishment will be to have neglected, in favorable time, the future, which is always an inheritance. Let each one think about this, at a moment when thinking can still be fruitful.

How many young people, who aspire to become workers, miserably waste their days, their strengths, their intellectual sap, their ideal! Either they do not work—and they have spare time!—or they work poorly, capriciously, without knowing who they are, where they want to go, or how to walk. Courses, readings, friendships, dosage of work and rest, of solitude and action, of general and specialized culture, spirit of study, art of extracting and using acquired information, provisional achievements that announce the next work, virtues to obtain and to develop, nothing is foreseen, and nothing will be achieved.

However, what a difference, in equal resources, between the one who knows and foresees and the one who follows the flow of currents! “Genius is long patience,” but an organized, intelligent patience. Extraordinary faculties are not necessary to accomplish a work; a simple superiority is enough; the rest is given by energy and its judicious application. It is like with an honest, economical, and faithful worker to his task: he fulfills it, while the inventor is often a failed and bitter person.

What I said applies to everyone; I apply it, however, especially to those who know that they only have a part of their life, the smallest part, to devote to intellectual work. These must, more than others, be consecrated. What they cannot distribute throughout their entire life, they will have to gather in a narrow space. Special asceticism and the heroic virtue of intellectual work should be their daily exercise. But if they consent to this double offering of themselves, I tell them, in the name of the true God, not to be discouraged.

If it is not necessary to be a genius to produce, it is even less necessary to have full freedom. Moreover, it contains traps that strict obligations can help to overcome. A chain compressed by narrow margins will go further. The discipline of a craft is a great school: it teaches the man of studies to expand his possibilities. Coerced, he concentrates more, learns the value of time, enthusiastically takes refuge in those rare hours when, duty fulfilled, he embraces his ideal, hours when he enjoys the chosen action, after the action imposed by harsh existence.

The worker who finds thus in the new effort the reward of the old effort, who makes of it his treasure like a miser, is usually a passionate one; one cannot separate him from what is thus consecrated by sacrifice. If his walk seems slower, he is capable of taking it further. Poor industrious turtle, he doesn’t have fun, perseveres, and at the end of a few years will have surpassed the lazy hare whose easy walk caused envy to his difficult journey.

Think now of the isolated worker, deprived of intellectual resources and stimulating contacts, buried in some end-of-the-world place, where he seems doomed to wither away, exiled far from the rich libraries, brilliant courses, vibrant public, having only himself and forced to extract everything from that inalienable background.

Ah, that this one also not be discouraged! If everything is against him, let him preserve himself and let this be enough for him. A burning heart has more chances of triumphing, even in the middle of the desert, than a wealthy young spendthrift from the Latin Quarter. Here too, difficulty can be a source of strength. In the mountains, we only tense our muscles on difficult passages; the flat paths leave us relaxed and inattentive laxity soon proves fatal.

What matters most of all is the will, a profound desire: to want to be someone, to get somewhere; already be, through desire, that someone qualified by their ideal. The rest always sorts itself out. Books are everywhere, and very few are necessary. Contacts, stimuli, can be found in solitude in spirit: great men are there, present to those who invoke them, and great past centuries impel the fervent thinker. Courses, those who have them do not attend them or attend them poorly if they do not have within themselves that which to resort to in the absence of such privilege. As for the public, if it sometimes stimulates, it often disturbs, disperses, and because of two cents found in the street, you could lose a fortune. Better is the passionate solitude, in which every seed produces a hundredfold, and every ray of sun, a golden autumnal.

Saint Thomas Aquinas, coming to live in Paris and catching sight of the great city from afar, said to the brother accompanying him, “Brother, I would give all this in exchange for Chrysostom’s comment on Saint Matthew.” When someone experiences such feelings, no matter where they are or what they have at their disposal; they have been marked with a sign; they are an elect of the Spirit; it remains to persevere and dedicate oneself to life as God ordains it.

Young man, who understands this language and whom the heroes of intelligence seem to mysteriously invoke, but who fears being unprepared, listen to me. Do you have two hours a day? Can you commit to preserving them carefully, to employing them ardently, and then, being also destined for the Kingdom of God, will you be able to drink from the chalice of delicate and bitter taste that these pages intend for you to experience? If the answer is yes, have confidence. More than that, rest in absolute certainty.

Forced to earn a living, earn it at least without sacrificing, as often happens, the freedom of your soul. Abandoned, you will be thrown with even greater violence towards your noble goals. The two hours I ask of you, many have already declared to be sufficient for an intellectual destiny. Learn to manage this little time; immerse yourself every day of your life in the fountain that quenches and yet gives more thirst.

Do you want to help with your humble part in perpetuating wisdom among men, to treasure the inheritance of the centuries, to provide the present with the rules of the spirit, to discover the facts and the causes, to guide the fickle eyes to the first causes and the hearts to the supreme objectives, to rekindle the fading flame, to organize the propaganda of truth and goodness? That is your share. Undoubtedly, this is worth an additional sacrifice and the cultivation of a burning passion.

The study and practice of what Father Gratry denominates as living logic, that is, the development of our spirit or human word through direct or indirect contact with the Spirit and the divine Word, this serious study and persevering practice will give you access to the admirable sanctuary. You will be among those who grow, acquire, and dispose themselves to magnificent gifts. You too, one day, God willing, will find a place in the assembly of noble spirits.

### 2. The Intellectual Man Is Not an Isolated Man {#sertillanges-intellectual.1.2}
Another characteristic of the intellectual vocation is that the Christian worker, who is consecrated, should not be an isolated man. Whatever his situation, however abandoned or withdrawn he may be materially, he must not fall into the temptation of individualism, a distorted image of the Christian personality.

As much as loneliness vivifies, isolation paralyzes and sterilizes. To the strength of being a soul, one ceases to be a man, said Victor Hugo. Isolation is inhuman, for to work humanly is to work with the feeling of man, his needs, his greatness, the solidarity that binds us in a closely common life.

A Christian worker should live constantly in the universal, in history. Since he lives with Jesus Christ, he cannot separate from him neither the times nor the men. Real life is a life in unity, an immense family life that has charity as its law: if study aims to be a vital act, not an art for art’s sake or pure abstraction, it must let itself be governed by this law of cordial unity. “We pray before the crucifix,” said Gratry—and we must also work before it—“but the true cross is not isolated from the earth.”

A true Christian will always have before their eyes the image of this globe where the cross is planted, where poor human beings wander and suffer, and where the redeeming blood, in numerous streams, flows towards them. The clarity they possess clothes them with a priesthood; what they seek to acquire is an implicit promise of a gift. All truth is practical; the most abstract in appearance, the highest, is also the most practical. All truth is life, guidance, a path to the purpose of man. That is why Jesus Christ said as a single statement: “I am the way, and the truth, and the life.”[2](part0018.xhtml#a1F0)

Work always in the spirit of service, as the Gospel says. Listen to the human race murmuring around you; distinguish these or those individuals or groups that you know are in need; discover what can pull them out of the darkness, ennoble them; what, near or far, saves them. The only holy truths are the redeeming truths, and it is as much about our work as about everything else that the Apostle affirms: “For this is the will of God, your sanctification.”[3](part0018.xhtml#a1CR)

Jesus Christ needs our spirit for his work as he needed, on Earth, his own human spirit. He left, we continue him; we have this immeasurable honor. We are his “members,” and therefore we participate in his spirit, and therefore we are his co-workers. He acts for us externally and through his inspiring Spirit internally, as when he lived he acted externally through his voice and internally through his grace. Since our work is necessary for this action, let us work as Jesus meditated, drinking, like him, from the waters of the Father, in order to share them.

### 3. The Intellectual Man Belongs to His Time {#sertillanges-intellectual.1.3}
Next, think that, if all times are equal before God, if His eternity is a radiant center from which all points of the circumference of time are the same distance, it does not happen the same with times and us, who inhabit the circumference. We are here, in the vast wheel, and nowhere else. And, if we are here, it is because God placed us here. Every moment of duration has a relation to us, and every century is our neighbor, just as every man; but the word “neighbor” is a relative word, which providential wisdom determines for each one, and which each one, in their wisdom, submissive to that, must also determine.

Here I am, man of the twentieth century, contemporary of a permanent drama, witness to convulsions perhaps never seen on this globe since the mountains arose and the seas were cast into their abysses. What should I do for this gasping century? More than ever, thought awaits men and men await thought. The world is in danger due to lack of life’s maxims. We are on a train at full speed, and without signaling, without switches. The planet does not know where it is going, its law abandons it: who will restore its Sun?

I don’t say this to restrict the field of intellectual investigation and confine it to exclusively religious study. Soon this will become clear. I’ve already said that all truth is practical, that all truth saves. But I point to a spirit, and that spirit, from the viewpoint of the present moment and also in general, excludes dilettantism.

It also excludes a certain archaeological tendency, a love for the past that neglects present pains, an esteem for the past that seems to ignore the universal presence of God. The times do not equate, but all times are Christian times, and there is a time that, for us, practically surpasses all others: ours. And for it there are our innate resources, our graces of today and tomorrow, and therefore also the efforts that must correspond to them.

Let’s not resemble those who always seem to be attending the funerals of the past. Let us, alive, make use of the value of the dead. The truth is always new. Like the morning grass that a delicate dew covers, all old truths have the desire to bloom again. God does not age. We must help this God to renew, not the buried past and extinct chronicles, but the eternal face of the Earth.

This is the spirit of the Catholic intellectual, this is his vocation. The sooner he specifies this general framework by discovering the kind of studies to which he should dedicate himself, the better.

Listen, now, to the virtues that God asks of you.
