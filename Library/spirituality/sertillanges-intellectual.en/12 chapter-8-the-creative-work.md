## Chapter 8. The Creative Work {#sertillanges-intellectual.8}
### 1. Writing {#sertillanges-intellectual.8.1}
Here is the moment to produce. One cannot always be learning and always preparing. Moreover, learning and preparing are not possible without a dose of accomplishment that favors them. It is by walking that one forges one’s own path. All life moves in circles. An organ that is exercised grows and strengthens; a strengthened organ acts with more power. One should write throughout one’s intellectual life.

First and foremost, one writes for oneself, to see one’s own situation clearly, to better determine one’s thoughts, to sustain or revive the attention that quickly weakens if action does not demand it, to gather the research whose necessity is revealed in production, to encourage the effort that would dwindle if it never perceived a visible effect, and finally, to create one’s style and acquire that value that crowns all others: the art of the writer.

After writing, it’s necessary to publish—provided that good judges find you capable of it and you yourself feel the aptitude for flight. The bird knows well when it can confront space; its mother knows it with greater certainty; supported by oneself and by a wise spiritual motherhood, fly as soon as you can. Contact with the public will compel you to produce better; deserved praise will encourage you; criticism will exercise its control; progress will be imposed on you, so to speak, instead of the stagnation that could result from perpetual silence. Intellectual parenthood is a sowing of goods. Every work is a fountain.

Father Gratry insists greatly on the efficacy of writing. He advises that one should always meditate with the pen in hand and that the pure hour of the morning should be consecrated to this contact of the spirit with oneself. Personal dispositions must be taken into account, but it is certain that for the majority the pen plays the same role as the coach in sports games.

Speaking and listening to one’s own soul is, within it, the truth; speaking solitarily and silently during writing and listening to oneself and experiencing the truth with the freshness of sensation of an early riser man who listens to nature at dawn.

In everything it’s necessary to begin. “The beginning is more than half of the whole,” said Aristotle. Whoever produces nothing, becomes accustomed to passivity; the fear that comes from pride or shyness increases more and more; one ends up retreating, getting tired of waiting, becoming unproductive like a stifled sprout.

The art of writing, as I have said, demands this long and early application that gradually becomes a mental habit and constitutes what is called style. My “style,” my “pen,” is the spiritual instrument I use to tell myself and others what I hear from eternal truth. This instrument is a quality of my being, an inner disposition, a disposition of the living brain, in other words, it is the self that has evolved in a certain way. “Style is the man.”

The style is formed, therefore, together with the writer; the muteness is a decrease of the person. If you want to be in fullness, from the intellectual point of view, it is necessary to think aloud, to think explicitly, that is, to shape inside and outside your word.

Perhaps this is the occasion to say in a few words what should be a style in relation to the purposes suggested here for the intellectual.

But, ah!, it would be better not to write than to dare to say how it is written. Humility is not difficult when facing Pascal, La Fontaine, Bossuet, Montaigne; we already feel the power or experience the tranquil expansion of a grand style. We can at least confess the ideal we aim for and which we fail to attain; to declare it is at once to accuse and honor oneself in it, for it judges us.

The qualities of style can be explained in as many articles as one desires; everything can be condensed, I believe, in these three words: truth, individuality, simplicity. Unless one prefers to summarize it in a single expression: to write with truth.

A style is true when it responds to a need of thought and remains intimately in contact with things.

Discourse is a vital act: it should not be a rupture with life, which happens when we fall into artificiality, into conventionality, Bergson would say in the tout fait.[68](part0018.xhtml#a1FP) To write, on one hand, is on the other to live one’s spontaneous and sincere life and to offend the word and the harmonious human unity.

The “speech of circumstance” is the kind of thing we say because they must be said, which are only thought of literarily, using that eloquence which true eloquence mocks. The speech of circumstance is often nothing more than a speech of occasion. It may even be brilliant, as Demosthenes or Bossuet prove; but it will only be so if the circumstance draws from our innermost being that which would also come by itself, what relates to our personal vision, to our perpetual meditations.

The virtue of the spoken or written word is selflessness and righteousness: selflessness that does not let personality stand between the truth speaking within and the soul listening; righteousness that ingenuously exposes what has been revealed in inspiration and does not add empty words.

“Look into your heart and write,” said Sidney. Whoever writes like this, without pride or artifice, as if for oneself, speaks truly to all humanity, if they have the talent to cast a truthful word afar. Humanity will recognize itself in this discourse, for it was it that inspired it. Life recognizes life. If I give to others only a printed paper, they may look at it with curiosity, but then they will abandon it; if I am like a tree that offers its foliage and its fruits full of sap, if I give myself fully, I will convince, and, like Pericles, I will leave a dart lodged in souls.

Following the laws of thought, I reveal myself in the proximity of things, or rather, in the intimacy of things. To think and conceive what is; to write with truth, that is, being faithful to thought, and to reveal what is, not to line up sentences. Thus, the secret of writing is to place oneself before things ardently, until they speak to us and determine for themselves what we must express.

The speech must correspond to the truth of life. The auditor is a man; the speaker cannot be a shadow. The auditor opens up a soul to him that must be healed or enlightened: do not just give him words. As his sentences succeed each other, he must look outward, look within himself, and realize that there is correspondence.

The truth of style repels the cliche. Thus is called an ancient truth, a formula that has become a commonplace, expressions that were once new, and are no longer precisely because they have lost contact with the reality from which they were born, for they float in the air, vain tinsel that replace living matter, the direct and immediate transcription of the idea.

As Paul Valéry observes, it is the automatism that wears out languages. Whoever is alive, he says, always uses syntax “with full awareness,” applying themselves to articulating with vigilance all its elements, avoiding certain effects that arise by themselves and claim primacy. This claim is precisely the reason that justifies discarding these parasites, these intruders, these impertinent ones.

The great style consists in the discovery of the essential relations between the elements of thought, and in the art of expressing them without any accessory babbling. “To write as dew accumulates on the leaf and stalactites in the cave, as flesh is born of blood and as the woody fiber of the tree forms from the sap:” what an ideal

The proud and disturbing person will be absent in such a discourse, as we said; and the personality of expression will be even more pronounced and clear in it. What comes out of me without my manipulation necessarily resembles me. My style is my face. A face has the general characteristics of the species, but it also has an extraordinary and incommunicable individuality; it is unique on Earth and for all centuries; and partly from this comes the lively interest aroused by portraits.

Now, surely our spirit is even more original; but we hide it behind acquired generalities, traditional phrases, verbal groupings that only translate old habits, and not love. To show it as it is, relying on acquisitions that belong to all, but without losing oneself in them, would arouse an inexhaustible interest, and this would be art.

The style that suits a thought is like the body that belongs to a soul, like the plant that comes from a certain seed: it has its particular architecture. To imitate is to alienate thought; to write without character is to declare it vague and puerile.

We must never write “the way of . . .”, not even the way of ourselves. We don’t have to have a way; truth doesn’t have it; it presents itself; it is always new. But the sound that transmits truth cannot help but be personalized by each of its instruments.

“All truly great men were original,” wrote Jules Lachelier; “but they neither wanted nor believed themselves to be so; on the contrary, it was by seeking to make their words and their actions the adequate expression of reason that they found the particular form under which they were destined to express it.”

Every instrument has a timbre. If the manner is an affectation, true originality is a concrete fact; instead of weakening, it reinforces the impression produced on the reader, who in turn will receive it in accordance with what he himself is. What should be proscribed is not the personal sentiment that renews and glorifies everything, but the self-will that opposes the reign of truth.

From this comes simplicity. The flourish is an offense to thought, or else it is a device to conceal its emptiness. There are no flourishes in reality; there are only organic needs. Which does not mean that there is nothing brilliant in nature; but brilliance too is organic, has its reason for being, sustained by substrates that do not unravel.

For nature, the flower is as serious as the fruit, the foliage as serious as the branch; the whole depends on the roots and is the manifestation of the seed, which contains the idea of the species. Now, style, when it leaves the hand of a worker, imitates natural creations. A sentence, a written passage, should be constructed like a living branch, like a root filament, like a tree. It has nothing superfluous, nor ornament; it contains only what leads from one seed to another seed, from the seed germinated in the writer to that which must germinate in the reader and thus propagate human truth or goodness.

The style does not have its reason in itself; to treat it as an object is to distort and degrade it. One must despise truth in order to dedicate oneself to “form,” to become, instead of a poet, a rhymer, instead of a writer, a stylist! Whoever possesses the genius of style must carry it to perfection, which is a right of everything that exists; it is a legitimate desire to become as skilled as the old blacksmith in his trade; but the blacksmith does not amuse himself by joyfully forging scrolls—he produces plates, locks, and grilles.

The style excludes uselessness; and a strict economy within wealth; it spends whatever is necessary, saves here with skillful arrangement, and spends there for the glory of truth. Its role is not to shine, but to make one see; it should not even be noticed, and it is then that its own glory shines. “Beauty is the purgation of all that is superfluous,” said Michelangelo, and Delacroix praised “the magnificent volumes, the simple faces, the noses without details.” And he observed that this must be accompanied by very firm outlines, as in Michelangelo, Leonardo, and especially in Velásquez, but not in Van Dyck, and this is also a lesson.

Seek to write in the inevitable form, starting from the precise thought or the exact feeling you want to express. Your goal is to be understood by everyone, as befits a man who speaks to other men, and strive to reach in them everything that is directly or indirectly an organ of truth. “A complete style is one that reaches all souls and all faculties of souls.”[70](part0018.xhtml#a1DG)

Do not court fashion; its time will influence you on its own and will find its way to conform to eternity. Offer water from the fountain, not acidic drugs. Many writers today have a system: every system is a pose, and every pose an affront to beauty.

Cultivate the art of omission, elimination, simplification: it’s the secret of strength. The masters, after all, keep repeating this, as Saint John used to repeat: “Love one another.” [71](part0018.xhtml#a1FT) The Law and the Prophets, in matters of style, are like innocent nudity that reveals the splendor of living forms: thought, reality, creations, and manifestation of the Word.

Unfortunately, the innocence of spirit is rare; when it exists, it often allies itself with nullity. Thus, only two types of spirits seem predisposed to simplicity: spirits of little stature and geniuses; others are forced to acquire it laboriously, hindered by their own wealth and unable to divest themselves as much as they would like.

### 2. Detaching from Oneself and the World {#sertillanges-intellectual.8.2}
Style, and creative work in general, demand detachment. The bothersome personality must be set aside; the world, forgotten. Can anyone who meditates on truth get distracted by oneself? What can we expect from a man who only thinks of himself? My hope lies in the one who launches oneself, forgetting their ephemeral personality, towards what is immense and universal, who walks, astronomer, in the company of the stars, poet, philosopher, theologian, in the company of animated or inanimate matter, of individual and social humanity, of souls, angels, and of God. And in this one I believe, because in them dwells the spirit of truth, not a miserable preoccupation.

To work only with understanding, we have already seen that it is not enough: the whole man must be involved. But the man who dedicates himself to work cannot be the passionate man, the vain man, the ambitious man, or lightly complacent.

Everyone falls in love at certain moments, but at no moment should passion dominate. Everyone is exposed to vanity, but work which is essentially vanity is a vice. It doesn’t matter what we can gain from science, but what we can give it. The essential thing is not the reception given to our words, but the reception we ourselves give to the truth and what we prepare for it in others. What weight do our tiny selfish calculations have in the face of this sacred objective? Many men who seem wholeheartedly dedicated to a work give it less importance than trivial successes. The formation of worlds, the ascent of species, the history of societies, the economics of labor are in service of their honor and prestige; their poetry aspires to the treatment of “dear master,” their painting dreams of the noble hall of an exhibition; Corneille interpreted by Talma reduces to “admire my immense talent.” It is evident that the spirit thus diverted degenerates. Such intentions can only degrade work, and even if one rises a little on the scale of ambitions, neglecting current success, counting on reaching there by an effect of one’s own disinterest, the result is the same.

Inspiration is not compatible with desire. Whoever wants something for themselves rejects the truth: the jealous God will not be their guest. It is necessary to work in the spirit of eternity, as we have said, and what is less eternal than an ambitious life? Dedicate yourself entirely to the truth; you must serve it, and not serve yourself with it.

We act with fullness for those causes for which we would accept to die. Are you ready to die for the truth? Everything written by a true friend of truth, everything he thinks, should be like the signs that Saint Peter the martyr wrote with the blood of his wound, dying: Creed.

The selfish personality is always shrinking; it contaminates everything, reduces everything; it disorients the forces. Those who walk without seeking themselves, inspired by truth and leaving to God the responsibility for what will come, this is a worthy thinker. “For to me, living is Christ,” said Saint Paul:[72](part0018.xhtml#a1EM) here is a vocation and a certainty of victorious action. Only a true intellectual can say: “For me, to live is truth.”

One form of personality particularly hostile to work and this almost universal hypocrisy which consists of displaying an appearance of knowledge when sincerity would confess its ignorance. Concealing one’s intellectual destitution in the shadow of words is what is criticized in the impromptu scribbler, the copycat journalist, and the ignorant deputy; but every writer with a straight conscience must confess that at every moment, in this regard, they yield to the suggestions of pride. They want to hide their secret; disguise their inadequacy; strike a pose of greatness, feeling small; “asserts,” “declares,” “is sure that . . . ;” deep down, they know nothing about the subject; they impose themselves on others and, vaguely deceived by their own game, seduce themselves.

Another madness is to seek in thought this false originality that we have just condemned in style. Wanting to mold truth to oneself is an unbearable pride and ends in foolishness. Truth is essentially impersonal. Let it use our voice and our spirit, and it will be nuanced by them without us seeking it; it will do so much better the less we think about it; but to force truth to resemble us is to falsify it, and to substitute this immortal for a profaner and an ephemeral.

“Do not consider where the truth comes from,” said Saint Thomas: also do not consider to whom it gives glory; wish that your reader, in contact with your work, also does not consider where the truth comes from. This sublime disinterest is the mark of greatness; to seek it, to make it a law always accepted, if not always obeyed, and to correct what cannot be eliminated in our own misery. Thus, we ennoble ourselves with the only true greatness. The humble pedestal has its share of glory when truth shines forth, authentic flame, on the candelabrum of the spirit.

And it is also necessary, as I was saying, to forget the public. “The farther a book is written from the reader, the stronger it will be,” said Father Gratry in his Sources, and the Pensées of Pascal, the works of Bossuet for the Dauphin, and the Summa of Saint Thomas Aquinas, above all, are given as examples; the comparison of the Petit Carême with the Discours synodaux of Massillon confirms it. It is true, and Vauvenargues agrees with it when he says: “Everything that is thought exclusively for others is usually unnatural.”

That doesn’t mean one should neglect others and lose interest in being useful to them. The intellectual belongs to everyone and should be aware of that. But worrying about being useful is not asking for a word of order. We cannot let ourselves be influenced by “what will they say?;” we cannot beat around the bush under the weight of cowardly conformity, which claims to be friends with everyone so that everyone will reciprocate with their complacency.

To seek public approval is to deny the public a strength it counted on. Are you not at its service? Does it not have the right to ask you: “Where is your work?” Now, thought will not be your work if the concern to please and adapt enslaves your pen. In this case, the public will think for you, who should have thought for it.

Seek God’s approval; meditate only on the truth, for yourself and for others; do not be a slave, and become worthy to say, with Paul: “The word of God is not chained.”[73](part0018.xhtml#a1JA)

That virtuous independence is even more necessary because the public, being a mass, has everything necessary to lower it. The public is primary. In most of its environments and by the innumerable quantity of its voices, it proclaims conventions, not truths; it wants to be flattered; fears, above all, to be disturbed. For the essential truths to reach their ears, it is necessary that you impose them with great force. You can do it, and it is this salutary violence that the solitary thinker must exert.

Their strength to accomplish it lies in relying on oneself and on the nature of things; and to “beat like a deaf person,” as Madame de Sévigné said of Bourdaloue, and to cry out the “every man for himself” that manages to seduce and conquer souls.

Nothing is as truly powerful or as truly overwhelming as a strong conviction united with a character that gives guarantees to weak mortals. Those who demand to be courted despise their suitors and surrender to their lord. If you are of this world, this world will love you, for you belong to it, but your silent disdain will give measure of your fall.

This perverse world only loves, deep down, the saints; that coward dreams of heroes; Roger Bontemps becomes serious and thinks of converting in front of an ascetic. In such a humanity, one must not yield to opinion and write as if it were reading over one’s shoulders. It is necessary to strip oneself of others as of oneself. In the intellectual domain, as in all others, to surpass man is to prepare oneself to accomplish prodigies, for it is to pave the way for the Spirit.

In the office and in the solitude in which God speaks to the heart, we must listen as a child listens, and also write as a child speaks. The child is simple and unassuming because it still doesn’t have its own will, nor a conquered place, nor fictitious desires, nor passions. Its naive trust and its direct speech cause profound interest. A mature man with much experience, and who still preserves that innocence, becomes a beautiful receptacle for the truth, and his voice echoes in the depths of souls.

### 3. Being Constant, Patient, and Perseverant {#sertillanges-intellectual.8.3}
The creative work requires yet other virtues; its demands correspond to its value. I gather here three of its requirements which support each other and will not allow a small or needy work to be made. It is necessary to work with a constancy that remains with the work, with a patience that endures the difficulties, with a perseverance that avoids the wearing down of the will.

“One should not imagine,” says Nicole,

that the life of studies is an easy life. . . . The reason for this difficulty is that nothing is more contrary to nature than uniformity and rest, as they require us to remain alone with ourselves. Changes and external occupations draw us out and amuse us, making us forget ourselves. Moreover, this language with words is always a bit dead and has nothing that strongly touches our self-love or that strongly awakens our passions. It is devoid of action and movement . . . It speaks little of ourselves and gives us few opportunities to see ourselves pleasurably. It flatters our hopes little, and all this contributes to strangely mortify self-love, which, finding no satisfaction, spreads discouragement and displeasure in all our actions.[74](part0018.xhtml#a1DK)

This analysis, which resembles Pascal’s theory of diversion, could take us far. I retain only that “discouragement and disgust,” being terrible enemies here, need to be overcome.

Everyone knows these intellectuals who work on tiptoes, in interrupted phases of laziness and neglect. There are holes in the fabric of their destiny; they make it a patched rag, sometimes well, sometimes poorly, instead of a noble tapestry. We want to be intellectuals all the time, and for that to be recognized. They will know who we are by our way of resting, of amusing ourselves, by the way we tie our shoes: with even more reason they will see it by our fidelity to work, that is, by the punctual return to the task and its continuity.

Someone once asked Edison to say a few words to a boy for him to memorize; the great inventor spoke, smiling: “My boy, never pay attention to the pendulum.” Edison himself paid so little attention to it that on the day of his wedding—a love marriage—they had to go fetch him; he had forgotten it, immersed in one of his researches.

It is beautiful to be entirely present in what one does, like God, who does not get distracted from His work. And if the occupation does not value the person, it is better not to engage in it.

Often, we are tempted to waste time because “it’s not worth starting now,” because “soon I’ll have to stop.” We don’t realize that these bits of time, which indeed are not suitable for a large undertaking, are very suitable for preparing or touching up work, for checking references, gathering notes, classifying documents, etc. It would be a great gain for real work sessions, and moments thus employed would be as useful as others, for these little attentions relate to them and are indispensable to them.

During the very sessions, the temptation is to interrupt the effort as soon as the slightest incident awakens the “disappointment” and “disgust” of which Nicole spoke. The traps of laziness are endless, like those of children. In search of a word that doesn’t come, one begins to scribble in the margin of the page, and soon the drawing must be finished. Opening the dictionary, one is attracted by one verbal curiosity, then by another, and one stays there, trapped in a labyrinth. Your eyes fall on an object; you go to arrange it, and a futile occupation takes up a quarter of an hour. Suddenly someone passes by; a friend is in the next room; the phone attracts your lips . . . or else it’s the newspaper that arrives, you glance at it and soon you’re immersed in it. As one idea leads to another, it may be that the very work distracts you from work, that a daydream uses a thought to drag you into its fantasies.

In moments of inspiration, these traps are not very dangerous, as the joy of discovery or production sustains you; but the ungrateful hours always appear, and while they last, the temptation is powerful. Many times, it takes a true strength of soul to escape these trivialities. All workers suffer these moments of depression that break through the fervent hours and threaten to ruin productivity. When the nausea prolongs, they wish rather to go plant cabbages than to continue a tiresome study; they envy the manual laborer, who in turn considers them idle, sunk in a tranquil armchair. What danger to abandon everything, in such a tepid boredom!

It’s especially in changes of direction that we must be attentive to the unexpected or the perfidy of attacks. All works have transitions that are painful; the connections are the great difficulty of studies and creations. Everything is related. An advance in a straight line is followed by a curve, whose angle is difficult to calculate; one loses direction; hesitates, and it is then that the demon of laziness appears.

It’s good, sometimes, to give oneself some time when one can’t see the sequence of thoughts and is exposed to the serious danger of artificial transitions. It may be that everything becomes clear without effort a little later. I’ve already said that the night, the clear morning, the moments of dreamy pause are propitious in these cases. But giving oneself some time is not giving in to laziness. Look at the work from another angle and dedicate yourself to it with fervent application.

Vigorously refuse any unjustified interruption. If your tiredness is great, take a voluntary break to recover. Irritation would lead to nothing. Reading a passage from a favorite author, reciting aloud, kneeling prayer to modify the organic state and free the spirit more or less, a session of outdoor breathing, some rhythmic movements: these are the possible remedies. Then, return to work.

Some resort to stimulants: and it’s a nefarious method. The effect is only momentary; it’s a trick that wears out; it’s necessary to increase the dose day by day; psychic and mental aberrations are the final result of this progress.

An innocent stimulant is walking, whether outdoors or in the office itself. Many workers engage their brains through muscular exercise. “My foot, also, is a writer,” Nietzsche would say.

But your preferred stimulant must be courage. Courage is sustained, beyond prayer, by the ever-renewed vision of the goal we seek. The prisoner who desires to escape knows how to gather all his energies; he does not tire with the long preparations, with the failed attempts: freedom is always calling to him. Do you not want to flee from mistakes, to achieve the freedom of spirit in a consummated work? Visualize your finished work: that vision will set your heart on fire.

Another effect of constancy is overcoming the impressions of false tiredness that affect both the spirit and the body. When someone begins an excursion, it is common for the first slope to leave them breathless and heavy; they become discouraged; they feel like turning back. Persisting, the joints loosen up, breathing expands, and one begins to experience the joy of action. The same happens with study. The first sensation of fatigue should not be taken into account; it must be overcome; one must force inner energy. Little by little, with the gears in motion, we adapt, and a period of enthusiasm may succeed the painful inertia.

Whatever the cause, it is necessary to know how to navigate through difficulties without faltering, maintaining self-control. A work session is like a racetrack full of obstacles. You leap over one hedge; just ahead, a ditch, then a ramp, and so on. Faced with the first barrier, we don’t stop, we leap, and the obstacles are separated by free zones where we advance without any effort. Overcoming one difficulty teaches you to overcome others; one effort prevents four others; a minute’s courage is worth a journey, and hard work is equivalent to fruitful and joyful work.

In the course of your life, this tenacity will contribute to making your activity increasingly easier. We become accustomed to thinking, as we become accustomed to playing the piano, riding a horse, or painting: Saint Thomas dictated while sleeping. The spirit molds itself to what we frequently demand of it. If you have no memory, you will acquire it for whatever is the constant object of your thought; if you tend to be scattered in spirit, the attention of the professional will concentrate you; if you are not very adept at unraveling ideas, contact with geniuses will give you a sharper and more confident judgment. In any subject, when you have started a certain number of times, the engine will warm up, and the road will be quickly traveled.

Amiel wondered one day to himself, in his diary: “Why are you weak? Because you yielded thousands of times, and thus became a plaything of circumstances. It was you who made your strength, not they who made your weakness.”

Learn to be constant through application and obstinate restarts: there will come a day when persistent prostrations dissipate, when momentary displeasures will have little effect; you will have become a man; the worker without constancy is nothing but a child.

With experience, we discover that many of the difficulties are overcome in advance by those who energetically engage in work, as runners do. However, there will always be other difficulties that must be addressed with a neighboring virtue: patience.

All the geniuses complained about the tribulations of thought, declaring that their works, although a necessity for them and a condition of their happiness, inflicted upon them long torments, often true anguish. I still hear Bergson lamenting about it.

The brain has obscure laws; its functioning depends very little on will; when it refuses, what to do? When the thread of science tangles and the hours pass in vain, when a painful feeling of powerlessness takes hold of you, without anything announcing that the end of the ordeal is near, which path to take or what help to implore beyond divine help?

Your reader will consider all your achievements very simple; they will harshly criticize your mistakes; they won’t even think about your sufferings. Moreover, they shouldn’t think about them. “Works accomplished at the cost of great effort,” said Michelangelo, “must appear, despite the truth, easy and conceived effortlessly. . . . The great rule is to expend much effort to create things that seem to have required no effort.” Boileau, in turn, did not boast of having learned from Racine the art of making easy verses difficult. In the scientific field, Biot said, “There is nothing simpler than what was discovered yesterday, nor anything more difficult than what will be discovered tomorrow.” But the public doesn’t know that. You must bear your burden alone, and great men warn us that this burden of thought is the heaviest a man can bear.

In investigation, you must be as untamed as the explorer of the pole or of Central Africa. In the struggle against error or in resistance to it, one needs the fortitude and ardor of Caesar or Wellington. The work demands the same heroism as a battle. An office is sometimes a trench in which we must remain steadfast, like an unwavering martyr.

When you feel disarmed, defeated; when the road stretches out before you, endless, or when, having taken a wrong direction, you have the impression of being lost, enveloped in thick mists, disoriented, this is the moment to appeal to the energies you have in reserve. Persist, withstand the blow, have patience, in the highest sense of this word, which evokes the Passion of the Lord. Ardor is easier than patience, but both are required, and success is their common reward.

To the mountaineer crossing a cloud, it seems that the universe is plunged into night: he continues and soon finds the Sun. When we are, because of bad weather, in a closed apartment, we have the impression that the world outside is impassable: as soon as the good weather returns, however, we go out and walk calmly through it.

It is mainly its long duration what makes the art of thinking so slow and so disproportionate to our ordinary audacities. Ars longa, vita brevis. The virtue of patience has in it a field favorable for its exercise. Respecting the laws of the hatching of things and not offending science with indiscreet haste, you will gain more than with fiery precipitation. Truth and nature walk at the same pace, and nature operates in spaces of time that, compared with the life and death of the globe, are like a sunrise and a sunset.

“Slow is the life of deep springs,” wrote Nietzsche: “they must wait for a long time before knowing what has fallen into their depths.”[75](part0018.xhtml#a1C5) This secret spring is the soul: do not seek to unravel its mystery prematurely. The reserves of time belong to God; He gives them to us little by little; but it is not up to us to demand them, nor to be impatient. The true treasures arrive in their own time.

Avoid, therefore, the agitation of the hasty man. Hasten slowly. In the domain of the spirit, calmness is more valuable than speed. Here is more evident than anywhere else the proverb: “Good things come to those who wait.” “A well-filled life is long,” said Leonardo da Vinci. To the man who respects his time belongs the entire duration, which is seated in eternity. Work, then, with a spirit of eternity. Do not confuse a generous stimulus with the excitements, which are almost their opposite, for they break the rhythm. You cannot, if you are disturbed, perform that peaceful work which is the ordering of ideas, the delicate elaboration of new thoughts. Do you want to waste time with the foolish anxiety that you will run out of time?

Christianly, you should respect God in His Providence. It is He who establishes the conditions of knowledge: impatience is in His eyes a revolt. When you are taken by fever, spiritual slavery lurks, inner freedom fades. It is no longer you who acts, much less is it Christ in you. You no longer do the work of the Word.

Why hurry indiscreetly when the path is already an objective, when the means are already an end? When we see Niagara, do we wish it would hurry? Intellectuality is worthwhile in itself, in all its states. Virtuous effort is an achievement. Whoever works for God and according to God finds their dwelling in God. What does it matter if time flows away when we are installed there?

The culmination of constancy and continuous patience is achieved through perseverance. “The one who endures to the end will be saved,” says the Gospel.[76](part0018.xhtml#a1GV) Salvation of the intellect follows the same law as total salvation. Also in it, “no one who puts a hand to the plow and looks back”[77](part0018.xhtml#a1GW) is worthy of the Kingdom of Heaven.

How many workers have thus abandoned the fields, the sowings, and renounced the harvests! The Earth is populated with these deserters. The first attempts in science have the character of elimination tests: successively, the weak characters give way, the brave resist; only the three hundred of Gideon or the thirty of David.

To persevere and to desire; those who do not persevere do not desire, they project. Those who give up never truly committed; those who stop loving never loved. Destiny is one, and even more so is a partial work. The true intellectual is, by definition, a perseverant one. They take on the task of learning and teaching; they love the truth with all their being; they are dedicated, and do not withdraw prematurely.

All great lives have this supreme mark. They end like a glorious day. The reds of sunset are no less beautiful than the light golds of dawn, and they even add a certain grandeur to these. The good man who has worked for a long time and has not weakened can also decline into a simple and sumptuous death; his work accompanies him, while it remains among us.

Don’t be, you who walks on the path of the great, one of those weak wanderers who desert, who cross a stage, park, get lost, sit down as if they were exhausted, and sooner or later return to ordinary regions. You must take the journey to its end. “Slow and steady wins the race,” and wide steps without perseverance are nothing but empty dances that lead nowhere.

Strengthen your will and entrust it to the Lord so that He may consecrate it. To desire and to submit oneself, and to shackle oneself. The need of duty or of a meditated resolution, even if it is not an obligation, should constrain us as much as the needs of nature. Is a moral alliance not more than a material alliance?

Therefore, after determining your task, know how to remain faithful to it with a flexible rigor; exclude even the lesser duties, and all the more so the infidelities. Strive radically to conquer duration in that dimension of yours which is directly accessible to you. Immersed in its current, you must not abandon it unless it abandons you. You will belong to the lineage of faithful thinkers. The giants of labor, the Aristotles, the Augustines, the Albertus Magnus, the Thomas Aquinas, the Leibnizes, the Littrés, the Pasteurs will recognize you as a son, and you will be able to meet with dignity with Him who awaits you patiently.

### 4. Doing Everything and Finishing Everything {#sertillanges-intellectual.8.4}
Having exercised these three virtues, there is no reason to fear that the result will be mediocre and imperfect. However, it is good to emphasize with energy the need to improve and the duty to conclude everything that was considered useful to undertake.

One should reflect before starting a job. It’s nothing but reckless to embark on an adventure, small or big, before taking time to, as the Gospel says, “estimate the cost.” [78](part0018.xhtml#a1JJ) Wisdom demands that one considers the obligation to conclude when deliberating on the opportunity to begin. Not finishing a work is to destroy it. “One who is slack in work is close kin to a vandal,” say the Proverbs. [79](part0018.xhtml#a1JK)

What is the use of a house that was not finished? What testimony does it give about those who built its foundations and the first pillars? Such ruin makes one think of disasters; it is not possible for a living being or a man who is not in disgrace to bear these walls that resemble the broken columns of cemeteries. And will you, builder according to the spirit, want to make your past a field of rubble?

There are people we can count on; when they promise, they deliver; now, every beginning is a promise, if it is not foolishness. Others commit themselves, swear by their gods, and nothing happens; we can affirm that they are not subjects fit for obligations; we cannot force them, nor can they force themselves: they are like water that flows.

Such people morally represent an inferior species; the intellectual who resembles them is not an intellectual, their very vocation condemns them. You, who are consecrated, decide to be faithful. There is a law within you: let it be obeyed. You said, “I will do it,” so do it. A case of conscience is placed before you: resolve it honorably; every unfinished task is a reproach made to you.

I see a cause of decadence in the abandonment of a sketch or an undertaking. Whoever does so becomes accustomed to giving up; takes the side of disorder and bad conscience; becomes one who does and does not. Hence a lowering of dignity that cannot favor progress.

Measure ten times, cut only once; baste carefully, and when the time comes to sew, may nothing in the world make you say: “I resign.”

You should therefore sew, as much as possible, with perfection. Finished means ended, but it also means perfect, and these two meanings corroborate each other. I truly don’t finish what I refuse to do in the best possible way. What is not perfected is nothing. According to Spinoza, being and perfection correspond to the same idea; being and good are convertible.

It is said that Ticiano vigorously sketched a canvas, worked on it until a certain point, and hung the work on the wall until it became somewhat strange to him. Then he would resume it and, casting it a “hostile glance,” produce the masterpiece.

So, when you’ve finished a piece of work, you need to let it rest, refresh your eyes, and take a certain distance from it. If it displeases you then, start over. If it is satisfactory, examine it thoroughly, in all its details, and continue to work on it until you can say, “I have exhausted my resources; as for what is still lacking, may God and my neighbor forgive me.” “Quod potui feci: veniam da mihi, posteritas,” said Leonardo da Vinci in his epitaph.[80](part0018.xhtml#a1H4)

It is not necessary for you to produce much. If what you do corresponds to your genius, your graces, and the time you have; if you are fully engaged in it and if the will of Providence for you has been fulfilled with complete obedience, all is well. You will always do enough if you bring fullness to what you do. What you would do wrong would not add anything to you and would even diminish you, like a stain on a precious silk.

The intellectual vocation does not admit concessions; you must surrender yourself to it entirely. Consecrated to the true God as a whole, your life is His in every situation that constitutes it. Before each detail, say to yourself: “I have the duty to do it, and also to do it well, for what is not completed is nothing. In the same measure that I do it badly, I thwart my life, disobey the Lord, and scorn my brothers. To that extent, I renounce my vocation.” To have a vocation is to have the obligation of perfection.

Here’s a practical piece of advice. When you’ve decided to do a piece of work, when you’ve conceived it well, prepared it well, and you’re ready to start it, determine immediately, with great vigor, the value it should have. And don’t go back on it. When laziness tells you, “Do it any old way, then come back to it,” reply that these returns are almost always illusory. Once you’ve descended, it’s hardly likely you’ll climb back up. You won’t have the courage to rethink ab ovo a mediocre work; your cowardice today is a poor guarantee for tomorrow’s heroism. As for the corrections you could make, even if they were perfect, they would still clash with the whole. A work, in its essence, should come all at once. Beethoven observed that a passage introduced later into a work never harmonizes with its spirit. A beautiful work is a torrent of lava. Titian revisited his works thoroughly, but according to the original form, solely to perfect it; he didn’t modify anything in the conception, composition, or fundamental lines. The effort had already been made; it was a matter of completing it.

Therefore, give your maximum effort at the time of creation. Once the work is generated, treat it like the child that is fed and educated, but whose heredity is already determined, whose fundamental characteristics are already defined. It will then be the moment to apply to your spiritual child the phrase from the Bible: “Those who spare the rod hate their children.” [81](part0018.xhtml#a1DF)

### 5. Not Undertaking Anything Beyond Your Capacity {#sertillanges-intellectual.8.5}
Such self-severity assumes that the undertaken tasks are suitable for you and within the measure of your resources. If the prey is stronger than the hunter, it devours him. It would then be derisory to establish rules. We do not say to the hare hunter: “Approach the leopard in the same way.”

The last of the Thomistic sixteen precepts is this: “Altiora te ne quaesieris,” do not seek what is above you. It is great wisdom. The ancient oracle had already said: “Do not widen your destiny; do not try to surpass the duty imposed on you.” Intellectual work is the extension of our innate tendencies. We are, we act, the work is born: this is the sequence. If you try to prolong lead with iron, cotton with silk, there will be no adhesion, it will be useless work.

Vocation utilizes our resources, it does not create them. The intellectually poorly endowed will end up being a failure; but we also use the expression “poorly endowed” relatively, regarding a particular work. Now, it is of it that we speak.

Seek to discern on each occasion the effort that suits you, the discipline you are capable of, the sacrifice you can make, the subject you can address, the thesis you can write, the book you can take advantage of, the audience you can serve. Consider all of this with humility and confidence. If necessary, seek advice, without forgetting that in advisors, levity and indifference are common. Focus on what you do best. Then, dedicate yourself to your work with all your heart.

Every work is great when measured with precision. The one that overflows is the smallest of all. Many times, we have already said it: your work is unique, as is someone else’s; do not mistake one for the other. Only you can do well what is your responsibility; you will do poorly what your neighbor will do well. God is satisfied with all.

To undertake according to one’s strengths, only to say what one knows, not to force oneself to think what one does not think, to understand what one does not understand, to avoid the danger of eluding the substance of things whose absence is disguised with grandiose words, what wisdom! Pride does not concern itself with this, but pride is an enemy both of the spirit and of conscience. The presumptuous succumbs with his work, ridicules himself, and annihilates his own strength. Unfaithful to himself, he is faithful to nothing; he is an extinguished flame.

Success in any field always results from the same conditions: reflecting at the outset, starting at the beginning, proceeding methodically, advancing slowly, employing all forces. But the primary objective of initial reflection is to determine what we are made for. Socrates’ “know thyself” is not only the key to morality but also to every vocation, for to be called to something is to see a path marked out for oneself among the breadth of human trajectories.
