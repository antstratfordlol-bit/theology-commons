## Chapter 3. The Organization of Life {#sertillanges-intellectual.3}
### 1. Simplifying {#sertillanges-intellectual.3.1}
For everything in you to be oriented towards work, it is not enough to organize yourself internally, define your vocation, and manage your strengths: it is also necessary to order your life, and I think here about the framework in which it unfolds, in your obligations, in your surroundings, in your scenery.

A word presents itself here as a summary of everything: simplify. You have a difficult journey ahead: do not overload yourself with too much baggage. It is certain that you will never have full control of your life, and then, you think, what’s the point of legislating? Wrong! In the same external situation, a spirit of simplification can do a lot, and what is not eliminated externally can always be eliminated from the soul itself.

“You shall not plow with an ox and a donkey yoked together,”[18](part0018.xhtml#a1EA) says the Law: peaceful and wise labor should not be associated with the capricious and noisy attractions of an outer life. A certain asceticism is also somewhat a duty of the thinker. Whether religious or secular, scientific, artistic, literary, contemplation does not mix well with overly burdensome facilities and complications. “Great men have small beds,” notes Henri Lavedan. One must pay a luxury tax for genius. Ten percent of this privilege will not ruin it; it will not be the genius who suffers, but rather our faults, or at least our temptations, and then the profit will be twofold.

To give hospitality to science, rare furniture or numerous domestic employees are not necessary. Plenty of peace, a bit of beauty, certain time-saving amenities, and everything that is needed.

Reduce your social life. Receptions, going outs with the implications of new obligations, ceremonies with neighbors, the entire complicated ritual of an artificial life which many mundane men curse in secret, are not adequate for a worker. The mundane life is fatal to science. Idea and ostentation, idea and the dissipation are mortal enemies. When we think of a genius, we don’t imagine him during dinner.

Do not let yourself be dragged by this mechanism that gradually dissipates time, worries, availabilities, strengths. Prejudices are not your commanders. Be yourself your own guide; obey your convictions, not rituals. An intellectual’s convictions should refer to their goal.

A vocation is a concentration. The intellectual is a consecrated one: who does not scatter in trivialities. Put all your resources in the fire of inspiration, as Bernard Palissy sacrificed his furniture. Work and its conditions, that’s all. Expenses and cares scattered in trifles would be much better used in forming a library, in taking an instructive trip, in restorative vacations, in musical auditions that rejuvenate inspiration, etc.

What favors work is always timely; what hinders and embarrasses it must be excluded, because, besides the immediate inconveniences, you are thus tempted to seek profits, and it disorients your efforts. The priest has the right to live from the altar, and the man of studies, from his work; but one does not say mass for money and one should not, for money, think and produce.

If you are among those who make a living in another profession, and not with the work you chose, how will you manage, if you have an overloaded life, to preserve the few hours you have? It’s a matter of minimizing matter, in order to relieve, to free the spirit.

Regarding this, the wife of an intellectual has a mission that perhaps it is good to point out: many times, she forgets it and, instead of being a Beatrice, she becomes a chatty and wasteful parrot!

Every woman should embrace her husband’s career; the center of gravity of the family is always the father’s work. That’s where productive life is, and therefore the essence of duty. And this is all the truer the nobler and more laborious that career is. Common life then has a peak at its center; and that’s where the woman should settle, instead of trying to distance herself from virile thought. Drawing him into trivialities unrelated to his aspirations will lead her husband to dislike these two lives that contradict each other. Let the daughter of Eve reflect and not give more reason than necessary to Saint Paul’s “divisus et.”[19](part0018.xhtml#a1G1) If the married man is “divided” in a certain way, let him also be duplicated. God gave him “a helper as his partner:”[20](part0018.xhtml#a1G2) let her not become another. Disagreements caused by misunderstanding the sister soul are fatal to production; they make the spirit live in a restlessness that corrodes it; there is no enthusiasm or joy left, and how would the bird fly without its wings, how would the bird and the soul live without its song?

May the guardian of the home not therefore be its evil genius, but rather its muse. Having wedded a vocation, may she also have one. To accomplish it for herself or for her husband, what does it matter? She accomplishes it as well, for she is one flesh with him who accomplishes it. Without the need to be an intellectual, and even less a scholar or an erudite, she can produce much by helping her husband to produce, leading him to control himself, to give his utmost, lifting him in inevitable falls, encouraging him in moments of weakness, consoling him in disappointments without accentuating too much her importance, calming him in sadness, being his sweet reward after labor.

Upon leaving work, the man is like wounded; he needs comfort and calm: do not violate him; quiet and encourage him; be interested in what he has done; expand him in this moment when he is as if reduced by perhaps excessive effort; let yourself finally be to him like a mother, and this strong one, who is all weakness, will feel his strength orient itself towards new torments.

Regarding children, this sweet complication should serve more to renew your courage than to steal your resources. They take much from you, these little ones, and what would they be for if they couldn’t occasionally “take you out of yourself?” But they give you as much or more affection than they demand; they can elevate your inspiration by inflating you with joy; they lovingly reflect to you nature and man, thus defending you from abstraction; they lead you back to reality when their inquisitive little eyes await your precise commentary. Their pure gaze teaches you integrity, the heart of knowledge, and their ease in believing, in hoping, in dreaming big and expecting everything from the parenthood that guides them, isn’t it also for you, thinker, an elevation and a reason for hope? You can see an image of God and a symbol of our immortal destinies in this image of the future.

Those who renounced family to dedicate themselves entirely to their work and He who inspires it have the right to rejoice in it, appreciating the freedoms granted to them because of this sacrifice. They will think of their brothers burdened with worries, repeating Lacordaire’s joyful comment about Ozanam—“He has a problem he couldn’t avoid: marriage.” But the worker engaged in these bonds can and should turn them into a strength, a source of enthusiasm, and one of the forms of their ideal.

### 2. Being Alone {#sertillanges-intellectual.3.2}
In the organization of life, the essential point to guarantee, and towards which everything else must converge, is the external and internal ordering of solitude. Saint Thomas is so convinced of this that, of the sixteen precepts for the intellectual, he devotes seven to relationships and withdrawal. “Be slow to speak and slow to go to the parlor.” “Never concern yourself with the actions of others.” “Be kind to everyone,” but “do not be too familiar with anyone, for excessive familiarity breeds contempt and provides an occasion to withdraw from study.” “Do not involve yourself in any way with the words and actions of laypeople.” “Above all, avoid useless walks.” “Frequent your cell with love, if you wish to be introduced to the wine cellar.”

The wine cellar referred to here, alluding to the Song of Songs and to Saint Bernard’s commentary on this book, is the secret refuge of truth, whose fragrance attracts the Bride from afar, that is, the burning soul; it is the dwelling of inspiration, the home of enthusiasm, of genius, of invention, of passionate pursuit, and the theater of the creations of the spirit and its wise intoxication.

To enter this dwelling, one must abandon banalities, one must practice seclusion, whose symbol is the monastic cell. “In the cells, as through the long corridors,” writes Paul Adam,[21](part0018.xhtml#a1D5) “silence is like a magnificent character, dressed in the whiteness of the walls, keeping watch.” And she keeps watch over prayer and work.

Be slow to speak and slow to make your way to the speaker’s platform, for many words cause the spirit to “overflow like water;” pay with your courtesy to everyone the right to truly frequent those whose company is profitable; even with these, avoid excessive familiarity that belittles and disorients; do not chase after novelties that occupy the mind in vain; do not involve yourself in secular actions and conversations, that is, without moral or intellectual dimension; avoid useless paths that consume hours and favor the vagrancy of thoughts. These are the conditions of sacred retreat. Only thus do we approach the real secrets that are the joy of the Bride; only with such conduct can we present ourselves worthy before the truth.

The retreat and the laboratory of the spirit; inner solitude and silence are its wings. All great works were created in the desert, including the redemption of the world. The forerunners, the continuators, and the Master suffered or must suffer the same demands. Prophets, apostles, preachers, martyrs, pioneers of science, inspired in all arts, simple men, or the God-Man, all paid their tribute to isolation, to silent life, to the night.

It was in the astral night and in the solemn void that the Creator formed the universe: whoever wants to enjoy the joys of creation should not hurry to pronounce the fiat lux, nor above all to review all the animals of the world; let them spend in favorable shadows, like God, the necessary time to arrange the matter of the stars.

The most beautiful corners of nature resonate at night. The nightingale, the frog with a voice of crystal, and the cricket sing in the shadows. The rooster proclaims the day and does not wait for it. All announcers, all poets, as well as researchers and fishermen of scattered truths, must immerse themselves in this great emptiness that is a fullness.

No great man tried to escape it. Lacordaire said he had created in his room, between his soul and God, “a horizon broader than the world,” and that he had generated within himself “the wings of rest.” Emerson proclaimed himself “a savage.” Descartes enclosed himself in his “heated room.” Plato declared that he consumed “more oil in his lamp than wine in his chalice.” Bossuet would rise at night to encounter the genius of silence and inspiration; great thoughts only came to him far from noise and futile concerns. And does every poet not have the impression that his art is to translate into verses the mysterious revelations of silence, which he hears, according to the expression of Gabriele d’Annunzio, as “a voiceless hymn?”

What is valuable must erect a barrier that separates it from everything that is worthless. The mundane life and the ludibria[22](part0018.xhtml#a1CT) of which Saint Augustine spoke, the childish games and quarrels that a kiss calms, must cease with the kiss of the muse, with the intoxicating and soothing caress of truth.

“Why did you come?”, Saint Bernard asked himself about his entrance into the cloister: “Ad quid venisti?” And you, thinker, why have you come to this life separated from everyday life? Why have you come to this life of dedication, of concentration, and therefore of solitude? Wasn’t it because of a choice? Did you not prefer truth to the everyday lie of a life that disperses itself, or even to the lofty but secondary objectives of action? Will you now be unfaithful to the worship you have chosen, allowing yourself to be dragged by what you freely abandoned?

To ensure that the Spirit takes us to the inner solitudes, as Jesus did to the desert, we must offer him ours. Without retreat, there is no inspiration. But under the light of the lamp, as in a firmament, all the stars of thought gather.

When the calm of silence settles within you and only the sacred fire crackles, far from the turmoil of the streets, and when peace, which is the tranquility of order, establishes the order of thoughts, feelings, investigations, you are in the ideal disposition to learn, you are capable of gathering, and then creating; you are precisely on the threshold of the work: it is not a moment to think about miseries, to let time pass, and to sell Heaven for trifles.

Loneliness allows you to be in contact with yourself, contact absolutely necessary if you want to fulfill yourself, you, someone who does not intend to be the parrot of some learned formulas, but the prophet of the inner God who speaks to each one in a unique language.

We will return with more attention to this idea of special instruction for each one, of training that is an education, that is, an expansion of our soul, a unique soul, of which there was not and will not be another like it throughout the ages, for God does not repeat Himself. It is necessary to know, however, that one does not leave oneself in this way except by living with oneself, very closely, in solitude.

The author of The Imitation of Christ used to say: “Never have I been among men without returning less of a man.” Take the idea further and say: without returning less of a man than I was, less of myself. In the crowd, we lose ourselves, unless we stand firm, and for that, it is necessary to create that firmness. In the crowd, we ignore ourselves, covered by a strange self that is a cluster.

“What is your name?”—“Legion,” would be the answer of the spirit dispersed and dissipated in outer life.

Hygienists recommend for the body the bath of water, the bath of air, and the interior bath of pure water; I would add for the soul the bath of silence, in order to tone the spiritual organism, to accentuate its personality and give it the active feeling of it, as the athlete feels his muscles and prepares his performance by the inner movements that are his very life.

Ravignan said, “Solitude is the homeland of the strong, silence is their prayer.” What prayer Truth indeed is and what power of cooperation with its influence is in prolonged withdrawal, frequently resumed, at certain times, like an appointed meeting that should gradually become continuous, a closely common life! “One cannot,” said Saint Thomas, “contemplate all the time; but he who lives for contemplation, who directs everything else towards it and takes it up whenever he can, gives it a kind of continuity, as much as is possible on this Earth.”[23](part0018.xhtml#a1E5)

Also there will be a proper healing, for “that which is well guarded becomes sweet:” cella continuata dulcescit. Now, the sweetness of contemplation is part of its efficacy. Pleasure, explains St. Thomas, tightens the soul around its object, like a clamp; it strengthens attention and expands the capacities of acquisition, which sadness or boredom reduce. When truth takes hold of you, and the feathers of its wings are placed under your soul to lift it with harmonious impulses, it is time to rise with it and, while it sustains you, soar in the highest regions.

You will not become the isolated one we condemned recently; you will not be far from your brothers for having abandoned the noise they make and that spiritually separates them, thus preventing true brotherhood.

The next one, for you, intellectual, is the one who thirsts for truth, as the next of the good Samaritan was the wounded man on the road. Before giving the truth, it is necessary to acquire it; and do not throw away the seed of your sowing.

If the sentence from Imitation is true, far from men you will be more man and will be closer to men. To know humanity and to serve it, one must enter oneself, there where all our objects come into contact with us and receive from us both our strength of truth and our power of love.

So only we can unite with something in inner freedom. Let oneself be trapped, disturbed, whether by people or objects, is to work for disunity. Far from the eyes, close to the heart.

Jesus shows us very well that we can have a whole inner life which is completely dedicated to others, all for men and all in God. He kept his loneliness; he always touched the crowd with a silent soul, whose word is like the narrow door to the commerce of divine charity. And what sovereign efficacy in this contact that reserved everything except the precise space for God to pass and souls to join Him!

There shouldn’t precisely be a place, between God and the multitude, except for the God-Man and the man of God, for the man of truth and of the gift. Those who believe they’re united with God without being united with their brothers are “liars,” said the apostle; [24](part0018.xhtml#a1JG) that man is nothing but a false mystic and, intellectually, a false thinker; but he who is united with men and nature without being united with God in his innermost being, without frequenting silence and solitude, is nothing but a subject of a kingdom of death.

### 3. Cooperating with Others {#sertillanges-intellectual.3.3}
All of our explanations clearly show that the solitude whose praise we have just given is a value that must be tempered with other related values, which complement and utilize it. We do not praise solitude gratuitously. The sacrifice of companionship and sympathy from our brothers has a compensation. We only have the right to splendid isolation. Now, won’t this be all the richer, all the more fruitful, if the superior companionship sought in seclusion is favored by wisely chosen and measured social interactions?

The primary engagement of an intellectual, that which qualifies him in accordance with what he is, without prejudice to his needs and his human duties, is the engagement with his peers. I say engagement, but it would be better to say cooperation, for to engage without cooperating is not characteristic of intellectuals. But how rare is this conjunction of spirits, in these times of individualism and social anarchy! Father Gratry already deplored it; he thought of Port-Royal and wanted to make of the Oratory “a Port-Royal minus the schism.”

“How much suffering could we avoid,” he said, “if we knew how to live united and cooperate! If in groups of six or seven, united in the same thought, we taught each other, becoming reciprocally and alternately students and masters; if even, by I don’t know what stroke of favorable circumstances, we could live together! If, in addition to afternoon classes and studies about them, we talked at night, even during dinner, about all these beautiful things, thus learning even more through conversation and infiltration than through the classes themselves!”[25](part0018.xhtml#a1F6)

The workshops of yore, especially art workshops, were composed of friends, they were families: the workshop today is a prison, or else a syndicate. But couldn’t we create, pressed by a need that grows ever more in our environment, an extended family workshop, open and no longer as concentrated as before? Would this be the moment to conceive and found the intellectual workshop, an association of enthusiastic and diligent workers, freely gathered, living in simplicity, in equality, without any of them seeking to impose themselves on the others, even if they had a recognized superiority, which would be precious for the group. Far from any competition and pride, seeking only truth, friends thus gathered would multiply each other, so to speak, and the common soul would have a materially inexplicable richness.

It takes a very strong soul to work alone! Being alone amidst an intellectual society, being one’s own stimulus and support, finding in a poor isolated desire as much strength as there can be in a mass movement or in harsh necessity, is true heroism! In the beginning there is much enthusiasm; then, with difficulties, the demon of laziness arises: “What for?” Our vision of the goal weakens; the fruits are too distant or seem bitter to us; we feel vaguely deceived. It is certain that someone’s support, cooperation, example, would have an extraordinary effectiveness against this spleen; they would give to many that power of imagination and that constancy of virtue which are the privilege of few and yet are necessary for the constant pursuit of a great goal.

In convents, where there are no conversations, where there are no visits, the influence of a series of laborious cells animates and invigorates each ascetic; these seemingly isolated alveoli form a beehive; silence is collective and work is joint; the agreement of souls ignores the walls; the same spirit reigns, and the harmony of thoughts encompasses each of them like a symphonic motif that the general wave of sounds carries and prolongs. When dialogues then intervene, the concert enriches; each one expresses and listens, learns and teaches, receives and gives, and receives according to what they give, and perhaps this last aspect of cooperation should be the most coveted.

Friendship is maieutic; it extracts from us our richest and most intimate resources; it opens the wings of our dreams and our obscure thoughts; it controls our judgments, experiments with our new ideas, preserves ardor, and inflames enthusiasm.

We have an example of this today in young groups and in young magazines, whose dedicated members take on a task and devote themselves to an idea. The Cahiers de la Quizaine were born from this vocation; Amitié de France, Revue des Jeunes, Revues de Juvisy and du Saulchoir increasingly imbibe this reality. In them, one does not always live together, but one works with a single heart, some cooperating with others, some correcting others, all protected and provoked at the same time by an environment whose essence is due to a creative thought or a great tradition.

Seek, if you can, to join a brotherhood of this kind, or create one if necessary.

In any case, even in material isolation, seek in spirit the company of friends of truth. Join your groups, sit friendly with them and with all seekers, with all workers that Christianity gathers. The communion of saints is not a phalanstery; it is, however, a unity. “The flesh”—by itself—“is useless;”[26](part0018.xhtml#a1FA) the spirit alone can do something. Useful unanimity consists less in being gathered with a group in a prison or in a cataloged group than in each one striving, with the feeling that others also strive, focusing on something that others also focus on, so that a task is accomplished, so that the same principle of life and activity presides, and so that the pieces of the clock, each being the exclusive object of attention of a worker in his workshop, have God as the assembler.

### 4. Cultivating the Necessary Relationships {#sertillanges-intellectual.3.4}
I have also said that the solitude of the thinker does not exclude his duties, nor does it ignore his needs. There are necessary relations. And since they are necessary, they are part of his life, even as an intellectual, for we do not separate the intellectual from the man. It is up to you to link them to intellectuality in such a way that not only do they not hinder it, but they are put at its service.

One can always do it. The time dedicated to duty or to a real necessity is never lost; the care devoted to them is a part of vocation and would only be contrary to it if it were considered abstractly, without taking into account Providence.

“We should not think,” wrote Maine de Biran in his diary, “that the only and best use of time consists of work of the mind, regulated, continuous, and calm. Every time we act well, in accordance with the situation in which we find ourselves, we make good use of our life.”

Don’t think that your work is worth more than you, and that even a supplement of intellectual possibilities can prevail over your own improvement. What must be done, what needs to be done, do it; if your humanity demands it, it will know well how to integrate it into itself. Good is the brother of the true: it will help its brother. Being where one should be, doing what one should do, is to prepare for contemplation, to nourish it, and to leave God to the sake of God, as St. Bernard used to say.

It is painful to sacrifice precious hours in companionships and matters that in themselves are inferior to our ideal; but as the course of this world is also made to ally itself with virtue, we must think that virtue will extract from it its part, intellectual virtue or moral virtue. On certain days, it will be solely through morality that intellectuality will earn its bread, despite its virtuous concessions; in other circumstances, it will earn it by itself.

Therefore, do not forget that in social interactions, even in the most mundane ones, there is always something to be gleaned. Excessive loneliness impoverishes. Someone recently wrote: “It seems to me that the problem of today’s novelists is this: if they don’t travel around the world, their books are unreadable, and if they do, they don’t have time to write them.” Anguish of measurement, which we always encounter! But novelist or not, you must feel very well that you cannot isolate yourself completely. Not even monks do that. It is necessary to preserve, in view of work, the feeling of common soul, of life, and how does one it if, breaking the relationship with men, they live only with a thought-out humanity?

The man too isolated becomes shy, abstract, a little bizarre; hesitates in the real world like a newly disembarked sailor; no longer has the sense of his destiny; seems to look at us as a “proposition” that must be inserted into a syllogism, or as an example to be recorded in a notebook.

The infinite wealth of reality also has much to teach us; it is necessary to approach it with a spirit of contemplation, but not to abandon it. And isn’t it what is most important for us in reality not man himself, the man who is the center of everything, the ultimate purpose of everything, the mirror of everything, and who invites the thinker of any specialty to a permanent confrontation?

As much as we can choose, we should organize our lives in order to be as much as possible in contact with superior people. Also, the wife of an intellectual should be attentive to this. Let her not open the door of the house to just anyone; let her tact be like a sieve; in the company of high society, prioritize dealing with great souls; those known as “witty,” prefer people of substance, educated and of firm judgment, aware that in the world one is considered all the more a man of spirit the more one has destroyed one’s own intelligence. And above all, let her not, through levity, vanity, or any inconsequential interest, lead her husband to the homes of fools.

What do I say? Fools also contribute to serving us and to completing our experience. But do not seek them out—they are legion! However, those whom you encounter, know how to use them: intellectually, as a sort of counterproof, and, humanely, Christianly, for the exercise of virtues of which they become the patrons.

Society is a book that must be read, even though it is a banal book. Solitude is a masterpiece; but remember what Leibniz said, that he would never find a book so bad that it could not offer him something. You do not think alone, as you do not think only with intelligence. Your intelligence associates with your other faculties, your soul with your body, your person with your relationships; all this is your thinking being: compose it in the best possible way; and may even your quirks, like your illnesses, become values, by some happy industry of your greatness of soul.

Otherwise, in your dealings, behave in such a way that your spirit and your heart always dominate the situation: thus, you will neither be invaded nor contaminated when the environment is mediocre, and, should it be noble, what you will do is reinforce within yourself the effects of solitude, the love for truth, and the lessons it gives you.

Our contacts with the outside should be like those of the angel, who touches and is not touched, unless they want to; who gives and loses nothing, since they belong to another world.

Through moderation of speeches, you will also achieve that constancy of introspection and that wisdom in relationships which is so urgently needed. Speaking to say what needs to be said, to express a timely sentiment or a useful idea, and then remaining silent, is the secret to preserving oneself without ceasing to communicate, without allowing the flame to extinguish, to ignite other flames.

It’s also the means to give weight to your words. Speech has weight when we perceive the silence beneath it, when it hides and allows us to guess, behind the words, a treasure that it distributes as it sees fit, without haste and without frivolous agitation. Silence is the secret content of weighty words. What gives value to a soul is the richness of what it does not say.

### 5. Preserving the Necessary Dose of Action {#sertillanges-intellectual.3.5}
What we said about frequentations applies to action with a few touches. It is always a matter of dosing inner life with outer life, silence with noise.

THE INTELLECTUAL LIFE

The intellectual vocation, taken strictly, is the opposite of action; contemplative life and active life have always been opposed as resulting from contrary thoughts and aspirations. Contemplation gathers, action expends; one attracts light, the other covets the gift.

Speaking in a general way, it is evident that we must resign ourselves to the division of tasks, each one contenting oneself with praising what one does not do, loving its fruits, and enjoying them thanks to the communion of souls. But real life does not allow such a rigorous separation.

Duty may demand action as it recently demanded social life, and it must also benefit from our observations. Action guided by conscience prepares that very conscience for the canons of truth, predisposes it to introspection when its time has come, unites it with Providence, which is also the source of truth. Thought and action have the same Father.

Furthermore, even without a specific obligation, it is always necessary for the thinker to reserve a part of their time and heart for active life. This part is sometimes small; in the wise, it is never null. The monk works with his hands or dedicates himself to acts of zeal; the good doctor has his clinic, his hospital; the artist has his exhibitions, his society, his tours, or his lectures; the writer is solicited in so many ways that he would have difficulty not engaging in any commitment.

All this is good, for if in this world everything has its measure, inner life must also have its own. It requires that action be limited and give way to solitude, because external action agitates the soul, and silence calms it; but if silence is taken too far, it also starts to agitate; the convergence of all man in his head disorients and causes dizziness; diversion is indispensable to cerebral life; we need the balm of action.

There are physiological reasons for this, about which I will not speak; psychological reasons support them and reduce themselves even to them, since the soul, while distinct from the body, does not know fatigue. But the animated compound tires of both rest and wear; it requires a balance whose center of gravity may indeed shift and vary from man to man. The motionless body soon atrophies and becomes enervated; the soul that imitates it atrophies and becomes irritated. By force of cultivating silence, one can end up in the silence of death.

On the other hand, intellectual life needs the nourishment of facts. We find facts in books; but everyone knows that a purely bookish science is fragile; it suffers from the evil of abstraction; it loses any contact and, consequently, offers to judgment a very diaphanous, almost illusory matter. “You are a captive balloon,” Amiel said to himself; “do not let the cable that binds you to the earth break.”

St. Thomas dedicates an article of the Summa to prove the need to rely on reality to judge, because, he says, reality is the ultimate end of judgment; now, the purpose must shed its light on the entire journey.[27](part0018.xhtml#a1C2)

Ideas are in facts, they do not live by themselves, as Plato wished: this metaphysical view has practical consequences. The thinking man must remain close to what is, otherwise his spirit falters. What is a dream if not a thought separated from communication with the outside, a thought that wants nothing? The inconsistent dream is the reef of pure thought; we must discard it, for it is a cause of impotence and downfall. Thought relies on facts like feet on the ground, like the sick on crutches.

The dose of action suitable for the thinker will then have this advantage: to stabilize his spirit. It will also have the advantage of enriching it. How many experiences life offers us every day! We let them pass, but the deep thinker gathers them and with them composes his treasure; his spiritual pictures will gradually be filled with them, and his general ideas, controlled on one side, will be on the other side illustrated with a living documentation.

The idea, deprived of its experiential elements, of its ghosts, and in us just an empty concept, which is not even possible to perceive. As ghosts are rich, thought is broad and strong. Now, action always finds in its path assimilable elements and “fragments of life,” which will be the figuration of its abstract ideas. It finds in them moreover more than it can use, for reality is a kind of infinity that no analysis, no rational computation is capable of embracing.

Place an artist before a tree; he will make countless sketches, and will never think that he has entirely translated what nature expresses; put him before a tree sketch, or even before a tree by Claude Lorrain or Corot: if he copies it meticulously, he will have exhausted the model.

The individual is ineffable, said the ancient philosophers. The individual is the real, in opposition to the themes of the spirit. Diving into reality through action, we find in this matter new forms, as the artist, in realizing his conception, nurtures it, rectifies it, and completes it.

Finally, this instructor who is action and at the same time a teacher of energy, whose classes will not be useless to a loner. Through its proposals and its resistances, through its difficulties, setbacks, successes, through the boredom and fatigue it forces us to overcome, through the contradictions it does not fail to emphasize and the new needs it creates, it stimulates us and restores our strength; it aids that fundamental laziness and that proud stillness that are no less hostile to thought than achievements.

The outer virtues will thus come to the aid of the inner virtues; active investigation will serve withdrawal; gathering will prepare honey. Thought, alternately plunged into the two abysses, that of the real and that of the ideal, fortified by a resolute will, illuminated and forewarned by the reasons of the heart that action constantly puts into play, will be an instrument of research and an arbiter of truth very different from a reason perched on the tree of Porphyry.

I’d like to see our scholarly man always involved with an endeavor that is little burdensome, to which he would dedicate a well-defined time, without yielding to excitement, but genuinely interested, and wholeheartedly, in the results, which should be to him like the firewood that some split to rest their heads. To act without fully surrendering to action is not to act like a man, and neither the repose of man, nor his instruction, nor his formation can result from it. Therefore, if you don’t have causes that impose themselves on you, seek those that can impassion you by virtue of their value, works of light, of ascension, of preservation, of progress, leagues for the public good, societies of defense and social action, any endeavor worthy of a man dedicating himself to it, if not a lifetime, at least his whole being. Dedicate to it the hours when inspiration asks for or even demands a pause for your own benefit. Then, you will resume it, and the Heaven to which it will lead you will be much sweeter for having experienced, along with its treasures, the dangers, the mires, and the harshness of the Earth.

### 6. Maintaining Inner Silence in Everything {#sertillanges-intellectual.3.6}
It seems to me that all this results in useful solitude, silence, the withdrawal of the thinker, are realities mitigated, and animated by a very demanding spirit. And in view of the withdrawal, silence, and intimate solitude that action and social interactions are admitted, and it is by them that they are measured. And that’s how it should be, if the intellectual is a consecrated one, and if “no one can serve two masters.”[28](part0018.xhtml#a1ER)

The spirit of silence, therefore, will always be required. And above all, it is the most important. It is common to say that solitude is the mother of works. Not she, but the state of solitude. So much so that we can, strictly speaking, conceive an intellectual life based on a two-hour daily work. You think that having fulfilled these two hours, you could then behave as if they did not exist? It would be a great mistake. These two hours are dedicated to concentration, but the consecration of the whole life is still demanded.

An intellectual must be an intellectual all the time. What Saint Paul advises to the Christian: “whether you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do everything for the glory of God,”[29](part0018.xhtml#a1EV) should also apply to the Christian who seeks enlightenment. The glory of God is for him the truth: he must think about it wherever he is, submit to it in all things. The solitude recommended to him is less a local solitude than a solitude of withdrawal; it is more elevation than separation; it consists in isolating oneself from above, thanks to the gift of oneself to higher things and by fleeing from whims, digressions, movement, and all capricious will; it realizes the Apostle’s conversatio nostra in caelis,[30](part0018.xhtml#a1EW) elevating our dwelling and our relations to the Heaven of spirits.

Staying at home and surrendering to inner chatter, the attraction of desires, the exaltation of pride, to the flow of thoughts that introduce in us an absorbing exterior full of discord, would this be true loneliness? There is a false loneliness as there is a false peace. On the contrary, going out and acting out of duty, out of wisdom, or to provoke a relaxation whose need we will emphasize later, can be a superior loneliness that nourishes and tones the soul instead of diminishing it.

What Saint Augustine calls “the purity of solitude” can be preserved anywhere; its impurity can contaminate your own refuge. “You may be in a city,” wrote Plato, “as a shepherd in his cabin atop a mountain.” Maintain inner inspiration, reserve, love for what you have committed to, keep the true God with you, and you will be alone in the full universe.
