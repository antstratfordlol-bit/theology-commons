## Chapter 6. The Spirit of Work {#sertillanges-intellectual.6}
### 1. Zeal of Investigation {#sertillanges-intellectual.6.1}
Having delimited the field of work, it is good to define the spirit that should animate the worker, and it is mainly, before any particular mode of its application, a spirit of zeal. “Clarify your doubts,” says Saint Thomas to his disciple.

An active spirit is constantly in search of some truth that is, for him, at the present moment, the figure of this integral truth to which he dedicates his worship. Intelligence is like a child, on whose lips the “whys” never cease to sprout. Does a good educator refrain from satisfying this fruitful restlessness? Does he not take advantage of a new curiosity, as of a new appetite, to solidly nourish the nascent spiritual organism? Our soul does not age; it is always growing; in the face of truth, it is always a child. Since we ourselves are responsible for its permanent education, we should not, as far as possible, leave any of the problems that arise in our work without a solution, nor any of our investigations without an appropriate conclusion.

May the studious man always be attentive to the truth. While he is immersed in his task, the Spirit blows within him, and also reveals itself from the outside, sending its prophets, men, things, books, events: the attentive soul must neglect nothing; this spirit of truth, like grace, often passes and does not return. Is he himself, however, not grace?

The great enemy of knowledge is our indolence; and this original laziness that detests effort, that likes to capriciously involve itself in this or that, and then returns to negligent automatism, considering vigorous and continuous movement a true martyrdom. Perhaps it is indeed a martyrdom, given our constitution; but we must be prepared for this martyrdom or renounce study, for what can be done without virile energy? “You, God, sell all goods to men at the cost of effort,” wrote Leonardo da Vinci in his notes. He himself never forgot this.

The spirit is like the aircraft that can only remain in the heights advancing with all the power of its propellers. To stop is to fall. On the other hand, a tenacious fervor can take us beyond all the limits foreseen in our dreams. We do not know how plastic intelligence is and how susceptible it is to development. Bossuet said, “The spirit of man can create infinitely; only his laziness puts limits on his wisdom and his inventions.” What we consider a barrier often amounts to nothing more than the undergrowth of our defects and sensual negligence. Between conceiving and projecting, projecting and executing, executing and finishing, how many procrastinations, how many falls! The habit of effort brings these stages closer and allows one to pass from conception to completion by a fast route. The strong man raises before himself Jacob’s ladder, for the ascents and descents of the angels who visit us.

Some spirits are quickly satisfied with what they have already acquired. Having worked initially, they ended up losing the feeling of their emptiness. They do not remember that we are always empty of what we do not yet have and that, in an unlimited field of discoveries, we can never say “enough!” If the objective is only ostentation or to obtain some advantage, a small stock of thoughts may suffice. Many thus use a thin screen to hide from themselves and others a vast ignorance. But a real vocation is not satisfied with so little; it considers everything it has acquired as a starting point. To know, to seek, to know again, and to distribute in order to seek anew, that is the life of a man consecrated to truth, just as acquiring, whatever his current fortune may be, is the goal of the miser. The sincere intellectual says to the God of truth every day: “It is zeal for your house that has consumed me.”[45](part0018.xhtml#a1HP)

And especially in maturity, we must be attentive to this temptation. We know the stories of those we call “bonzos,” old sages suffocated by honors, who are crushed by demands and who lose in ceremonies the time they once devoted to study. They are well-equipped, and they no longer produce; they enjoy a high reputation, and yet they are nothing but a shadow of themselves. It was said of the painter Henner, at the end of his life: “He only paints false Henners.” I do not subscribe to this judgment, but the phrase is cruel and, for all those to whom it can be applied, it is terrible. We must not conceal that, even among the young, we observe this premature senility which, happy with a real or apparent discovery, tirelessly exploits it and wastes, in stretching an ever-thinner thread, the attention that would be better employed in melting an ingot or minting a medal.

A true thinker approaches his work with a spirit totally different; and moved by an instinct of conquest, by a flame, an impulse, and a heroic inspiration. A hero does not stop, does not know limits. A Guynemer considers a victory as a rehearsal for a new victory; as if he were omnipotent, he flies, restarts, reaches the opponent, turns against another, and only in death sees the end of his career.

It is necessary to always seek, to always strive. Nature leads the wild tree to bloom again, the star to shine, the water to flow, descending the slopes, circumventing obstacles, filling voids, dreaming of the sea that awaits it down there, where it may arrive. Creation is at all its stages a continuous aspiration: the spirit, which potentially is all things, cannot limit its ideal forms by itself, just as natural forms cannot, which are its reflection. Death will limit it, and also its impotence: may at least its courage break the lazy borders. The infinity that lies before us calls for the infinity of our desire to repair as much as possible the fainting of our strength.

### 2. Focus {#sertillanges-intellectual.6.2}
This spirit of zeal must reconcile itself with the focus that all men of deep thought recommend to us. Nothing more disastrous than dispersion. To spread the light is to weaken it in geometrically increasing proportions. On the contrary, concentrate it with a magnifying glass and what was only warmed by free radiation burns in the focus where the heat intensifies.

May your spirit learn to be a magnifying glass thanks to convergent attention; may your soul be wholly focused on what exists in you in the state of dominant idea, of absorbing idea. Arrange your work so that you can fully dedicate yourself to it. May each task absorb you deeply, as if it were the only one. That was Napoleon’s secret; and the secret of all great men of action. Geniuses themselves were only great by applying all their strength to the point they had decided to focus on.

It is necessary to take each thing in itself, do it in the time it demands, gather the conditions it asks for, dedicate to it the fullness of the resources available, and, once it is accomplished, pass calmly to another. Thus, one accumulates incredibly without losing oneself in agitation.

It’s not that we can’t have several jobs underway: that’s even necessary, because to have a certain distance, to evaluate oneself better and correct what is necessary, to rest from an effort dedicating oneself to another, perhaps also for accidental reasons, we cannot avoid interruption and task change. So what we said about concentration applies to each job and each time we resume it. At the moment we dedicate ourselves to one, we have to exclude any other, establish a system of watertight compartments, delve deeply into the point addressed and only then move on to another.

The ups and downs do not yield good results. The traveler who hesitates and successively takes different paths exhausts himself, becomes discouraged, and does not progress. On the contrary, continuity on one path and energetic restarts followed by timely pauses, namely when the first phase of action is fulfilled, is the means to produce to the maximum, and at the same time, keep the mind alive, courage intact. The soul of a true worker, despite his worries and successive multiplicity, should always, between two fervent assaults on an obstacle, be serene and noble like a group of clouds on the horizon.

Let’s add that this law of all activity is reinforced when it comes to pure thought, because of the unity of truth and the importance of keeping all its elements under our eyes, so that clarity is achieved. Each idea, however little truly unified, is infinitely rich; related to all others, it can regenerate incessantly. As long as these enlightening dependencies present themselves, as long as truth shines, do not distract your gaze: keep in your hand the thread that guides you through the labyrinth; sow the seed of fruitful thought, then the seed of the new plant. Do not neglect either the cultivation or the seeds: one germ alone is worth an entire plantation.

All the works of a well-formed mind should be developments of a single thought, of a feeling of life that seeks its forms and its applications. Bergson has not long since told us: “A philosopher worthy of the name never said more than one thing.” With even more reason all the operations of a defined period, of an enterprise, of a study session, must be oriented, gathered under strict discipline. Digging always the same trench is the way to descend deeply and extract from the earth its secrets.

One of the effects of this concentration will be to make choices in the midst of the confused mass that almost always appears in our early research. Little by little, the essential connections present themselves, and it is above all in this that consists the secret of powerful works. Value is never in multiplicity; it is in the relationships of some elements that govern the whole, or the whole being, which provide us with their law and thus allow us to make an original creation, a relevant and far-reaching work about it. A few well-chosen facts or some strong ideas—strong in their coherence and their connections more than in their content—are sufficient material for a genius production. Directing investigations well and centralizing one’s work is the whole art of the great; and it is what, following their example, everyone should try to do in order to reach the limit of oneself.

### 3. Submission to Truth {#sertillanges-intellectual.6.3}
But yet another thing is even more important: to submit not only to the discipline of work, but also to the discipline of truth, an indispensable condition to intimately relate to it. Prompt obedience: that is what truth demands from us. In this solemn union, we must participate with a respectful soul. Truth only reveals itself to those who are already stripped and very determined to dedicate themselves solely to it. The intellect that does not surrender lives in a state of skepticism, and the skeptic is ill-prepared for truth. Discovery is an effect of sympathy; now, sympathy is also a gift.

Through thought we find something, we do not create it; not submitting to it is like not having found it, and not submitting beforehand prevents it from being found. Yielding to truth and expressing it in the best way we can, but without any criminal alteration, we exercise a worship to which the inner God and the universal God will respond by revealing to us that they are the same God and establishing a society with our soul. In this, as in everything else, self-will is the great enemy of God.

This submission presupposes humility, and we must recall here what we said about the importance of virtues in the realm of intelligence, for all virtues are based on the exclusion of the proud personality, which repudiates order. Intellectually, pride is the father of aberrations and false creations; humility is the gaze that reads the book of life and the book of the universe.

We could define the study by saying: it’s God becoming conscious within us of His own work. Just as all action, intellect comes from God and returns to God through us. God is its first cause; and its ultimate purpose; between one and the other, our overflowing emotion can create a deviation. Let’s open our eyes with wisdom, so that our inspiring Spirit sees.

Our intellect is fundamentally a passive power; we are intellectually powerful to the extent that we are receptive. It’s not that we shouldn’t react; but the vital reaction, which we’ll talk about more later, shouldn’t change anything in the content of our acquisitions; it simply makes them ours. A great culture, enriching a spirit, opens up new paths and increases its capacity; but, without humility, this attraction exerted by the outside world will also be a source of lies. On the contrary, to the cultivated and humble spirit, lights come from all sides, interpenetrating like dawn on high mountains.

Besides humility, it’s advisable for the thinker to have a certain passivity of attitude that corresponds to the nature of the spirit and inspiration. We know little about the spirit; but we know that passivity is its first law. We know even less about the ways of inspiration, but we can observe that it uses in us more unconsciousness than initiatives. We advance through obstacles like a knight in the middle of the night: it’s better to trust our mount than to pull indiscriminately on its reins.

Too much voluntary activity makes our intelligence less secure and less receptive; too agitated, we remain in ourselves, and to understand and become another is to receive a precious gift. Try to think about the object of science, not yourself, in the same way that when we speak, we do it in space, not inside our own cavities. Singers know what I mean; those who have experienced inspiration can also understand me. One must look through the spirit to things, and not in the spirit, more or less forgetting about things. In the spirit is that through which one sees, but not that which is seen: let the means not distract us from the end.

“The essential is to be in ecstasy,” wrote the fresco painter Louis Dussour, “while at the same time seeking to understand how everything intertwines and is built.” Sometimes ecstasy is lacking, sometimes construction. And here we speak of the former.

Here’s the deep work: allowing oneself to be penetrated by truth, to be submerged in it gently, to drown; no longer thinking that we think, nor that we exist, nor that anything exists, except for truth itself. This is the blessed ecstasy.

For Saint Thomas, ecstasy is the offspring of love; it transports us outward, to the object of our dreams: to love truth so ardently that we concentrate on it and thus transport ourselves to the universal, that which is, to the bosom of permanent truths, and the attitude of contemplation and fruitful production. Then we will be turned towards ourselves, but with attention on the prey, like a predator, and inner life will be intense, but with a sense of extraordinary distances, as if we were circulating among the stars. We feel expanded and chained at the same time, free and enslaved; we are fully ourselves surrendering to something much higher than us; we exalt ourselves by losing ourselves: it is the nirvana of ravished and powerful intelligence.

Do not go, therefore, if visited by that spirit, discouraging and expelling it by doing a completely artificial and exterior work. If it is absent, hasten its return with humble prayers. Under divine radiance, you will gain much more in a short time than you would by dedicating yourself to your abstract thoughts. “For a day in your courts is better than a thousand elsewhere.” [46](part0018.xhtml#a1DH)

Avoid as much as possible the return of voluntary activity, the awakening of the Wife. Let your spirit be the wax, not the seal, so that the seal of truth remains pure. Practice holy abandonment; obey God; be like the inspired poet, like the speaker whom an inner wave lifts and in whom thought no longer weighs.

On the other hand, having to receive from men through reading, teaching, and socializing, take advantage of this golden rule inserted by St. Thomas in the sixteen precepts: “Do not consider from whom you hear things, but whatever good is said, trust it to your memory.”

The history of science is full of resistance from one talent to another, from one genius to another, from one brotherhood to another, from one association to another. Laennec opposes Broussais, Pouchet to Pasteur; Lister has all of England against him, Harvey has all of humanity over forty. As if the truth were too exuberant and we needed to prevent its proliferation. While the laws of the world subjugate matter, why does the spirit have so much difficulty in submitting to the spirit?

In the First Epistle to the Corinthians (Ch. 14), it is written that if anything is revealed to the least of the faithful in prayer, the others should keep silent and listen to him. St. Thomas reflects on this phrase as follows, “No one, no matter how wise they may be, should reject the doctrine of another, no matter how small it may be,”[47](part0018.xhtml#a1JM) which relates to another Pauline counsel: “Do nothing from selfish ambition or conceit, but in humility regard others as better than yourselves.”[48](part0018.xhtml#a1JN) And at a certain moment, one is superior who is closer to the truth, receiving its light from it.

What matters in a thought is not its provenance, but its dimensions; what is interesting in genius itself is neither Aristotle, nor Leibniz, nor Bossuet, nor Pascal, but the truth. The more precious an idea is, the less it matters where it comes from. Elevate indifference towards sources. Only truth has rights, and it has rights wherever it presents itself. Just as one should not be a vassal to anyone, even less should one despise anyone, and if it is not opportune to believe in everyone, we should also not refuse to believe in anyone, as long as they present their credentials.

In this lies the great freedom, and its reward is so ample that avarice itself would act thus, if it did not believe itself better inspired guarding its coffers. We like to believe that we possess everything, that we are capable of everything, and only listen distractedly to foreign voices. Only a few privileged ones, men or books, deserve our attention and serve as our inspiration. Now, in truth, inspiration is all around; the Spirit blows both in the valleys and on the peaks. In the poorest intelligence there is a reflection of infinite Wisdom, and deep humility knows how to recognize it.

How does one not feel in the presence of God when a man is teaching? Is he not His image? An image sometimes deformed, but often authentic; and this deformation is always partial. Asking ourselves at what price we can operate its restoration and to what extent righteousness is maintained would be a much more fruitful work than shrugging or opposing with harshness. It is always vain to oppose; it is much more worthwhile to reflect. Wherever the God of truth has left something of Himself, there we must be ready to gather, religiously venerate its fruits, and use them diligently. Where the eternal sower has passed, must we not gather?

### 4. Developments {#sertillanges-intellectual.6.4}
Finally, to ennoble the spirit of work, it is necessary to add to the fervor, concentration, submission, an effort of expansion that gives to each study or each production a dimension in a certain way total.

A problem cannot remain enclosed in itself; it overflows by its very nature, as the intelligibility it invokes comes from sources that are far above it. What we have said about comparative science can enlighten us here. Each object of our study belongs to a set in which it acts and undergoes actions, a set that has certain conditions and to which the object imposes its own; it cannot be studied separately. What is called specialization or analysis may well be a method, but it should not be a spirit. The worker cannot be a victim of his own stratagem. I isolate a piece of a mechanism in order to examine it better; but while I have it in my hands and observe it with my eyes, my thought must keep it in its place, see it in the whole to which it belongs, otherwise I alter the truth of the whole, which remains incomplete, and of the gear, which becomes incomprehensible.

The truth is one; everything belongs to the unique supreme truth; between a particular object and God, there are all the laws of the world, whose amplitude increases from the norm applied to a certain object to the eternal Axiom. On the other hand, also the spirit of man is one; his formation could not be satisfied with the lie of specialties considered as a dismemberment of the true and the beautiful into scattered fragments. However restricted his investigation may be, however narrow the field in which he concentrates, it is the whole man and the whole universe that are at stake. Both the subject and the object aim at the universal. To truly study one thing is to gradually evoke the feeling of all others and their solidarity, and to participate in the symphony of beings, to unite with the universe and oneself.

We were talking about focus a moment ago; but it was evident that we did not intend in any way to restrict the study. To concentrate and to expand are not, like systole and diastole, but one and the same movement. I call focus the convergence of attention on a point; I call expansion the feeling that this point is the center of a vast whole, and even the center of the whole, for in the immense sphere “the center is everywhere and the circumference is nowhere.”[49](part0018.xhtml#a1JD)

Our spirit has this dual tendency: to unify the details to reach a comprehensive synthesis; to lose in detail the feeling of unity, forgetting it. We must balance these two inclinations. The first corresponds to the objective of science, the second to our weakness. We must isolate to deepen, but then we must unite, in order to understand.

While working, therefore, do not set the aim too low. Think big. Have the soul of one who examines carefully and discerns the small sprouts of truth, and above all, do not belittle the sublime questions. Feel yourself in contact with the great secrets, in the atmosphere of the great beings; perceive the light that filters here and there, but beyond, in continuity with that faint stream, floods the universe and joins the pure Source.

Corot does not paint a tree ignoring the horizon; Velásquez places his girls in full Escorial, in full life, it would be even more appropriate to say in full being, for it is this feeling of the mystery of being that makes of this prodigious talent a genius that haunts the soul and enchants the eyes. It is a rule of the art of painting that one should think above all about the section that is not painted, and that the painting itself must yield to the character, the overall dimension of the subject, its expansion beyond the canvas.

The artist, leaning over the smallest detail, must be in a state of universal rapture; the writer, the philosopher, the speaker, in a state of universal thought and emotion. Placing a finger on a point of the world map, we must feel its entire extent and roundness. And it is always about the whole that one speaks.

Escape from the spirits that never manage to escape from schooling, that are slaves of work instead of placing it before them, in full light. Allowing oneself to be imprisoned by narrow formulas and one’s spirit to petrify in bookish forms is a sign of an inferiority that clearly contradicts intellectual vocation. Helots or eternal children: this is how these supposed workers should be called, those we see displaced in any higher region, before every broader horizon, and who would very much like to reduce others to their orthodoxy of simplistic mediocrity.

The genius is in seeing in the work what is beyond it, in the books what they could not say. The between the lines of a great text are its true treasure; they suggest, imply that nothing is strange to the deepest thoughts of man. Instead of reducing them, emptying them, extract from restricted subjects what gives them a solid substance, that is, what does not belong to them, but is common to them and to others, to them and to all, like light is common to colors and their distribution through objects.

The ideal would be to establish in your spirit a common life of thoughts that interpenetrate and form, so to speak, a single thought. In God it is like this; could we find a better model to guide our poor science?

The spirit of contemplation and prayer that we have already recommended naturally brings us closer to that state; it contains within itself that fruit. Adopting God’s point of view, through which each thing obtains its supreme meaning and all things their cohesion, we feel ourselves at the center of everything, instigated by inexhaustible riches and possibilities.

If we reflect well, we will see that the kind of dazzlement that takes us before a new truth comes from the suggestion of these undefined perspectives and these universal relations that it supposes. This unique step in the direction of truth is like a luminous excursion. We see the world in a new light; we feel the whole pulsating upon touching the fragment found. Later, this idea, brought far away from the confines from which it was the precursor, may seem paltry to the same one who was dazzled; evoking nothing but itself, it loses life, extinguishes the feeling of the infinite which is the soul of all seeking.

The great men suffered from this drying up of thoughts. Their vision was grand: they found their results diminutive. Therefore, we must read them, also them, with a spirit not literal, not bookish, but with a spirit of transcendence, which will place them in the place that is proper to them. The letter kills: may reading and study be spirit and life.

### 5. Sense of Mystery {#sertillanges-intellectual.6.5}
It is essential that the sense of mystery never extinguishes, even when we exhaust our efforts and even when it seems that truth has smiled upon us. Anyone who thinks they have understood everything proves, thereby, that they have understood nothing. Anyone who is satisfied with provisional answers to problems that are always in question falsifies the given answer, not realizing that it is partial. Every question is an enigma that nature—and through it, God—poses to us: what God poses, only God can answer. The doors of the infinite are always open. What is most precious in each thing is what is not expressed in it. Biot, approached by a colleague in these terms: “I want to ask you an interesting question,” replied: “Useless; if your question is interesting, I don’t have the answer.” “We don’t know everything about anything,” said Pascal, and “to fully understand a thing,” adds Claude Bernard, “one would need to know everything.” We can say of the complete truth about any subject what Saint Augustine said of God: “If you understand, say to yourself that it is not that.” But the small-minded spirit believes it encompasses the cosmos and all its richness; holding in its hands a bucket with three liters of sparkling water, it says, “Look, I managed to capture the sea and all the stars.”

Saint Thomas, at the end of life, taken by this feeling of the mystery of everything, replied to Brother Reginald, who urged him to write: “Reginald, I can’t do it anymore: everything I’ve written seems like straw to me.” Let us not presume to want this high despair to come too soon; it is a reward; and silence is the precursor of the great outcry that will spring from the soul invaded by light; but a little of this state of soul is the best corrective for the pride that blinds and for the pretensions that lead us astray. It is also a stimulant for work, for distant lights will continue to attract us as long as we have hope of reaching them. On the contrary, when we think that everything has already been said and that all we lack is to learn it, we work in a narrow circle and immobilize ourselves.

A character of high caliber knows that our enlightenments are nothing more than steps of shadows through which we ascend towards the inaccessible light. We babble, and the enigma of the world is perfect. To study is to determine some conditions, to classify the facts: we only study with greatness when we place this little under the auspices of what we still ignore. This is not to put it in the darkness, for the light we do not see is what feeds the reflections of our astral night.

The mystery is the light of what we know in all things, as well as the unity and origin of number, like the immobility and secret of dizzying torrents. Feeling within oneself the murmur of all being and all duration, invoking its testimony, and yet, despite its silence, surrounding oneself with the best guarantees for the acquisition of truth. Everything depends on everything, and the clear relationships between beings are immersed in this night into which we grope.
