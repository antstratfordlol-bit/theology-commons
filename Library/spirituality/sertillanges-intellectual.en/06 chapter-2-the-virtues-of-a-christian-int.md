## Chapter 2. The Virtues of a Christian Intellectual {#sertillanges-intellectual.2}
### 1. The Common Virtues {#sertillanges-intellectual.2.1}
Icould say: virtue contains intellectuality in potential, for, leading us to our end, which is intellectual, virtue is equivalent to supreme knowledge.

We can draw many things from this; we could even draw everything, because to this primacy of moral order is linked the relative dependence of the true, the beautiful, harmony, unity, and being itself on morality, which is thus related to the first principle.

But I prefer to follow a more modest road.

The qualities of character play a preponderant role in everything. The intellect is nothing more than an instrument; its use will determine its effects. Is it not evident that, to govern intelligence well, several other qualities are required, beyond intelligence itself? Instinctively, every upright spirit proclaims that the superiority of any species includes a dose of spiritual superiority. To judge with truth, one must be great.

Would there be something shocking in seeing a great discovery made by a scoundrel? The candor of a simple man would be offended by that. We are scandalized by a dissociation that offends human harmony. We do not believe in those jewelers who sell pearls and do not use them. Living close to the sublime source without participating in its moral nature sounds like a paradox. Using the power of intelligence and making it an isolated force, an “excrescence,” seems to us a dangerous game, for every isolated force, within a balanced whole, becomes its victim.

If character decays, it is to be expected that the sense of great truths will suffer the consequences. The spirit, no longer under examination, no longer moving on the level that is proper to it, will embark on disastrous paths, and we know that “a small error in the beginning becomes large in the end.”[4](part0018.xhtml#a1H3) The logical force may precipitate downward one whose soul has left discernment without safeguards. Hence so many thunderous falls, and so many errors often ingenious, of disoriented masters.

Life is a unity: it would be very surprising if one could fully work in one function while neglecting another, and if living the ideas did not help us perceive them.

Where does this unity of life come from? Love. “Tell me what you love, and I will tell you who you are.” Love is the beginning of everything within us, and this common starting point of knowledge and practice cannot help but make the right paths of one and the other somewhat united.

The truth approaches those who love it, those who embrace it, and that love does not exist without virtue. Therefore, despite its possible flaws, the genius involved in his work is already virtuous; to achieve sanctity, it would be enough for him to be more fully himself.

The true springs from the same soil as the good; their roots communicate. Separated from this common root, and therefore less rooted in their ground, both suffer, the soul dwindles or the spirit faints. Conversely, by nurturing the true, we enlighten consciousness; by fostering the good, we guide knowledge.

Practicing the truth we know, we deserve the one we ignore. We deserve it before God; we also deserve it for a merit that crowns itself, since all truths relate to each other, and being indeed homage what is most decisive, when we render homage to the truth of life we approach the sovereign clarities and what depends on them. If I embark on a tributary, I will reach the river, and from there to the sea.

Let us examine more closely this doctrine so important, of such importance that it would already be worth producing this booklet just to recall it.

Is virtue not the health of the soul? And who will argue that health has no influence on vision? Ask an ophthalmologist. A smart clinician doesn’t just measure the curve of the lens or prescribe glasses, eye drops, or local asepsis; he concerns himself with your overall health, with your teeth, with your lifestyle, with your viscera. Don’t be surprised if this specialist in one organ asks you about your virtues. Spiritual vision is no less demanding.

Do you think we only think with intelligence? Are we not a bundle of powers, in which we choose, for this or that, the adequate instrument? We think “with our entire soul,” Plato affirmed. Let’s go even further and affirm: with our whole being. Knowledge concerns everything within us, from vital idea to the chemical composition of the smallest cells. Mental disorders of any nature, delirious states, hallucinations, asthenias and hypersthenias, inadaptations to reality of any kind, abundantly prove that it is not only the spirit that thinks, but the whole man.

How is it possible to think well with a sick soul, with a heart wrought by vices, divided by passions, disoriented by violent or guilty loves? There is a clairvoyant state and a blind state of the soul, Gratry used to say, a healthy state, and therefore sensible, and an insensible state. “The exercise of moral virtues,” St. Thomas Aquinas tells us in turn, “virtues by which passions are restrained, is of utmost importance for acquiring knowledge.”[5](part0018.xhtml#a1JX)

I fully agree! Let’s analyze. What does the effort of science depend on, first of all? On attention, which determines the field of research, concentrates us on it, and sustains all the forces we apply to it; then, on judgment, which gathers the fruit of investigation. Now, passions and vices diminish attention, disperse it, divert it, and reach judgment through meanders whose paths Aristotle and many others after him have scrutinized.

All contemporary psychologists agree with this; the evidence leaves no room for doubt. “The psychology of feelings” governs action, but also a good part of thought. Science depends on our emotional and moral orientations. To pacify ourselves is to release within us the sense of the universal; to rectify ourselves is to release the sense of truth.

Let’s analyze further. What are the enemies of wisdom? Unintelligence, evidently; for everything we say about vices, virtues, and their role in science presupposes subjects who fundamentally have the same nature. But, besides foolishness, what other enemies do you fear? Do you not think of laziness, where the best gifts are buried? In sensuality, which weakens the body and makes it heavy, clouds the imagination, dulls the intelligence, and dissipates memory? In pride, which sometimes dazzles, sometimes obscures, and imprisons us so much in our view of things that the vision of the universal can elude us? In envy, which obstinately refuses the light coming from others? In irritability, which repels criticism and clings to error?

Overcoming these obstacles, a man of study can rise to varying degrees, according to his resources and environment, but he will reach the level of his own genius, of his own destiny.

All the fixations mentioned stimulate more or less one another, confirm themselves, branch out, and are all, in relation to the love of good or its contempt, what the crossed threads of water are in relation to their source. The purity of thought demands the purity of the soul: here is a general truth that nothing can shake. Let the neophyte of science imbue himself with it.

Let’s go higher, and since we’re talking about sources, let’s not forget the first one. The most secure metaphysics teaches us that at its peaks, truth and goodness are not only interconnected, but identical.

To be precise, we must say that the good in this case is not, properly speaking, moral good; it specifically concerns the desirable; but one reasoning leads us from one to the other.

The moral good is the desirable, measured by reason and proposed to the will as an end. The ends are interrelated. All of them depend on a final end, and it is this that joins truth and identifies with it. Join these propositions and you will see that moral good, if it is not identical to truth in all aspects, depends on it, through the ends of willing. There is therefore between the two a more or less loose or narrow connection, but unbreakable.

It is not through what is individual in us that we access truth: it is by virtue of a participation in the universal. To this universal, which is at the same time true and good, we cannot honor as true, intimately unite with it, discern its aspects, and receive its powerful influence without equally recognizing it and serving it as good.

Ascend the Great Pyramid through those giant steps that so well represent the ascent to truth: if you climb up the north slope, can you reach the top without approaching the south slope? To stay away from it and to stay on the lower levels; to distance oneself from it, to walk sideways, and to descend again. Thus, the genius of truth tends by itself to unite with the good: if one moves away from it, it is to the detriment of its impulse towards the heights.

“Blessed are the poor in spirit,” said the Lord, “for theirs is the kingdom of heaven.”[6](part0018.xhtml#a1K3) “Guard the purity of conscience,” said Saint Thomas to his student; “do not fail to imitate the conduct of the saints and righteous men.” The obedience of the soul to the ineffable source, its filial and loving dispositions open it to the invasion of illuminations and ardors and virtues. Loved and accomplished as life, truth reveals itself as principle; we see according to what we are; we participate in truth by participating in the Spirit by which it exists. Great personal intuitions come, as well as value, from moral perfection, detachment from oneself and from routine banalities, from humility, simplicity, discipline of the senses and imagination, from the effort to achieve great ends.

Here it’s no longer about proving your skill, making your faculties shine as if they were a jewel; what is wanted is to communicate with the source of light and life; this center is approached in its unity, just as it is; and worshipped, and we renounce what opposes it so that its glory may flood us. Is that not more or less what the expression “Great thoughts come from the heart” mean?

### 2. The Intellectual’s Own Virtue {#sertillanges-intellectual.2.2}
We are now sure that virtue in general is necessary for science, and that the greater the moral rectitude, the more fruitful the study will be. However, there is a virtue proper to the intellectual, and it is worthwhile to insist on it, although it will reappear frequently in the pages of this book.

The proper virtue of the studious man is evidently studiousness. Let no one judge this as simplistic: our masters in this doctrine included many things in this virtue and excluded several others.[7](part0018.xhtml#a1H2)

St. Thomas submitted studiousness to moderate temperance, to indicate that knowledge is always welcome in itself, but that the constitution of life requires us to temper, that is, to adapt to circumstances and relate to other duties an appetite for knowledge that easily becomes excessive.

When I speak of excess, I understand it in two senses. In the realm of studiousness, two vices oppose each other: negligence, on one hand; vain curiosity, on the other. Let’s omit the former here: if it isn’t odious to the reader when they close this book, it’s either because they got bored along the way or because we’ve constructed the path very poorly. But I can’t say the same about curiosity. It can take advantage of our best instincts and corrupt them at the very moment it aims to satisfy them.

We’ve already mentioned the ambitious aspirations that disorient an intellectual vocation. Without going that far, ambition can alter studiousness and its good effects. An act of ambition in the field of science is no longer a scientific act, and whoever does it no longer deserves the name of intellectual.

Any other sinful purpose would require the same verdict.

On the other hand, the study, even disinterested and straight in itself, is not always timely; if it is not, the scientist forgets his duty as a man, and what kind of intellectual is one who is not a man? There are other human duties beyond study. Knowledge taken as an absolute is undoubtedly our supreme good; but what we experience here is often subordinated to other values that will be equivalent to it under the auspices of merit.

A village priest who dedicates himself to his parishioners, a doctor who neglects science to provide urgent assistance, a son who practices a trade to help his family and thus renounces a broad culture, do not profane in any way their inner genius; they pay homage to this Truth which is, with the Good, one and the same Being. If they were to act otherwise, they would offend both truth and virtue, for, through a deviation, they would set living Truth against itself.

We thus see many curious about science who do not fear sacrificing their strictest duties to it. They are no longer scientists; they are dilettantes. Or else, they abandon the study that corresponds to their obligations and dedicate themselves to what satisfies their desires, and fall into the same disqualification.

Those who aspire higher than their strengths allow, exposing themselves to error, those who waste their real faculties to acquire illusory ones are also curious in their ancient sense. Two of Saint Thomas’s sixteen precepts on study are for them: “Altiora te ne quaesieris,” do not seek what is above you; “Volo ut per rivulos, non statim, in mare eligas introire,” choose to enter the sea through the streams, not directly. Precious advice, beneficial for both science and virtue, as they balance the man.

Do not overload the soil; do not raise the construction beyond what the base allows, or before the base is firm: that would make everything collapse.

Who are you? Where are you? What intellectual foundations do you already possess? This is what determines your endeavors in the field of science. “If you want to see the verdant, plant the small,” say the foresters, which is, in other words, the Thomist advice. The wise person starts from the beginning and only takes a new step after securing the previous one. That’s why autodidacts have so many weaknesses. No one can start from the beginning alone. When we join a group that has already been walking, it gives us the result of the stages traveled without showing us the corresponding trajectory.

On the other hand, what applies to each one regarding the stages of their development applies to each one in relation to others. We should not overestimate ourselves, but evaluate ourselves. Accepting ourselves as we are and obeying God and preparing ourselves for secure victories. Does nature intend more than it can? Everything in it is measured accurately, without futile efforts and illusory assessments. Each being acts according to its quantity and its quality, its nature and its strength, and then rests in peace. Only man lives with pretensions and sadness.

What science and what virtue there is in judging oneself well and persevering in oneself! You have a role that only you can play and that must be performed perfectly, instead of trying to force fortune. Destinies are not interchangeable. Whoever tries to rise too high or lower themselves, loses themselves. Walk forward and remain faithful to yourself, with God as your guide.

Saint Thomas adds to these necessary precautions the care not to put your curiosity in the lower objects to the detriment of the supreme object. We will draw from this, later on, an important consequence regarding the organization of work;[8](part0018.xhtml#a1D9) but, first of all, the study must preserve the place of worship, prayer, direct meditation on the things of God. He himself is a divine office, but reflected; he investigates and honors the “traces” of the Creator or then His “images,” according to whether he scrutinizes nature or humanity; but he must yield, in his time, to His direct attendance; if he does not do so, besides neglecting a serious duty, the image of God in creation becomes opaque, and its traces only serve to distance oneself from the One they witness.

Studying so much that one stops praying, withdrawing, no longer reading the Holy Word or the words of the saints, or those of the great souls; studying so much that one forgets oneself and that, being so concentrated on the objects of study, the inner guest is neglected, is an abuse and a cheat. Supposing that there will be greater progress this way and that more will be produced, and thinking that the stream will flow better if we dry up its source.

The order of the spirit must correspond to the order of things. In reality, everything rises to the divine, everything depends on it, for everything proceeds from it. In the effigy of reality within us, the same dependencies exist, unless we have disarranged the relations of the true.

### 3. Spirit of Prayer {#sertillanges-intellectual.2.3}
These dispositions will be guaranteed if, independently of the piety that precedes study, we cultivate in our own work the spirit of prayer.

And still St. Thomas tells the science lover: “Orationi vacare non desinas,” never abandon prayer, and Van Helmont explains this precept to us with these sublime words: “Every study is a study of eternity.”

Science is knowledge through the causes, they always tell us. The details are nothing; the facts are nothing; what matters are the dependencies, the communications of influences, the relations, the exchanges that constitute the life of nature. Now, above all dependencies, there is the first Dependency; in the midst of all relations, the supreme Relation; at the top of communications, the Source; under exchanges, the Gift; under the systole and diastole of the world, the Heart, the immense Heart of Being. Should the spirit not constantly refer to it and not lose for a single minute the contact with the one who is the everything of all things and consequently of all science?

The intelligence fully plays its role by performing a religious function, that is, rendering worship to the supreme truth through the reduced and scattered truth.

Each truth is a fragment that displays in all its aspects its connections; Truth itself is one, and Truth is God.

Each truth is a reflection: behind the reflection, and giving it value, is the Light. Each being is a witness; each fact is a divine secret; beyond is the object of revelation, the hero of the testimony. Every truth stands out upon the Infinite as upon its background of perspective; it manifests it; it belongs to it. A particular truth can occupy the entire scene; the immensities are beyond. We could say: a particular truth is simply a symbol, a real symbol, a sacrament of the absolute; it expresses, and is, but not by itself; it is not sufficient unto itself; it lives for another and would die if abandoned to its inconsistency.

For the fully awakened soul, therefore, every truth is a meeting place; Sovereign Thought invites ours: shall we miss this sublime encounter?

The life of the real is not all in what we see, in what we analyze through science. The real has a hidden life, like Jesus, and this life is also a life in God; and like a life of God; it is a revelation of His wisdom through laws, of His power through effects, of His goodness through goods, of His tendency to spread through exchanges and growth: it is fitting to venerate and love this kind of incarnation in contact even with Him who incarnates. To separate this “body of God” from His Spirit is to belittle it, as it is to belittle Christ to see in Him only the man.

The incarnation of Christ is consummated in communion, where the body, the blood, the soul, and the divinity of the Savior are not dissociated; the almost-incarnation of God in being, of the eternal Truth in each true case, must also be consummated in a heavenly ecstasy, and not in our distracted investigations and in our banal admirations.

Let us decide to work under the wings of the great laws and under the supreme Law. Neither knowledge nor any manifestation of life should be separated from its roots in the soul and in reality, where the God of the heart and the God of the Heavens reveal themselves and unite. There must be unity among our acts (including the act of learning), our thoughts, and our ultimate realities. In everything, let us have all the soul, all the nature, all the duration, and the very divinity with us.

To attain this spirit of prayer in science, moreover, it is not necessary to resort to any mysterious enchantment. No extrinsic force is required. Undoubtedly, the invocation of God and His special intervention have their place here. Saint Thomas always prayed before dictating or preaching; in this intention, he composed an admirable prayer:[9](part0018.xhtml#a1GX) the son of science who stutters naturally seeks the word that eludes him in the divine gaze. But in science itself, in Christian science, we find the footstool which, raising us to God, allows us to return to study with a more enlightened soul and, as it were, with the gifts of the prophet.

All that instructs leads to God by a safe path. Every authentic truth is in itself eternal, and its eternity guides us to the Eternity of which it is a revelation. Through nature and the soul, where else can we go but to its origin? If we do not reach it, it is because there was a deviation in the path. With a leap, the inspired spirit straightly transcends the intermediate degrees, and to every question that arises for it, whatever particular answers it may present, a secret voice answers: God!

Therefore, it is enough to let the spirit follow its impulses, on one hand, and to remain attentive, on the other, so that a constant back and forth is established between the object of a particular study and that of religious contemplation, which is beneficial for both. With a quick leap, often unconscious, we pass from the trace or image to God, and from there, returning with renewed strength, we again follow the traces of the divine Wanderer. What we discover is then commented on, expanded; we see in it an episode of an immense spiritual event; even occupying ourselves with something minute, we feel ourselves trustees of truths before which mountains are ephemeral; the infinite Being and infinite duration envelop us, and our study is truly “a study of eternity.”

### 4. Discipline of the Body {#sertillanges-intellectual.2.4}
We have already said that the doctrine of the human compound prohibits the dissociation of spiritual functions and bodily functions, even those that seem most alien to pure thought. St. Thomas signs off on this ironic thought of Aristotle’s: “It is as ridiculous to say that only the soul understands as to say: it weaves, or constructs.”[10](part0018.xhtml#a1H6) He himself makes these seemingly materialistic statements: “The various dispositions of man for the works of the soul come from the various dispositions of their bodies.”[11](part0018.xhtml#a1H7) “The good constitution of the body follows the nobility of the soul.”[12](part0018.xhtml#a1H8)

That’s nothing surprising. Thought is born in us after long preparations in which the entire body machine works. Cellular chemistry is at the base of everything; the darkest sensations prepare our experience: this is the product of the work of the senses that slowly elaborate their acquisitions and fix them in memory. And it is from physiological phenomena, in continuity with them and dependent on them, that the intellectual fact is produced. No one thinks, even if they are just using an acquired idea, without evoking a whole set of images, emotions, sensations that are the crucible of the idea’s culture.

When we want to provoke in someone a thought, what means do we have? Only this one: to produce in them through words, through signs, states of sensitivity and imagination, of emotion, of memory, in which they will discover our idea and will be able to make it their own. Spirits only communicate through bodies. Thus also the spirit of each one of us communicates with truth and with itself through the body. So much so that the change we undergo from ignorance to knowledge must be attributed, according to Saint Thomas, directly to the body and only “accidentally” to the intellective part.[13](part0018.xhtml#a1J7)

Such doctrine, incessantly resumed by the doctor, so essentially, so providentially modern, should not lead to the conviction that to think, especially to think ardently and wisely throughout a lifetime, it is indispensable to subject not only the soul and its various powers to thought, but also the body and the whole set of organic functions. Everything in an intellectual must be intellectual. The physical and mental complex, the human substance, is at the service of this special life that in certain aspects seems so little human: let it not hinder him then! Let us become a harmony whose result is the conquest of truth.

Well, there are two things to be considered in this without any human respect, although the first of them usually scares men with little firm judgment.

First, don’t be ashamed to worry about your health.

Many geniuses had very poor health, and if God wills it to be so with you, let’s not argue. But if your actions harm your health, that is tempting God gravely. You are secure, disciple of the geniuses, that you have as much vigor as they do to triumph in the incessant struggle of the soul against the weakness of the flesh. Nothing guarantees that the geniuses themselves have not seen their physiological flaws divert or reduce their talents. Many intellectual anomalies, even in the most gifted, perhaps can be explained by this, and also the weak production of many is explained in the same way.

Assuming the equality of gifts, it is certain that illness is a serious inferiority; it diminishes productivity; it hampers the freedom of the soul when it performs its most delicate functions; it diverts attention; it can distort judgment because of the movements of the imagination and emotivity that suffering provokes. A stomach illness changes a man’s character; his character changes his thoughts. If Leopardi had not been the abortion that he was, would he be among the pessimists today?

When it comes to leading an elevated life, don’t think that lowering the level of discussion is worrying at the same time about thought and all its organic substrate. “A healthy mind in a healthy body” is always the ideal. The man of thought has a special physiology; he must take care of it and not hesitate to consult a specialist in the field.[14](part0018.xhtml#a1DN)

In any case, the usual prescriptions must be followed. Good hygiene is an almost intellectual virtue. Among moderns, in whom philosophy is sometimes so poor, hygiene is rich: do not despise it, it will enrich your philosophy.

As much as possible, lead a life outdoors. It is proven that attention, that nerve of science, has a close correlation with breathing, and we know that the abundance of oxygen is a fundamental condition for health in general. Windows open or slightly ajar night and day, when prudence allows, frequent sessions of deep breaths, especially combined with movements that expand and normalize them,[15](part0018.xhtml#a1CS) walks before and after work, or combining with it, according to Greek tradition, are excellent practices.

It’s important to work in a position that leaves the lungs free and doesn’t compress the viscera. It’s good to interrupt a study session once in a while to breathe deeply, to make two or three rhythmic gestures that stretch the muscles and prevent them, so to speak, from crumpling up. It has been discovered that deep breaths taken on tiptoe, the body upright, next to an open window, are even more effective. Neglect nothing, thus avoiding congestion of your organs and their consequent weakness.

You need a workout session every day. Remember the phrase from that English doctor: “He who does not find time to exercise, will have to find time to get sick.” If you cannot exercise outdoors, there are excellent substitute methods. J.-P. Muller’s is one of the most intelligent;[16](part0018.xhtml#a1CH) but there are others.

A light and recreational manual labor would be equally precious for the spirit and for the body. Our parents did not ignore it; but our century has become a fool that mocks nature; and therefore, nature takes revenge. Reserve once a year, and at some other times of the year, periods of vacation. I do not refer to the absence of any work, which would slacken faculties that are naturally inconstant, but to the predominance of rest, fresh air, and exercise close to nature.

Take care of your diet. A light, simple, moderate meal in quantity and condiments will favor a more attentive and freer work. A thinker does not spend life in digestion sessions. Even more important is your sleep. Neither sleep too much nor too little.

Much sleep makes the blood and thought heavy, greasy, thick; little sleep makes you susceptible to dangerously prolonging and increasing the excitations of work. Observe; in matters of sleep, as for food, find the measure that suits you and make the firm resolution to maintain it. In this matter there is no general law.

In short, understand that caring for the body, instrument of the soul, is for the intellectual both a virtue and a wisdom; Saint Thomas clearly recognized this character and included this wisdom of the body among the elements that contribute to temporal happiness, a prelude to the other.[17](part0018.xhtml#a1JE) Do not become a weakling, a weak person, who would later end up becoming an idiot, an untimely old person; do not be a foolish manager of the talent that the Lord entrusted to you.

But the care with the bodily organism also involves other elements. We speak of passions and vices as formidable enemies of the spirit. We then thought of their psychological effects, the disturbances they cause in judgment, in the orientation of the spirit, which transform us, after a certain point, into a power of darkness. Now let’s deal with their bodily effects, which become, indirectly, diseases of the soul.

Whoever is greedy, lazy, a slave to the pillow and the table; whoever abuses wine, alcohol, tobacco; whoever forgets oneself in wicked excitements, in habits both debilitating and enervating, in sins more or less forgiven periodically, but whose effects remain, how will they manage to practice hygiene, the necessity of which we have just explained?

A friend of pleasure and an enemy of one’s own body soon becomes an enemy of one’s own soul. The mortification of the senses is necessary for thought, and only it can lead us to the clairvoyant state of which Gratry spoke. Obey the flesh, and you will be becoming flesh, and what is necessary is to become all spirit.

Why do they call Saint Thomas “the Angelic Doctor?” Is it solely for his winged genius? No, but because everything in him was subordinated to the genius and holiness of thought, because his flesh, originating from the Tyrrhenian shores, had been clothed with the whiteness of Carmel and Hermon; because, chaste, sober, ready for ardor and distant from any excess, he was entirely a soul, “an intelligence served by organs,” according to the famous definition.

The discipline of the body and its mortification, allied with the care necessary for it, of which they are the best part: this is, Christian workers, and especially you, young people, one of the most precious safeguards of your future.
