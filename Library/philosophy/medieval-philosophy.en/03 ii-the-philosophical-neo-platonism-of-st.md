## II. The Philosophical Neo-Platonism of St. Augustine {#medieval-philosophy.2}
### 1. Introduction: Role, Life and Philosophical Formation {#medieval-philosophy.2.1}
If St. Justin and Clement of Alexandria are the first ones to hold a positive attitude with regard to philosophy, St. Augustine is the one who overcomes any hesitation, so that with him the foundations of Christian philosophy are established. The lines of force of St. Augustine's thought arise from the experience of his own life. He was born in Tagaste, in northern Africa, in 350 of a pagan father and a Christian mother (St. Monica). After his early education in that city, he studied rhetoric in Carthage in 370 and three years later he read Cicero's "Hortensia" which aroused in him the passion of reaching the possession of truth. He now read the Holy Scripture but he didn't understand it and he gave it a Manichaean interpretation. (He had never been baptised). Manichaeanism states that we must reject the Old Testament because it teaches an anthropomorphic notion about God. Manichaeanism teaches that there are two principles of reality, that of good or light, which is God, and that of evil or darkness, and the two struggle between themselves for the mastery of this world.

This gave St. Augustine a satisfactory explanation for the existence of evil in the world and in his life. Since it was necessary to read the Latin and Greek classics in order to be a good teacher of rhetoric, little by little Augustine was improving his philosophical formation, which led him to abandon his Manichaeanism and his hostility to Christianity, but he nevertheless fell into a type of despair about ever being able to reach the truth. In this state of mind he went to Rome in 383 and from there he obtained the municipal chair of rhetoric in Milan. His reading of the translation of Plotinus' "Enneads" strongly impressed him and led him out of scepticism. He rapidly assimilated Neo-Platonism in the conditions of that epoch.

Enthused by the light of the intellect, he began a reform of his customs at the same time in which he found Christianity. Attracted by the fame of St. Ambrose, he went to listen to his sermons, but this of course also meant listening to the content of his sermons, and in them he found the Catholic interpretation of the Old Testament. Of course, he also learned the letters of St. Paul and his teachings about grace. Augustine was baptized on Holy Saturday of the year

387. The greater part of his philosophical works were written in the time between his catechumenate and his priestly ordination, from 385 to 391. Four years after his priestly ordination, he became Bishop of Hippo. From now on, his works are predominantly theological, but many philosophical elements appear in these works. St. Augustine died in the year 430 in Hippo due to an illness while the siege of Hippo by the Vandals was in progress.

The immense work of St. Augustine is on the one hand a collecting, assimilating and transmitting of the Greco-Roman and the Judeo-Christian cultures and on the other hand he marks a new route of thought on explaining in depth the relations between faith and reason which will be taken up, perfected and completed by St. Thomas Aquinas 8 centuries later.

### 2. The Augustinian Philosophy as Itinerary towards God and Happiness {#medieval-philosophy.2.2}
a) HAPPINESS AND TRUTH. The thought of St. Augustine is always related to his own life. His reading of "Hortensius" awakens in him the great passion to possess the truth and he is also intranquil about the experience of evil. These two currents combine in what will be his favourite topic: THE SEARCH FOR THE TRUTH. For Augustine happiness and the knowledge of the truth are the same thing. Everyone wants to be happy. How do we find happiness? We have to know what to look for and who is looking for it.

The first must be something of such nature that it satisfies all desires and gives peace; with regard to the second, one must know oneself in order to know what is better and how to be good. Those who don't want to be happy are already very unfortunate persons, but not all those who want to be happy achieve this end. We must ascertain what we must desire in order to be happy. Augustine states that the object of our desire must be something permanent and independent of chance or fortune and must exclude anything contingent, for if we love what can perish we always have the fear of losing it. Since only God fulfils all these conditions, since He only is eternal, He will be Happiness Itself and hence the desire for God is the only way to happiness.

Nevertheless, this desire for God is not sufficient. One must also live an upright life, for the real possession of the good desired is a condition for happiness. Since happiness in this world is rooted basically in the human spirit, it will be Wisdom which will teach the full measure in which virtue must be practised. In fact, Wisdom corrects the causes of our miseries, lust, ambition, pride, etc., in which immoderate men believe that they will obtain joy. Here St. Augustine bases his arguments on Holy Scripture in order to tell us what wisdom is, namely, the Wisdom of God and more concretely, the Son of God is the Wisdom of God because He is God. Thus, whoever possesses God, possesses wisdom and hence also happiness.

Along another way St. Augustine arrives to identical conclusions. Since man is composed of body and soul, that which is of lower condition (matter, animal, etc.) does not have the sufficient capacity to make him happy. Since the soul is higher than the body, the cause of man's happiness is only to be found in a spiritual good, that is, virtue. From where does Wisdom which makes virtue possible come to man? From the soul itself it can't come, because that is precisely what is lacking in the soul, as is evident from its desire for happiness. Consequently, the good which is desired is not of human order and cannot be anything else but God.

Happiness is the possession of God, the Highest Good and Immutable Truth. Therefore, the authentic philosopher is truly happy, that is to say, the Christian, who is the only one who can achieve the possession of the Truth in a complete way. Thus, Christianity is the SACROSANCTA PHILOSOPHIA (the most holy philosophy). This conclusion imposes the study of the relations between supernatural faith and human reason, a task which St. Augustine carries out on the basis of his own spiritual experience.

b) FAITH AND REASON. For St. Augustine, the act of faith is not something strange or antinatural in man, since of all creatures only the one endowed with reason can believe. In fact, all of man's knowledge, of whatever kind it may be, proceeds either from what he has seen himself or what he believes from others. With this approach, the solution for the problem of relations between reason and faith requires a clarification of the terminology that St. Augustine uses. For him every man has a spirit (MENS) whose discursive activity is called RATIO, and its beginning and result - INTELLECTUS - is the comprehension or understanding of the things. Thus RATIO, or reasoning, is in a certain way prior to understanding and all to the act of faith.

For St. Augustine, the despising of reason is the despising of the image of God in us. It belongs to RATIO to show the credibility of Christianity, to acquire a certain comprehension of the faith and to show that there is nothing more reasonable than to believe. INTELLIGE UT CREDAS, or: understand so that you may believe. Once supernatural faith has moved one to have faith, faith awakens the desire of knowing and understanding better what is believed. St. Augustine says: CREDE UT INTELLIGAS (believe so that you may understand) and St. Anselm, his 12th-century disciple, will say FIDES QUAERENS INTELLECTUM (faith seeking understanding).

In both cases it is a question of the faith which is seeking understanding, and from this arises a positive attitude about philosophical activity as an instrument for theological knowledge or discourse. In St. Augustine nothing escapes this programme of CREDO UT INTELLIGAM (I believe so that I may understand). Therefore, faith and reason are two ways of knowing which must collaborate closely in the search for truth. Certainly St. Augustine distinguishes between the Truth and the truths, but to attain them belongs to the same effort. He thinks, along with St. Justin, that any truth, whatever it may be, always pertains to God and consequently is a good proper to Christianity. RATIO, or reason, has a function to perform before the act of faith, but its authentic function and best service is exercised when it discourses in the higher sphere of faith, giving rise to theology. St. Augustine will give a good proof for this on tracing the itinerary of the soul towards God.

c) THE KNOWLEDGE OF GOD. For St. Augustine the knowledge that God exists is universal and natural; Revelation is not indispensable for that knowledge as is proven by the fact that pagan philosophers have arrived at that knowledge. Therefore, not to know that God exists is culpable ignorance, the fruit of evil passions and of the influence of a bad will. Although the nature of God cannot be known in itself, His existence is easily recognized by those of clean heart, upright reason and good moral disposition. Before proposing rational demonstrations of this to the hard of heart, it is necessary to lead them to the faith. In St. Augustine, good conduct will always be the door which leads to the right use of reason and therefore to faith. Well now, the believer who knows that God exists cannot be dispensed from demonstrating His existence with rational arguments, for that would be the same as despising reason, and therefore despising God, precisely in the name of faith.

First of all, the arguments of the sceptics about the truth must be discarded, for when we experience doubt, we are aware that we think, and therefore we have arrived at the truth with full certainty (the COGITO of St. Augustine is not a starting point for knowledge, as it is in Descartes, but rather only an irrefutable argument against scepticism). St. Augustine argues thus: there are beings which have neither life nor knowledge, like the minerals; other beings have life, like the plants, still others have life and knowledge. If we are attentive, we see that we know only those which we have experienced and those which we can imagine, based on what we have experienced. Well now, our knowledge is composed of the exercise of the EXTERNAL SENSES, which have as their object what is material, and of the exercise of the INTERNAL SENSES, which inform from the corporal sensations, and of the exercise of REASON.

What proceeds from the external senses is fleeting, because their impressions disappear rapidly. If at any rate we desire to know, the internal sense must judge and order the external senses to repeat the observation. In an analogous way reason acts with respect to the internal senses for the same motives: the impressions of the internal or external senses have little reality or being, and short duration. Nevertheless, in reason there appear necessary and immutable realities, such as that of 7 + 3 = 10. This truth is not found in sense experience, but is imposed on reason by its own evidence. Is there anything higher than reason? There must necessarily exist a higher reality, because reason possesses realities, that is, immutable and necessary truths, but reason itself is contingent, that is, man changes and perishes. Therefore, there is something that transcends man and which has to be the purely intelligible, necessary, immutable and therefore eternal truth, which we call God.

All the arguments which St. Augustine presents in order to arrive to God are reduced in practice to one single proof, the itinerary of which is to pass from the exterior to the interior (the soul) and from the interior world to ascend to the higher (God). This second step is based on the fact that much of man's knowledge is immutable and superior to man himself, and consequently demands something that does not change: the intelligible, necessary, immutable and therefore eternal truth, which is God.

d) DIVINE NAMES. Initially, for St. Augustine, God is understood, rather than as Pure Act or cause of being, as That Being which is always the same or ESSENTIA PLENA (Full Essence), before which everything else is radically contingent. In this sense, Sacred Scripture supplies him with the name which is best applicable to God; EGO SUM QUI SUM (I am who am), or QUI EST (He Who Is), which is conceived by St. Augustine as the existence of That Being which remains always identical to itself. To affirm that God is He Who Is signifies that He is the Being of the full and supreme essence, the Supreme, Immutable and Eternal Being. Therefore, to prove His existence is the same as showing that what is mutable requires an Immutable from which it depends by participation. That is to say, the imperfect is explained by the Perfect, of which it is the trace and image.

In depth, the demonstration of the existence of God starting with the immutability of the truth coincides with the other metaphysical proofs since traces of the cause are found in the effect. Thus, the very existence of truth in the reason indicates to us that man is a reflection and an image of the divine immutability. It is still fitting to make an important observation: the God that is reached with St. Augustine's method is simultaneously transcendent and immanent to the world.

The first because, faced with the Immutable Being that is God, any other being exists only by participation and between the One and the other there is an infinite gap. But He is also immanent to the world and to the soul since His presence is ascertained in each truth that thought considers or verifies in whatever sphere to which it refers (scientific, moral, etc.). St. Augustine says: "the discourse of the mind doesn't create the truth". And he adds: "Don't seek Him outside. Turn into yourself. The truth dwells within man. And if you discover that your own nature is also mutable, transcend yourself," because "whatever the understanding finds of truth is not due to itself". Hence God is like the intelligible Sun in whose light reason finds the truth, and the interior Master who replies from within to the questions of reason: "You are more intimate to my very self than my own intimacy," St. Augustine says to God.

e) CONCLUSION. The metaphysical stance of St. Augustine transcends his corrective work on Platonism, which has not always been noticed by his followers. Plato did not know the notion of being as act, and thus he moved in the sphere of the essence, of that which makes something be what it is. To speak of essence is to refer to an existent reality, but by concentrating not on the act of being, but rather on being something. In this perspective God is, in fact, highest reality or "full essence" for some Fathers, equivalent to the "ocean of reality" of St. Gregory Nazianzen. St. Anselm of Canterbury and St. Bonaventure, to name some authors who belong to what would later be called the Augustinian tradition, depend on this notion of God as "full essence" in order to demonstrate His existence.

Moreover, they will think as a consequence, in opposition to St. Augustine, that the knowledge of God's existence is immediate, because they will begin from this notion of God that will demand His existence. They will say that since God is "full essence", He must exist. That Being which is the fullness of reality cannot not exist. But St. Augustine always remained faithful to his definition of God as QUI EST (He Who Is), the centre and root of all that is real. Certainly, in order to prove the existence of God, St. Augustine does not begin with the question about why things exist or who has caused them, but that underlies his whole exposition, in such a way that God above all is Being, the cause of every being by participation, and in order to explain the nature of the things he starts off from the notion that God is the Being of the full essence.

### 3. Creation, Exemplarism and Seminal Reasons {#medieval-philosophy.2.3}
In its most summarized form, the metaphysical elements of St. Augustine's doctrine on creation are the following:

a) God created all things simultaneously out of nothing.

b) All beings have matter. He distinguishes between "spiritual matter" which is proper to the angels and simple matter proper to the beings which are not spiritual. Before receiving the form this matter is absolutely indeterminate, and has some reality, though very precarious before it receives the form. Thus to speak of matter and of change or mutability in beings is to refer to aspects which are intrinsically linked together. By their mutability things show that they have been created by God out of nothing. Hence, between God and the other beings there is a metaphysical abyss which separates the complete self-sufficiency of God and the intrinsic lack of necessity of creatures.

c) Before speaking of the reception of forms in matter, it is necessary to point out that in St. Augustine's vocabulary, "idea", "form", "species" and "RATIO" mean the same thing, the first two being especially indistinct in the whole Augustinian tradition.

For St. Augustine, following Plato, the Ideas are principal forms, the stable or immutable essences of the things, and are eternal since they remain identical to themselves as contained in the intellect of God. All things of this world are found at the same time in the divine Ideas. What remains to be explained is the origin of the Ideas. Since God is the supreme Being, He is at the same time the Highest Intellect and the Immutable Truth. Hence, He knows Himself perfectly and knows Himself in so far as He is and in what He is able to do and in the fullness of His essence He knows that He knows Himself as the exemplary cause or model of whatever can be made if He wants. In effect, since He is the Highest Reality or BEING, He doesn't have to create, but He is free to do so or not. But if He creates, He makes the things to His own likeness, that is, in accordance with the eternal models or Ideas of the things contained in the Word.

The exemplary Ideas, according to St. Augustine, are consubstantial with the Word and participate in His immutability and consequently are eternal.

d) To sum up, creation is the production of things out of nothing, that is, of a matter and a form which reproduce the model or Eternal Idea. In this way, the Ideas are the immutable foundation of the mutable and contingent realities of the sensible world and at the same time the source of the truth and the intelligibility of the things. Thus, the world is a manifestation of God. Each creature is a limited aspect of this manifestation and taken altogether, a poor image, since experience shows that in a certain sense the creation of the world is not finished. In fact, there are beings which are born and then disappear and there are some which still do not exist but which will come in the future. Nevertheless, it has been said that God created all things simultaneously.

Here, St. Augustine accepts the Stoic doctrine of the "seminal reasons" and says that matter, though very indeterminate, already contains in an incipient way everything that will come to be, so in this sense it can be said that creation is complete. In the very instant of creation, all creatures have been finished.

### 4. The Creatures. {#medieval-philosophy.2.4}

We are now ready to affirm that, according to St. Augustine, creation is organized in conformity with the model of the divine Ideas. Hence the order, the hierarchy of beings, the beauty, etc. that we find show the degree of likeness of creatures to God. The things that God has brought out of nothing do not receive the being which is proper to God, but rather being according to a more or a less, that is, according to an order and a hierarchy. This inequality arises from the diverse ways of participation in the ESSE SUBSISTENS, which ways are represented by each one of the divine Ideas. To be more specific, the things are likenesses of God according to the way in which they imitate the Ideas. There will be men who are more or less just or wise in the measure in which they participate more or less in the divine Ideas of Justice and Wisdom (which Ideas are consubstantial with the Divine Essence).

But the relations of the things with the Ideas have a source and a model which is the relation of God the Father by which He begets the Word, which reproduces His nature in an absolutely full and perfect manner. By analogy it is the Ideas which permit the creatures to become and to subsist. Thus the things are copies of the Ideas. Therefore in order to ascertain the perfections of the creatures it is necessary to refer back to God. Here St. Augustine shows his own way of being Platonic: God is the Being QUI EST Who presents Himself as the One, the Beauty and the Truth. Since God is the source of all the participated perfections, He confers to creatures being, unity, truth and beauty in diverse degrees. All creatures reflect or are the image of the transcendental properties or perfections of God in a greater or lesser degree, according to a perfectly established hierarchy.

### 5. Man {#medieval-philosophy.2.5}

The highest of the creatures are the angels, whose existence we know only by faith. We know that they are made of a "spiritual matter", completely and definitively "formed." Due to this material substrate, they are mutable beings, but, because of their contemplation of God, they have remained in a state of immutable happiness. Man is very close in nature to the angel, for man is a spiritual soul which makes use of a body. St. Augustine thinks of man under two aspects: a) man as a complete substance, a composite unity of body and soul; b) man considered philosophically.

In this sense St. Augustine, influenced by Neo-Plationism, does not succeed in explaining the unity and the consequent relations between the body and the soul. For St. Augustine, the soul is a complete rational substance, fitted out so as to be able to govern a body, and which has as its end union with God. If the body in its turn is a complete substance, how do we then explain the unity of man? The topic of the origin of the body was an easy task for St. Augustine, but not that of the soul. He expressly declares that he doesn't know the concrete way in which God creates the soul, and he makes several conjectures. On the one hand, he thought in terms of the "seminal reasons", that is, that all souls were created from the beginning in indeterminate matter. On the other hand, he believed in the immediate creation by God of each soul. Finally, his repugnance to admit that something spiritual would arise from matter led him to say that the souls were confided in the beginning to the angels, so that they might infuse them into the bodies at the opportune moment. In either case, we must respect St. Augustine's freedom to say that he does not know. Nevertheless, he knows how to tell us that the rational soul is immortal, because it is indestructible, since it is the seat of the immutable truth and its contrary, error, is not able to destroy it.

Finally, for St. Augustine, since matter - created by God - is good, so also is the human body. In this way faith permits him to overcome the Manichaean dualism. It no longer makes any sense to speak about the body as a prison of the soul, based on the fact that the end of the soul is the beatific vision. If the body does become the prison of the soul, that is due to sin as we shall see later on.

### 6. Psychology and The Doctrine of Illumination {#medieval-philosophy.2.6}
The metaphysical background of St. Augustine's psychology is constituted by the doctrine of the hierarchy of beings, that is to say, the set of existing beings is arranged perfectly in a hierarchy, at the peak of which is God, followed by the angels, human souls, the other living beings and inanimate beings. God, as the supreme and total reality, has created in such a way that each creature occupies a determined place, and which can influence and cause in lower beings because of its participation or image of God's being. The inverse operation is not possible, because the inferior being lacks sufficient reality to be able to act on the higher being. Hence, the perfect explains the imperfect, both in the reality of the things as well as in knowledge. Well then, the soul which has been made in order to animate the body is whole in the whole body, giving life not only to the complete man, but also to each of his organs and faculties.

For St. Augustine, the soul cannot know anything naturally unless it is through the senses and the higher faculties, that is, there are no innate ideas in the soul. In these conditions, SENSE KNOWLEDGE is described by St. Augustine as follows: when the soul receives, through the senses which it constantly vivifies, a sensation or sense impression, it forms in itself a likeness of the thing and in this likeness knows the corresponding reality. It can't be otherwise for St. Augustine since the object of sense knowledge are the mutable realities and from them true knowledge in the proper sense cannot proceed, that is, the knowledge which is stable and necessary such as 7 + 3 = 10, that good must be done, or that God exists.

Something analogous occurs with INTELLECTUAL KNOWLEDGE. The soul acquires no knowledge until it discovers it by itself. In fact, in listening to a conversation, we easily skip over many opinions until the speaker states a general or necessary truth and then our assent is produced, that is, the soul recognizes the truth and embraces it. St. Augustine then teaches that although the soul finds in itself the immutable truths, it knows that it is not their source, for it judges by them the results of knowledge and moreover discovers in its need that these truths are something superior.

Well then, the soul finds or recognizes the truth because God is its interior Master, more intimate to it that it is to itself. The soul consults with the Intimate Truth about the knowledge it acquires and finds the truth in itself. In order to explain the nature of this divine teaching St. Augustine refers to the metaphor of the "light", known as the THEORY OF ILLUMINATION. In order to understand it, we must keep in mind that the act by which thought (the soul) knows the truth is comparable to the way in which the eye sees the bodies. In order to be seen, the things must be illuminated by the sun, even though this star is not directly seen. By analogy, the scientific truths must be intelligible by a certain light, so that they may be understood by the reason. If the sun is the source of light, God is the source of truth.

For St. Augustine, the object of illumination is the truth which God pours into the soul, that is, into our intellect. This light is not supernatural, nor is it a revelation, but rather a natural participation of the intelligible objects or ideas. The doctrine of illumination signifies the knowledge of creatures and of scientific truths, which are "copies", from the Ideas. For God is the intelligible Sun which illumines our intellect and makes it participate in the ideas or intelligible objects. Along this path, St. Augustine affirms the soul as the seat of truth. Since the truth is immutable, the soul itself must be immortal once it has been created. Consequently, the soul is basically spirit or MENS, that is, intelligence or thought. Since the soul cannot expect any help from what is inferior, it can know itself only by a purely interior way.

On the other hand, since it has been created to the image and likeness of God, we can find in it images of the Trinity. Here St. Augustine will begin the illustration of the dogma of the Trinity. For St. Augustine the triad mind, knowledge and love constitute an image of the One and Triune God.

### 7. Evil and Freedom {#medieval-philosophy.2.7}
The "full Essence" or God is the Good, both for St. Augustine and for Plato. Hence, by creation all reality is good in as far as it is. The perfections of the creatures are only participations of the Good, according to a more or a less. Evil has not been created by God, but it exists. Therefore, it can only consist in the privation of a due perfection, and as privation, in order to exist it is supported in the good as in a subject.

This identification of being with good and the concept of evil as a privation of good (and hence of being or reality) we will find constantly repeated by Christian thinkers, starting with St. Augustine. One usually speaks of many classes of evil, such as physical or natural evils, but the only true evil is moral evil, or sin, which proceeds from the free will of rational creatures. How is this possible? Is the free will evil? St. Augustine's answer is clear: The human will, considered in itself, is good, and free will, in itself, is good and the condition for attaining happiness. But the created will is fallible, it can err, and the exercise of free will brings with it the risk of sin. Thus, free will becomes evil when it is deprived of the DUE ORDER, as St. Augustine expresses in his "Confessions." The rebellion of the body against the soul is the consequence of original sin, from which concupiscence and ignorance proceed. Since then the soul, which had been created in order to govern a body, finds itself ruled by the body and is thus oriented towards the material. And since it takes from itself the sensations and images, it exhausts itself and ends up taking itself to be a body. St. Augustine's doctrine is no different than the experience of his life. For this reason he traces with strong and vivid lines the drama of evil as he recalls it in his adolescence. He shows how one evil leads to another in his "Confessions" and he also shows how the gravity of the sin has great influence on man's life: the successive falls, the excuses that become justifications, etc. In short, he shows that evil which is not cured grows and builds for itself ways without outlet. Therefore St. Augustine understood the need of grace in order to be able to come out of sin and to fulfil the Will of God. In fact, Divine Grace restores to the free will the power of doing good. Thus for St. Augustine freedom consists in using well the free will. "LIBERTAS VERA EST CHRISTO SERVIRE" (True freedom is to serve Christ). Hence freedom is greater when man is more united to God and thus is less able to carry out evil. Thus St. Augustine ties in with the theological topic of Grace.

### 8. The Two Cities {#medieval-philosophy.2.8}
St. Augustine gives us his moral doctrine and that of happiness in his work "De civitate Dei" (The City of God). In man virtue is the condition for happiness, and the ordered will is the condition for virtue, but the free will is raised to the love of God by grace and manifests itself in a multitude of concrete actions which affect a double aspect of the natural sphere: the individual and the social. Since the human body is good, the temporal societies (CIVITAS TERRENA) are destined to achieve the goods needed for life, but there also exists another city (CIVITAS DEI) which is formed by Christians of all nations, the construction of which is what gives meaning to universal history. Men and States are ruled by the will, but by a will that must be ordered and subject to norms.

In other words, every society requires peace, and peace is "the tranquillity of order." But two cities exist, one ordered to the material, the other to the spiritual, which are distinct and even opposed to one another. The two cities are mixed and intermeshed with one another. They are not two types of historical realizations, but opposite principles of personal conduct and of social realities.

The sense of history is that these two cities oppose one another and fight between themselves. Nevertheless, and this is St. Augustine's conclusion, whatever the history of man may be, at the end the CIVITAS TERRENA will perish and the CIVITAS DEI will be victorious in virtue of the love of God.

### 9. Influence on St. Thomas Aquinas {#medieval-philosophy.2.9}

St. Thomas Aquinas recognizes the indisputable authority of St. Augustine as Doctor of the Faith, but as philosopher, he accepts some of his doctrines and rejects others. Concretely, St. Thomas accepts three important doctrines: the causal transcendentalism or metaphysical abyss which exists between God and the creatures; exemplarism, accepting the doctrine of participation at the apex of his metaphysical thought; and the solution of the problem of evil. Nevertheless, he has serious reservations about other points of St. Augustine's metaphysics, as for example the metaphysical structure of the creature and the doctrine of knowledge. For St. Augustine, the "light" of the intellect is the reception of ideas from God, but for St. Thomas it is the light of the agent intellect by which what is intelligible in material things is abstracted or grasped. All of this is related with the doctrine of hylomorphism which we shall see later.

### 10. St. Augustine and Augustinianism {#medieval-philosophy.2.10}

We can consider two principal sources in St. Augustine's philosophical work. On the one hand, Neo-Platonism and on the other hand, Revelation. St. Augustine does an analogous work with Neo-Platonism to what St. Thomas Aquinas will do centuries later with Aristotle. He christianizes it, that is, he converts an emanationist doctrine into a creationist doctrine. In this task we find the original lines of his thought, which can be synthesized thus:

a) there is no true philosophy unless man adheres to the supernatural order, which frees man's will from concupiscence through grace and safeguards thought from scepticism through the certainty that comes from Revelation, the source of light;

b) between two solutions of the same problem, one must grant more importance to the one which explains things through God, than through nature and man (the inferior is understood better and is justified by the superior);

c) one cannot separate the life of thought from practical conduct. Thus the true philosophy not only provides the end, but also the means;

d) every intellectual itinerary must begin from the principle of the interiority of thought, that is to say, the soul is the seat of truth.

This is not the complete formulation of St. Augustine's doctrine but it is a correct expression of its spirit and constitutes the framework of the whole Augustinian tradition, which will exercise an enormous influence throughout the ages, even to our time. Till the 12th century, St. Augustine will be the great source, if not to say the only important one, of medieval thought. Augustinianism is renewed, by opposition to the stands which abuse logic, in the schools of St. Victor of Chartres (12th century) and has influence on all the directions of thought, both in the mystical thought of St. Bernard as well as in the metaphysics of St. Anselm of Canterbury, in the voluntarism which comes to Duns Scotus and in the contemplative philosophy of St. Bonaventure, in the Thomism and in the Franciscanism which claim for themselves fidelity to St. Augustine, though in both of them it coexists with Aristotelianism.
