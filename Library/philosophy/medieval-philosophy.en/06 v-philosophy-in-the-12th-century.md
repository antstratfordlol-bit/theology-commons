## V. Philosophy in the 12th Century {#medieval-philosophy.5}
The chief characteristic of the 12th century is the maturation of the whole cultural and philosophical patrimony received from Boëthius until St. Anselm, and constitutes the preparation of the new age of Western thought which the 13th century will constitute. We must highlight two lines:

a) The first line runs through St. Anselm and continues in the 12th century in Gilbert de la Porrée. It gives rise to a more abstract and technical Platonism, which will permit the assimilation of the Arabic philosophical doctrines. Since, in last instance, the framework of this speculative task is the Neo-Platonism of St. Augustine, this current is usually called "Augustinianism."

b) The second line involves the progress of dialectics or logic which will lead to the discussion of the problem of the universals and, with Abelard, to the necessity of entering into the metaphysical terrain for a correct solution. Along this line the logicians come into contact with the metaphysics of knowledge and the psychology of Aristotle. Thus, in the 12th century the conditions are set for a more intense absorption of both Neo-Platonism and of Aristotle.

### 1. The Controversy about the Universals {#medieval-philosophy.5.1}
Along one path or another, the philosophers have always stumbled upon the problem of the universals. In the 12th century the approach has three modalities:

a) Those who follow Plato ask whether the ideas or the particular things are the true being.

b) According to Porphyry, are the genera and species (universals) real things or mental structures of our thought? Do they exist in the particular things or also separated in themselves?

c) And finally, as the question seems to have been put since Roscelin, are the genera and species (universals) only names or are they things? Those who give the first answer are called "nominalists" and those who give the second answer are called "realists."

a) Roscelin of Compiègne

Little is known about this man. His thought has come down to us through his adversaries, St. Anselm and Peter Abelard. According to them, for Roscelin, there is not colour, there are only coloured things; there is no wisdom, there are only wise men. In reality, we should give each thing a distinct name, but since the number of our words would be insufficient, we group several individuals together under each name. The universals, then, only respond to two concrete realities, neither of which is genus or species or general idea:

a) to the physical reality of the word, taken as "flatus vocis" or emission of the voice;

b) to concrete individuals, which each word has the task of naming. It is right that he has been called the initiator of nominalism. At any rate, Roscelin would have to explain how these sounds, which make up a language, offer meaning to thought. One might have forgotten his doctrine but for its scandalous application to the Trinity.

b) Peter Abelard (1079-1142)

Peter Abelard is one of the most important personalities of the 12th century. He was a disciple of Roscelin and of William of Champeaux. His most important work is theological, but he was a philosopher also. He had a rare skill for his times, that of posing and solving philosophical problems without referring to theology. In the profession of faith that Abelard made at the end of his life, he said that dialectics were his ruin, since he was constantly using it in polemics which caused him to lose masters, friends and disciples. He also showed excessive confidence in the application of logic to dogma.

The one who first suffered his attacks was William of Champeaux, who at that time maintained that the universals were real, that is, things actually existing outside of the mind, and that the individuals to which the universal refers, are only distinguished from one another by accidents. Abelard objected that if the universal "animal" exists really and entirely in the species "horse" and in the species "man", then the animal nature is rational and irrational at the same time. The observation is correct. William changed his argument and said that the individuals of the same species have nothing in common, neither accidents nor essence, but rather an identity of "indifference" or absence of difference which is expressed by the universal. Abelard's reply was that "not to differ" is the same as "agree with", so that the same difficulties reappear. William of Champeaux then retired from teaching.

Abelard is both anti-realist as well as anti-nominalist, though his stance is influential in the nominalism of William of Ockham who later on absorbed or reduced metaphysics into logic. According to Abelard, universality is not of the things, but only of the words which have meaning (SERMO). If words were mere "flatus vocis" without meaning anything, it would be the same to say "man is stone" as to say "man is animal." But this second phrase is correct, thus the things have something which justifies the validity or invalidity of what we affirm about them. The foundation of the universal in the things is what is called "state" (STATUS) or the manner of being which is proper to the individuals. Well then, the error of those who say the universals are real consists in confusing "man", which is nothing, with "to be a man = John." Likewise "horse" does not exist, but "to be a horse" is real. Definitively, Abelard observes that for a thing to be determinate, as the principle of noncontradiction demands, it must be determinate to individuation. Therefore all beings are completely distinct. Then what does the universal signify? The STATUS in which various individuals are found which are totally distinct. The solution of Abelard is clearly contradictory, for if the individuals are different, they cannot meet in any "state". Nevertheless, he will complete his theory with a psychological explanation which does not resolve the contradiction. Reason has the faculty of abstracting, that is of distinguishing what in reality is united, centring the attention on a single aspect. In this way, if we observe a group of things, we find that a determinate aspect is found in several of them and from this collective we obtain a confused image which we designate with the word "universal" or SERMO.

Thus, if I say the word "tower", I have in my mind a weak, confused and very indeterminate image. On the other hand, if I say "the tower of Cluny", I represent a vivid and determinate image even in the details. The word "tower" is a universal and "the tower of Cluny" is real. Therefore, the word with meaning, or universal SERMO, is not a precise idea or concept. For this reason it can be said in general that man only has opinions, but man is capable of exact knowledge (science) of the concrete beings considered in an isolated manner. In summary, the universal or SERMO is only in the intellect, though it signifies in a confused way a plurality of real things. Only God distinguishes perfectly and clearly each one of the existent beings, in a unique and simple act of knowing, or, if one prefers, in a single Idea.

This solution that Abelard gives to the problem of the universals is basically purely logical, because he doesn't face up to what it is in the things which is the foundation of the universal predication. For St. Thomas, the universal expresses the participation which an individual has in a determinate nature. The nature "man" is not realized totally in the individual, (if it were, there would only be one man), but is participated by it, by the fact that its substantial form, the root of the perfection of the nature, is received in matter. In the mind, the NATURA HOMINIS acquires some special properties, since due to abstraction, it is separated from the individuating conditions.

Abelard is also important for his contribution to the scholastic method, especially accepted by St. Thomas. He describes it in his "Sic et non", in which he proposes technical rigour in treatises, and incites to investigation, bringing together around one and the same subject apparently contradictory testimonies of the Fathers, in order to look for their concordance.

### 2. Neo-Platonism in the School of Chartres: Gilbert de la Porrée {#medieval-philosophy.5.2}
The school of Chartres is the most flourishing intellectual centre of the 12th century. Founded by St. Fulbert (960-1028), it acquires its maximum prestige with Bernard of Chartres (+1124) who establishes its definitive orientation. A certain Platonism dominates in this school, coming from very diverse sources, which include commentaries by Calcidius of some dialogues of Plato, some doctrines of St. Augustine, Boëthius, PsD and John S.E. Taken as a whole, three important positions are affirmed:

a) the creation of matter;

b) the identity of the Ideas and the divine Intellect;

c) that things are composed of idea (form) and matter.

The masters of Chartres are considered as "grammarians" in the ancient sense of the word, that is, educators who through classical literature form the style, the taste and above all the culture of the students. Nevertheless, in this century the "grammarian" undergoes the invasion of logic and consequently changes orientation. He no longer fosters humanistic culture, but gives rise to speculative grammar. Gilbert de la Porrée (1076-1154) is the greatest representative of the School of Chartres, and will be its chancellor from 1121 to 1140. He died as bishop of Poitiers, the city where he was born. His most characteristic points are described in the following sections.

a) The Forms

Gilbert's "De Sex Principiis" was studied widely until the 15th century. It is a metaphysical interpretation of the logic contained in Aristotle"s "Categories." Here he begins to deal with the principal problem which he posited, viz., the intrinsic formal cause of natural or composite beings. In accordance with the Platonic tradition of the reality of the universals or ideas (the Latins translated the latter as "form"), he divides the 10 categories into 2 groups:

i) the "inherent" forms, or substance, quantity, quality and relation;

ii) the "accessory" forms, or place, time, posture, "habitus", action and passion (the "sex principia" or six principles).

By "inherent" forms he understands the forms or principles which are the substance itself or inhere in it as such, which is so with the quantity or quality which are proper to the substance. Nothing hinders the substance from being caught up in a set of relations, but Gilbert doesn’t explain their nature, thus giving rise to the controversy as to whether the relation is a real being or a being of reason, a topic of undoubted interest for the understanding of the dogma of the Holy Trinity. The "accessory" forms are only determinations or adjacent properties to the substance, which they affect more or less proximately.

b) Substance and Subsistence

In his commentary on Boëthius' "De Trinitate", Gilbert distinguishes between substance and subsistence. The first is an actually existing individual, a substance because it supports (substat) a certain number of accidents. As substance it is cause and principle of the accidents participating in its being. Subsistence is defined as the property of that which doesn't need accidents in order to be what it is. This condition is fulfilled on the one hand by the substance - every substance is subsistent - and on the other hand by those realities which are the genera and species, that is, the universals. The universals and species are not substances, but they are subsistences which for Gilbert have been created by God to the image of the divine Ideas (pure substances which subsist without ever mixing with matter). These genera and species taken in themselves are "subsistent forms". If they are considered with respect to bodies, they are the "substantial forms" which, united to the corresponding prime matter, give rise to the corporeal substance. That is, they are the substantial forms of the substance.

Consequently, each individual is informed by a generic subsistence, a specific subsistence and the substantial properties already mentioned above. In order to grasp the universals one must begin from these "substantial forms" in virtue of which the substances exist. Human reason can abstract in a body one of the "forms" and compare it with other analogous forms found in other bodies, so that it can come to construct a specific form. If it continues this work on a group of similar species, it obtains a generic form, another universal. If the intellect succeeds in transcending this universal it reaches the eternally subsisting Ideas. It's good to recall that prime matter is not intelligible. Thus, Gilbert inherits from Boëthius the tendency to fix his attention on the form of the beings, which is intelligible, and he reduces the real beings to their intelligible elements. Thus he thinks about them abstractly, converting them into entitative constituents of the real being. We shall find this procedure later in Avicenna; Duns Scotus will develop it fully.

c) Degrees of Being

God, essential reality by excellence, is found at the apex of being. Everything which exists receives from Him its own essence. Taking Boëthius' distinction between "quo est" (form or "esse") and "id quod est" (the whole), he says that divinity is communicated to creatures by conferring "esse" on them. Creation consists precisely in producing a form to the image of the divine Ideas. This generic form determines the union of matter and the particular forms. Led by the absolute primacy of the "quo est" over the "id quod est", it seems that he maintains, along with the absolute divine simplicity, that the "id quod est Deus" is due to His "esse" or "essentia." Thus he was accused of distinguishing between God and Divinity.

Prescinding from the theological aspect, this distinction manifests the ultimate conception of Gilbert. For him, the things are composed of a subject and a set of determinations which make them to be what they are. Along this way, the topic of the formal constituent of the corporeal beings has been resolved, in a clearly Platonic line, but with metaphysical depth, which will render difficult and even slow down the understanding of Aristotle's hylomorphic theory. Gilbert is without doubt the most rigorous speculative spirit of the 12th century. If Abelard stands out by far in logic, Gilbert surpasses him as a metaphysician.

### 3. Speculative Mysticism {#medieval-philosophy.5.3}
St. Bernard of Clairvaux (1090-1153) is the outstanding mystic of the 12th century, and the Augustinian monastery of St. Victor in Paris represents a corrective reaction in the face of the free application of dialectics in the field of faith. The personality of St. Bernard dominates his whole age and he is considered the "guide of the West", both in politics and in religion. He argues against Abelard, saying: "To want to know only to know, is shameful curiosity. To want to know in order to be known, is vanity. To want to know in order to sell knowledge, is a despicable business. To want to know in order to edify, is charity."

He elaborates in depth his doctrine of mystical love: True wisdom starts by meditation, by which the intellect places itself at God's service. One continues by contemplation or rest of the spirit in the certainty of eternal truths and finally with ecstasy the soul detaches itself from the activity of the senses and is divinized. Those who follow St. Bernard will incline more and more toward a religious moralism with Platonic elements. But those of St. Victor will show much more openness to profane knowledge. They teach that knowledge is always useful for union with God. Their considerations will have dialectic speculation with Platonic elements. This mysticism offers the first studies of psychology: the soul, the relation of the soul and the body, and of intellect and will, which will be systematized later on.
