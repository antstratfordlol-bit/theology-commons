## VI. Jewish and Arabian Philosophy {#medieval-philosophy.6}
### 1. Introduction {#medieval-philosophy.6.1}
One of the elements that most contributed to the development of Western philosophy at the end of the 12th century and above all in the 13th century was the contributions of the Arab and Jewish philosophers. It is thus of interest to study their historical genesis and their principal representatives. Before Justinian finally closed the Athenian philosophical schools, the cultural patrimony of the Greeks had come to Syria and Mesopotamia in the hands of the Christians, for they needed it in order to read the Old Testament. When, two centuries later, Islam conquered that zone, it was usual that, along with theology in the schools, philosophy, mathematics, medicine, etc. were taught. The works of classical authors such as Euclid, Archimedes, Aristotle, etc. were translated into Arabic, either from Syriac or from Greek directly, thanks to the Abbasid dynasty, as well as works of more recent authors like Alexander of Aphrodisias and John Damascene.

In this way they received an Aristotle full of Neo-Platonic influences, so that other false works could be attributed to him. All of this will give rise to a Platonic-Aristotelian synthesis which will be held to be the doctrine of Aristotle in the 13th century. On the other hand, something occurs to Arab philosophical speculation similar to what occurs to that carried out by Christian authors. They also bring up the relations between philosophy and theology, reason and faith, that is, between a world which calls for an explanation and a unique God Creator Who is also known by revelation.

A positive attitude towards philosophy is found in Al Kindi who taught in Basra and Baghdad. He was an encyclopedist who died in 873. In his "De intellectu" he wants to explain the Aristotelian distinction between agent and possible intellect. He conceives the intellect which is always in act as a supreme Intelligence, a spiritual substance higher than the individual soul, which permits with its action in the soul the intellect of man to pass from potency to act. Thus is stated for the first time in Arab philosophy the thesis that there exists a separate Agent Intellect common to all men.

The work of Alfarabi (+950) who lived and taught in Baghdad is more fruitful. He opens the way for a metaphysical distinction between God and the world based on distinguishing between essence and existence in the creature. He indicates that natural beings are contingent, which is manifested by our notion of them. In fact, the idea of what a thing is does not include the fact of its existence. Thus if we pass from the logical to the metaphysical order, this means that existence isn't included in the essence of things, so for him existence is an accident which comes from the necessary Being. In another work he speaks of the separate Agent Intellect as a spiritual being which transcends the world and is the Giver of Forms.

In a diametrically opposed direction we find Algazel (1058-1111) who proposes combating the philosophers with intransigent fideism. He proclaims God's absolute omnipotence, denying causality in creatures.

### 2. Avicenna (980-1037) {#medieval-philosophy.6.2}
Avicenna (Ibn Sina) is the summit of Arab metaphysics and one of the great philosophers of medieval history. Excellent in memory and intelligence he precociously assimilated all the knowledge of his time, especially medicine and philosophy. He led an intense political and intellectual life. Particularly important are his writings on logic, philosophy of nature, psychology and metaphysics. His philosophy can be characterized as a synthesis of Aristotelianism and Neo-Platonism which, as opposed to PsD and "Liber de Causis", grants no importance to the doctrine of participation. On the other hand, his philosophy is in full concordance with Islam. Thus the first point to deal with is the distinction between God and creatures.

a) Proof of the Existence of God and Creation

When several creatures are considered in thought, says Avicenna, one notices immediately that each of them is "something that is", but this notion is not simple, since it implies that in the being there is distinction between essence and existence, as Alfarabi had correctly said. The beings which have no real existence or which have only accidental existence are POSSIBLE BEINGS and that is what all creatures are. In other words, the possible being is either that which has no obstacles to exist on the part of the essence and it lacks only the accident of actual existence, or that being in whose definition of the essence existence is not included, as for example, any material being. Since possible beings exist, their cause, or necessary Being, must exist. The necessary Being is that which it would be contradictory to conceive or think as not existing, since its ESSENCE cannot be anything else than its ESSE, that is, the ESSENCE and the ESSE are the same thing.

For Avicenna the distinction between God and creatures is the same as between necessary Being and possible being. Now it happens that in the one, simple, unique, necessary Being, the action of creating is not voluntary and free, but is concomitant with its own nature, and in order to explain this, Avicenna finds support in the emanationist doctrine of Plotinus, binding it, like all the Arabs, to the astronomical knowledge of his time. From the emanationist principle that the One can only give rise to one, Avicenna deduces that since God is the intelligent Being par excellence, He knows Himself eternally and thus causes the First Intelligence. The tenth emanation gives rise to the separate Agent Intellect. The fundamental conclusion is that creation is necessary on the part of God and hence is eternal.

Moreover, Avicenna carries to the maximum the contingent character of the creatures. In fact, on one hand he affirms the distinction between necessary and possible being and on the other hand he maintains the absolute necessity of creation (necessitarianism). When he later on withdraws this doctrine in order to separate God from creatures duly, he necessarily will have to resort to the absolute affirmation of the divine freedom to create as we shall occurs also with Duns Scotus.

b) Philosophy of Nature

Avicenna conceives heaven as a series of 10 compact, concentric spheres, each with its own soul and body. The last, sublunar sphere is that of the separate Agent Intellect from which emanate the substantial forms of bodies and also the intelligible species or the knowledge of those forms by human intellects. The substantial forms must be received by prime matter, but this does not occur in just any way, for prime matter in reality is matter deprived of form, that is, a kind of common nature which must be prepared to receive a form. What is needed is the action of external or second causes which take matter out of its indetermination and dispose it to receive some form. Also a principle of individuation is needed so that the prime matter is conditioned to receive a concrete form.

Avicenna was the first to teach that the principle of individuation is "materia signata quantitate" (matter designated by quantity). The "forma corporeitatis" and the second causes dispose matter to receive the specific form. The "forma corporeitatis" gives matter dimensions, and so the quantified matter is capable of individuating the specific form which it later receives, and the matter-form composite is the concrete being. One error of this explanation is that it admits the plurality of substantial forms in the being.

Avicenna established the distinction between the causes of being (ESSE) and of becoming (FIERI). God is the agent cause of being because He permanently produces actual being. The creatures, on the other hand, are causes of becoming or of movement. Moreover, the second causes (creatures, among which is the First Intelligence) are only able to dispose their effects. Together with this, he maintains a determinism in the physical order: in the same way that God cannot not create, once there is a cause an effect is necessarily produced. Along this way he will come to deny human freedom.

c) The Soul

Man is one of the beings of this world which, besides having a body, also has a soul, produced by the last heavenly sphere which in its turn is produced by the separate Agent Intellect (Dator formarum, or giver of forms). Thus it is an intelligible substance which can think and knows that it thinks, independently of the body which it animates. There is no substantial union between body and soul. In spite of assigning this origin to the soul, Avicenna maintains its immortality. Because of the soul, man has intelligence. How does he know? The intellect is a passive faculty in which the separate Agent Intellect impresses the intelligibles, that is, the form of the sensible beings.

Nevertheless, in order that the human intellect may be actuated in the indicated way, it must be disposed or prepared, by considering the images of the bodies perceived by the senses, by comparing them, classifying them, etc. Thus, the primary object of the intellect are the concrete individuals, and the secondary object is the knowledge of the essences. It is precisely at this last point that Avicenna begins his metaphysics.

d) Influence on St. Thomas Aquinas

St. Thomas knew very well the doctrine of Avicenna and he took many elements from it, though often he correctly them substantially. Here are some of the most important elements.

St. Thomas accepted the terminology "essence" and "esse", but he declares that "esse" is no accident, but the fundamental act of the being. For his third way of demonstrating the existence of God he makes use of the "necesse esse" and "possibile esse", but he restricts the range of contingent or possible being to the strictly corruptible, to corporeal beings.

St. Thomas does not accept the Avicennian necessitarianism or causal emanationism, because it opposes the Christian doctrine of immediate creation. Nor does he accept the principle of Plotinus that the One causes only the one, for the same reason and also because from that it follows that God has only indirect knowledge of creatures. For St. Thomas "esse" is a universal effect which is caused directly by a universal cause, God. St. Thomas makes use of the distinction between the causes of "esse" and the causes of "fieri", and also of some of the doctrine of abstraction, but he rejects the existence of a separate Agent Intellect, inadmissible for a Christian, and the Neo-Platonic error which reduces the role of the senses in knowledge. Thus, while St. Thomas moves on the plane of "esse", Avicenna moves on the plane of essence. In this sense he has much influence on the late Scholastics and through them on the modern epoch when the notion of being as act is lost.

### 3. Averroes (1126-1198) {#medieval-philosophy.6.3}
Averroes (Ibn Rushd) must be placed in the same category with Avicenna as a philosopher. His most important works are the very literal "Commentaries" on works of Aristotle in which he wants to expound the genuine doctrine of the latter, purifying it of the Neo-Platonic and theological elements, so that the teaching of reason alone remains patent. If Avicenna represents a Neo-Platonic Aristotelianism accommodated to Arab theology, Averroes is much rather the figure of rigid Aristotelianism closed to theology with rationalist tendency. Hence the bad name which the first medieval Aristotelians had and the effort of St. Thomas to restore the true Aristotle. In fact, the main difficulties of the faith in the 13th and 14th centuries come from Averroism.

a) Reason and Faith

Averroes finds the cause of diversity in philosophical opinions in the constant interference of religious truths. Thus he tries to determine the relations between reason and revelation. For a Muslim divine revelation is contained in the Koran, the holy book which can and must be understood by all, each according to his own capacity. There are three levels of understanding of the Koran: the vulgar or literal, proper to simple people who like symbols and metaphors; the theological or dialectical, conforming to probable arguments; and finally the philosophical, demanding rigorous, conclusive demonstrations. Thus, it is the philosophers who discover the true sense of the Koran, the allegorical. Thus it is that every man must understand the only truth, the Koran, on his own level without trying to climb higher, without divulging what he knows to his inferiors for they wouldn't understand. The conclusion for Averroes is simple: the philosopher has the last word, because he judges according to rigorous or necessary reasons, whereas the theologian remains in probabilities. This doctrine led to the theory of the double truth, which states that something can be true in philosophy and false in theology, and viceversa, though both can be simultaneously admitted. His ideas on the relation between reason and faith will be repeated in modern philosophy (Spinoza, Hegel).

b) Metaphysics

For Averroes metaphysics is the science of being as being and of its properties. "Being" must be understood as that which is here and now, whether substance or accident (which participates in the being of the substance). Hence it makes no sense to consider, as Avicenna did, being or "esse" as something apart and even less to affirm that it is an accident. Thus, Averroes identifies the actuality of being with the essence which makes it be what it is here and now, that is, he remains on the Aristotelian plane of the substance in act. In agreement with Aristotle's physics, he comes to the demonstration of the existence of God as Pure Act by the way of movement and of finality. The world is necessary and eternal, for otherwise why would God create? The world was possible always, therefore it has been created by God from all eternity. Since God does not change and the world exists, he deduces that the world has been created necessarily. Averroes affirms the existence of beings necessary "ab alio" (the heavenly bodies). They are necessary because they are indestructible, not because they are necessary in themselves, but because of God, and they are subject to His domain.

St. Thomas will make use of this doctrine to combat Avicenna's contingentism, affirming that not all creatures are contingent, though created. Some substances - the spiritual creatures - are necessary "ab alio".

c) The Soul

All the obscurity of Aristotle's "De Anima" is equally obscure in Averroes' commentary, and he even adds new complications. The problem is basically this: in principle the soul should be a form "educed" from the potentiality of matter by the creative action of God. But Averroes doesn't understand that this form can be a spiritual substance. He didn't doubt the spirituality of the soul or at least of a part of it, and consequently he affirms that it is not the form of the human body, that it constitutes a separate substance. Precisely because a determined body doesn't correspond to the soul, the soul is not multiplied nor individualized, and it is therefore universal. It is a Soul common to all men, like a collective "I". Whether one thinks that the intellect is what constitutes the soul or is simply a faculty of the soul, to affirm the existence of a universal Soul means recognizing the existence of a single Intellect for all men.

This is how the Averroists of the 13th century logically interpreted this doctrine. This common Intellect acts in man in a way similar to that of the separate Agent Intellect which Avicenna spoke about, but Avicenna admitted a personal intellect for every man. Regarding knowledge, the specific faculty in man is the cogitative, the highest sense faculty. Its function is to construct an image or phantasm with the impressions and sensations that the senses receive from the outside world. Thus, the unique intellect produces the intelligible knowledge in man, like the sun, by means of light, produces vision in the eyes.

St. Thomas, a commentator of the works of Aristotle who is just as literal as Averroes, though deeper, criticizes this by saying that man has the experience that he is the one who knows and who understands, and no one else. Hence, in each human subject there is a proper spiritual form or rational soul. Moreover, if only the cogitative were proper to each man, there would be no personal spirituality nor immortality, nor personal freedom and responsibility. Once this is said, St. Thomas accepts the doctrine of the cogitative (which Averroes formulated for the first time in history), giving it a central place in his metaphysics of knowledge, and showing the continuity between the personal intellect and the sensitivity (phantasm of the cogitative. With this he explains how man can grasp the material singular or the concrete thing.

### 4. Avicebron (1021-1058) {#medieval-philosophy.6.4}
The Jews received the influence of the Arab philosophers. From this contact arose several Jewish thinkers in the 11th and 12th centuries. With Avicebron (Salomon Ibn Gabirol), Judeo- Spanish philosophy reaches a considerable height. Some of his 20 works are conserved. His main philosophical work is the dialogue "Fons vitae". Avicebron has passed into history as the defender in the West of the doctrine of universal hylomorphism and of the plurality of forms. This stance found notable echo in the Augustinian tradition, due to a common Neo-Platonism. The starting point of "Fons vitae" is the radical distinction between a personal God, simple and one, and creatures. To explain it, he affirms that all creatures, spiritual included, are composed of matter and form.

For Avicebron there is a universal essence or "prime matter", made up of a universal form and a universal matter, which is the only substrate or support for all the other forms. Thus, one being is distinct from another because of one or several complementary forms. There is a hierarchy of forms according to degrees of perfection so that the higher perfections include the lower, and according to the number of forms received, there is a mineral, a plant, an animal, etc. In its turn, there are two types of prime matter, simple matter and spiritual matter, which explains the difference between purely spiritual beings and material beings.

This doctrine arises from a confusion between logic and metaphysics. Avicebron identified the logical binomial "genus-difference" with the metaphysical binomial "matter-form". Hence the supreme genus is universal matter which in itself exists in potency, existing in act when it receives the successive forms. St. Thomas criticizes this doctrine for falsely affirming that potency cannot be found outside of matter. Every being is different from God because it receives "esse" as potency receives act.

### 5. Moses Maimonides (1153-1204) {#medieval-philosophy.6.5}
Born in Cordoba and died in Cairo, with him Jewish philosophy reaches its apex. A very prolific writer, with his "Guide for the Perplexed" he exercised great influence in the West, especially on St. Albert and St. Thomas. This work, considered as the "Summa theologica" of Judaism, is based on a certain allegorical and rationalistic interpretation of Scripture and on philosophy (mainly Avicennian-Aristotelian, but without ignoring doctrines of Averroes). For Maimonides the Mosaic Law (Torah) and philosophy are knowledge of distinct kind, but absolutely incompatible. In reality, the proper object of philosophy is nothing other than rational confirmation of the Torah.

To show the existence of God he takes the arguments of Aristotle and Avicenna on motion, causality, and necessity and concludes that God is the Necessary Being, because He is Being. Moreover, God is absolutely simple, in Him essence and existence are the same, but creatures have being as an accidental addition. Though we know that God exists, it is impossible to know His essence. Any name given to Him expresses what He is not, even the positive names. On the other hand, he shows that Aristotle's arguments proving the world's eternity are not conclusive, not even those trying to establish creation in time. It seems more probable to him what is known by Revelation. The world has begun in time. Maimonides also points out that the proofs of God's existence don't depend on this point and thus keep all their value.

St. Thomas will accept this doctrine. Maimonides maintains, with Avicenna, that there is a separate and unique Agent Intellect which puts in act the passive intellect of each man. In this way Maimonides wants to stress the Old-Testament idea that God, thrice holy, is inaccessible. He says that man's end is to contemplate God, beginning with sense knowledge and philosophical speculation aided by Revelation. To reach this end, he must be united to the separate Agent Intellect in its own realm, science. Since only the wise possess science, only they will achieve immortality.

### 6. The "Liber de Causis" {#medieval-philosophy.6.6}
An anonymous Arab author of the 9th or 10th century wrote the "Liber de Causis" which passed as a work of Aristotle and influenced greatly Christian theologians. St. Thomas uncovered the falsity of this attribution and the Neo-Platonic basis of the work. He showed that its content is a summary of a work, Elementatio theologica of Proclus (410-485), a disciple of Plotinus. The author adds the correction that the first Principle, the One, is Creator and is God. The creation of the world is understood as a mediate action on the part of God since, according to this doctrine, what comes before everything else in the One or the first Principle, which in its turn is the Good in the Platonic sense. From the One everything else proceeds by steps. First of all is Being or Ens, the first hypostasis of the One, from which follow those of the first Intellect and the first Life. Beginning with these three realities or hypostases all things arise by participation from the three.

According to the work, "Esse" or "primum Ens" is a separate form created immediately by God. Then it gives being to things by way of creation, for it presupposes nothing, but the first Life and the first Intellect cause their effects in things by presupposing being, that is, by way of informing. Hence the creation of things is not caused directly by God. It must here be observed that this doctrine is the Platonic identification of the logical and the real orders. For the "Liber", the logical genera are subsistent realities and hence the maximum degrees of perfection. St. Thomas doesn't admit this concept, since "esse" is perfection itself, not a degree of perfection, but he does accept some few texts from this work, like "prima rerum creatarum est "esse" (the first of the created things is "esse").

For St. Thomas, this serves to indicate that the first act of creatures is their "esse". He also accepts the idea that the human soul is like the horizon of time and eternity, for it is the form of a body and at the same time a spiritual substance, and it is the lower limit of the purely spiritual creatures.
