## IV. The First Scholastics {#medieval-philosophy.4}
### 1. The Carolingian Renaissance {#medieval-philosophy.4.1}
The origins of scholastic thought are linked to the attempt of culturization which, begun by Charlemagne, lasts till the 10th century. This task was possible thanks to England which had remained untouched by the invasion of the Gothic peoples. England had received the Catholic Faith before the disappearance of the Roman Empire in the West and the consequent cultural upset, and of course the Holy See played a very important role in the evangelization and culturization of England. The classical Latin culture was flourishing there by the middle of the 7th century. We find in Canterbury the use of a plan of studies very similar to the Trivium and the Quadrivium proposed by Cassiodorus, but with the aim of understanding Holy Scripture better. Scarcely a hundred years later, the English undertake the task of evangelizing and culturizing the pagan people on the European continent. This was accompanied by the establishing of excellent libraries in Britain, around which important cultural centres of classical Latin culture grew. Among the important figures we find St. Bede the Venerable (673-735), who carried out a work of compiling knowledge, a work analogous to that of Cassiodorus (480- 579) and St. Isidore of Seville (+636).

When Charlemagne conceived the plan of constructing an empire like that of the Romans, he considered as the first duty of his subjects their keeping themselves completely at the service of God in accordance with the Christian religion. He ordered in 789 that every diocese and monastery should open schools to teach not only reading and writing but also liberal arts, grammar, etc. Little by little these schools developed into the centres of intellectual life of the Middle Ages, till the birth of the universities in the 13th century, and from them comes the name of "scholastic philosophy" which is the name usually given to the philosophical speculation of this epoch. Moreover, Charlemagne assembled in his court the best minds of that time from different countries. The outstanding master was Alcuin (730-804), an Englishman educated in York, who enjoyed great prestige. More important than his writings is his work of civilization. He wanted to establish in France a new, a better Athens. Fulfilling his ideas, he organized the palace school, introduced the study of the Trivium and the Quadrivium which was followed in York and sent monks to Britain to copy the books in the best libraries, with the aim of distributing them in France.

Alcuin's influence was extended to Germany through his disciple Rhabanus Maurus (784-850), bishop of Mainz, whose influence on German culture earned him the title "praeceptor Germaniae." Under these two great men the cultural and literary restoration of Europe began. However, concern for philosophy is absent during this period of reconstruction. Only at the end of the 8th and in the 9th century do topics such as the substance of the soul, proof of God's existence, etc., appear again. For this reason, we easily see that the works of the ancients are accepted without criticism, like those of PsD.

### 2. John Scotus Eriugena and his Version of the Philosophy of the Fathers {#medieval-philosophy.4.2}
John Scotus Eriugena (or Erigena) was born in Ireland in 810, and died probably about 877 in Paris. In 845 he was named head of the palace school. His figure dominates that whole epoch for he was a thinker with a well-defined philosophical doctrine. His mentality made him arrive - without theological caution - at the last logical consequences of his reasonings, some of which were condemned by the Church in 855 and 859. With John S.E a new Neo-Platonic current erupts with force from which the West will not be free till the metaphysical decadence of the 14th century. The genesis of his thought must be sought in the long work of translation of the "Corpus Areopagiticum" in which he wanted to offer the emperor Charles the Bald a more intelligible version of that work than Alcuin's translation. His main work is "De divisione naturae" which is full of very precise formulations in which he tries to transmit the philosophy of the Eastern Fathers, but he doesn't succeed in assimilating it adequately.

a) Reason and Faith

This is one of the main topics of the history of philosophy. In the case of John S.E. it becomes necessary to establish his concepts about faith and reason in order to grasp the meaning of his philosophy. He says that once God has been revealed, human reason finds itself illumined by faith. In this state, true wisdom consists in accepting the truth just as God has handed it down. On the other hand, human nature has an innate desire to know the truth. Therefore God wants faith to arouse in us a two-fold task, that of living it in our works and that of exploring it rationally in the contemplative life. What is first is faith. In it truth is given to us with absolute certainty and, following St. Augustine and PsD, he will sum up his thought with a phrase taken from the Bible: nisi credideritis, non intelligetis" (Unless you believe, you will not understand) (Ps.7, 9b).

Faith is a starting point, not a point of arrival, both for a holy life as well as for rational intelligence and for a knowledge which completes the faith. In order to achieve its aim, faith needs philosophical speculation which prolongs the effort of faith to reach its object. Though they are two distinct ways of knowing, they have the same content and in a certain sense are mixed together. In fact, one of the sources of Revelation is Scripture, but it is the task of reason, illumined by faith, to investigate the spiritual sense hidden in the words of Scripture, for an exclusively literal interpretation will lead to grave errors. The work of John S.E. is a philosophical exegesis of the Scriptures, in the style of Origen whose works he studied.

John S.E. lacks clarity and has too much enthusiasm. He affirms the primacy of faith and upholds the indisputable role of reason, but he is neither fideist nor rationalist. One must observe that he does not distinguish, within the revealed truths, between those which are and those which are not within the reach of reason. Thus he seems to rationalize dogma, and his philosophy is blurred within his theological and exegetical work.

b) Division of Nature

After pointing out that faith is the condition for rational knowledge, he indicates the method - Platonic in origin - for understanding what is believed: the dialectic (meaning something completely different from what Hegel, etc. mean by this term), in which one can distinguish two moments; namely, division, which begins with the unity of the supreme genera and comes down to the individuals, and analysis, which goes the opposite way. In reality it is a single movement of going and coming. These two operations are not arbitrary inventions of man, nor rules based on our way of knowing, but they make up the very law of beings, since what descends from the genera are the individuals themselves and not our reasonings. In other words, it is a law which is derived from reality and is in reality. The universe comprises all reality and at the same time makes up the concept of most universal content and thus receives the name "nature".

This notion, quite distinct from our notion of nature, includes all that is and all non-being. For John S.E. "being" and "non-being" are relative notions. Thus, "non-being" must not be understood as "nothing" but as anything which escapes the method of knowledge which dialectic teaches, such as privation, evil, the ineffable, the potential, the negative, etc. Limiting himself to the sphere of "nature" which includes being, he establishes the following division:

a) nature which creates and is not created (God the Creator);

b) nature which is created and creates (Ideas);

c) nature which is created and does not create (angels, man and other participated beings);

d) nature which does not create and is not created (God as End).

As can be observed, we have here really two distinctions, God and Ideas, and God and creatures, though the first distinction must later be clarified. Now "nature" is not a genus to which these four divisions belong, nor a whole divided into parts. His notion moves in the sphere of the Neo-Platonic concept of creation, which, like that of PsD, is equivalent to a real explanation of the production of the multiple starting with the One, which is difficult to reduce to the distinction between cause and effect.

c) Natural Theology

The nature which creates and is not created is God. From Him everything begins and to Him everything returns. God transcends all of our concepts, He is ineffable. Therefore, in order to arrive at Him, one must follow the method of PsD: affirmation, negation and supereminence. The last word always corresponds to negative theology, for to affirm, as example, that God is "hyper-essential" or "hyper-being" means that God is essence or being and at the same time that He is neither of the two, but rather a greater reality which is not specified. Hence, the way of supereminence expresses what God is not, though in an affirmative way.

d) The Ideas

The nature which is created and creates are the Ideas. John S.E. understands the Ideas as models of the creatures and their creative cause. But they in their turn are created by God the Father in the Word (the principal Idea) and thus they are co-eternal with the Word in time, but not in being, for they receive their existence from the Father. On the other hand, since they subsist eternally, they cannot introduce multiplicity into the Word for then they would go against the divine simplicity. Thus they are only distinguished in their effects (the creatures). The first consequence of this concept is that the Ideas are identical with the Word, which is absurd since they are models of the creatures, and hence they are creatures and finite, which impedes the identification.

Those who follow this doctrine stumble on this. This ambiguity is due to the concept of creation, which is conceived as a revelation of God - following PsD - and not as a production or causing of being out of nothing. To identify creation with revelation is to stress more the order of knowledge than the order of reality. The God of John S.E. would be like a Principle which, knowing that It is incomprehensible even for Itself, would unfold at one go Its power to reveal Itself, in which case the Ideas are the first "self-creation" of God. Since God is infinite and beyond being, to know Himself He must begin to be, and He can achieve this only by converting Himself into something else. Being is the first of His participations, but Being is not God. Thus, the divine nature is created and is known in the Ideas though they are finite.

It was said above that the two moments of the dialectic are complementary, and here we have an application of this. The Ideas can only appear by division of the first principle - it is God who gives them existence - and thus they are not God. In spite of this, the Ideas as expressions of God, are God Himself. In these conditions one understands that John S.E. can easily be accused of pantheism. Here, he introduces the topic of illumination. All the creatures are lights in which the divine light shines.

e) Man and the World

The nature which is created and does not create is grouped into three blocks, angels, men and material things. In the thought of man, the corporeal universe subsists, as the inferior in the superior. Here John S.E. explains how the material world could have been created by God in man. He beings by defining matter as a union of accidents unintelligible in itself, though the accidents separately are understood. He concludes that matter is composed of intelligibles. God created the material universe in the Idea of man which subsists eternally in God. When man sinned, he dragged the world down, but man and the world must return to God. Since man can't do that now, it must be done by divine love. This return is done in stages and is the effect of nature and of grace at the same time. Evil will disappear, matter will be re-absorbed in the human body, this in the soul, the soul in the Idea and the Idea in God. This doctrine goes against several articles of faith, above all with regard to the disappearance of matter.

### 3. The Struggle Between Dialectics and Anti-Dialectics in the 12th Century {#medieval-philosophy.4.3}
During the 11th century, the cultural panorama in the West was broadened, though the only profound thinker was St. Anselm. The Trivium was divulged more, while the Quadrivium maintained a timid life. The laity began to take interest in these studies. Some clerics took such an interest in dialectics and rhetoric that they relegated theology to second place and tried to subject dogma and Revelation to the rules of logic, thus making the instrument the centre of rational work and forgetting that all logic needs a metaphysics to sustain it.

One typical example is that of Berengarius of Tours (+1088), who considered logic as the best method for discovering the truth. He didn't hesitate to subject faith to reason and to translate dogmas in terms of reason. Along this path he came to deny the Transubstantiation and the Real Presence of Christ in the Holy Eucharist. His major work is one of the most complete summaries of Eucharistic heresies. Prescinding from the fact that he retracted his error before dying, one understands that there would be a strong reaction against those who maintained a philosophical attitude, for the faith was at stake.

A relevant representative of the anti-dialectic movement was St. Peter Damian (1007-1072). He defended theology against the abuse of logic and philosophy. He said that the only important thing is to be saved. Philosophy is not necessary for salvation, but is, rather, an invention of the devil. For him, philosophy must be "ancilla theologiae"

### 4. St. Anselm of Canterbury (1033-1109) {#medieval-philosophy.4.4}

After John S.E., he is the first great philosopher of the Middle Ages. The greatest and most original part of his literary production is the fruit of his years in the monastery in Bec. In philosophy his greatest works are "Monologium", on the existence and essence of God without the use of Scripture, "Proslogium", "De Veritate" and some replies to the monk Gaunilon. St. Anselm discovers St. Augustine and tries to follow his example. Once a question is brought up, one must finish it. His work presents many ideas which will be developed later. In this sense it can be affirmed that he prepares the way for the great syntheses of the 12th and 13th centuries.

a) Reason and Faith

St. Anselm maintains an equilibrium position between dialectics and anti-dialectics. He holds that man has two sources of knowledge, faith and reason, and that faith has absolute primacy. He refuses to subject Scripture to logic. Man must always begin with faith and with everything which is proposed by way of divine authority: dogma, Scripture and the testimony of the Fathers. On the other hand, he defends the role of reason for the understanding of the faith. If it is presumption to subject faith to reason, he says, it is negligence to disdain reason because one possesses faith. What is bad about wanting to understand what one believes? The Fathers did so, and especially St. Augustine. The truth, however, is too vast and profound for mortals to grasp it all at once. Finally, to understand the faith to is come close here below to the very vision of God.

Thus, St. Anselm stresses that he does not want to understand in order to believe, but rather to believe in order to understand. But he lets himself be led by optimism about the interpretative role of reason by not setting a limit to this "understanding of faith." According to him, one can always come to understand what one believes, or at least, to understand the very necessity of believing it. St. Anselm thinks that through pure dialectic, one can come, not to understand the divine mysteries, but to prove by "necessary reasons" that reason, well conducted, must necessarily affirm them. He tries this with the dogmas of the Trinity and the Incarnation and St. Thomas will show later that this is impossible.

b) Natural Theology or Theodicy

What is most important in St. Anselm are the demonstrations of the existence of God. In the "Monologium" he has three of them, which complement one another. His reasoning is A POSTERIORI. He states in the first proof that we verify by the senses and grasp by reason that there are many different goods. Since they all have causes, we must see whether there is one cause or several causes of these goods. It is evident that where a more or a less of perfection appears, this is due to the fact that all participate in the one same principle. Therefore, all beings which have the aspect of good participate in the Good in Itself, which is God.

The proof of being follows that of good. The diversity of beings demands ascertaining their causes, which are evidently reduced to one single First Efficient Cause, because it is impossible that various causes exist which produce one another reciprocally.

The third proof is that of the degrees or the hierarchy of beings. There is a natural ordering, but this scale cannot be infinite. There can only exist one nature which is higher in perfection that all others, for if there were at the apex of this hierarchy of beings several equal ones, they would either have the essence in common, and hence would be one and the same being or they would have something else in common. Hence a being exists which is higher and more perfect than all the others.

In his "Proslogium" St. Anselm formulates his famous ontological argument, which is his desire to produce a rational proof for the existence of God. He states it in this way: One can say that, at least in one's mind, there exists such a being greater than which none can be conceived. If we are convinced of this, which is easy for everyone to understand, it means that that being is or has an existence in the mind. Since it is a question of the greatest being which can be thought, it must also exist in reality, for it is greater to exist in reality than only in thought. Therefore, that being undoubtedly exists, both in reality and in thought, and we call it God.

This argument has attracted many, for a philosopher can feel attracted by the notion of absolute being in his thought. For this reason the ontological argument has come up many times in history, though in distinct forms, in philosophers as unlike as St. Bonaventure, Descartes, Leibniz and Hegel. But not everything was clear in it. Very soon, the monk Gaunilon objected that one cannot argue from the existence of something in thought in order to affirm the existence of that thing in reality, that is, outside of thought.

St. Anselm acknowledged that one cannot and must not make this step or jump, but in the case of the greatest being which can be conceived, it is not only possible but necessary to make an exception. If one thinks about the greatest being and this does not exist in reality, it is always possible to conceive another greater being which does exist. The reply to Gaunilon leaves us back at the beginning, but it allows us to observe one of the presuppositions of the reasoning of St. Anselm which is none other than the notion of God that we have from faith. Who is this God in Whom we believe? The answer is obvious, the greatest Being Which can be thought. In order to affirm or deny His existence, one must begin with an idea about God, which, being the greatest and richest in content, cannot not exist.

In his turn, St. Thomas Aquinas also rejects the ontological argument, precisely because it does not begin with something existing in reality. Moreover, before bringing up the possibility of the existence of the absolute Being, it is necessary to define what one understands by greatest and most perfect Being. Well then, "a priori" it is impossible to know the nature of God, because of the weakness of the human intellect. Consequently, not everyone understands God as "that being, greater than which nothing can be conceived." In reality, the knowledge of God begins "a posteriori", that is, by the consideration of the effects (creatures) and along this way we arrive at a Being whose essence is His very Being.

Finally, St. Thomas affirms that it is not licit to go from the ideal or logical order to the real order. From the existence of something in thought, it does not follow that it exists in reality. St. Anselm has been able to formulate this proof because God is the one who cannot not exist. He is Being by excellence or ESSENCE, which means "full reality" and to say that ESSENCE does not exist means that the one whose nature it is to exist does not exist. Everything that is not God and yet exists is not "full reality" and has received being from it. Thus there are two modes of existing, that of God, in which essence and existence are identified, and that of the other beings, whose essence does not imply existence and in order to exist need to have God confer being to them. God has created the world out of nothing. To admit a pre-existing matter would be pantheism. Before creation, everything existed in God as an idea, but these ideas are not creative, as opposed to what John S.E. thought. After creation, God conserves the creatures.

c) Knowledge

In the face of anti-dogmatic nominalism, St. Anselm, imprecise in his explanation of knowledge, nevertheless upholds the reality of genera and species (the universals), though not as subsistent and as separate realities, otherwise theological orthodoxy would be endangered. If we don't grasp how several men joined in a species form one man, neither will we grasp that one God is in three persons. For St. Anselm, the ideas are beings, a metaphysical stance which reminds us of the "exaggerated realism" of Plato.
