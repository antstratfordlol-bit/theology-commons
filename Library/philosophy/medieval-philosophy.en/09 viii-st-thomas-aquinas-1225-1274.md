## VIII. St. Thomas Aquinas (1225-1274) {#medieval-philosophy.8}
### 1. Introduction {#medieval-philosophy.8.1}
St. Thomas comes from a Lombard family which settled in the castle of Roccasecca, near Aquino in the province of Naples. He was educated in the monastery of Montecassino where the abbot was an uncle of his. When Frederick II expelled the monks in order to make a fortress of the monastery, St. Thomas continued his studies at the U. of Naples. There he met Peter of Ireland who introduced him to the philosophy of Aristotle. About 1244 he became a Dominican, against the wishes of his widowed mother and he was imprisoned at Roccasecca by his brothers. He made use of his imprisonment in order to reread the Holy Scriptures and to study the "Book of Sentences" of Peter Lombard and a work of Aristotle. At the end of 1245 he fled from his prison and after a brief stay in Naples, he went to Paris where he was taught by St. Albert the Great. Both of them were together for 4 years in Cologne, where St. Thomas studied the works of St. Albert as well as the "corpus dionysianum", the "corpus aristotelicum" and the Arab commentaries.

After being ordained a priest, he returned to Paris in 1252 as professor and wrote his commentary on the "Book of Sentences". At that time opposition arose against the Franciscans and the Dominicans being named as masters at the U. of Paris. The question was resolved by Pope Alexander IV granting them permission to do so. During this time in Paris, which lasted till 1259, St. Thomas wrote, among other works, "De ente et essentia", "De principiis naturae", "De veritate" and some Scripture commentaries.

He was called to Rome in 1259 to be Master of the pontifical Curia and he remained there till 1268. He taught in Rome, Viterbo and Orvieto and he organized the Studium Generale of the Dominicans in Santa Sabina in Rome. At the pontifical court he met William of Moerbeke who supplied him with first-hand translations of the works of Aristotle which he used for his commentaries. During this period he wrote "Summa contra Gentiles" and the first chapters of "Summa Theologiae" as well as a commentary on PsD's "De divinis nominibus" and some other works.

In 1269 he returned to Paris to defend the rights of the mendicant orders to teach at the University. He got involved in the defence of Christian doctrine against the Averroism which had engulfed the Faculty of Arts and wrote several works, among them "De unitate intellectus", for this purpose. His own doctrine on the unicity of the substantial form was condemned by the Bishop of Paris in 1270 and St. Albert spoke up to defend his disciple.

In 1272, St. Thomas was destined to Naples where he had the chair of theology. There he continued the "Summa Theologiae" and wrote several other works, such as "De substantiis separatis". He was invited by Pope Clement X to take part in the Second Council of Lyons, but he died on the journey to Lyons in Fossanova on 7th March, 1274. Pope John XXII canonized him in Avignon in 1323.

### 2. Principal Works of St. Thomas {#medieval-philosophy.8.2}

The works of St. Thomas can be put into two groups: first, the works destined for teaching, bound to the method of university education of his time; second, those written for apostolic needs and assignments and for preaching. To the first group belong the commentaries on official text books. We refer to the Holy Scriptures (Job, Psalms, Jeremias, Isaias, Lamentations, Gospels of St. Matthew and of St. John, Pauline Epistles); the "Book of Sentences" of Peter Lombard (which was used as a theology text till the 16th century); PsD's "De divinis nominibus"; "De Trinitate" and "De hebdomadibus" of Boethius; "Liber de causis"; etc. and the commentaries on 13 works of Aristotle.

With regard to these latter, an interesting novelty is observed. He keeps faithfully to the literal exposition of the text and points out errors committed by Averroes' commentaries. Once the Aristotelian doctrine is reached, St. Thomas strives to attribute to it the greatest possible degree of truth, but without doing violence to the text. On other occasions, St. Thomas finds a deeper meaning in the same line as that of the author he is commenting on, whether Aristotle or Boethius or another, and he indicates it even though that doctrine assumes a personal interpretation of St. Thomas beyond what that author has explicitly said.

On the other hand, we observe in St. Thomas a great sense of history which leads him to ask for better translations of the classical works of philosophy in order to compare them with one another with the aim of ascertaining the meaning intended by the author in his explanations. For example, he discovers that the "Liber de causis" isn't Aristotle's work but is inspired by the Neo- Platonist Proclus. St. Thomas makes a great effort to know all the previous thought as widely as possible, from the great philosophers of antiquity to the Fathers. In his "Catena aurea" he uses some writings of the Fathers which had been forgotten and he is the first one who uses council documents in theology. Also to be included in the first group are the various "Quaestiones disputatae" and "Quodlibetales", such as "De Veritate", "De potencia", "De malo", "De anima" etc.

In the second group we find a good number of treatises and philosophical and theological opuscules which he was asked to write. Here we find material for preaching, like the commentaries on the "Our Father", the "Hail Mary", the Creed, sermons, prayers, etc. The most important treatises in this group are the "Summa contra Gentiles", requested by the General of his order, St. Raymond of Penafort; and the "Summa Theologiae", the fruit of his concern to facilitate the learning of theology, which was found too widely dispersed in the "Book of Sentences". In summary, the writings of St. Thomas contain mature thought, expounded with clarity and order, without rigidity or improvisations. It is no surprise that the treatises present a very careful editorial work. It is also good to keep in mind that St. Thomas is above all a theologian. Therefore, what is most original in his philosophy is to be found in his systematic works, the "Summae" and "Quaestiones", rather than in his commentaries on Aristotle, and hence in a theological context.

### 3. Sources of the Philosophy of St. Thomas {#medieval-philosophy.8.3}

St. Thomas respected the previous thought worthy of credit and didn't want to build a brand new philosophical doctrine. Thus we seek the origin of his work of synthesis in traditional sources. A review of his work lets us appreciate his abundant use and deep knowledge of Latin, Greek, Arab and Jewish authors. St. Thomas came into contact with Aristotle in Naples and assimilated his doctrine through the teaching of St. Albert and the translations of William of Moerbeke.

St. Thomas did not choose between Aristotle and the historical Plato but rather made a synthesis of the work of Aristotle and Neo-Platonism. He only knew Plato indirectly and he died before he was able to interpret his doctrine. St. Thomas took the main theses of Aristotle's metaphysics, such as act and potency, the primacy of the singular being, the distinction of substance and accidents and a number of concrete points. But Aristotle has as his central reality the singular substance, and within it the form, while for St. Thomas the "actus essendi", not the form, is the most profound act of things. Here he refers to Plato, accepting his doctrine of participation, for the topic of being. The singular things are beings by participation, and thus depend on a Cause which is Being by essence. He corrects in this way Plato, who had put the separate Forms (Ideas) as principles of things. Aristotle was right in saying that forms are intrinsic to things and can't be substantialized, but he didn't arrive at a radical First Cause of the world. For St. Thomas, that Cause is "Esse Subsistens", which we call God.

St. Thomas also accepts the indisputable authority of St. Augustine, especially in theology, but he is more cautious in philosophical topics. He accepts the doctrine of causal transcendence and divine exemplarism. When he disagrees with him, he shows that he is combating Plotinus or the Arab interpreters instead of St. Augustine. Along with St. Augustine, he is influenced by PsD's "De divinis nominibus" from which he takes a good part of the doctrines on transcendentals, the intensive notion of being, participation, the knowledge of God (affirmative and negative theology), the problem of evil and the doctrine on the cosmological order of the world.

Three other sources of St. Thomas' philosophy - Boëthius, "Liber de causis", and Avicenna - join in suggesting to him the main doctrine of his philosophy, namely the real distinction between essence and "esse" in creatures. From Boëthius he takes also the explanation that the correspondence between the logical and the real orders in the doctrine on universals is a participation. The "Liber de causis" inspired him in elaborating his doctrine that "esse" is the fundamental act of being, and also in going deeper in the doctrine on participation. Finally, the Neo-Platonist Avicenna accompanies all the intellectual work of St. Thomas, though his influence is less in the works of maturity. By correcting Avicenna, St. Thomas gives us the best and most original of his doctrine; for example, the solution of the problem of universals, the distinction in creatures between essence and the act of being, and the distinction between the causes of "fieri" and of "esse", the foundation of his metaphysics of causality. To sum up, to an Aristotelianism conforming with faith as a basis, St. Thomas adds Neo-Platonic elements, having "esse" as the nucleus.

### 4. Some Important Doctrines of St. Thomas {#medieval-philosophy.8.4}

St. Thomas elaborates a philosophy according to theological needs, based on the observation of real things. Thus he doesn't construct a system in the modern sense by deduction from "a priori" principles. His doctrine arises in harmony with the supernatural truths of Catholic dogma, from which he strives for better comprehension, without adulterating it or replacing it by his own axioms. At the same time, he has in mind what we now call particular sciences, but his perennial doctrine is independent of the state of the sciences of his time. Sometimes in his works we find examples taken from the physics or astronomy of his time, but these points do not affect his philosophy or theology. St. Thomas doesn't defend them nor is he interested in them. He simply receives opinions which he uses in order to give examples, to contribute some argument of fittingness.

At other times he does make metaphysical judgements about them in order to warn about doctrinal errors which could follow from them (like denying human freedom or lessening the causality of God). On the other hand, the work of St. Thomas has a structure, an order and a spirit that is well defined which explains the prestige and authority he has enjoyed for centuries. Technically speaking, the Thomistic metaphysical "corpus" has emerged unscathed in discussions, which often are charged with unscientific prejudices, with idealist or materialist immantentism or with positivism. Without giving here a detailed exposition of his doctrine, which is studied throughout the Biennium, we limit ourselves here to the indication of the most fundamental and original aspects.

#### a) REASON AND FAITH.
The medieval authors had always recognized the supremacy of faith over reason for the elaboration of their philosophico-theological doctrines. However, this simple relation of order left a broad field of confusion in the manner of dealing with the distinct materials. St. Albert the Great clearly separated the two realms and St. Thomas follows this distinction, making some points more precise in a very interesting manner. First of all, theology is a science and not only some articles of faith which one must believe. His starting point is dogma, whose truths, certified by the authority of God who reveals, supply absolute certainty to reason.

Theological activity consists in obtaining argumentative conclusions, starting with dogma. Theology as a science is subordinate to the knowledge of God and of the blessed, in which we participate by faith. God is the source of all truth, both natural and supernatural. Thus there can exist no conflict between theology and the truth acquired by unaided reason, which is the content of philosophy. Nor is there any obstacle in theology suggesting some points to philosophy so that it can reach them by its own natural way. Theology becomes a positive and a negative norm of philosophy, positive because it moves to investigate, negative because it clearly points out errors. Based on principles taken from experience, philosophy is a useful instrument, a necessary aid in more perfectly contemplating God, who is the last end both of philosophy and of theology. St. Thomas says that philosophy will be recompensed for its service since theology gives many philosophical concepts a higher evidence and sense, though philosophy can also work autonomously.

#### b) THE NATURAL AND THE SUPERNATURAL ORDERS.
Just as philosophy and theology are distinct sciences united in God, the one source of both, and in the believer without any confusion, there exists analogously a supernatural order, or of grace, and a natural order, both instituted by God, which are ruled by distinct principles and yet merge in man without confusion. The clarity with which St. Thomas expresses this distinction allows him to draw consequences of maximum importance. On the one hand, it implies the distinction between reason and faith. Faith refers to the One and Triune God; the reality of the world allows reason to undertake the task of its comprehension and to be raised to demonstrate the existence of God, and if reason works correctly, it comes to demonstrate the preambles of faith, that is, to know the most important natural truths which dispose it easily to receive and maintain the faith: the existence of God and His nature as One and Perfect; divine creation of the world and the condition as creatures that the things have; the spirituality and immortality of the soul; God as last end of life; the norms of the natural law.

On the other hand, we find divine grace, understood as a gift inhering in the soul by which the whole man is made a participant in divine nature. Augustinian tradition didn't come to be aware of this and remained in the consideration of grace as an extrinsic gift to man. Precisely this Augustinian conception, accepted by nominalism, will be converted with Luther into "extrinsic justification" by faith alone. For St. Thomas, habitual grace interiorly heals the whole man, because it inheres in the soul in a way analogous to how the accident is found in the substance, and since the soul is essentially the substantial co-principle of man, the sanctifying grace accompanying it is the principle or formal cause of justification. In the same way, the infused virtues are supernatural habits which are in the subject, and the sacraments are efficacious instrumental causes of grace, not just preparations or disposing causes for receiving it. Evidently, underlying this argument is St. Thomas' metaphysics.

#### c) FROM PHILOSOPHY OF NATURE TO METAPHYSICS.
i) The World of Nature. Aristotle makes St. Thomas understand the importance of the immediate or spontaneous knowledge of the natural world - an idea neglected by the Augustinians and Neo-Platonists - according to which it is evident that there exists in the world a multitude of corporeal beings. A first reflection about them allows us to be aware that in one and the same thing changes occur without the thing itself ceasing to be. In this way there arises the distinction between substance and accidents and of accidents from one another. If we pursue this observation, we notice that some substances are converted into others. In this change there is something which remains while one substance disappears and another appears. We call this substrate PRIME MATTER and the element determining the substance FORM.

St. Thomas accepts hylomorphic composition of material substances, understanding prime matter as pure potency, and substantial form as the principle giving a body its specific essence. They are two mutually implied principles. St. Thomas accepts Aristotle’s philosophy of nature, correcting some Platonist deviations. In particular, he had to fight against the theory of the plurality of substantial forms which the Neo-Platonists maintained because of their exaggerated realism. ii) The Metaphysical Structure of Beings. St. Thomas doesn't limit himself to describe the world of bodies, composed of form and matter. The most central aspect of his philosophy is to analyze things as creatures not only by the fact that they are created by God (transcendental causality) but because their created character is reflected in their intrinsic constitution itself. For St. Thomas, all things are radically ENTIA, subjects which participate in ESSE. The things are composed of act and potency at diverse levels, as all theologians of his time admitted. But the most profound act and the first that there is in each being (ENS) and the root of its perfections (which are such because they are) is the act of being (ESSE), which for St. Thomas is act in the strong sense and not the simple fact of existing. Thus all created things have a real composition of ESSE and essence, which are two constitutive co-principles of any real object. The resultant whole is called ENS, that which has being.

Being (ESSE) is act and essence, together with the accidents, is a receptive potency, a subject which possesses or participates in ESSE. The binomial esse-essence is the key to St. Thomas' metaphysics, that which characterizes all the things of the universe.

Philosophers before St. Thomas had not perceived this clearly and so many only considered that the most central aspect of things is their essence and relegated ESSE to accidental category. This allowed St. Thomas to surpass universal hylomorphism which some Augustinians posited in order to safeguard the difference between God and creatures. For St. Thomas, angels have no matter at all, they are pure forms but without doubt are finite beings, for these forms participate in esse, they enter into composition with it. Thus the angel doesn't have God's simplicity, that of pure act of being without limit.

Another important aspect of St. Thomas' metaphysics is the relevance that it gives to the concrete being (SUPPOSITUM), or subject or subsistent. The only thing that really exists is that which has being (esse), the individual being (ens). Its essence or nature, its accidents, its relations, etc. are parts of the ENS, they must not be considered as something subsistent, they must not be made into things. Everything comes together in the concrete beings or they vanish, for outside of concrete things there is nothing real. Thus the philosophy of St. Thomas can be considered also as a metaphysics of the concrete, endowed with a maximum value, for it is a frequent defect of many philosophies to deviate to the plane of ideal abstractions.

iii) The Ascent to God, Esse by Essence. St. Thomas begins examining things of the universe by reason and finds that they are finite, that they have being and are not unlimited being. He notices that they participate in esse, in a partial way, and from this he infers rationally the existence of a First Creative Cause, something which up to then no philosopher had done clearly. He argues that if the things have esse by participation, they can't be the causes of their own esse. Thus, this participation in esse requires that these things be caused, and caused in their very being, that is, produced out of nothing. Only a Being which is Esse Itself can do this. Thus, for St. Thomas God's most proper name is He Who Is. His 5 ways are diverse modes of ascending to God beginning with creatures. In them he considers aspects of the world which show its ontological insufficiency in order to arrive to God.

d) MAN.

The philosophical anthropology of St. Thomas is based on the consideration of the structure of man as a composite of body and soul which together make up the unity of a single person. The human soul is the form of the body but at the same time it is a spiritual substance, capable of subsisting by itself in being, as something independent of the body. The spiritual condition of the human soul, however, is not opposed to the unity of the complete man, but rather guarantees it even more, for the soul informs the whole body and communicates to it being and substantiality. The spirituality of the human soul is known because of the complete immateriality of its operations of intellect and will. The spirituality consists precisely in the fact that the soul is not limited to being simply a form or act of the corporeal matter, but goes beyond this function and is a true substance, i.e., a form which has "esse" in itself, as opposed to the forms of the other natural bodies which have "esse" only in union with matter. Thus the human soul does not come from matter nor is it educed from a body but it is created by God immediately, who infuses it into the body generated by the parents.

In this way, St. Thomas overcomes the difficulties of pure Aristotelianism which didn't see how the form of a body could be immortal; and he also surpasses Platonism which, on insisting on the spirituality of human souls, didn't explain the substantial union with the body. In the metaphysical psychology of St. Thomas there are details and profound analyses of the potencies of the human soul and its activity, the passions, and very much stressed is the mark of person which all individuals of rational nature have. This is a logical consequence of the insistence of St. Thomas on the "suppositum". In his study of human operations St. Thomas gives particular importance to the primacy of freedom, in the face of the excessive intellectualism of Neo- Platonist doctrines and of Aristotle himself. Arab philosophy also subjected man to the necessary influence of the stars and of separate intellects. For St. Thomas, human conduct is free, thanks to the will, a potency whereby we act with self-determination in the order to an end. The will is the power by which man moves himself, by putting into act his potencies, in intimate union with the intellect but with dominion even over it in the order of exercise.

Furthermore, St. Thomas situates human freedom principally before God as the last end. Free choice is the capacity which man has in this life to choose his own last end and the means leading to it, a capacity which is actualized in the moment of choice. But since our freedom is imperfect, there is the possibility of choosing evil, the will being able to refuse to love God above all things in order to prefer the created good as last end, a choice which constitutes the very essence of sin.

#### e) KNOWLEDGE.
Intellect and Senses. The immediate principle of human knowledge according to St. Thomas is the faculty of the soul that we call intellect, and not divine illumination, as the Augustinians said, nor a higher and impersonal Intellect as the Averroists thought. If one wants to be more precise one must say with St. Thomas that the intellect is a participation of the divine light in us and it must be understood as a power or capacity of knowing in its purest form. At the start, the intellect is empty, "tabula rasa", without content and without innate ideas. With time and by induction from experience, the intellect acquires the first principles of being and of operation. The whole person and all his powers intervene in the operation of knowing. The most notable characteristic of Thomist gnoseology is the union that it maintains between intelligible operation and sense activity, a point which the Neo-Platonists neglected.

This is in conformity with the doctrine of the substantial union of the soul with the body. Human understanding of things is produced by means of the senses, according to a double movement. First of all, the intellect obtains, through sense perception, abstract and universal knowledge. Secondly, the intellect refers the universals to the concrete things, returning to the experience from which it had begun, thus reaching its proper object, the "quidditas" or the nature existing in matter. Consequently, an intellectual knowledge of singular things is produced, something that the nominalists of later centuries will not understand, thanks to the continuation of the intellect in sensitive knowledge. This continuation becomes effective especially through the function of the cogitative or particular reason, which is a sensitive cognoscitive power, though it is rational by participation. St. Thomas lays special emphasis on this faculty, which serves as a bridge uniting abstract thought with the concrete experiences of life.

Starting with this basis, St. Thomas explains that human intellectual knowledge can have three fundamental objects: the external things, the soul itself, and God. The first and most adequate knowledge of the soul in union with the body is the knowledge of sensible things external to ourselves. This takes place according to the process which we have indicated above. Selfknowledge is that which man has of the interior acts of his spirit. For St. Thomas, the intellect has the property of reflecting on its own acts. Self-knowledge is produced after the intellect has entered into contact with the external world, and not before. Through it the soul explores its own inner state, its acts and its nature. It is knowledge of great importance, for through it man is aware of the morality of his acts. This is moral conscience.

For St. Thomas, the natural knowledge of God follows from the contemplation of natural things and has the mark of analogy. He makes ample use of analogy in all his metaphysics and affirms that it is a property of some concepts which are applied to diverse realities in diverse ways, as far distant from equivocity as from univocity. For example, "ens" is not said in the same way of God as of the creatures, the accidents, etc. According to St. Thomas, effects are similar to their causes, because a cause can only transmit its own perfections. Thus it is natural for the world to be a likeness of God, even though there is a disproportion between the Exemplary Cause and the creatures. Hence we can form a certain idea of God, an analogous concept of His Perfection, based on the perfections we observe in things. Here St. Thomas takes especially the doctrine of PsD. The world has limited and graduated perfections. We must purify them of all imperfections, then affirm them in God and deny that God has these perfections as deficiently as we conceive them and as they are found in creatures. Finally we must attribute them to God in a supereminent way. Thus we say that God is Subsistent Being.

#### f) ETHICS.
The moral teaching of St. Thomas comes as a consequence of his metaphysics. If God is the supreme Principle of the universe, which in its turn is a participated reflection of His perfections, then logically the universe has no other sense than that of giving glory to God. The last and transcendental end of the world is to be like God. But the free spiritual creatures are ordered to this same last end in a higher way. God has created them to know Him and to love Him forever. St. Thomas brings up in this point the ethical elements of Aristotle, by elaborating a moral doctrine which expresses the natural order of things, that is, that is not purely supernatural. (In his theological works St. Thomas studies the supernatural order). Aristotle had said only that man tends to happiness, which is ordered to the good in general, without being more precise. St. Thomas affirms that if the good follows from the being of the things, logically the ESSE SUBSISTENS is the Highest Good and therefore the Last End to which all creatures aspire by nature, even though they might not know it. Hence sin is the greatest transgression against the very structure of the nature of things, and thus it is natural that the consequence of sin is the loss of happiness for man.

The ordination to God assumes, however, that man must live an order also in his relations with things, with his fellow men and with himself. Thus St. Thomas shows that the various commandments of the Decalogue, which impose various virtues, are consequences of the fundamental precept of the love for God above all things. The natural moral law is the ordination of human acts to their due end. It is like the measure or rule to which human conduct must submit itself in order to arrive at the end. For St. Thomas the moral law exists above all in God as Supreme Legislator (Eternal Law), but it also exists in rational creatures in a participated way (natural law). In fact, the moral law is impressed in human hearts and man can know it by the light of his reason, if it is upright and has not been weakened. It is not a question of divine illumination nor of the possession of innate ideas (Augustinianism), but rather it is known starting with the experience that man has of his acts.

Finally, let us point out that the natural ethics of St. Thomas also has as a fundamental tenet that the temporal life of man is on the way to eternal life, to the moment in which man reaches God as last end or loses Him definitively. Natural reason makes us aware that God is a Rewarder who will reward the just and punish those who do evil and that this will happen permanently at the time that the soul is separated from the body. Naturally, about these points we have many more lights thanks to Revelation. In the works of St. Thomas the philosophical considerations are intimately united with the strictly theological explanation which is realized beginning with faith.

### 5. Doctrinal Authority of Thomist Philosophy Recognized by the Magisterium of the Church {#medieval-philosophy.8.5}

The penetration of the doctrine of St. Thomas in the intellectual life of Europe begins with his canonization by John XXII in 1323 which removes any suspicion about undue or dangerous assimilation of Aristotelian philosophy for the faith. Since that time and for the next seven centuries the Church, in the exercise of her Magisterium, has not ceased to indicate that the theological and philosophical doctrine of St. Thomas is the most certain and most in conformity with Revelation. In modern times, the Church has insistently recommended that St. Thomas be followed as a means to overcome the immanentist basis of many modern philosophers who subvert and nullify the contents of the Catholic faith.

The encyclical "Aeterni Patris" of Leo XIII, dated 4th August 1879, can be considered as a return to Thomist sources in the face of the doctrinal confusion which was infiltrating among Catholics. It was seconded by the creation, in 1879, of the Academy of St. Thomas and by the preparation of a critical edition of all his works. In 1880 Leo XIII proclaimed St. Thomas patron of Catholic schools. His successor, St. Pius X, insisted repeatedly on the need of a direct study of the works of St. Thomas, indicating the importance of following him especially in questions of metaphysics. A set of the 24 most important theses of St. Thomas was published in the first days of the pontificate of Benedict XV. Pius XI and Pius XII also insist on the importance of following the method and spirit of St. Thomas in the teaching of philosophy and theology in all ecclesiastical centres. The same idea is contained in the Decree "Optatam Totius" of Vatican II. Paul VI and John Paul II have also taught along the same line.

### 6. Reactions to Thomism. First Followers of his Doctrine {#medieval-philosophy.8.6}

If we examine attentively the doctrine of St. Thomas, we notice immediately that it affects the whole field of theology and philosophy in a way which was completely new for his time. The two "scandalous" theses of St. Thomas are the unicity of the substantial form, and the real distinction between "esse" and essence, which some were not able to understand. Thus, it could appear that St. Thomas made common cause with the Averroist Aristotelianism of the Faculty of Arts in Paris, and he was considered as having been included in the condemnations of 1270 and 1277. However, his doctrine was not in the field in which it was thought to be combated, and the polemic redounded to his benefit. Perhaps at the insistence of St. Albert the Great, the General Chapters of the Dominican Order, from 1278, insistently invited its members to study the doctrine and the writings of St. Thomas, and so very soon it found welcome among the Dominicans as well as among the secular clergy and some Franciscans who abandoned the Augustinian tradition.

More than a century and a half would pass before disciples appear who faithfully follow St. Thomas in the greater part of his doctrine, but by then what we call "Thomism" had already crystallized, that is, that philosophical tradition which defends theses of St. Thomas, the fruit of a polemic with Franciscanism which lasted more than 50 years. Thus, from the first moment, it would suffice to defend one single thesis of St. Thomas in order to be considered "Thomist". This is the case of Giles (Aegidius) of Rome (1247-1313), who for three years was a student of St. Thomas in Paris and later became General of the Order of Augustinian Hermits. He left behind a doctrine which is quite different from that of St. Thomas except regarding the real distinction between essence and "esse" which he vigorously defended against Henry of Ghent. At any rate, in two of his works he gives a very personal interpretation of this real distinction. With new terminology, he speaks of "esse essentiae" and "esse existentiae", that is, he conceives essence and existence as two different things.

Moreover, he already begins to translate the "esse" of St. Thomas as "existentia", thus losing its character of act. This interpretation differs completely from the doctrine of St. Thomas, for whom "esse" is really distinct from essence of which it is act, just as it is distinct from the act which is form with respect to matter. Moreover, the act of "esse" and the essence are entitative co-principles which can't exist separately although they are really distinct from one another. Giles of Rome understood the "esse"-act of St. Thomas as "esse"-form, due to his reading of Proclus and Boëthius.

Consequently he made the principles of "ens" into things, an error which many beginners in the study of St. Thomas are prone to. Thus, it isn't enough that an author profess that he follows St. Thomas, in order truly to be a Thomist.

### 7. Henry of Ghent (+1293) {#medieval-philosophy.8.7}
In the midst of the growing prestige of the professors of the religious and the mendicant orders in the Universities of Paris and of Oxford, the figure of Henry of Ghent stands out. He was a secular priest who enjoyed great authority during the last quarter of the 13th century. He was Master of Theology in Paris from 1276 to 1292 and advisor of Stephen Tempier in the condemnations of 1277. He was a protagonist in the controversy on the real distinction between essence and "esse" against the "Thomists" up to the point of giving the impression that this was the only important problem. Henry of Ghent is usually considered as an independent philosopher, rather eclectic but with a certain originality. Above all, it is indispensable to know him in order to understand the way in which Duns Scotus, on whom he exercised great influence, will posit problems. Although at times Henry accepts Aristotelian doctrines, such as the unicity of the substantial form, in later works he corrects himself in an Augustinian sense, perhaps because he thinks that Thomism is a dangerous invasion of pagan philosophy. This rejection will bring him close to Avicenna and St. Augustine.

He begins his discussions by asking that if essence and existence are distinct, what is existence or "esse"? Since it seems, according to common opinion, that everyone agrees that "esse" is neither substance nor accident, then it appears that it is not really distinct from essence. On the other hand, the concepts of essence and existence do not have the same content, for it seems that the second adds something to essence. Thus he says that the distinction between essence and existence is purely intentional in creatures, because the essence relates the creature with God as exemplary cause, and existence relates the creature with God as efficient cause. That is to say, that essence and "esse" only indicate a distinct relations of dependence of the creature with God according to our way of understanding, but they are not really distinct in the "ens".

In order to understand the metaphysics of Henry of Ghent, we must place ourselves in the scheme of thought of Avicenna. For Avicenna, metaphysics is the science of "ens" as "ens", but considering that "ens" encompasses the division between necessary being and contingent or possible beings. Henry of Ghent begins with this notion of "ens" and in order not to fall into the Neo-Platonic necessarianism of Avicenna, instead of dividing "ens" into necessary and possible, he distinguishes it analogously. There is "something which is being itself", which is God; and there is "something to which it is fitting or can be naturally fitting to be", or all the other beings. Consequently, the notion of "ens" is predicated in an analogous way of God and of creatures, but it cannot signify the two at the same time, but rather one or the other. The idea of "ens" is evidently applied first of all to God, "ens per se", in Whom essence is identified with existence.

Since God is the first being that is, He will be the first in man's knowledge ("primum cognitum") and the reason of knowledge ("ratio cognoscendi") of all the other beings. This notion of "ens" then suffices in order to demonstrate the existence of God. Therefore, the "a posteriori" proofs which begin with the sense world are unnecessary, though valid.

Returning to creatures, it is clear that their actual existence depends on the divine Will, and from this point of view there can be a distinction from the essence, but from the moment in which creatures exist, that real distinction has no meaning.

Finally, in order to explain creation Henry has recourse to the double dependence of the creature on the Creator mentioned above. The essence of every (possible) thing has its corresponding exemplary or divine idea. These ideas are in God, but they represent the creatures as distinct from God, in such a way that the divine ideas contain the "esse essentiae" or the "ideatum" of all things. God knows His essence and, in it, all possible creatures, but He knows them as already distinct from His own essence and therefore with their own proper being. On the other hand, the divine Will freely grants, when He wants, existence to those possible beings. Creation consists precisely in this. In the face of the problem of how God knows the creatures that He has already created, since He knows all the possible beings in His own essence, Henry responds that the divine Intellect knows their existence in the very act of creating.
