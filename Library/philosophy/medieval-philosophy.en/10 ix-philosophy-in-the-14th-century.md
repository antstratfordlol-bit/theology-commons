## IX. Philosophy in the 14th Century {#medieval-philosophy.9}
### 1. John Duns Scotus (1266-1308) {#medieval-philosophy.9.1}
The last great Scholastic philosopher is John Duns Scotus, native of Scotland. He became a Franciscan at the age of 15 and was ordained priest 10 years later. From 1297 he taught in Oxford, moving to Paris in 1301. Shortly before he died, he was destined to Cologne. He published nothing in his life, but left behind a large collection of manuscripts, among them two commentaries on the "Book of Sentences" as well as several other works.

a) Philosophy and Theology

For Duns Scotus, the field proper to philosophy is limited with respect to theology. The latter has as its object God as God, whereas metaphysics, as the highest part of philosophy, is concerned with being as being. For this reason, metaphysics cannot reach God in Himself, just as theology teaches.

In fact, history shows that reason has never been able to reach the Trinitarian dogma, nor the concept of creation, nor the definition "God is Love" which Holy Scripture gives us. If from above metaphysics is limited by theology, from below it is limited by the nature of our knowledge, which is precarious since it begins with the sensitive (as Aristotle says) and consequently can never give a concept of immaterial beings, such as angels or God. Metaphysics will be possible if it succeeds in starting from an idea of "ens" which is so abstract and indeterminate that it can be applied indiscriminately to everything in the same sense. Duns Scotus finds this notion on considering "ens" as opposed to "nothing".

The very first thing that appears in this opposition is that "ens" is "ens", and this fact is common to infinite and to finite being, to material and to immaterial. Thus the notion of "ens univocum" arises, which corresponds to "ens" before any specification, even that of existence. The "ens univocum" is neither a physical reality, nor a universal concept, but rather the very "nature" of "ens" as "ens", because of which it can be predicated of everything. Consequently, the "ens univocum" of Duns Scotus looks like the logical universal and will be the form of all possible forms which is emptiest of content. Of course, from such a notion it is impossible to obtain any real knowledge.

The first intrinsic property of "ens" as "ens" is that which permits considering its modifications, that is, establishing the division between finite and infinite. This division comes before the division of being according to the 10 Aristotelian categories. Thus, reason is circumscribed to the sphere of the demonstrable. What cannot be demonstrated becomes object of theology. Duns Scotus expresses this opinion very clearly: Nothing that is demonstrable is revealed by God, and nothing that is revealed is demonstrable unless it comes from Revelation. And so, the way is opened that leads in the 16th century to the complete separation of metaphysics from theology. Duns Scotus works with the same philosophical terms as St. Thomas, but he understands them almost always in a different way. Like St. Thomas, he knows that the intellect elaborates concepts from experience, but on beginning to reason, he will make a turn that is not small. For St. Thomas, based always on sense evidence, a universal concept expresses the very essence of things, because it is bound indirectly but immediately to experience. On the contrary, Duns Scotus, without denying this, in order to reason fixes his attention on concepts already formed and on necessary relations among them, omitting further sense evidence. For example, in the demonstration of the existence of God, St. Thomas always starts with sense observation, and Duns Scotus with the contents of the concept. In summary, the work of Duns Scotus becomes a critique, in the name of theology, of the possibilities of metaphysics, for he refers to theology for important points of metaphysics. Now this theology isn't speculative, but rather a practical science which regulates our actions, even the exercise of thought ordered to happiness.

b) The Knowledge of God

Another central point in the philosophy of Duns Scotus is the almost exclusive value he gives to "a priori" demonstrations. St. Thomas recognized the fundamental demonstrative value of "a posteriori" arguments which go from effect to cause and made good use of them in his 5 ways. On the contrary, Duns Scotus puts all his attention in "a priori" proofs, because if they exist, the others follow necessarily. Thus he maintains that demonstrations from effect to cause, though valid, don't even deserve that name and have only a relative value. This turn, only apparently small, will substantially modify the proof of the existence of God, the doctrine on divine attributes and the soul, etc.

In order to demonstrate the existence of God, one can't start with sensible beings, because along this way one only arrives (with Aristotle) at the first mover or first cause, without transcending the physical world of nature. Nor should one, says Duns, recur to theology for the definition of God, as St. Anselm did. Thus, the only starting point is the notion of univocal being. We can use it because this notion suggests immediately the need of establishing something first in the order of being, in relation to which everything else can be explained. Since it's not a question of postulating God's existence in the name of intellectual necessity, it is above all necessary to prove the possibility of the existence of a first cause and then demonstrate its existence.

The first cause is possible because there is no other cause which can impede its existence and hence it is possible by itself. Therefore it cannot not exist. But the possibility of any other cause doesn't necessarily imply its existence since it can be impeded by a higher cause. So whatever excludes any cause with respect to its being cannot not exist. Hence, since the first being is possible, it necessarily exists. For identical motives, the first being is infinite, for nothing limits it and thus it enjoys all perfections in highest degree since it is first in all of them. God is one, infinite, wise, good, etc. But for Duns Scotus another series of divine attributes can't be proven "a priori" nor "a posteriori": Omnipotence, Providence, Truth, etc. which are known only by faith, and the same thing occurs with the immortality of the soul.

c) God and Creatures

Duns Scotus realized he had just demonstrated the existence of the necessary Being in a way analogous to that of Avicenna and thus could fall with him into the error of explaining creation as an obligatory emanation of the necessary Being in the mode of Greek necessitarianism. Moreover, his notion of univocal being erased the boundaries between necessary being and contingent beings. In these conditions he had to take a radical decision: to speak of the dominion of divine Freedom as cause of contingent beings.

According to Duns Scotus, God creates what He wants, when He wants and because He wants and all creatures are contingent. Let it not be thought, however, that God is arbitrary. In fact, His Will can create what He wants, this world or another, etc., but once done, that is converted into immutable law, in such way that whatever follows cannot contradict it. The same occurs in the moral order. God could have established other laws distinct from the present ones, which also would be correct because they come from His Will. The only thing that He could not change are the first two precepts of the Decalogue for He is bound to them. Outside of these two restrictions, nothing limits the freedom of God. He is not even conditioned in creation by the knowledge He has of all things in His infinite essence. Therefore, the only cause for which God wanted things is His Will.

Duns Scotus could not go farther in his voluntarism. If he does not fall into Cartesianism, it's because he still distinguishes clearly between the divine Intellect and the divine Will. To sum it up, for Duns Scotus, creation doesn't depend on the Wisdom of God, it doesn't have intrinsic necessary laws, but it is as it is by pure Will of God. For St. Thomas, on the contrary, creation has elements of necessity, given the free and creative Will of God.

d) The Principle of Individuation

In order to explain the essence of purely contingent beings, Duns Scotus rejects the real distinction between essence and "esse", which seems false to him. He accepts the doctrine of universal hylomorphism, taking up again in a very peculiar way the ancient doctrine of the plurality of forms. Since the mission of matter is to receive the form, logically matter will exist first. This is perfectly possible on the part of divine Omnipotence. The composite of matter and form (essence) possesses a reality of such character that it is possible to observe "formalities" in it.

In order to understand what these are, one must refer back to the theory of "formal distinction". The Scotist formal distinction is intermediate between the real distinction and that of reason, but it is not to be confused with the distinction of reason "cum fundamento in re". There is a formal distinction every time that the intellect can conceive in a being one of the formal constitutive principles apart from the other, for example the corporeality and the spirituality of man. These formal constitutive principles are the formalities, which are not the logical universal, but rather something of more entity because they are indifferent with respect to the individual and to the universal. Nevertheless, they contain both virtually, as for example, the formality "horse" is not of itself a universal, for otherwise no particular horses would exist. Nor is it a singular, in which case there would be only one horse. In itself, "horse" contains both possibilities. The realism of matter and form brings as a consequence that the essence has for Duns Scotus more autonomous consistency than in the case of St. Thomas, for whom the essence is fundamentally potency with respect to act. But Scotus gives to understand the existence of a "nature" or essence which is common to several beings. Then, in order to explain the singular being he resorts to a new act or individualizing determination which he calls "haecceitas" (thisness). For example, the person Socrates is the general form of man plus "Socratesness". Since "haecceitas" is the ultimate founding act of the individual concrete being, we must ask what relations it has with the general form. If we assign it an exclusive role, difficulties arise: for example, how can the unity of the human race be safeguarded and, in this case, how can theology explain that Jesus redeemed all men, or how to explain that there is knowledge of natural things, if what exists are individual beings with nothing in common.

The answer of Duns Scotus is that among these forms a formal distinction mediates. Also among the diverse "haecceitas" of the same species is found a formal distinction, that is, a distinction lower than real, but higher than that of reason. Naturally this is the only solution at hand if one wants to safeguard the unity of the human species and that of all the other species.

e) Balance

Duns Scotus could have followed Alexander of Hales or St. Bonaventure, great masters of his order, or even St. Thomas, but he preferred to correct the first Thomists with Augustinian doctrines. Thus, by keeping the Aristotelian vocabulary and technique, he somehow retrogressed to before St. Augustine in so far as he arrives at a more methodical Platonism in which the logical plane predominates over the ontological plane. To illustrate this point briefly, it suffices to say that on his going back to the absolute divine freedom to create or not, he overcame necessitarianism in the order of being, but not in the order of knowing. In fact, knowledge follows essence and hence it turns out that, starting with the notion of univocal being, it is the same as affirming the existence of an indeterminate essence (Avicenna's "natura communis"). Duns Scotus always moves on this plane. From this viewpoint, evil is an essence and not a privation of due being (St. Augustine). Therefore Scotus could not prove the Omnipotence of God since that would be equivalent to saying that God is the cause of evil, and this was inadmissible for Duns Scotus.

### 2. William of Ockham (1300?-1350) {#medieval-philosophy.9.2}
More responsible than Duns Scotus for the new routes of philosophy in the 14th century is William of Ockham. Born in England, he was already a Franciscan when he studied in Oxford from 1312 to 1318 and there he remained until 1320 when he was called to Avignon in order to justify his philosophical and theological doctrines, some of which were later condemned as heretical. About 1327, he joined in the ranks of those who opposed the temporal powers of the Papacy, writing doctrines similar to those of Marsilius of Padua (+1342), and for this he had to seek refuge in the court of Emperor Ludwig of Bavaria. He died, probably of the black plague, in Munich around 1350. His philosophical doctrine is found in his writings on the logic and the physics of Aristotle and principally in his commentaries on the Sentences and in two other works. Taken as a whole, it can be characterized as a radical empiricism which will provoke the fall of Scholastic philosophy long before the arrival of modern philosophy.

a) The Starting Point

Ockham considers that the philosophers have wasted too much time seeking sure or certain demonstrations of things, when there is a most clear and universal argument: the immediate evidence. To prove that an affirmation is certain is to show that it is immediately evident or that it is deduced from something immediately evident. His zeal for the concrete will lead him to identify immediate evidence with sense experience. He says: "Intuitive knowledge is that in virtue of which we know that a thing is when it is, and that it is not when it is not." This can also be expressed in another way: Direct experience of the existence of a thing is the only guarantee that it exists.

Ockham will never yield in this principle, not even in order to explain causality. One can not affirm that an effect proceeds from a cause if this is not experienced, reasoning with the presence and absence of the diverse factors in the event. Consequently, intuitive knowledge is the beginning of experimental knowledge, which is the only thing evident. On the contrary, abstract knowledge is concerned with ideas or concepts, which arise from the trace that experiences leave in the intellect. Nevertheless, although it may find in them the existence of necessary relations, it can never assure that they correspond to real things. It's as if he were to say that in order to build a wall, the bricks must be supported on the ground, adding that all the bricks must always be on the ground, for otherwise no one can assure that they are well supported. It seems, then, that in this way no one will be able to build. Precisely this is what he is going to say in his theory of knowledge.

b) Theory of Knowledge

Ockham is concerned with closely related topics: the universals, in which he claims to be the first one who doesn't grant them any reality at all; and the nature of the knowing process.

i) The Universals. Ockham centres all of his attention on particular beings since there is no reality other than the singular or individual, as experience testifies. It is absurd for the universal to correspond to anything in reality, for if it is something multiple, how can it be one? If it is one, how can it be distributed in many? He explains that in order to speak or to have knowledge, we use terms or words which sometimes represent concepts and sometimes things. Logic teaches that the function of words is to take the place of objects (this function is called "suppositio"). We can distinguish two kinds: "suppositio personalis", which is when the term replaces the real individual; and "suppositio simplex" when the term means something common to many individuals. In the first case, the term refers to an individual, which can be immediately experienced; in the second case, it refers to the universal. It is of interest to metaphysics to ascertain to what reality the second case corresponds. Since what is real is precisely individual, the universal can signify nothing concrete and real except the pure and simple collectivity of individuals and nothing else.

To sum up: the universal does not express anything that exists in reality and since it exists, its place can only be in thought. What reality do universals have in thought? The same that the individuals have which are assigned by the word or term. Sometimes, the terms designate a concept. This occurs when we want to signify objects which are known in a confused manner, that is, when the intellect isn't able to distinguish several objects among themselves. Thus the universals are words chosen arbitrarily to designate several things which are known in a more or less confused way. Ockham uses genera and species (universals) because in his opinion individuals lend themselves to be classified, but not because these universals signify a nature common to all of them. With this, he leaves the question unanswered.

ii) The Knowing Process. Philosophers have always said that in order to know it is necessary to have a subject and an object. Nevertheless, Ockham says, when he explains how in the act of knowing the sensible (object) and the spiritual (intellect) are related to one another, that philosophers usually insert some intermediate realities, such as impressed species, "verbum mentis", etc., without realizing that in each step they are making a leap from the material to the spiritual.

Under these conditions, he attempts to confront radically the problem of entry, keeping in mind that experience does not show the existence of these intermediate realities and therefore no argument can demonstrate the need of a cause whose reality is not given by experience. In order to actualize the intellect or explain the assimilation of the object, it isn't necessary to admit intermediaries, just as there are none between fire and light or agent and patient. Hence the subject immediately knows the object. Thus, by the action of individual things, there are produced in the intellect some confused sensible images of similar things. From this arises the indeterminate idea of universal.

c) Dissolution of Metaphysics and of Psychology

If the only method of valid demonstration is sense evidence, one understands that little or nothing can be done in natural theology. For Ockham it is not possible to prove the existence of God by means of change, since the principle that nothing is moved unless moved by another is not evident in itself. It seems that it is fulfilled in many cases, but not in others. In fact, there are things which move themselves, like angels or men. Moreover, the existence of an infinite series of motor causes is not impossible. Analogously, there are also no conclusive proofs that God is one, though that seems the most probable. However, there could be several worlds and several Gods, but this cannot be proven.

The same thing occurs if we want to demonstrate that God is infinite, omnipotent, etc. Consequently there is no motive to speak of divine attributes or of establishing an order among them. In reality, it is a question of simple names which we apply to the divine Essence beginning with its effects. Nor can we say that they signify distinct realities. Faithful to his criterion, Ockham now dismantles psychology. In the same way that there is external intuition, there is also internal intuition in man, by which the states of soul, wanting and knowing, are discovered. But this internal intuition does not teach how one knows nor how the will is determined to operate. Hence, we cannot refer back to a substantial and immaterial soul. Therefore, we cannot demonstrate philosophically the immortality of the soul.

d) Ethics

Duns Scotus had arrived at the conclusion that the divine Will was only limited by the principle of non-contradiction and that in His Will ethics finds its foundation. Thus, things are good because God orders them. It is not certain that God orders things to operate which in themselves are not good. For Scotus, the whole moral law depends on the pure Will of God, except the first and second commandments which refer to God Himself. But Ockham is much more radical. He affirms that the divine Will is not conditioned even by what Scotus says, so that God could have commanded us to hate Him and make that this would be good. In other words, the goodness or malice of human actions is rooted exclusively in obedience or disobedience to God's Will, understood as apart from His Intellect and His Being.

Thus, human acts aren't intrinsically good or evil, and God doesn't command that we do good and avoid evil, but simply that we obey Him. Hence it is also that there are no meritorious acts and so God could condemn the good and save the guilty. Perhaps Ockham, like Scotus before him, had arrived at these conclusions through his effort to stress the divine transcendence and the poverty of the contingent. However, this exasperation of divine transcendence is apparent, for God is lowered to the category of a capricious being deprived of the perfections of His Wisdom and His Being. An ethics in which man claims to obey God without wanting to understand Him in anything is very close to subjectivism. One must not be surprised the Ockham's morals had great repercussions in Luther.

e) Balance and Influence

Ockham isn't very original in his principles, for two currents of thought come together in him: Abelard's nominalism, always present since the 12th century; and the doctrine of the absolute supremacy of divine freedom, developed as a reaction to the Greco-Arab determinism or necessitarianism. What is original in him is the combination of both, since he converts nominalism into the instrument to fight against speculative philosophy and all who seem to him to be connected with it, such as the grand syntheses of the 13th century and that of Duns Scotus. The viewpoint from which Ockham considers things is that of divine Omnipotence, and he eliminates all obstacles which could signify its limitation. In this, he is more radical than Scotus who had already said that divine ideas (or God's knowledge of things in His own essence) cannot influence the Will of the Creator. Ockham adds that if one acknowledges that the divine ideas have no influence, it is preferable to economize concepts and simply affirm that they don't exist.

In these conditions, in face of the powerful divine Will, everything else is radically contingent; that is, beings and our knowledge have a very precarious consistency. It can be no surprise, then, that he says that it suffices that two things be distinct so that God can make one to exist without the other and consequently that all our knowledge about the relations among things is simply doubtful. Even sense intuition itself, the only basis of knowledge, doesn't guarantee the existence of its objects, for God can arouse it without the presence of the object. The result of this doctrine is, on the one hand, that theology can find no support on destroyed metaphysics and finds itself obliged to be enclosed on itself. Since speculative reflection tends little by little to disappear in theological work, in fact it will become a simple collecting of data from Revelation (positive theology) without wanting to understand them.

In its turn, philosophy only reaches a probable knowledge of things, which is in practice a scepticism very close to the anti-philosophical pessimism which will follow Ockham. This will bring with it the profound decadence of metaphysics. On the other hand, the philosophical scepticism of Ockham wants to be compensated by fideism in theology. Thus a more and more unbridgeable abyss is opened between reason and faith. With St. Thomas and the masters of the 13th century, metaphysics had come to explain, by way of natural reason, a nucleus of truths about God. With Duns Scotus the nucleus was reduced quite a bit, but still it was known that God is One, Infinite and Creator. Nothing of this remains in William of Ockham, and there is nothing else to do but embrace these truths with blind faith. Only one more step is needed for the rupture between reason and faith to be complete.

### 3. The Ockhamist Tendency of the 14th Century {#medieval-philosophy.9.3}
Ockham's doctrines were quickly accepted among very diverse groups of people. Very soon they were called nominalists or terminists and above all "moderni", as opposed to the followers of St. Thomas and of Duns Scotus, who were called "antiqui" and realists. Ockham's ideas took root especially in the Faculty of Arts of Paris and continued being spread in spite of the Church's condemnation of their heretical errors. In 1347 the complete council of the University of Paris met in order to dismiss Nicholas of Autrecourt (+1350) and to witness the burning of his works. Nicholas, with the doctrine received from Ockham, had run through and invalidated all the philosophy of nature of Aristotle and had applied his own conclusions to theology.

The first principle of his philosophy is that of sense evidence, which can have two sources: experimental verification and the principle of identity, which can be reduced to that of noncontradiction. The fundamental criterion of truth, the principle of non-contradiction, states that contradictories can't be simultaneously true. If this principle is applied to causality, it turns out that, beginning with the fact that a thing exists, one cannot deduce that another thing exists. Moreover, one can only speak of causality when effect and cause are seen at the same time. Consequently, the existence of God can't be deduced from the world's existence nor can it be demonstrated that the world would not exist if God did not exist.

What then is substance, for we speak of it when a determined number of events occur around a thing? Experience tells us nothing about its existence but only shows us accidents. Therefore, the rule cannot be true: "every accident supposes a substance." It is really a question of a particular case of the impossibility of applying the principle of causality. Not even in this case are cause and effect, substance and accident, seen simultaneously. In other words, the phrase "every accident supposes a substance" is a tautology, coming from the principle of identity. Analogously one can proceed for the perfection and end of beings. If we say that one thing is the end of another, we attribute to the effect a cause which is separated, which is not evident. In order to verify that one thing is more perfect than another, two different things must be compared. This is the reality that we find; namely, that they are distinct and equally different. Certainly, each one of them contributes, according to its nature, to the perfection and harmony of the whole. But if they are equally different, they will also be just as perfect. Thus, to try to make a hierarchy is arbitrary.

On the other hand, Nicholas has a very strange idea about experience. He says that from the fact that an event now occurs, it doesn't follow that it will be repeated, even under the same conditions. Thus, if the hand is burned by fire, we don't know if this will occur again if the hand touches fire. Once the notion of causality is thus undone, Nicholas embraces Epicurean atomism, which says that everything is made of atoms and all change consists in local movement of atoms. From this position he affirms that intellect and senses of a dead person could return to action if the atoms return to the same combination as in the living body, deducing from this strange theories about rewards and punishments in eternal life.

In another direction, this atmosphere of philosophical scepticism comes indirectly to be an auxiliary factor in scientific investigation, not because it contributes a method or material or interest in this task, but rather because it was creating an intellectual atmosphere in which scientific results were finding echo and understanding. The concerns of thinkers in the 12th and 13th centuries moved in the sphere of logic, metaphysics, theory of knowledge, etc. The study of experimental sciences was considered to be inferior and aroused less interest. On the contrary, in conformity with the acceptance of the fact that the only authentic knowledge comes from sense evidence, the hour arrived in which the scientists of the Faculty of Arts of Paris, like John Buridan and his disciple Albert of Saxony, took charge of the rectorate of the university.

Thus, the 14th century is an age of gradual progress in natural sciences, by means of observations, analyses, etc., though many of the discoveries were not fruitfully utilized until they were accepted by the new physics of the 16th and 17th centuries. As examples we can cite the explanation of uniformly accelerated motion, gravity, the co-ordinates, reasoned hypotheses of heliocentrism, which fell into oblivion and which the new physics would have to rediscover.

### 4. Other Authors of the 14th Century {#medieval-philosophy.9.4}
In 1322 Peter Aureolus, the Franciscan and Master of Theology in Paris, died, leaving behind his "Tractatus de principiis" and a commentary on the Sentences. In his books he criticizes the majority of theologians and philosophers who preceded him, like St. Bonaventure, St. Thomas and Duns Scotus. With Aristotle he maintains that matter has no actuality without form, and inversely, with Averroes he says that the form can neither exist nor be conceived without matter. With this assumption, he can't understand the definition of the Council of Vienne that the rational soul is truly and in itself the form of the body. For Aureolus form is act of matter and therefore incapable of thinking (like that of a stone). Moreover, the form is born and dies with the body, and so its immortality is not explained. However, that conciliar definition must be accepted without restrictions only by faith, without trying to understand it. In these conditions it becomes very difficult to explain knowledge, for his concept of form hinders it, but experience shows that knowledge exists.

Thus he affirms simply that in the knowing subject there is a union between the imaginable and the intellect. He attributes to the imagination the role of grasping reality. There is neither impressed nor expressed species, neither empirical nor intellectual, and this can be maintained with two more reasons: that all the elements that philosophers find in knowledge are not experimental; and the Fathers of the Church make no mention of them. On the other hand, the soul is not distinguished from its faculties; that is, intellect and will are the soul itself in two distinct actions. So it is the soul which wants to know and which, as intellect, returns to the sense impressions ("phantasma" of the imagination). Since only sense experience gives notice of the individual, it turns out that the soul or intellect intuites the essence of the things directly.

After the condemnations of 1277, Averroism shut itself off without accepting the philosophical and theological discussions of its postulates nor admitting the new physics. While it languished in France and England, it was spreading in Italy in the first half of the 14th century. In Padua, the theses of Siger of Brabant were being insistently repeated. Marsilius of Padua (+1342), with the collaboration of John of Jandun (+1328?), initiated the political version of Averroism. Possibly both authors only wanted to defend, in their work "Defensor pacis", the existence of a lay state as opposed to the temporal power of the ecclesiastics. But the way chosen could hardly be more revealing. Faith and reason are not only distinct things, but they are separated.

Therefore, there are two modes of life, which correspond to the two ends of man. To achieve the natural end, the princes regulate temporal life in conformity with philosophy, and the priests lead men to eternal life in conformity with Revelation. In a certain way both missions complement one another, for religion, in spite of its indemonstrable truths, helps men to behave well in social life. But the two must never be confused and must remain separated. "Defensor pacis" was condemned in 1327 and its authors sought refuge in the court of Emperor Ludwig of Bavaria. Behind this book there was no exclusively political problem, since the rupture between the natural and the supernatural which they advocated will spread through Italy in the first half of the 17th century as the banner of radical religious incredulity.

### 5. Speculative Mysticism {#medieval-philosophy.9.5}
In the 12th century, mystical literature had become more speculative and had preferred Plato and Neo-Platonism as its philosophical substrate. Meister Eckhart (1260-1327) was the master of numerous spiritual authors. Born in Germany, he was a Dominican and taught in Cologne until he died. In explaining the communication and union of the soul with God, he prescinds from the official doctrine of the Order. He interprets St. Thomas and St. Albert according to the doctrine of Proclus. His fundamental thesis is that God doesn't know Himself because He is, but He is because He knows Himself. From the Neo-Platonic trilogy "esse", "vivere", "intelligere", Eckhart chooses the last as basis of the other two. He justifies this by saying that "esse" befits creatures and is in God only as cause. The words of Exodus, "Ego sum qui sum", don't refer to God as ESSE but rather it is a divine way of eluding an answer or else a way of affirming that God is beyond being. In this way he implied that thought is above being.

For this reason, the idealists will refer to him as a distant predecessor. For him God the Father is "Intelligere", the Son is "Vivere", the Holy Spirit, "Esse". At the end of his life it seems that he subordinated "Intelligere" to "Unum" in order to affirm that the divine Essence is Unity. He says that unity is the exclusive property of the intellect and so spiritual creatures are not completely one because they are beings that have, but are not, "intelligere". From this point of view, creatures are a nullity, for they are only in so far as they participate in the intellect, which is a spark of the Divine Intellect. A study of the human soul verifies that there is a residue which contains "something uncreated and uncreatable, which is the intellect", in which man is not distinct from God, because "the Father begets me His son and His own Son. Everything that God does is one." The nearness to pantheism is evident. These and other propositions of Eckhart were condemned by the Church. But his influence felt in his disciples John Tauler (1300-1361), Blessed Henry Suso (1295-1366) and John Ruysbroeck (1293-1381), all spiritual writers.

### 6. Concluding Views {#medieval-philosophy.9.6}
John Gerson (+1429), chancellor of the U. of Paris from 1395, complained that the masters did not keep to their own subjects. From this, he begins a critique of speculative Scholastic theology whose arguments will permeate the 14th and 15th centuries. He points out that men think they are theologians if they have only learned philosophy, as well as similar ideas. For him the cause of all these evils is, on the one hand, the treating of metaphysics as logic, and on the other hand, pride in trying to demonstrate those things which depend exclusively on God's free Will, such as creation. He says that this erroneous attitude comes from the Neo-Platonic naturalism of the Arabs which led Christian thinkers to introduce in God distinctions "ex natura rei" or as Duns Scotus says, "formalitates", which are similar to real distinctions.

Arab Neo-Platonism has led to an exaggerated realism of the ideas, which makes of God a being according to the measure of thought, which destroys the simple and free God of faith. Gerson detects well the error of radical Platonism when he affirms that the realism that must be avoided consists in believing that everything that man knows in a universal mode exists separately in things and in God, because this means transforming logic (the universal concept) into metaphysics and later into theology. By this road a general confusion has been created in the orders of knowledge which is responsible for many heresies. As a result of this critique, Gerson tends toward nominalist theses which seem to him, as theologian, to prevent these dogmatic errors. He also reminds his contemporaries that they must dominate the daughters of pride: vain curiosity, desire for singularity and the aggravated "esprit de corps" of the diverse tendencies, which make scarce and render difficult the peaceful possession of true Christian wisdom. For this he proposes as models the great masters of the 13th century, especially St. Bonaventure.

Thus, at the end of the 14th century we are present at the decadence of Scholastic philosophy. Certainly the principal doctrinal tendencies are preserved, but without sufficient internal force to hinder nominalist havoc. On the other hand, the University of Paris finds itself involved in the struggles between the Papacy and France and little by little ceases to be the University of Christianity, in order to be reduced to a national phenomenon, above all from the time of the Western Schism (1379-1418).

Only with the danger of the Protestant pseudo-reform, connected with nominalism, will the Christian spiritual forces be reunited and will give rise to a rebirth of Scholastic philosophy, which will render great service in the Council of Trent.
