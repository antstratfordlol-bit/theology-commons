## III. The Patristic after St. Augustine {#medieval-philosophy.3}
After St. Augustine and till the beginning of the 9th century, there are no creative philosophers in the East or in the West, but only authors who systematize the previous thought. One must keep in mind that during this time Europe suffered invasions from the Eastern peoples from the 5th century onwards and the East was invaded by Islam from the 7th century, and this rendered the diffusion of culture very difficult. However, these summarizing writers became fundamental links in the transmission of philosophy in such a way that they can be considered in all truth as the remote founders of the medieval philosophical systems.

### 1. Boëthius {#medieval-philosophy.3.1}
He was born in Rome in 470 and died by execution in Pavia in 525, where he receives local cult with Church approval. He commented and translated Greek philosophy and transmitted it to the Latin world. His translations of Aristotle and of Porphyry and his treatises on logic will make him an indisputable master of this material till the 12th century. His influence will also extend for centuries to the method of intellectual work - the commentary of a text -, to the way of condensing thought into precise formulae - the sentences - and in particular, his classification of theoretical sciences. As philosopher, his main work is "De consolatione philosophiae" which teaches that philosophy is a concrete reality consisting in loving and seeking wisdom, which is the same as loving and seeking God.

a) Hierarchy or Classification of the Sciences

To love and seek God requires some beings who know and some acts to be practised. Thus Boëthius distinguishes between theoretical philosophy and practical philosophy. The first admits as many divisions as there are types of beings which are known. They include the INTELLECTIBILIA, the beings which exist without matter - this division is called theology -, the INTELLIGIBILIA, which are the souls in matter - this division is called psychology - and the NATURALIA, the sciences of nature - this division is called by Boëthius physiology. This last division includes the so-called QUADRIVIUM, or arithmetic, astronomy, geometry and music. He adds to this classification also the TRIVIUM, or grammar, rhetoric and logic, which are necessary not in order to know, but rather for the correct expression of knowledge. Here Boëthius also posits the problem about the nature of the general ideas or universals.

b) The Universals

Porphyry (232-304) in his "Isagoge" asked some questions about the nature of general notions or ideas, as for example, whether the genera and species exist in themselves or only in the mind, and if in themselves, whether they are corporeal or incorporeal, and if the latter, whether they exist united to the things only, or also separated from the things. Boëthius, in his two commentaries on "Isagoge" denies two extremes, namely that the universals are simply fabricated by the mind and that they are corporeal realities or substances. Thus, universals are incorporeal because as universals they exist only in the intellect which abstracts them from the many material things. Boëthius concludes that they subsist united to the sensible, but are conceived by transcending what is corporeal. He recognizes that this solution is Aristotelian, but he doesn't make it his own and agrees with Plato in saying that a reality corresponds to the universals, the reality of the IDEA. Perhaps for this reason he omits in his commentary on the "Isagoge" this idea of Aristotle.

c) God

God is the most excellent "intellectible." Therefore the highest science deals with God. In his "De consolatione philosophiae" he makes a sketch of natural theology. God is the highest Good, the perfect Being so that none greater than He can be thought. To show His existence, he uses the principle which states that the imperfect in its genus is always a diminution of the perfect. Since there is obviously imperfection, the perfect being also exists, which is God. Since He is perfect, God is the Goodness and the Happiness. Boëthius defines happiness as "the perfect state in which all goods are possessed." St. Thomas will later make this phrase a classic. Since God is perfect He is not only happy, but Happiness Itself. Thus man's happiness is attained by participating in the Divine Happiness. God's transcendence makes Him escape the categories and the determinations of the intellect. We know that God is the First Cause of the world. With respect to Himself, we only know that God, being perfect, is "by Himself everything that He is."

d) The Souls, Freedom and Providence Human souls are "intellectibles" which degenerate to "intelligibles" by their contact with the body. They pre-existed with the angels but once they have fallen into the bodies, they can reach happiness by applying reason to the "intellectibles." For Boëthius, the natural beings tend by nature to their natural places and man is no exception. He tends to God but he must do so with his will. For this end he is endowed with reason and is capable of knowing and choosing. Man will thus be freer and happier the more he agrees through rational knowledge with the Will of God. Here the way of reconciling human freedom with Divine Providence is brought up. If God knows what man is going to do and at the same time governs the world by Providence, how can we explain human freedom? If man is free, is God's Providence infallible?

For Boëthius this approach is inexact, for they are two distinct matters, since the foreseeing of human acts by God is realized in eternity, whereas man's exercise of freedom is realized in time, in which there is a before and an after. God is eternal and he defines eternity as "perfect and simultaneously complete possession of an interminable life." God lives and knows in a continuous present, and thus He sees eternally the necessary as necessary and the free as free. By contemplating the totality of the world, Boëthius understands Divine Providence as the order of things considered in the knowledge of God. Boëthius understands destiny as the interior law of the world. They are two distinct realities, one eternal, the other temporal, though destiny is nothing else than the temporal realizations of the decrees of Providence. Therefore, one does not oppose Providence but rather serves it. Consequently it also does not destroy human freedom.

e) Metaphysical Structure of Natural Beings

In "De hebdomadibus" Boëthius brings up the metaphysical structure of natural beings when, following Plato and St. Augustine he identifies being with good and non-being with evil, so that every being, in so far as it is, is substantially good. In these conditions it becomes necessary to distinguish between any being God Who is Being in Itself. God is perfectly simple and He has no parts, but in the creatures there is diversity between the "being" and the "whatness". The creature is composed of determining elements and determined elements, but none of these parts is the whole, as we find for example in the case of man who is composed of soul and body, neither of which is the whole man.

To sum up, the form is the determining element of the composite and Boëthius designates this as "esse" or "quo est" and this is what gives being. Therefore the formula given above about the diversity of being and of that which is establishes the distinction between the form and the whole (substance), but not between the co-principles of essence and esse. Boëthius in this way had a glimpse at the distinction of esse and essence, which is of key importance in St. Thomas, but he didn't gain anything from it. He rather came to identify the esse of the creature with its form. For Boëthius, God is distinguished from the creatures by the fact that in His perfect simplicity His Esse and His Whatness are the same thing, which does not occur in creatures.

### 2. Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite {#medieval-philosophy.3.2}

An unknown author writing in Greek about the end of the 5th or the beginning of the 6th century wrote a number of works which constitute one of the principal sources of medieval thought on central topics such as the divine attributes, the angels, evil, the transcendentals, the degrees of being.

On the one hand his work signifies a Christianization of Neo-Platonism, correcting works of Plotinus and Porphyry, though using their terminology. For example, evil is a privation, therefore matter is good because is was created by God.

The influence of this writer began to make its influence felt in Constantinople in 532 and it would be admired by Nicholas of Cusa in 1464. This extraordinary influence was due to a confusion which lasted till the 15th century. The author of the "Corpus Areopagiticum", which work had the signature "Dionysius", presents himself as a disciple of St. Paul who transmits a doctrine for "the Apostles to their disciples, that is, for those already initiated into Christianity. His testimony will be accepted by St. Maximus and from then on the author will be identified as the convert at the Areopagus who is mentioned in the Acts of the Apostles, XVII, 34.

a) The Knowledge of God

Pseudo-Dionysius (PsD) presents his work as a meditation on Scriptures. God is absolutely transcendent and so one must not try to verify rationally His ineffable nature. Only God knows Himself, but He has revealed Himself to man in Scriptures, giving Himself various names which simple people accept in all simplicity. Whoever wishes to have a clear idea of God must grasp the content of these names and see how they can be applied to God. We must affirm about God the names given by Scripture (Love, Goodness, Life, Being, etc.), (affirmative theology) but at the same time deny them, for none of them expresses God adequately (negative theology). Both attitudes are reconciled in a third, which consists in saying that God deserves each of these names in a way which is ineffable, for God is "hyper-being", "hyper-love", etc. (superlative theology). PsD inaugurates a method which will have much historical importance: to deny in God all things as they exist in the reality known by man.

b) The Hierarchy of Beings

PsD, a Christian with Platonic education, identifies God the Creator with the Good. This is the name which best designates the cause of all beings. The Good spreads its rays through all creatures (like the sun does), giving to each of them its corresponding degree of being and fixing each in its proper nature and relations towards other creatures. Thus, the higher beings have influence over the lower beings and since the universe is disposed in degrees, a "metaphysical continuity" prevails in creation. St. Thomas will accept this harmonic vision of the degrees of created being, as well as the principle that the good spreads itself (BONUM DIFFUSIVUM SUI) as applied to the free generosity of God's creative act and to the constant influence of higher creatures upon the lower. Hence, the world with its variety is a revelation of God and of His perfections. Inversely, all beings and their perfections are contained supereminently in God, and hence God is the Being of all things in the sense that He is the cause of the being of the things.

Once we understand how PsD links divine transcendence with the participation of creatures in descending degrees in order to give a basis for the hierarchy of beings, it is fitting to point out that if being and good are identified, at once the question of evil crops up. For PsD, evil is nothing other than privation, non-being, apparent reality, which God permits in the order of greater goods. Deep down, it is the same concept as that of St. Augustine. Both of them will have influence in the Middle Ages, and especially on St. Thomas.

c) God in Himself

If God is the Good in as much as He distributes goods, in Himself His most appropriate name, according to Scriptures, is that of Being, "He Who Is", and thus cause of every creature. But in the strict sense God is not Being. If we consider God from the viewpoint of creation, evidently He is Being and the cause of all beings, but considered in Himself, He should have another name, the One. Indeed the One contains everything in a pure unity which is exempt from multiplicity. The multiple cannot exist without the One, but the One can exist without the multiple. Everything which is participates in the One, and He participates in nothing.

This preference for the name of the One is due to the strong Neo-Platonic influence received by PsD. We have already arrived at the predominant mystical idea of PsD: the One also does not properly designate God. In reality, God has no name, nor is He anything of that which is, nor of that which is not (negative theology). He transcends all being and all knowledge. Whoever understands this has reached mystical ignorance and the highest degree of knowledge.

d) Balance

The "Corpus Areopagiticum" enjoyed great prestige for the reasons already mentioned and became one of the fundamental books of study in the Middle Ages and will be interpreted by such diverse authors as Hugh of St. Victor, St. Albert the Great. St. Bonaventure, Meister Eckhart, etc.

The extraordinary influence of PsD is exercised over the main topics of metaphysics: the divine attributes, the angels, evil, the transcendentals, the degrees of being, and many others. St. Thomas Aquinas quotes him more than 1500 times and owes him, among other things, his theology of the angels and especially the notion of being as an act which is possessed by God with infinite and total intensity, whereas in creatures it has diverse degrees of limited intensity.

### 3. John Philoponus, Christian Commentator of Aristotle {#medieval-philosophy.3.3}
John Philoponus is an exception to the Neo-Platonists in the later Eastern Patristic period. He lived in the first half of the 6th century and commented on Aristotle, raising questions which St. Thomas Aquinas would answer later. Of special importance is his commentary on "De Anima", which is an excellent summary about the ideas regarding the nature of the agent intellect. Some identify this with God, others with some demiurge while others think that man has two intellects, one passive, the other active. He says that these last ones have read Aristotle with Platonic mentality. He says that his own interpretation is the true one and the only one possible for a Christian; namely, that man has one intellect which is sometimes in act (agent) and sometimes in potency (passive). This solution also permits the demonstration of the immortality of the soul. John Ph. was read by the first Arabian theologians and had great influence on them, and St.

Thomas was also grateful for some commentaries on "De Anima" which confirmed his own interpretation of Aristotle.

### 4. St. Maximus the Confessor, Commentator of Dionysius {#medieval-philosophy.3.4}

Maximus of Chrysopolis (580-662) is closely linked to the long Christological controversies of his century (monothelism). He was educated in Neo-Platonism, wrote extensively in various fields of theology though only one work, not an original one, would have a great influence in medieval thought. Imbued in the doctrine of PsD, it is surprising that he interprets him in his own way. In several points, the influence of PsD is exercised mainly through the interpretation of Maximus. In fact, the high Middle Ages will make no distinction between the work of the one and the interpretation of the others. John Scotus Eriugena translates his main work under the name of the "Ambiguous" and converts its content in the framework of his own metaphysical, moral and mystical doctrine which will leave a profound trace on the Middle Ages.

### 5. St. John Damascene (+749) {#medieval-philosophy.3.5}

St. John Damascene (675-749) belonged to a well-to-do Christian family which was working in the court of the Arab dynasty of the Omeyas. He is considered as the last of the Eastern Fathers. In him there coincides the circumstance of belonging culturally to the Byzantine world while living within the territories governed by the Arabs. His principal work, "Sources of Knowledge" is a very personal summary of the teachings of the Greek Fathers. It is divided into three parts. It was translated into Latin in the 12th century, serving as a model for the "Book of Sentences" of Peter Lombard.

a) Natural Theology

One idea which the high Middle Ages owes to St. John D. - to approve it or to dispute it - is that there is no man who does not know that God exists. To know that He exists, it suffices to contemplate the order and the conservation of creatures and to pay attention to the Revelation of the Old and the New Testaments. For John D., it is natural that man knows spontaneously that God exists, but he points out that the malice of Satan and the effects of sin darken the heart of man, at times up to the point of not knowing of God's existence. For this reason we must make use of rational arguments to establish this truth. Making use of the Pauline principle that God can be known from the creatures, he develops three demonstrations, by way of the mutability of beings which necessarily demands the existence of an uncreated creator, by way of the conservation and governing of things, and by way of the order in the world, which is not a fruit of chance.

b) The Nature of God

On the other hand, he vigorously stresses the transcendence of God. Only God knows Himself fully. Though we can know His existence, we cannot know in depth His nature which is incomprehensible. When we affirm that God has no limitations, by saying that He is infinite, incorporeal, immutable, etc., we are only expressing what He is not, and when we apply to Him the positive attributes like one, good, just, etc., we are also not expressing adequately the divine essence, but we are only affirming something that befits His nature, but which is infinitely richer. So the divine declaration of Exodus 3, 14: "I am He who is" expresses above all the incomprehensibility of God on the part of man. God is beyond being and knowledge. Therefore that expression of Scripture signifies, for John D., that God possesses and unites in Himself the totality of being like an ocean of reality (OUSIA) which is infinite and unlimited. This formula will be commented many times in the Middle Ages, especially by St. Thomas.

c) Influence

During the life of John D., probably most of his works were translated into Arabic. In the West, the first translation of "Source of Knowledge" dates from 1150. In spite of this late entry, this work will spread quickly in all Scholastic thought, precisely because it contains so much that agrees with it, such as its structure, its precision and its Aristotelian content.

### 6. The Culture Handed Down by the Fathers {#medieval-philosophy.3.6}
Medieval philosophy is an exclusively Western phenomenon, and its immediate precedents are found in Latin culture. more exactly in the Christianized classical Latin culture. The majority of the Fathers had been educated in conformity with the cultural ideal proposed by Cicero and Quintillian, the first of whom deplored the separation of wisdom from eloquence, saying that the philosophers, on not knowing how to speak, and the rhetoricians, on not knowing how to think, stun our ears. Thus it becomes necessary to educate the youth to form them to be learned orators who would be able to govern society correctly.

On the other hand Quintillian, due to the influence of Seneca and the Stoics, proposes the formation of honourable men who know how to speak. In conformity with the penetration of Christianity into the teaching structure, a change of meaning will be given to Quintillian's ideal, namely the eloquence will now be Christian. In this Christian eloquence the wisdom of the faith replaces the pagan philosophy. The sciences and the liberal arts are not rejected, but now they are placed at the service of the comprehension and exposition of the Holy Scriptures. This will be the educational programme which will last until the 12th century.
