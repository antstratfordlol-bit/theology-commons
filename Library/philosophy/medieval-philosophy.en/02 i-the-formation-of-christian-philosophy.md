## I. The Formation of Christian Philosophy in the Patristic Age {#medieval-philosophy.1}
### 1. The Doctrinal Contribution of Christianity {#medieval-philosophy.1.1}
Christianity contains revealed truths of the strictly supernatural order as well as some truths of the natural order. These latter are object of philosophical study, such as God and the origin of the world, man and his relations with God. The revelation of these truths makes it easier for more men to know them with certainty, but does not render it useless or unnecessary for these truths to be studied by reason alone. Thus, the Christian already knew by revelation that God exists, that He is transcendent to the world, that man was created to His image and likeness, with a spiritual and immortal soul.

### 2. Overall View of the Formation of Christian Philosophy {#medieval-philosophy.1.2}
In the beginnings, Christian doctrine was based on the oral preaching of the witnesses of the life of Christ and on Holy Scripture. However, already in the 2nd century it was seen that it was necessary to make use also of theological speculation, both in order to make more precise the dogmatic formulations of the Christian truths as well as to combat heretical teachings.

The principal enemy of Christianity in those times was the Greek GNOSIS, an intellectual movement of the 2nd century which claimed to use the teachings of Jesus, reducing them to the limits of reason and interpreting them in conformity with philosophical schemes of the ancient mythology, thus adulterating the true content of the faith.

From this perspective, the Christian philosopher, rooted in the faith, began a laborious task of purifying philosophy from the many elements coming from Greek culture, bringing it into agreement with the faith and converting it into an instrument to serve the faith. This prevented the adaptation of Christian doctrine to any Greek philosophy and permitted a certain utilization of Greek philosophy on the part of Christian thinkers. In this sense we can establish two historical landmarks: St. Augustine with respect to Neo-Platonism; St. Thomas Aquinas, with respect to Aristotelianism.

At the height of Christian philosophy we find the work of St. Thomas Aquinas, with a body of doctrine based on the natural truths revealed by God, having as its central point the notion of the act of to-be and at the same time constituting a higher synthesis of the truths found in Platonism and in Aristotle's thought. For this reason philosophy, or more properly the metaphysics of St. Thomas Aquinas, represents the reference point in order to evaluate all the previous solutions found in Christian philosophy.

### 3. The First Fathers of the Church {#medieval-philosophy.1.3}
In theology, the name "Father of the Church" is reserved for the author who meets the following four conditions:

a) orthodoxy of doctrine;

b) holiness of life;

c) antiquity;

d) recognition by the Church.

However, many authors who do not meet all these conditions are included in the study of patristics. The first Christian writers who had to face up to the pagan culture were the Apologists who roundly affirmed the superiority of Christian doctrine over pagan philosophy. We must recognize two basic attitudes, one of criticism and rejection of pagan philosophy as the source of heresies, which is the attitude of Tertullian (150-225) and Tatian (living in 170), the other the attitude of those who tried to purify Greek philosophy so as to convert it into an instrument which could be used in spreading the faith and understanding it. In this second group we have authors such as St. Justin (+165), St. Irenaeus (126-203) and other apologists. With these writers the long and hard task of purifying and bringing together the first philosophical elements begins.

### 4. The Neo-Platonism of the School of Alexandria and of the Cappadocians {#medieval-philosophy.1.4}
Alexandria was the city of the Hellenic world in which there was the greatest mixture of cultures, beliefs, philosophies, etc. It is the city where Philo (30 B.C. - 40 A.D.) was born and where he taught as one of the founders of Neo-Platonism, which began the allegorical interpretation of the Old Testament using elements of Platonism and Stoicism. A Christian community was flourishing in Alexandria and from this community arose the so-called school of Alexandria, founded by a Christian convert from Stoicism named Pantenos (+200). His teachings were collected by a young Athenian who succeeded him as director of the school, who was Clement of Alexandria (150-215). He considered that God in His Providence had prepared the coming of Jesus Christ with the Old Testament and with Greek philosophy and thus the faith in Jesus Christ has not eliminated philosophy but rather philosophy is useful to the Christian as long as it is used in service of the faith. For Clement philosophy is the handmaid of theology.

The most relevant figure of this school is Origen (185-253), who already at the age of 18 was the leader of the catechism school of Alexandria. He tried to extract everything that seemed useful from all the philosophical systems of his time and uses this in his commentaries on Sacred Scripture. In this way he transmits Platonism and other philosophical concepts to medieval Christianity. He does not always succeed in subordinating philosophy to the faith, however, and at times he falls into very serious errors. Around the year 230, he went to Caesarea in Cappadocia where he founded a school in which, a century or so later, the three Cappadocian Fathers will receive their formation. These Fathers were defenders of the supremacy of the faith over the rationalism of the Arians, who tried to reduce the faith to pure philosophy and used Platonic ideas to show that the Son cannot be of the same essence as the Father, and thus is not God.

St. Gregory Nazianzen states that the essence of God cannot be defined in negative terms, but rather must be defined positively as FULL REALITY. St. Gregory of Nyssa, the brother of St. Basil, offers us a Platonic vision of reality which will have visible influence in the Middle Ages in the West.

If we would want to make a summary of this whole period, we would have to characterize it as the period in which, in one way or another, a Christian outlook on man is formed, influenced by Platonism which will be propagated by various authors, mainly by St. Augustine and by Pseudo- Dionysius, in medieval thought. This current, called Augustinianism, will understand the soul not as the form of the body but rather as a living, rational substance, incorporeal and complete in itself, which makes use of the body in order to reach its end. Some authors distinguish in man a triple reality: the body or SOMA, the soul or PSYKHE , and the spirit or PNEUMA, as if we could speak of a higher soul (the PNEUMA) and the lower soul (PSYKHE). Nevertheless, all the authors stress the unity of man as opposed to any sort of dualism which separates matter and spirit into two stagnant compartments.

### 5. The Transmission of Greek Philosophy in the Schools of Syria and in the Neo-Platonic Latin Christians {#medieval-philosophy.1.5}

Up to now we have been concerned with the Eastern Fathers, but we must remember that Christianity spread from Palestine in all directions within the Roman Empire. Rome contributed the political and organizational genius, while the Hellenic world supplied the culture and with that the philosophy. It is also well known that the Church did not emerge officially until 313 and that in the meantime Greek thought had taken new directions. In the first years of the Christian era, the speculative force of the Greek philosophies was dying out and these philosophies were being replaced by a notable scepticism. Nevertheless, the swan song of Greek philosophy was launched in Neo-Platonism, born of the union of philosophy with the religion of the Old Testament through the work of Philo of Alexandria and the ex-Christian Ammonius Saccas (175-242).

The principal representative of this movement was the Egyptian Plotinus (205-270). At the age of 50 he wrote the Enneads, in which he teaches, among other things, that the nature of things is explained by their proceeding from the One by way of necessary emanation, since the multiple is based necessarily on unity. Well now, since God comes before all things, we cannot call Him essence or being, but rather One. In other words, the One (God) is the most radical reality, and therefore is more than being, more than essence, more than existence. Beginning with the One, reality appears by division. Nevertheless, having arrived at this point Plotinus affirms that something, in the measure in which it participates in the intelligible, is real and good, and is not real and good in the measure in which it is corporeal and material. Matter is of itself non-being and evil, as the extreme opposite of the absolute and the One. With this metaphysical basis, his disciple Porphyry (232-304) will begin a Neo-Platonic interpretation of the works of Plato and Aristotle which will very soon remodel the Greek philosophical patrimony. All of this was what the Christian philosophers were faced with from the beginning of the 3rd century, both in the East and in the West. We have already taken a glance at the Eastern Fathers but before mentioning those of the West, it must be pointed out that when the Byzantine Empire fell into decay, this Greek cultural patrimony passes to Antioch and Edessa, and from there to the Arabs, as we shall see later.

The Latin Fathers were less interested in speculation than in moral topics. For this reason the spreading of a Christian Platonism in the West was much slower. In St. Hilary of Poitiers (315- 359) we shall find a short philosophical explanation about God. He says that God is THE VERY ONE WHO IS, that is to say, there is nothing more proper to God than being. Hence, God is pure Being, immutable and eternal and all divine attributes are deduced from ESSE. St. Thomas Aquinas will follow him in this point. Nevertheless, St. Hilary afterwards identifies being with the immutable and the unbegotten, as Plato had done centuries earlier, and this will influence St. Augustine.

St. Ambrose of Milan (333-397) studied Philo and Origen very amply, but he did not develop any metaphysical speculation about God. The little bit that he says is that God is He Who Is, that is, that He always exists. In this way, he unites the notion of being with that of eternity. Perhaps for this reason he affirms that the word which best suits God is OUSIA or ESSENTIA, which agrees with the Platonic doctrine, but also supposes identifying the notion of being with that of essence, thus "essentializing" the notion of being, which will also have much influence in later thought

With respect to cosmology, Platonic influence comes through authors who were not very original, such as the pagan Macrobius at the end of the 3rd century, a declared disciple of Plato and Plotinus. More useful was the translation into Latin of the Enneads of Plotinus by Marius Victor (+363), since that permitted the use of the Neo-Platonic notion of God, as the One previous to Being and substance, in order to combat some Arian objections.

### 6. Balance {#medieval-philosophy.1.6}
To end this very brief exposition of some of the philosophical elements of the Father, let us point out that the Fathers are fundamentally theologians, not philosophers. Necessity led them to make use of philosophy in an exclusively instrumental way, discriminating its truths and errors with respect to the Faith, so that it is dangerous and erroneous to forget that when the Fathers use certain philosophical terms in their works, they are replacing their original meaning with a new religious meaning, born under the light of the divine revelation which they are serving. What has been said up to now is the basis and the conditions in which the intellectual process and the great work of St. Augustine is developed.
