## VII. The Philosophy of the 13th Century {#medieval-philosophy.7}
### 1. The Reception of Aristotle {#medieval-philosophy.7.1}
About 1250, medieval philosophy reaches its greatest splendour. The most important cause is the arrival in the West of Arab and Jewish philosophy at an opportune moment, that is, when the state of thought was in condition to understand it and thus to assimilate it or reject it. In 1126 in Toledo the work of translating the works of the Arab philosophers began. With this impulse, the Neo-Platonic current of Arab philosophy finds ample echo in the traditional central European Neo-Platonic line, fed by Boëthius, PsD, John S.E. and the school of Chartres.

What the Christian thinkers will reject are those things involved in the Neo-Platonic concepts of creation, understood as a necessary and eternal emanation from God, and the structure of the universe as well as the separate Agent Intellect. The Christians will correct Arab philosophy in these points, but not without work and vacillation.

For example, Amalric of Bène (+1206) seems to have been influenced by the pantheist interpretation of creation, which underlies the emanationist explanation, and affirmed that God was everything, as we see in the Church's condemnation in 1210. Also David of Dinant, influenced by a false interpretation of Aristotle, falls into heterodoxy. According to the condemnation of 1210, it seems that he maintained that God is prime matter, that God and the human intellect are one and the same thing, and hence all things are essentially the same, a pantheist affirmation.

Together with the "Metaphysics", the "Physics" of Aristotle arrived in Europe about the middle of the 12th century, contributing a set of useful principles and concepts to explain in a unitary and systematic way the corporeal beings. It had a great success and a rapid expansion in such a way that it was believed to be the only physics possible, while in fact this physics was impregnating the Arab treatises on astronomy, physics, medicine, etc.

The somewhat indiscriminate assimilation of this Aristotelianism and the change of mentality it implied provoked the preventive prohibition of the study of Aristotle's "Physics" in Paris in 1210. Later on Gregory IX, though he renews the prohibition in 1231, names a committee to expunge the errors of the Philosopher, a task which had already been initiated, more or less spontaneously, by the principal masters of the age.

By way of summary, the philosophical tendencies of the 13th century can be grouped together, according to the attitude and degree of assimilation of Aristotle, in the following way:

a) Franciscan Augustinianism which, for fear of pantheism, admitted some Aristotelian notions to distinguish God from things.

b) The Dominican current, which assimilated Aristotle better, so that it could disqualify Averroist naturalism which claimed to be the authentic Aristotelianism.

c) Latin Averroism, which accepted Averroes' Aristotle, not the historical Aristotle, doesn't reconcile religion and philosophy.

d) The development of the natural sciences, mainly in Oxford, under Aristotelian and Arab influence.

### 2. The Universities {#medieval-philosophy.7.2}

The turn of the century brought with it an event of utmost importance for the cultural development of the 13th century: the foundation of the universities through the union in the same city of several schools. The first one was in Bologna, which was limited only to Law. The second and undoubtedly most important was that of Paris, where the masters and students of the cathedral schools came together to form a corporation recognized by Pope Innocent III as a university to which he granted statutes. From this moment, the Holy See will watch over the University of Paris, seeing that it could be teacher of truths or source of errors. In 1231 Pope Gregory IX recommended that the theologians should not boast about philosophers but should rather study human sciences only in so far as they can serve theology.

The University of Paris had four faculties: Church Law, Medicine, Liberal Arts, which will always tend to make itself the centre of purely scientific studies, taking no part in theology. Especially after the "arrival of Aristotle", its masters will not limit themselves only to dialectics, but will teach natural sciences. The fourth faculty is that of Theology, where Augustinianism predominates, with the instrumental aid of Aristotelian logic. After Paris in importance comes Oxford, which led a more autonomous life, since distance impeded the Popes from following its progress very closely. There the most traditional and Platonic Augustinianism predominated, and mathematics and positive sciences were cultivated.

In order to evaluate duly the philosophical and theological effort of this century, it must be noted that it is an eminently scholarly task, bound to the method of teaching. This consisted above all in the commentary of texts (LECTIO) of renowned authors like Aristotle or St. Augustine, and in the study of determined topics which, according to their importance, lasted 7 to 14 days (QUAESTIONES DISPUTATAE). At Easter or Christmas the monographic courses (QUAESTIONES QUODLIBETALES) were held, in which the great masters used to intervene. Finally there were three academic degrees: bachelor, license and Master (DOCTOR) and they were obtained in order to teach.

With regard to contents, the cathedral and monastery schools had revived, since Alcuin, the ancient patristic ideal of Christian "eloquence" as well as the study of classical Latin culture. Now the appearance of the universities coincides almost exactly with the "arrival of Aristotle" by many channels, so that little by little logic displaced the Latin culture of Cicero and St. Augustine and grammar was reduced to the indispensable, a school Latin to be able to read the Bible and the liturgical texts.

From the end of the 12th century, logic is converted into the new instrument of theology. On using the Aristotelian logic, the philosophy and metaphysics of Aristotle will be introduced. This is not something insignificant, for the vogue of logic transformed the ancient grammar, based on "authorities", into speculative grammar and thus into a study of the universal rules of language, rationally deduced.

### 3. The First Franciscan Masters in Paris {#medieval-philosophy.7.3}
In the intellectual atmosphere just described, the "scholastic" philosophy is formed, which will remain united to theology for many centuries. If Plato and Platonism had been gradually "Christianized", now it's Aristotle's turn. If the first work had been slow, the Christian reinterpretation, distinct from mere exegesis, was comparatively quite rapid, presenting as a peculiarity the great diversity of points of view about the same topic. All of this is an unmistakable symptom of great activity and intellectual initiative on the part of the professors of the universities and of the schools. In this context, it became difficult to fulfil exactly the indication of doing theology without any mixture of other sciences.

The first great figure of scholastic philosophy was Alexander of Hales (1170-1245) who settled down in Paris after finishing his studies in 1210. He became a Franciscan in 1231. Around that time the attitude of the Franciscans regarding studies began to change and the welcome accorded to Alexander in fact converted his chair into a centre of Franciscan studies. As professor, he shows confidence in Tradition, the Fathers and especially in St. Augustine. Also Boëthius, PsD, St. John Damascene and St. Anselm find a place in his "Summa Theologica." At the same time he opens the way for the assimilation of the new philosophy, though proposing numerous cautions, for he will always see Aristotle as a pagan and thus incapable of making authentic "philosophy" because he lacks the data of Christian Revelation.

John de la Rochelle (+1245), Alexander's successor in the chair of theology in Paris, is decidedly in favour of studies. He says it is diabolical to foster ignorance and those who attack philosophy must be considered as diabolical. The greater part of his writings is theological, though he wrote a work commonly accepted as a summary of Aristotle's philosophy of man, though he tries to harmonize it with the Augustinian viewpoint. In order to establish the distinction between God and creatures, Alexander of Hales had accepted the traditional formula of "quo est" and "quod est" and also Avicebron's doctrine of universal hylomorphic composition. But John de la Rochelle rejects this last doctrine, knowing that he is opposing his master, but he will not find Franciscan followers. On the contrary, his successors, Odo Reginaldus and St. Bonaventure return to Alexander's position. John holds that accepting universal hylomorphism means assuming the existence of spiritual matter which he says is not possible. In fact, intellectual substances cannot have matter, for the intellect knows by means of intelligible forms which it receives in the condition of being completely immaterial. On the other hand, the intellect is fully immaterial, since it can receive any form, both of material and of immaterial beings. Moreover, when we abandon quantity, all determination of matter disappears, and so it makes no sense to speak of spiritual matter. Thus there is no room to speak about the composition of matter and form in spiritual substances, but only of the difference between "quod est" (concrete individual or subject) and "quo est" (form), a distinction which can also be applied to corporeal beings. This thesis will be defended by St. Thomas.

### 4. St. Bonaventure (1221-1274) {#medieval-philosophy.7.4}

He studied in Paris under Alexander of Hales and taught until 1257, when he was named General of the Franciscan Order which he had joined around 1243. He was named Cardinal Bishop of Albano in May of 1273 and died a year later at the Second Council of Lyons. A man of intense and active life, he gave impulse and organization to the intellectual formation of the Franciscan Order. He wrote many works of philosophy, theology, ascetism and mysticism. His two greatest works "In IV Sententiarum" and "Itinerarium mentis in Deum" were written while he was Master in Paris.

a) The Spirit of St. Bonaventure's Philosophy

He was principally a soul of prayer, constantly seeking union with God. The knowledge that we have of God now is imperfect, but it is certain for it comes from faith, the starting point for the speculative force which the Christian needs if he wants to assimilate as much as possible the faith he possesses. In reality, life is a pilgrimage of man towards God, man raised to the supernatural order then fallen into sin. Thus, the effort is rendered more difficult by ignorance and concupiscence. In order to overcome these difficulties, struggle and divine grace are needed. Grace is conceded through prayer and supernatural means. The spirit of prayer guarantees the rectitude of the will and is the foundation of the clarity of the intellect, the two conditions needed to be introduced into the life toward God. The pilgrim who follows this way finds God in every act of knowledge. Truth is hidden in the interior of things, for all of them are like a book which speaks about God and leads to Him. Thus, theology and philosophy are like two guides along this path which, though distinct in their methods, complement and continue one another.

For his work, St. Bonaventure leans on the theological and philosophical tradition of St. Augustine and St. Anselm, who have ploughed a broad furrow and like them he does not reject what can be useful in the "new" philosophy which invaded Paris. He in fact accepts the metaphysical doctrines of act and potency, form and matter, substance and accident, abstraction, agent and possible intellect, etc. But he will keep a serious distrust for Aristotle whom he considers a pagan philosopher and who is of no use to him for finding the One and Triune God whom he loves, nor for demonstrating the non-eternity of the world.

In summary, the line of force of St. Bonaventure's philosophical system is the itinerary which leads to God. For this he gathers the materials which are useful to him, whether theological or philosophical. Though he distinguishes perfectly between one and the other (reason and faith) he is more concerned in showing that the man who must travel along this path is wounded by sin, and that only by ascetical exercise and the grace of God will he reach his goal.

b) Natural Theology

St. Bonaventure makes his own the favourite proof of St. Augustine for the demonstration of God's existence, going so far as to make it implicitly the conducting line of his reasonings. It is the argument based on truth and the existence of eternal truths. He proposes it in this way: in every proposition something is affirmed as true, even when one says "there are no truths", for this denial is affirmed as true. To affirm a truth implies the existence of the basis and the cause of the truth. Since the truth or the light which the intellect possesses is limited, it necessarily demands the existence of the Immutable Truth or Infinite Light, which is God, cause of all truth. The human intellect is certain of a truth when it is guided by the light which brings forth the truth itself. Hence to deny the existence of God supposes denying the source of light and of truth, precisely in the name of what comes from that source, which is absurd.

With these pretexts, it seems clear that the truth in things is only a representation of the divine Truth. Thus the first step of the itinerary towards God is to find the traces of the Creator in the sensible world, something which seems easy. But St. Bonaventure does not present any way as an argument, but rather tends to sketch a multitude of ways, since he thinks that they are not necessary when one has arrived to God along any of them. The second step consists in discovering the image of God infused in the human soul. In the corporeal world we see only reflections and shadows of God in things, but on looking at the soul we contemplate an image of God since the condition of the soul is nothing else. This presence of God in the soul makes His knowledge immediate. If the presence of God in the soul is the basis of the immediate knowledge of God, evidently the idea that we have of Him necessarily implies His existence.

Therefore, illumined by this ever-present idea, it is impossible for the soul to think that God doesn't exist.

Here St. Anselm's ontological argument crops up, which St Bonaventure accepts and formulates: "if God is God, God is." In another direction, he will reach the consequence that the idea of God is implied in every intellectual operation, not only from the point of view of the truth, but also of the contents of thought: it is possible to know the particular as something imperfect by the presence in us of the idea of the Perfect. But through all this knowledge man will never arrive at the full comprehension of the Divine Essence. The third step of the itinerary is the mystical knowledge of the divine presence, which is the concern of theology.

c) Psychology

Attracted by the Augustinian concept of man, an attempt to synthesize Aristotle and Plato, St. Bonaventure holds the soul to be a complete, simple, immortal substance created by God, form or act of the human body and present in the whole body. The intellect is not in the same situation when looking at bodies as when looking at the world of the soul and of God. In the first case it is "tabula rasa" and to know bodies it needs abstraction, an operation common to the agent and the possible intellect, by which it retains data of the sense images. In the second case, it isn't possible to know God or oneself by abstraction. The soul always is present to itself and God even more so and there is no sense image of either. Nor is there intuitive knowledge of either. St. Bonaventure uses a Platonic solution, his illumination doctrine.

d) Theory of Illumination

God is immutable and infallible, but man is not. Nevertheless, man is capable of possessing certain and firm knowledge. The only way to explain this fact is that God illuminates the intellect by means of His ideas. The action of God's Ideas consists in arousing some necessary truths, in relation to which we know, and in giving certainty to the intellect. These Divine ideas are never the object of knowledge but rather in them and through them we know how the things must be. For this reason, on earth our knowledge is never perfect. Man has, then, his own natural light in order to know what is particular in the sense world, but in order to know what is universal he needs an additional light, by means of which he can relate the particular being with its eternal and necessary essence. The imperfect is known through the notion of the perfect which is in us (the necessary truths or principles of philosophy), through the light due to the divine Ideas.

But one must not understand the Ideas as beings distinct from God, for that would be heresy. The divine Ideas are God, at once transcendent and present in man and in things. St. Bonaventure does not explain the nature of this new or added light, if natural or supernatural, only that it comes from God. Here his disciples will stumble and will neutralize his theory of illumination.

e) Creation: Exemplarism, Principle of Individuation and Seminal Reasons St. Bonaventure reacts strongly against the Arab philosophers' theory of necessary and eternal creation and comes face to face with its Aristotelian source. In many ways he argues that it is a contradiction to think of an eternal creation on the part of God, as it is impossible to go through an infinite series of beings. If the world had existed from all eternity, by now it would have reached an infinite duration, which is impossible, etc. St. Thomas will show that neither Aristotle's arguments nor those of St. Bonaventure are conclusive. Nothing can be proven metaphysically and only through faith can we know that the world has been created in time.

According to St. Bonaventure, to explain the fact of creation it is completely necessary to accept the doctrine of exemplarism as St. Augustine proposed it. God knows Himself perfectly and this act of knowledge is the image and expression of Himself, that is, the Word or Son, who shows all that the Father is and can do. Hence, the "Ideas" of all creatures, past, present and future, are contained in the Word, from genera and species (the universals) to singulars. This doesn't mean that in the Word there is an infinity of ideas, but only one, which is not distinguished from the Divine Essence. Nevertheless, reason can differentiate them, not really in God, but in the multiplicity of creatures by distinction of reason. God's creative efficacy causes the being of creatures in conformity with the Idea or exemplary ideas. There is a real distinction between God and creatures, in which it is necessary to distinguish the "quod est" and the "quo est", as Alexander of Hales teaches.

St. Bonaventure's explanations of this point don't let us decide whether he arrived at the distinction between "essentia" and "esse" or if he remained on the logical plane, like Boëthius. St. Bonaventure is enthusiastic about universal hylomorphism. Every creature is composed of matter and form. Matter is pure potency. The form determines whether a being is material or immaterial, and thus is established the existence of a corporeal or a spiritual matter. From this he deduces the immortality of the soul. Since the soul is a complete substance, it can subsist independently of the body. In fact the soul, though it has the aptitude and inclination to inform the body, has principally been created to possess God. Likewise, the angels are also composed of matter and form and hence can be individuals of the same species, like the souls. Since all creatures have the same metaphysical structure, the individuation of each one cannot come from matter or form, considered apart, but originates from the union of both.

On the other hand, the form is not unique in the Aristotelian sense, but each being has a plurality of forms in a perfect hierarchy, so that they constitute a unity. The form common to all beings is light, the first substantial form to which specific forms are added. Thus there is a scale of beings. Here we are fully in the Augustinian line and in that of Plato, who had compared the Good with the sun which spreads its rays of light and illuminates the things. God does not limit Himself to creating and conserving things, but He penetrates them interiorly in all their actions. The world and the corporeal beings are not outside of the divine terrain, and St. Bonaventure accepts the theory of the "seminal reasons" of St. Augustine, since it permits him to accentuate the work of the Creator and lessens the independence of natural beings. The matter of the universe contains the seminal reasons, virtual forms in latent state of everything which comes afterwards. Just as the seed can't modify its fruit, the efficacy of the second cause ends precisely in the effect foreseen by the Creator.

f) Balance of the Philosophy of St. Bonaventure

St. Bonaventure didn't fix any philosophical principles in order to elaborate a metaphysical system afterwards. He is oriented in another direction, with a peculiar spirit. First of all, he wants to gather the philosophical elements which are offered to him from different sources, especially from St. Augustine, either directly or under the interpretation of other authors, as for example the universal hylomorphism of Avicebron, or the plurality of forms of Avicenna, etc., a fact which St. Thomas will criticize.

Secondly, the plurality of sources must not disorient since the materials are grouped around the three central points of St. Bonaventure's metaphysics: creation, exemplarism and illumination. Creation explains the world as the work of the Creator beginning from nothing and depending entirely on God, exemplarism reveals the intimate union between God and creatures, as the model and the copy. Illumination deals with the threefold path of the soul to God. St. Bonaventure is a mystic. His philosophy is that of a theologian, with theological method and aim. This implies at times a confusion of planes, since he doesn't distinguish well between the natural and the supernatural. His work leaves behind a collection of Augustinian ideas which will be diffused by the Franciscan schools for a long time.

### 5. The Franciscan School {#medieval-philosophy.7.5}
Begun by Alexander of Hales and brought to its splendour by St. Bonaventure, it finds in Matthew of Aquasparta (1240-1302) a defender of such drive that he brings doctrinal positions to a limiting situation. His works are practically unpublished and thus aren't very well known. What we do have are some points of the doctrine of his master, St. Bonaventure. He rejects the real distinction between essence and esse, which seems to him a merely verbal distinction. In fact, the old Franciscan school thought always that existence adds nothing to essence, and that existence is nothing else but the essence placed in relation with its efficient cause, God. Hence Matthew of A. tenaciously holds the hylomorphic composition of the soul. He opposes the Thomist doctrine of causality with the Augustinian theory of seminal reasons.

His psychology is more radical. St. Bonaventure had admitted a natural knowledge, starting with experience and completed by abstraction, even though the intellect was assisted by an additional light. Nevertheless, for Matthew of A. all knowledge requires the light of the divine Ideas (immediate action of the Idea in the intellect), but this light is somehow superior to the simple natural influence of God. In these conditions, his version of the doctrine of illumination signifies that the formal cause of all human knowledge is a supernatural action or light which only theology can explain. Thus any frontier between natural and supernatural knowledge is erased. Therefore, Matthew of A. maintains that the object of intellectual knowledge is not the things just as they present themselves exteriorly, but rather the Ideas of the things in God.

Rather than making a contribution, Matthew of Aquasparta aggravated the situation. Therefore other Franciscans appear, like Peter Olivi (1248-1298), Peter of Traves, Richard of Middleton, who desire to be faithful to the traditional opinions of the Order and fall back to more moderate positions. Nevertheless, in some cases the revision will impose a renunciation of some point of Augustinian doctrine. The topic of seminal reasons will be modified as will the doctrine of illumination, with the aim of salvaging natural knowledge. Limiting themselves to this last point, some authors will expressly say that God is not the light of the intellect in all knowledge, but that the intellect has its own light in order to reach its object, and thus the way is open for other authors to make a psychological description of the process of knowledge.

There is also a broadening of intellectual perspective on the occasion of the controversies between Augustinians and Thomists, which in first instance can be codified in the condemnations of the Bishop of Paris, Stephen Tempier, in 1277. One of the reproved theses was directed against Greco-Arab necessitarianism and signified a revolution in the traditional cosmological conceptions. In fact, the condemnation was equivalent to acknowledging that the world in which we live isn't necessary nor is it the only one possible for God. In this line we find Richard of Middleton who dares give peculiar hypotheses about the world: that God can make the universe grow indefinitely, that a plurality of worlds is possible, that all the heavenly bodies can have a rectilinear motion, etc. The important thing here is that these ideas broke the unity of the Augustinian doctrines and the diversity increased in spite of the good intentions of their defenders.

Here we must also include two diverse figures such as Duns Scotus (1266-1308) whom we shall study more later on and Raymond Llull (1235-1315). This latter dedicated his life to the ransoming of Christian captives of the Arabs. His apostolic zeal led him to make many trips to North Africa to preach the faith. He posed the following problem: the Moslems deny Christian divine revelation and the Averroists reject it in the name of the supremacy of reason. In order to resolve this conflict between religion and philosophy it is necessary to start with concepts common to both Christians and Moslems and construct a system of possible combinations of these common concepts (Leibniz confessed that this was the first antecedent of one of his own philosophical ideas) and the relations of the doctrine of illumination. One of the conclusions is that if creatures are illuminations of God, the art of combining the perfections of creatures of all possible types will show us all possible combinations of divine perfections. Thus Llull repeats the idea that the one existing God has created or illuminated the one world which manifests him, a conclusion of his doctrine of illumination.

### 6. The Masters of Oxford {#medieval-philosophy.7.6}

The University of Paris becomes more and more dominant in the cultural atmosphere in France in the 12th and 13th centuries, imposing on it a method of work. Since Abelard, logic constitutes the basis and the technique of any philosophical and theological activity. However, the Neo-Platonic spirit of the school of Chartres is reborn in Oxford, permitting the assimilation of Arab science, charged with Platonism in philosophy and with Aristotelian elements in the natural sciences.

The first representative of this tendency is Robert Grosseteste (1175-1253). He knew Greek very well and he translated and commented several works of PsD., as well as several works of Aristotle, though he constantly disagreed with the latter. Under the influence of the Arab treatises on optics, he wrote "De luce" in which he develops a theory of light, and with the help of geometry, he tries to explain the structure of the universe. The world is the result of a point of light created by God out of nothing, in which the luminous form and matter are evidently unextended.

Roger Bacon (1210-1292), a commentator on the books of physics and metaphysics of Aristotle, went to Oxford in 1247 where he was a disciple of Robert Grosseteste and became interested in experimental studies of nature. He became a Franciscan in 1257, but he was neither understood nor tolerated by his companions. His astrological theories were considered as being included in the condemnations of 1277 of the see of Canterbury and he was imprisoned until shortly before his death.

Like all the Scholastics of his time, Bacon was first of all a theologian. For him the only perfect wisdom is theology and in order to explain it one needs philosophy and canon law. Like St. Bonaventure, he reduces all arts to theology. In his "Opus maius" he explains that philosophy is the fruit of divine illumination in man. Everything began with the Revelation of God to our first parents, but soon sin obscured this wisdom and human history is nothing other than the rediscovery and relosing of this wisdom. Here Roger Bacon feels that he is a prophet in a century of barbarism, for he thinks he knows the method of reconstructing that science whose first principles were contained in the primitive Revelation.

The first condition for restoring philosophy is to free it from the superstition of authority, of which something is admitted as true only because it is maintained, for example, by Alexander of Hales or by Albert the Great (expressly citing representatives of the Franciscan and Dominican schools). The only masters whom he admires are those, like Grosseteste, who have shown the necessity of mathematics and of experience. Bacon's affection for mathematics was great, up to the point of considering it necessary for every science, even theology. In part six of his "Opus maius" he explains his position: "there are two ways of knowing, by demonstration and by experience. The demonstration concludes and obliges to admit the conclusion, but it doesn't give us certainty or take away all doubt, in such a way that the soul rests in the truth. For this, experience is needed." Thus experience is what is most necessary because it can even reinforce mathematical evidence.

There are two classes of experience: internal, which leads us to the heights of the interior life and mysticism; and external, acquired through the senses. This latter is the origin of all scientific knowledge and is thus the basis of the perfect science, which Roger Bacon calls, for the first time in history, experimental science. Experience calls forth a complete certainty and it can also be continued at the point in which all the others come to a halt and allow us to discover the secrets of nature.

Robert Kilwardey, O.P. (+1279) is another student of Paris and professor of Oxford who became Archbishop of Canterbury in 1272. Concerned with the controversies about Aristotle he took advantage of the condemnations of Stephen Tempier - 7th March 1277 - to maintain the Augustinian positions. Thus on March 18th he published 16 propositions prohibited to be taught in the University. The doctrines prohibited imply the acceptance of seminal reasons in explaining the causality of creatures and also of the plurality of substantial forms.

His successor as Archbishop of Canterbury, John Peckham (+1292) renewed in 1284, though less zealously, the condemnations of 1277 and added a few more to them. Nevertheless, his concerns were of diverse nature. He was sorry to see that the opposition of doctrines led to opposition between Franciscans and Dominicans and he wanted to avoid that. He detected that the points of dispute were the doctrine of illumination, the seminal reasons and the unity of the potencies with the essence of the soul. In short, if one can prescind or not from God in order to know and to explain the things of nature. The attitude toward this problem defines the value which was granted to the doctrine of St. Augustine. It is not longer only a critico-philosophical problem, but rather it underlies a whole theological problem.

### 7. St. Albert the Great {#medieval-philosophy.7.7}

A German by birth, he studied arts in Padua where he became a Dominican at the age of 17. He obtained the doctorate in Paris where he taught theology. Among his students was St. Thomas Aquinas. He fixed his residence in Cologne in 1248, where he died. He enjoyed great prestige in the whole Western cultural world. He was praised even by a man so hard to please as Roger Bacon. St. Albert was an avid thinker in science, one who was well versed in the various schools of philosophy and who thought about bringing together the doctrines of Plato and Aristotle. His ample work can be considered as encyclopaedic. He wrote treatises on theology, philosophy, natural sciences, physics, biology, etc. He is not a simple compiler. Though he undertook to transmit the logic, metaphysics, physics and ethics of Aristotle and make them intelligible to the Latins, he also purified them as much as he could from the Arab interpretations, pointed out the errors of Aristotle and often commented on them, giving his own personal interpretation. He didn't systematize his immense work, but it supplies precious material in philosophy and theology to St. Thomas Aquinas, who uses it for his profound doctrinal synthesis.

a) Reason and Faith

St. Albert starts from two things, the data of faith and those of experience. Thus his attitude towards the sources of knowledge is very determined. He recognizes in St. Augustine an indisputable authority as theologian, but not in other fields, in which he asks for explanations. The principle "credo ut intelligam", which without doubt affirms a truth, contains a habitual confusion in his followers between believing and knowing. Nevertheless, "to know" is distinct from "to believe". Thus St. Albert succeeds in distinguishing between philosophy and theology. The latter is concerned with data of faith, of the indemonstrable mysteries such as the Trinity, etc., that is, of all that is known exclusively through Revelation. Philosophy is guided by the faculty granted to all men, which is reason. Under its light one can acquire truths from sense experience.

Thus the authority of a philosopher is based on the value of his demonstrations. Together with theology, in which the intellect is guided by Revelation, there is also a theological metaphysics which is concerned with God as First Being in the light of reason. It is not surprising then that St. Albert severely censured the detractors of the use of philosophy with expressions such as: "brute animals who blaspheme what they don't know." Let us observe that here we notice the spirit of Thomist philosophy, along with some fundamental theses: reason has its own sphere of operations; natural knowledge always begins with sense experience. It is St. Albert, and not Luther or Descartes who establishes the rights of reason back in the 13th century.

b) Psychology

In general, the doctrine of St. Albert is little studied, perhaps because its dispersion throughout his works makes it difficult to analyse its evolution. Nevertheless, his doctrine on the soul is an exception. In it we find an amalgam of Greek and Arab elements and an attempt to reconcile Plato and Aristotle. St. Albert thinks, like Aristotle, that the soul is form of the body, but this functional definition doesn't reflect its essence, so he prefers Avicenna's definition that the soul is an intellectual substance. As such, it knows universals, or ideas "post rem". The universals "ante rem" are the Ideas in the divine intellect or separate agent intellect, which is God. Divine illumination of creation radiates forms or ideas in matter which gives rise to the universals "in re", which are the causes of the being and the intelligibility of substances. Finally, when the soul frees the form from matter, it knows the universal "post rem", which evidently must coincide with the divine Ideas. Since the soul is a complete spiritual substance, it possesses in itself the principle of knowledge: the agent and the possible intellect.

In man, the light of his own agent intellect makes the forms of the sensible things intelligible. Thus, the process of knowledge can discover them. This process begins with the images coming from sense experience. The agent intellect illuminates them and abstracts the forms which the possible intellect will receive. This latter relates them with the first principles of natural reason in such a way that they give rise to, so to speak, a cultural patrimony which will allow one to judge other experiences and to formulate judgements. It becomes necessary to know and to study, for the soul, by means of knowledge, is more itself. In fact, through the agent intellect man is similar to God.

c) Disciples

The immense cultural contribution of St. Albert will be continued in two directions. On the one hand, by St. Thomas Aquinas, who will re-elaborate a good part of all this material, mainly his doctrine on knowledge. On the other hand, a set of disciples will perpetuate his doctrine without any special corrections, stressing perhaps the Neo-Platonic elements of Avicenna and Aristotelian concepts, used more as philosophical technique than as science. Through this set of disciples St. Albert becomes a link in the intellectual current which starts with PsD and comes to Meister Eckhart and Nicholas of Cusa.

### 8. Latin Averroism {#medieval-philosophy.7.8}
Under Latin Averroism we designate the assimilation of the "new Aristotle" by the Faculty of Arts of the U. of Paris in the 13th century. We shall make more precise the meaning of "new". It is not the use of logic introduced already by Abelard which we find throughout the 13th century, nor is it the better knowledge of Aristotle's doctrines and their assimilation, in different ways, in the theological syntheses of the great masters of the Faculty of Theology of Paris, like St. Thomas and St. Bonaventure. It is the "Averroism" which arises in the Faculty of Arts when some masters abandon the habitual commentaries of Aristotle's logic and begin studying the scientific works with a "new" spirit. They conceive philosophy as an end in itself, outside of the synthesis of theology and philosophy into which it must enter. They also understand the doctrine of Aristotle as that which Averroes attributes to him (the "new Aristotle") and that this doctrine is identified with the full philosophical truth. When such an attitude took shape, a rash of doctrines charged with pagan naturalism broke out in the University, which provoked numerous violent discussions up to the point that the Church authorities had to intervene.

In 1256 the Pope asked Albert the Great to refute the error of Averroes that there is only one agent intellect for all men. In 1270, Stephen Tempier, Bishop of Paris, condemned 15 theses, 13 of which were Averroist. Those condemned excused themselves on the basis of the theory of the double truth: as theologians they denied what they affirmed as philosophers. Consequently, early in 1277 Pope John XXII issued a very severe bull admonishing the masters of the Faculty of Arts of the U. of Paris. Stephen Tempier took advantage of this on 7th March of the same year to extend his condemnations of 1270 to 219 theses, including many of those previously condemned, but now going more to the root. Some of the theses condemned were later on accepted, after a further study had been made, such as St. Thomas' doctrine of the unicity of the substantial form. Since the discussions were becoming more and more bitter and professors were anathematizing one another, the Roman Curia ordered the suppression of the excommunications attached to Tempier's 1277 condemnations, for the only thing of interest was safeguarding the faith. A more precise study of the 219 theses and their exact meaning was undertaken.

One of the most characteristic exponents of Averroism was Siger of Brabant (1235?-1282), a restless and very talented cleric who headed the rebel movement against the Rector of the U. of Paris, Albaric of Rheims. Obliged to give up teaching in 1277, Siger died in Italy, leaving behind a trail of rationalist shading that remained in Italy until the 16th century. Siger wanted to base his teaching on Aristotle and thought during his lifetime that he was doing that. Nevertheless, rather than reproducing Aristotle’s doctrines, he was taking Averroes' version of the Philosopher’s teaching. To hear Aristotle, he said, is to hear the voice of science and of reason. Of course, there is another absolute and true science, which is Revelation and therefore, when there is a conflict between them, one must acknowledge the supremacy of faith, but maintaining the other doctrine on the philosophical plane (the theory of the double truth).

One example of this is Averroes' doctrine of the unicity of the intellect and of the soul, which one must accept while at the same time accepting theologically that each person has an individual soul. Siger had many other such teachings. His doctrine was strongly attacked by St. Thomas Aquinas, up to the point that it is one of the few occasions in which he manifests great vehemence in his refutal of error, as opposed to his habitual serenity. See, for example, his work "De unitate intellectus", chapter 5.
