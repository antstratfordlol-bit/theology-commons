CIRCA THE MIDDLE OF THE XIV CENTURY.
THEODORUS MELITENIOTA
NOTICE
(Leo Allatius, *De Theodoris*, in Mai, *Biblioth. nov. PP.*, vol. VI, p. CXXXIII.)
The Greek exposition of the Gospels by Theodorus Meliteniota exists at Rome in the Vatican Library, Gesnerus. Theodorus Meliteniotes has interpreted the Gospels in Greek; it exists as a manuscript in the Vatican library, Possevinus. I myself read the fourth book of his commentaries on these Gospels, the title of which was as follows: *Of the Great Sacellarius of the most holy Great Church of God, and master of masters, and archidiaconus Theodorus Meliteniota, commentary on the triple triad of the Gospel, which was made one out of four, book four.* His introduction is this: God who is in three persons and omnipotent, being invoked, receives [the work], and with the first book and the second, the third also of the present compilation, in which said compilation this is the goal for me, the Great Sacellarius of the most holy Great Church of God, and master of masters, and archidiaconus Theodorus Meliteniota, to interpret, as much as possible, every part of the holy Gospel book of the divine Luke, and to append to this [the] commentary, according to the time befitting the discourse, and those things which, having been omitted by [me] concerning other evangelists, were stated, to one who has made—as it were—one written work out of the [Gospel] holy by four, and has demonstrated the harmony of the divine evangelists in all things, and at the same time to divide the present treatise into a triple triad of books, and each of these again into a triple triad of discourses, and thus in some way to entitle the entire written work: *On the triple triad, expositions of the holy Gospel by the four.* The fourth book of the present compilation, taking its beginning, has the things that follow from the steps of the holy Matthew set forth according to the first
discourse of his, proceeding in this manner. By the help of God, who is one in three persons and also omnipotent, the third book of these commentaries reaches its conclusion, in which it is my purpose, as great sacellarius of the most holy Great Church of God, master of masters, and archdeacon Theodore Meliteniota, to expound the entire most holy Gospel of the divine Luke, as I am able, and to add to the exposition—at an appropriate time, however—the things that were spoken by the other evangelists and omitted by him, just as in a single composition of the Gospel, which is made from four, and the harmony of the divine evangelists demonstrated in all things. It is also my intention to divide the present work into a triad of three books, and again to divide each of the books into a triad of three discussions, and to set this title over the whole work: "A triad of three narrations of the holy Gospel, which has been made one from four." The fourth book of the work indeed begins from those things which follow from the words of Matthew in his first discussion in this manner: "Meliteniota therefore made one out of four Gospels, and expounded them in nine books, of which each was completed by nine discussions." It is a work that I myself have seen, learned, filled with divine teaching, and worthy to see the light of day. May God grant that the remaining eight books may not perish, to the grave detriment of the Christian republic! In the same library are held his *Astrologoumena*, or *Astrological* works. But at the royal palace in the city, there is his *Prooemium to Astronomy, from the first of the astronomical books. What is astronomy, and by whom discovered did it come to the Greeks?* And the proem to the first astronomical book on astronomy. What is astronomy, and from whom did it come to the Greeks? Nearby there is also his *First of the Three Astronomical Books, or Introduction to the Arithmetic*, the first of the three astronomical books, or introduction to the method of calculating. [P.] "Let God be the guide of every work and word," says one of the wise men of old. "Second, or interpretation and harmony of the arithmetic," the second, or interpretation and harmony of the calculation. [P.] "With God helping, who has made all this in wisdom, including man." "Third, or the arrangement of the arithmetic according to the Persians," the third, or description of the methods of calculation according to the Persians. [P.] "Of the blessed, omnipotent, holy, and life-originating Trinity." The same author pursues the argument of these books in the introduction to the third book in this manner: "Having set forth in the first of the three astronomical books the introduction to the arithmetic; and in the second having traversed, as far as possible with accuracy, the calculations of the mathematical and great composition, and having set forth along with them those that are ready at hand by way of examples, and having accurately demonstrated the harmony of these; here in the third, that is, the remaining one of those propounded, we have been led to rearrange the calculations of the Persian ready-at-hand canons, which have already been studied by others, yet which were in need of greater clarity, and especially because they were not edited according to their proper interpretation among the Greeks, which we shall attempt to set forth as clearly as we are able, and also we shall append the titles of the canons in accordance with our own." When we have described the introduction to the method of calculating in the first of the three astronomical books; and in the second have accurately digested, as we were able, the methods of calculating the great mathematical construction, together with those canons that are ready at hand disclosed by examples, and their harmony exquisitely shown; here in the third, which is the remaining one in this treatise, we are led to digest the calculations of the Persian easy-to-use canons, which were formerly worked out by others but are in need of greater explanation; and this especially because they were not translated into the Greek language as they should have been, which we shall strive to explain clearly to the best of our ability, and we shall also write out the descriptions of the canons, following our own.
There were not lacking those who would rail against him and speak ill of the work, to whom whosoever he was responded in the very prospect of the work:
"This man, who has written the present book, appears to me to yield to none of those renowned for wisdom in the mathematical type of philosophy, if I am at all able to judge such things. One ought therefore to use what is said by him without concern, accounting those who attempt to disparage him of no importance. For there are some few, indeed, of those of my time who, through perversity of mind, have dared to do even this." This man, who composed this book, seems to me, among those who have attained any name in wisdom, to be second to none in that part of philosophy which treats of mathematics, if I am considered a fit judge to pass judgment upon such writings. Therefore, one must assent to his words without examination, neglecting those who sharpen their teeth against him. For there are some few, though very few, who, having injured minds, have shamelessly dared to do just this. He was writing in the year of Christ 1361, under John Palaiologos. There are also read other treatises of his, *On the Conjunctions and Full Moons*, and others absolutely countless in astronomy.
