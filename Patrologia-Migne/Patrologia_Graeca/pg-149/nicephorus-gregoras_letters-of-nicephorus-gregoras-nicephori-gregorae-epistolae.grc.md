LETTERS OF NICEPHORUS GREGORAS.
I. To the Prefect of the Table.
[From the Manuscript of the Electoral Library of Munich, edited and illustrated with annotations and Latin version by Fr. Xav. Berger in *Beiträge zur Geschichte und Literatur*... June 1805.]
You indeed, best of men, persist in raising our speeches with great encomiums to the height of the ether, so to speak; I, for my part, not having the means to compensate for your disposition [Greek equivalent: I, for my part, not having the means to repay your disposition according to its worth, extend to you long thanks from my tongue. But I wish...]
also, by the very words which you approved of so highly, to provide an occasion to disclose to you a small part of my soul's mysteries, so that you might know clearly how, in fact, I was affected when I first saw you—if indeed the soul is a divine thing and its gaze extends with precision—and how subsequently time has confirmed my opinion of you; and why I prefer the praises that have come from you to the riches of Croesus and to all the things with which the majority of people shower me today.
There was once a wise man in Phrygia named Aesop, not by the wisdom of Pythagoras and Plato, nor by that of Empedocles of Acragas, nor yet of those others whose memory fleeting time has not been able to destroy; but the wisdom of Aesop the Phrygian had this particular quality, that he was self-taught and wise by nature, not through the company of wise men, nor through the constraints of art—such as the books of earlier times hand down to the ears of those who come later—for he strengthened his own natural gifts, whereas it has been the lot of those who later became objects of admiration for their wisdom to derive their greatness in different ways, some by associating with the wise men in Egypt, others by poring over their books, or [by associating with the] Stoa or the Academy.
