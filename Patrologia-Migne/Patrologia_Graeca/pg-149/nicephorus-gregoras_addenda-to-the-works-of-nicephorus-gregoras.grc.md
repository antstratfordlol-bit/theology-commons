ADDENDA TO THE WORKS OF NICEPHORUS GREGORAS
OF THE MOST WISE AND LEARNED LORD NICEPHORUS GREGORAS: AN ADDRESS TO THE EMPEROR
How pleasant and radiant is your return to us from your victories, most divine Emperor, and today more than before, and you more than all the emperors who preceded you, toward those who were previously your subjects. For what those others had the misfortune to lose, and what they had not the strength to recover, this you have taken back with much greater power and have restored to the broken chain of the Roman hegemony. Whence you are fair, most philanthropic Emperor, and fair are your feet, bringing us, your subjects, as they do, many good things, partly bringing them to completion, and partly announcing them as good news. Now the sun seems to us to shine more radiantly, and the air bestows a more cheerful countenance; now all things that were contrary to our joy have been turned around, and better things flourish while worse things wither away. And just as after winter, when spring has arrived, every bird that journeys through the air makes the groves and meadows ring, singing hymns to the giver of spring, so too now, besides other things, the Muses themselves, having put aside their silence, sing joyful hymns to you, the provider of...
ALLOCUTION OF THE MOST WISE AND MOST LEARNED LORD NICEPHORUS GREGORAS TO THE EMPEROR
With how joyful and hilarious a countenance you return to us as victor, most divine Emperor, and now more than before, and even more gloriously than your predecessors. For if they were unable to recover what they had lost, you, with the enhancement of the empire's forces, have received them back after the breaking of the Roman servitude. Hence you yourself are beautiful, and your feet are beautiful, which bring or evangelize such perfect things to us, your citizens. Today for us the sun shines more brightly, today the air blows more pleasantly upon us; today those things which seemed to hinder our joy have fled; now all things are proceeding for the better, and those things which are evil are passing away, and just as after winter, when spring arrives, the woods and meadows resound with the song of birds, so now the Muses, having laid aside their silence, sing back to you, the leader of the Muses, joyful hymns; the fields themselves rejoice, clothed in the beautiful garment of gladness, wearing a city-like face... For you...
are indeed the life-giving heart of affairs, the wall, the foundation, and the wealth of the cities; and wherever you may be, they look to you just as the growths of the earth look toward the sun. But the wise men and the Hellenic accounts say, among other things, that Heracles, having become a great benefactor of countless men and cities, filled Greece and the cities and lands beyond Greece that required his aid with his own great glory; for by driving forward, he would cleanse whatever diseases and outrages of that land were produced by wild beasts and the footsteps of robbers. But those were stories, after all, of myths striving for autonomy, which mostly abound in wealth and easily beguile the hearing; whereas now Greece has seen another Heracles in you, cleansing the long-standing diseases and outrages of that land—not waiting for summer or spring for the need of arms, but innovating the natures of the seasons and the elements, deeming the cold of winter to be summer, fasting to be luxury, wakefulness to be sleep, and the passing of the night under the open sky to be royal rest. You prefer common utility over your own comforts; to laborious courses you add new contests and toils, sparing neither time nor circumstances, and stirring up both maritime and land battles. And of such contests, some admire their multitude, others their continuity, and some the swiftness of your courses, others the sharpness and effectiveness of your mind, and how you follow the ends of the struggles with the additions of your initiatives; for you surpass by the speed of your running your own message, and before you are heard, you appear present, and by your sudden arrival you shatter the schemes of the enemies. In this way you struck with terror the leaders of the Triballians and Mysians, appearing often to them while they slept, unexpected like some heavenly thunderbolt; thus you captured Chios, thus you brought Phocaea into subjection, thus you overran the Illyrians, thus you have now led the Aetolians and Acarnanians back to the ancient form of Roman rule.
