A 1. If there is any other good thing of great use in life, history is no less beneficial than it, or indeed even more so, being a truly useful and salutary thing. For since it is a messenger of various deeds of every kind—whatever the course of time and the nature of events tend to bring about, and especially the will of men engaged in affairs—it teaches men to grasp and pursue some things, while rejecting and spurning others; so that they do not, through ignorance, thoughtlessly neglect useful and superior things, and entangle themselves in things that are to be avoided and harmful. Therefore, since it is acknowledged that history is a fruitful thing among the other safeguards of life, and that it restores life or youth to mortal affairs, and does not permit them to be snatched away and overwhelmed in the whirlpools of oblivion: for this reason, because many monstrous and truly wondrous things have occurred within my own memory, and because I deemed it worth the effort that these things—both the many that have occurred and especially those that happened in my own age—should be recorded, lest oblivion erase them and almost completely abolish them.
Wonderful portents and religious omens appeared in the heavens, and earthquakes occurred that were past belief; lightning bolts were cast down, and heavy rains descended; battles were fought, and armies were moved about here and there across the world; towns and regions were displaced, so that it seemed to many that the regeneration of mankind was immediately impending, and that the second coming of the Savior and God was before our very doors. It seemed to me, therefore, not right to pass over in silence matters so full of horror and worthy of wonder, but to proclaim them openly, so that they may serve as a lesson for those who come after—if, indeed, it has not been determined by Providence to bring the small boat of human life, even now and immediately, into the harbor of the consummation and to alter the fashion of this world. But may it be granted to me, since I have undertaken a task so far beyond my powers, not to fall short of my longing, but on the contrary to rise to the height of the events that have taken place, and to relate them in the manner that is fitting. But let us now come to the recounting of public affairs, so that we may prize truth most highly, since it is that which pertains especially to history. For those skilled in eloquence maintain that to rhetoric belongs the faculty of speech, to poetry the invention of fables, and to history the truth. Therefore, whatever occurred during the time of the Emperor Constantine, the son of Leo, who was surnamed Porphyrogennetos (at whose birth and death they say a comet appeared in the heavens, foretelling his birth and his departure from life), I think should be omitted: for they have been related sufficiently and copiously by others. He was otherwise a good man, of honest countenance, of charming speech, and handsome appearance, full of every probity, and kind and gentle toward all his subjects; yet he was also too much given to youthful pleasures and pastimes. Those who cultivated these things for him, he brought into the palace, contrary to what was appropriate. It had occurred to this Emperor Romanus to overthrow, with the help of God, the power of the Cretan Arabs, which was haughty and breathed slaughter against the Romans. For, having been puffed up beyond measure by the defeat recently inflicted upon us, they were making great depredations along the maritime border of the empire. I shall now tell how the Romans suffered this calamity. Emperor Constantine, being unable to bear the arrogance and sudden incursions of the Cretans, having hired an excellent army and equipped many triremes with fire-bearing devices, undertook an expedition against Crete, intending to occupy the island in a first assault. But because of the cowardice and inexperience of the leader—who was a chamberlain eunuch, a man who lived in the shadow, sprung from Paphlagonia, Constantine by name and Gongyles by surname—although he gloried in the highest dignity of the patricians, all this army, except for a few men, was slaughtered by the barbarians and perished.
