Who Suidas may have been, or when he lived, he who inquires in vain, if no Suidas ever existed: as many writers report that the most refined man, Angelus Politianus, believed; A looking back without a doubt to these words of his, which are read in bk. XI, epist. 20 (1). "Suidas himself (if indeed this Suidas ever existed), is not an altogether bad author." But that Suidas truly existed, and that his name is not fictitious, is proven not only by the manuscripts of this Lexicon, in which the name of Suidas, or Sudas (2), appears; but also by Eustathius, who in his verbose commentary on Homer cites our Suidas several times (3). Who Suidas may have been (4), however, is difficult to say; since not only other writers, but he himself is silent about himself, and he has not revealed it in this notorious Lexicon. It seems indeed in the entry *Samothrace* to indicate his country or race, when he says: « For in the beginning that island » (that is, Samothrace) « was inhabited by Samians, from whom it receives its name. » But those words are not Suidas's own, but of an anonymous writer, from whom this passage is excerpted.
2. As regards the age of Suidas, the opinions of learned men are various, which we will recall to examination. Hugo Grotius, in ep. 110 to the French (5), thinks that Suidas lived a little after Constantine, the son of Leo (6), who began to rule in the year of Christ 912; with this added reasoning, that insofar as he himself remembers, he is the last of the emperors.
CONCERNING THE LEXICON OF SUIDAS
Although we posit that Suidas is more ancient than is commonly believed, we nevertheless do not, with Lilius Gregorius Gyraldus, deem him so ancient that Strabo, who is known to have flourished under Augustus, could have known of him. The most learned man, who was lately praised, evidently supposes that our Suidas is the same as the ancient historian Suidas, of whom, besides Strabo, the scholiast of Apollonius of Rhodes and Stephanus Byzantinus make mention. His words in Dialogue III, "On the history of poets," are these: "To which this may truthfully be replied, that Suidas was an ancient writer, of whom mention is made primarily by Strabo, and by those who wrote commentaries on Apollonius of Rhodes, and by Stephanus, and others. This Suidas, therefore, among other things (for he also wrote a History), composed commentaries, as is reasonable to believe, not very large, which were later much enriched and augmented by others, to this magnitude which we see, and first obtained the name of Suidas. And a little later: "At the same time, we must also discard the distinction which some grammarians make, as if that [ancient] one were a historian, and this one a more recent grammarian." By these words he contains a twofold opinion; namely, that Suidas first composed a lexicon, which was later enriched and augmented by others; and furthermore, that Suidas was both a historian and a lexicographer. We approve of the former opinion, and many others agree with us. The latter lacks supporters, and our Suidas is rightly considered by the common consensus of the learned to be different from the ancient historian, and consequently more recent. And thus far on the age of Suidas.

5. Otherwise, of the ancient scholiasts, he has compiled none more diligently than the scholiast of Aristophanes; for he has poured almost the whole of him into his Lexicon. Yet it is asked among the learned whether Suidas borrowed from the modern scholiast of Aristophanes, or rather the scholiast from Suidas; or whether the modern scholia on Aristophanes were propagated to us from some ancient codex, or were refined by Musurus or someone else from Suidas’ Lexicon. We believe that the modern scholia on Aristophanes have reached us for the most part transcribed from some ancient codex, and that the same were nevertheless interspersed with some passages by later writers, partly from Suidas, partly from other writers. Indeed, that the things which are read in that scholiast on the *Equites*, p. 323: "The strigil, of things towards another, etc.," and likewise on the *Acharnians*, p. 396: "Sicyon, from the nominative, etc.," were inserted from Suidas (in v. Στλεγγίς and v. Σίκυον), readers of refined taste can easily perceive. For we could dismiss those things from the lexicographer without great loss to literature. But he had the books of Polybius, which now have perished for us. Whether he derived the lost books of Dio Cassius from elsewhere, I would not dare to define. He certainly had a few things of others; and the minor works of Aelian, Iamblichus, Eunapius, and Damascius, of which we have hitherto been deprived; not indeed that they are of such great moment, if anyone should so wish; yet from these unedited books, lovers of ancient literature eagerly excerpt the things for which Suidas had a craving.
6. As far as the mode is concerned, these collections were digested; in which one might not rarely desire the judgment and diligence of the compiler. For in the first place, our author, when he gathered into his Lexicon passages excerpted from various writers, was often misled by faulty codices, whence it happens that he sometimes palms off on the reader adulterated and corrupt words instead of true and genuine ones. Thus you have *Amastes* in him for *Akmaes*, *Erithmesen* for *Eremesen*, *Pepiasmenois* for *Memiasmenois*, *Talesin* for *Telesin*, *Istinos* for *Ysqinos*, *Koilidian* for *Kyloidein*, *Phremas* for *Di’ eremas*, *Deixantes* for *Diexantes*, *Alibyes* for... Sometimes, however, the reading of Suidas is better than that which our present-day codices exhibit. For example, in Aristophanes, *Thesmophoriazusae*, p. 811, it is read today: *Tenioisi kai Skirois*: where the codex of Suidas formerly had *Stenioisi* for *Tenioisi*; which reading we prove to be true in the notes on the word *Stênia*. For the conjecture of Bisetus, who thought that *threisioisi* should be substituted there, is deservedly to be rejected. In the same comic poet, in the *Thesmophoriazusae*, p. 777, the verse is read here:
*Tas symphoras gar ouchi tois stenasmasi*.
The last word there is corrupt; in its place Suidas, in his own series, correctly has *technasmasi*. In the play *Ecclesiazusae*, this verse occurs:
*He graus anestekuia para ton nekron*.
The meter is obviously limping; which you may restore in its entirety from Suidas in v. *pleionon* thus:
*He graus anestekuia para ton pleionon*.
For a scholium has today wrongly occupied the place of the genuine word in the comic poet. In the play *Thesmophoriazusae*, p. 794, among other things, these are also read:
*Leon, leon soi gegonen, aut' aigma syn*.
Great Scaliger, when he saw that the measure did not fit this verse, thought it should be rewritten thus:
*Leon, leon soi gegonen, aut' eigmi son*.
But in vain. For we prove in the notes on that passage that from Suidas, in v. *Leon*, *exagma* should be substituted for *aigma*. In the *Anthologia*, lib. III, cap. 14, p. 343, an epigram on Protesilaus is read, a part of which is this hexameter:
*Dendrea dysmimêta, kai ên pote teichos idôsi*.
As for this *dysmimêta*, it is manifest that it is adverse to the sense; in its place Brodaeus thought *dysmenêta* should be substituted; and Obsopoeus, *dysmeilikta*. But both wandered from the mark. For we note in our remarks on that passage that in Suidas, under v. *dysmimêta*, it should be *dysmeleta*. Among other things, these are also read in Josephus, lib. 1 *De bello Jud.*, p. 739: *hê oikia hoplitôn ektato*. The last word there lies in error; in its place, from Suidas v. *Nênakto*, it should be *nenakto*; the sense manifestly urges this. The diligent reader will be able to collect more examples of this kind from our notes. For we have proposed these things here only as specimens.
Furthermore, it must be observed that Suidas is accustomed to recount the names of men in a continuous series, and sometimes to refer them to one head: from which it happens that matters pertaining to different people appear to be attributed to one, and everything is thrown into confusion in the histories, unless you distinguish the narratives and carefully note to whom each entry is to be referred. You have examples in v. *Eumenes*, v. *Heron*, v. *Severos*, and elsewhere.
7. Sometimes, however (which is a greater sin), our grammarian mixes up the affairs of men who are entirely different, and who do not even have a name in common, and refers historical excerpts to an entirely different head; of whose *ablepsias* (blindness) I shall produce a few examples here. In v. *Maximos*, what is read from the beginning up to the words *epibouleuôn* is to be understood regarding the emperor Maximinus Thrax. But those things which follow thereafter, without any note of distinction, are subject to Galerius Maximianus, as is apparent from Euseb. *Hist. Eccles.*, lib. VIII, cap. 14 and 16. In v. *Alyattês*, the words *tôn Lydôn basileus* — *adelphên êschynen*, cited from Niccolò Damascenus in *Collectaneis Constantini* (edited by Valesius, p. 453), whence that passage is taken, are said of Sadyattes, the father of Alyattes: our author wrongly refers them to Alyattes. 10. The *Collectanea* of Suidas appeared first at Milan (29), A.D. 1499, in Greek only; in which edition some places are missing. That book is, indeed, very rare, and much is to be attributed to it. Certainly, very many excellent things, worthy of being known, would be hidden from us in the same darknesses of antiquity as countless other things, were we to lack this work of Suidas.
The first edition, which appeared at Milan (not Rome, as Michael Neander, in the *Notitia auctorum* prefixed to the Gnomology of Stobaeus, p. 68, opined after Gesner), on large and clean paper, with elegant type, is owed to Demetrius Chalcondyles, a most learned Greek, and to Joannes Maria Catanæus, who prefixed the Latin preface—the teacher—and to Joannes Bisolus and Benedictus Mangius of Carpi, who provided the labor of the press and the expenses along with him. This is the Catanæan Suidas, which is mentioned in the secret epistles of C.S. Schurtzfleisch, p. 5. Chalcondyles, in the Greek preface he prefixed, testifies that he used several manuscript codices, and in correcting the work, spared no effort: however, when he could not correct some things, he left them as he found them, and some he added, having taken them from elsewhere.
"The one who has performed the correction of this book is Demetrius Chalcondyles, who, having made use of more than one copy, and finding in all of them no small number of errors—the majority of which were the same in all—labored much and, having arranged them, corrected them. There were some, however, which he was not able to correct with the proper emendation, but he was careful to preserve the interpretation of the word; the cited passage itself, however, which was introduced for the sake of clarity but was corrupt, he left as it was. Yet he also added some words that were altogether absent from Suidas, having collected them from elsewhere, so that those seeking their meaning might have them more easily and readily available from this source, and he also made it his concern to explain some words that were uninterpreted where it was possible. He adds furthermore that the book of Suidas was once so rare and precious in Greece and Italy that he who possessed it would boast of it as if it were a notable treasury and the horn of Amalthea that he possessed."
