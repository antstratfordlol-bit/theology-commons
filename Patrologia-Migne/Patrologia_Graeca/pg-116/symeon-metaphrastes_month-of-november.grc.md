Cum autem et id, quod laudatur, fuerit mulier, id scilicet, quod est humanæ naturæ maxime imbecillum, et ad asperos suscipiendos labores mollissimum, quemadmodum non est laus conducibilis viris simul et feminis, quæ potest utrosque excitare ad ea quæ sunt pulchra et honesta? Feminas quidem, ut, cum sint ejusdem generis, eosdem quoque labores suscipiant et easdem remunerationes; viros autem, ne, cum sint viri, cernantur cedere mulieribus, et ad labores suscipiendos eis esse ignaviores. Ex iis ergo quæ vitam laudabiliter egerunt et ex virtute, fuit etiam Matrona, quæ cum viris quidem virtute contendit, præclaris vero et admirandis factis eos superavit, ut in processu ostendet ea quæ dicentur.
II. Est quædam regio subjecta imperio Romanorum, sita in confinio Cilicum et Isaurorum, nomine Pamphylia, quæ propterea quod sit populo frequens, et habeat habitatores ex omnibus civitatibus, sic fuit appellata. Una autem ex iis quæ sunt in ea, civitatibus vocatur Perge, in qua fuit educata hæc beata Matrona, quæ a parentibus adepta liberalem disciplinam et educationem, cum jam esset nubilis (erat enim insigni pulchritudine), jungitur matrimonio marito non vulgari, non plebeio, nomine Domitiano, fitque mater unius filiæ, cui statim ex futuris tribuit appellationem, ut quæ eam nominarit Theodoten, Deo autem eam dederit ex ipsis fasciis. Atque ut post nuptias quidem et cohabitationem, moderationem exerceret, et a fastu ac superbia alienationem, et ornaretur non ornatu, qui consistit in ficta et fucata pulchritudine, sed in interno animæ homine, adhibebat studium, et ut in temperantia vitam degeret et honestate.
III. Laudabile quidem, et alioqui gratum est hæc persequi. Supervacaneum autem visum omnino fuerit, si præteritis majoribus et perfectioribus, voluerimus occupato tenere auditores in horum recordatione. Quod autem ipsa aliis prætulit, hoc etiam convenit præponere in narratione. Sta- tuebat enim id, quod dicit Apostolus, a se summe esse servandum. Ille enim dicit, oportere eos qui habent uxores, affici perinde ac si non habeant. Quamobrem cum sua sponte excessisset ab ea quæ illam tulit patria, divina, inquam, providentia, quam ejus consilium habuit adjuvantem, in eam quæ primam sedem obtinet inter civitates, nempe Byzantium, cum marito proficiscitur: quem habebat quidem simul peregrinantem, et ne minimum quidem recedentem, non tamen eorum conscium, quæ animo agitabat, neque scientem aliquid, quod ea mente versabat. Deinde urbem ingressam regiam, toto die assidebat in templis sanctorum, nec diu nec noctu ab eis recedens, jejuniæ et precum curam gerens et facultates in-

IV. Rogabat ergo, sicut diximus, ut liberaretur a carnis molestiis, ut, cum posset vivere sine maritio, libere vacaret spiritualibus et divinioribus. Quæ autem eam ad ea, quæ sunt honesta, incitabat, erat quædam Eugenia, quæ convenienter nomini erat moribus ingenuis, et una, quæ totam vitam consumebat in nocturnis stationibus et orationibus. Cum qua ipsa quoque bona decertabat contentione exercitationis, ea quibus exercentur monachi, diligenter exercens. Hæc vero, quæ quotidie fiebant, ægre ferebat Domitianus, et absurdæ  quædam et turpes cogitationes ei veniebant in mentem, dicendo : Non recte habet continus hic progressus, sed fit animo, qui ad malas tendit cupiditates, et exhinc omnem adhibebat custodiam, ne eam quidem sinens prodire extra domunculam, et ambobus erat constitutum certamen : illi quidem, ne dimitteret eam facere ea quæ consueverat ; huic autem, ne abesset a sacris cœlibus. Sed cum is valde repugnaret, ea multis urgens precibus, vix tandem persuadet ut sibi liceat facere quod vellet.
V. Magno itaque studio venit in templum sacrorum Apostolorum. In quo cum fuisset, eos precans intensius, petebat ut sibi nihil esset impedimento, quo minus fugeret mundum, sed eam quoque Christo offerrent cum eo cœtu, qui jam ab eis prius ei fuit oblatus. Cum ea sic esset precata, et jam adventasset vespera, et qui id templum custodiebant juberent eam exire, ipsa cum in quodam porticu ex iis qui illic erant, Sosannæ cujusdam, ei jam olim familiaris, invenisset habitaculum, quæ ab ineunte ætate virginitatem conservare constituerat, et ex Deo vitam agere, cum ea totam noctem peragit. Die vero jam apparente, Matrona ventilat ad magistram Eugeniam, de rebus propositis cum ea communicatura. Cum autem multa ultro citroque dicta essent, magistra dicebat hoc ante alia esse considerandum, quomodo se habitura esset filia Theodote. Matrona autem, quæ nullam  alium scopum sibi proponebat præter Deum, et omnem aliam negligebat sollicitudinem : Me, inquit, accipiat solitaria vita et monastica : Theodoten autem Sosannæ et Deo trado : et de ea jam omni cura liberor. Verba res ipsa quoque mox est consecula.
VI. Rursus autem quomodo oporteat silentio vivere, et Deo placere quærens Matrona, per evidentem in somnis visionem docetur a Domino. Fuit autem hæc visio admirabilis. Videbatur enim vir quidam eam persequi, ipsa autem fugiens, servata esse a quibusdam monachis, quod quidem ei significabat, quod in monasterio oporteret eam suscipere vitam virorum monachorum. Ἡ Ματρώνα δὲ σπεύδουσα καὶ αὖθις λαθεῖν εὐθὺ τῶν Ἱεροσολύμων ἐχώρει, οὐδὲν ἕτερον ἢ ῥάκος ἐνδεδυμένη τὸ τρίχινον, καὶ ἄρτον τι ἐλάχιστον ὅσον τρύφος ἀπαγομένη. ΙΖ΄. Διαγενομένων δὲ τῶν ἑπτὰ ἡμερῶν κατὰ μὲν τὸ συντεταγμένον αἱ γυναῖκες ἧκον· παρὰ δὲ τῶν μοναζουσῶν ἤκουον πολλῇ καὶ αὐτῶν τῇ λύπῃ βεβαρημένων, ὅτι ἀφ᾿ οὗπερ εἰς λόγους ἦλθον αὐτῇ, Οὐδὲ ἡμῖν τὸ λοιπὸν ὤφθη οὐδὲ ὅπου γῆς τυγχάνει γινώσκομεν. Ἐκεῖναι ταχὺ ἀπελθοῦσαι, ὡς ἔμαθον ταῦτα, τῷ Δομετιανῷ ἔλεγον, καὶ οὗτος λύπῃ στεῤῥότερον τὴν καρδίαν πληγεὶς οὐκ ἀνίει τὰ κατ᾿ αὐτὴν περιεργαζόμενος ἕως καὶ εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα παρεγίνετο. Καὶ αὖθις ἑτέραις περιτυχὼν γυναιξὶν ὅπως τε εἶχεν ἡ ζητουμένη κατὰ τὴν ἰδέαν ὑπογράφων αὐταῖς, πάντα λίθον ἐκίνει, μαθεῖν ἐθέλων τὰς ἐκείνης διατριβάς. Αἱ δὲ ἰδεῖν μὲν τοιαύτην ὄψιν γυναικὸς ἔλεγον, οὐκ ἔχειν δὲ εἰδέναι σαφῶς ὅπου καὶ εἴη· ἔνθα γὰρ ἂν εἰς καταγωγὴν ὀφθείη, ἔν τινι μὲν τῶν ἐκεῖσε ναῶν καταλύει, σπεύδει δὲ πάλιν ἀλλαχοῦ μεταβαίνειν. Ὅμως ἡμεῖς σοι, φασί, πρὸς τὴν αὐτῆς εὕρεσιν ὑπουργήσομεν· ἑαυτὰς γὰρ κατὰ μίαν διελόμεναι σπεύσομεν ἥτις ἂν αὐτὴν πρώτη καὶ ἴδοι, τάχιστά σοι προσελθοῦσα καταμηνῦσαι. Εὐθὺς οὖν δήλους ὄντας ἑώρα τοὺς ἀφανεῖς ἀστίμους τε φωνὰς ἀφιέντας καὶ πῦρ ἐπιφερομένους, αὖθις τε παρ᾿ αὐτῆς ἐπιτιμωμένους καὶ ἐκχωροῦντας καὶ ὡσεὶ καπνὸς ἐκλείποντας. Ἐν κασώδει γὰρ χώρᾳ καὶ πολὺν τὸν ἥλιον δεχομένῳ τόπον εὐροῦσα νωτίδος μικρᾶς ἐγκύμονα, καὶ τοῦτον ὀξέσι λίθοις ὅσον ἐφικτὸν ἦν κοιλαῖς ἀρύσασθαι ταῖς χερσὶν ἀνορύξασα, ἐπιλαβούσης ἤδη νυκτὸς αὐτὴ μὲν τὰς συνήθεις εὐχὰς ἀποδώσασα τῷ Θεῷ ἐπῄει· τῇ ὑστεραίᾳ δὲ πάλιν ἀφικομένη μεστὸν εὑρίσκει τὸν λάκκον ὕδατος, βρωσίμων τε βοτανῶν τράπεζαν οὐδαμῶς ἄτιμον τῇ ἑστιωμένῃ, ἀλλὰ πολλῷ μᾶλλον τῶν τοὺς ἁδροὺς θεραπευουσῶν καὶ πλουσίων αὐτὴν εὐφραίνουσαν.

XIX. Cum in eo ergo esset, et operam daret hymnis ad Deum et precibus, audivit dæmones contra psallentes, et voces ei similes emittentes : et hoc factum est multis diebus. Ei itaque terrerem afferebat id quod fiebat. Deinde cum se crucis figura muniisset, egressa est adversus terribiles. Sed cum nullum posset videre, divinam rogabat gratiam, ut manifestum fieret id de quo dubitabat. Statim ergo vidit manifestos eos, qui prius non cernebantur, et voces emittentes obscuras, et ignem inferentes : qui rursus cum ab ea increparentur, excedebant, et tanquam fumus deliciebant. Nonnunquam autem cum generosam feminam sitis invasisset, illa parum progressa e delubro, cum ad herbam se convertisset, roscidas et virides herbas ad modicam consolationem ori admovit. Qui autem admirabiliter manna aliquando pluit Dominus, et populum pavit ad satietatem, qui erat malignus et ingratus, sua sponte enatum potum et mensam ei simul ostendit. Nam cum in loco ardente, et soli valde exposito, invenisset locum pauco humore gravidum, et cum acutis effodisset lapidibus, quantum satis erat ad concavis manibus hauriendum, nocte jam adventante, ipsa quidem ascendit, consuetas preces Domino redditura. Postridie vero cum rediisset, invenit cisternam aqua plenam et mensam herbarum esculentarum, quæ nequaquam quidem parvifiebat ab ea quæ accipiebat convivio, sed multo magis eam lætificabat, quam lautæ et opiparæ cœnæ paratæ divitibus.

Cum ergo eam virtus magis ac magis redderet manifestam, fama in omnem partem pervadebat, quod Matronam malitiæ spiritus fugerent, et sint idolorum delubra vacua dæmonibus. Quamebrem multi ad eam veniebant, vel eam solum convenire plurimi facientes. Hinc factum est, ut mulier quoque quædam, nomine Sophrone, temperanter et convenienter suo nomini vitam agens, sed quæ erat gentilium dedita falsæ religioni, et cum ea aliæ quoque, quæ eandem tenebant opinionem, cum renuntiassent parentibus et amicis, et omnibus hujus mundi, seipsas Matronæ tradiderunt, et patentes aures ei præbebant, quæ etiam brevi sunt baptismum consecutæ salutarem : inter quas virgo quoque quædam gentilis, quæ erat sacerdos idolorum, cum de sancta audivisset, et deos suos condemnasset, et divino zelo esset repleta, ea quidem quæ a gentibus offerebantur dæmonibus et consumenda erant in nefaria sacrificia, præbebat pauperibus. Ipsa autem cum discessisset ab omnibus, ad Matronam ardenti studio accedit, et se facit unam ex iis quæ illam sequi statuerant. XXII. ΚΓ΄. Ἡ Ματρώνα δὲ πρὸς αὐτοὺς φιλίως τε καὶ εἰρηνικῶς, Ταύτην ἐᾶτε, ἔφη· αὕτη τοῦ Θεοῦ τῶν ὑμετέρων θεῶν ἤδη δούλη καθέστηκεν. Οὐδὲν αὐτῇ λοιπὸν καὶ ὑμῖν κοινωνόν. Ταῦτα ἀκούσαντες ἐκεῖνοι μὲν ὑπέστρεφον ἐμπρησμόν τε τοῦ ναοῦ, καὶ ἄλλα τὰ χαλεπώτατα ἀπειλούμενοι. Ἡ δὲ κόρη πρὸς τοῖς ποσὶ τῆς μακαρίας κειμένη πολλή τις ἦν δεομένη Χριστοῦ τῇ σφαγίδι ἐνσημανθῆναι καὶ τοῦ ἱεροῦ βαπτίσματος ὡς τάχος ἀξιωθῆναι. Τούτων ἐκεῖνοι ἀκούσαντες καὶ τὸ τῆς ἐπινοίας σοφὸν καὶ τὸ τοῦ φρονήματος ἀγασάμενοι· πάσης ἠπόρουν ἀνταποκρίσεως μηδὲ τὴν ἐκεῖσε ὑποστροφὴν τὸ μετὰ ταῦτα ἀναθαρρήσαντες. Τότε δὴ ταῖς ἀναστρεψάσαις ἑτέραν αὖθις ἡ μακαρία ὑπουργίαν ἐπέταττεν, ἀπιέναι τε καὶ τῷ ἐπισκόπῳ ἐντυχεῖν καὶ μεθ᾿ ἑαυτῶν ἀγαγεῖν τῶν ἱερέων ἕνα τινὰ καὶ διάκονον. Οὗ δὴ καὶ κατὰ πολλὴν γενομένην τὴν ταχυτῆτα τοῖς ἱερεῦσιν ἤδη ἀφικομένοις τὴν ἔναγχος αὐτῇ προσελθοῦσαν ἱέρειαν παρέδιδου, κατηχῆσαί τε ταύτην ἐπέτρεπε, καὶ βαπτίσαι, καὶ πρὸς αὐτὴν  αὖθις ἀγαγεῖν.

XXIII. Matrona autem eis dixit amice et pacifice : Hanc sinite, inquit, hæc est Dei ancilla, quæ prius erat ancilla vestrorum deorum. Nihil est de cætero ei vobiscum commune. Hæc cum audissent, illi quidem sunt reversi, templi conflagrationem et alia minantes gravissima. Puella autem jacens ad pedes beatæ Matronæ, Christum valde rogabat, ut obsignaretur signaculo, et quamprimum divinum baptismum consequeretur. Cum Matrona ergo reliquas jussisset exspectare, ipsa processit ad aridam colligendam materiam : et cum multum collegisset et rediisset : Quædam ex vobis, inquit, euntes quam celerrime, renuntiate iis qui paulo ante venerunt, dicentes : Ignis est vobis paratus, et satis materiæ. Quanam autem de causa tardat vester adventus, ut finem accipiant minæ, quæ sunt a vobis intentatæ? Hæc cum illi audivissent, et sapientem inventionem, et generosos spiritus essent admirati, nesciebant quidnam contra responderent : et nec ausi sunt postea illuc reverti. Tunc iis quæ redierant, beata rursus aliud jussit : nempe ut episcopum convenirent, et secum ducerent unum ex sacerdotibus et diaconum. Quod quidem cum factum esset magna celeritate, iis qui jam venerant sacerdotibus, tradidit eam quæ nuper accesserat, sacerdotem : et jussit eam catechismo initiare et baptismo, et rursus ad se adducere.

Erat autem sancta adeo jucunda in sermone, et bonum odorem spirans in Deum, ut multi sæpe ex iis qui eam convenerant, postquam cessasset loqui, volentes domum reverti, ægre ab ea discederent : ut velut quibusdam vinculis essent ab ea constricti sui in eam amoris, aut qui cum in siti pulchra aqua se exsatıaverint, et propterea graviter ferant ab ea recessum. Cum autem is esset rursus in regina urbium Constantinopoli, in ipsa vero adesset etiam Domitianus eius maritus, repellebat desiderium eius mariti metus, ne forte in manus eius incideret, si venisset in urbem. Quamobrem cum distractus ei esset animus his duabus cogitationibus, nempe patris spiritualis desiderio, et metu mariti, in animo habebat ire potius Alexandriam, aut, cujus spe fuerat frustrata, Antiochiam. Hac de causa intentius vacabat orationibus, et a Domino exspectabat significationem ejus, quod erat ei futurum conducibile.
XXV. Cum in somnum ergo esset resoluta, videbatur aspicere tres viros, quorum unusquisque vehementer contendebat, quisnam ex his tribus esset præferendus, ut eam duceret uxorem. Cum ea autem aures averteret, et putaret rem esse valde absurdam (terrebat enim somnium, ut quod esset contrarium iis quas interdiu susceperat, cogitationibus) : Quinam autem vos estis, videbatur eis sancta dicere, et quæ sunt vestra nomina? Illi vero dixerunt, unum quidem nominari Alexandrum, alterum autem Antiochum, tertium vero Constantinum. Sorte autem conabantur dirimere controversiam; ea vero cecidit in eum qui erat ætate juvenis, nomine Constantinus. E somno ergo excitata, quidnam sibi vellet visionis revelatio, intelligens, statuit quidem relinquere viam, quæ Alexandriam ducit, aut Antiochiam : tota autem in hoc erat, ut proficisceretur Constantinopolim. Hoc enim dicebat placere Deo, qui evidenter ostendit significationem eorum quæ erant obscura.
XXVI. Cum suis itaque sororibus somnium communicasset, videbat eas suæ voluntati resistere; non quod judicarent id, quod visum fuerat, aliud significare, sed quod eam valde amplecterentur, et non sustinerent eam ab ipsis discedere : ut quæ non secus ac recens plantatæ plantæ, ab illius verbis irrigarentur, et venirent in periculum, ne ejus privatione flaccescerent. Tamen quoniam, dixerunt, divina quædam providentia opem tulit tuo consilio, nequaquam possumus tuum interrumpere propositum. Sed secundo loco, inquiunt, sit tibi curæ, quibusnam sis nos relictura post tuum hinc recessum. Illa autem de eis protinus episcopum facit certiorem, et petit, ut ad ipsam mittat duas diaconissas, quibus tempus virtutem confirmavit. Quæ cum accessissent, beatissima Matrona suas, veluti quoddam depositum, credidit earum fidei, et suasit plurimi facere animas earum fidei creditas : ne, quas fecit Deus mundum relinquere, rursus sinerent accedere ad mundi vanitatem, et pacta conventa negligere.

Deinde breviter persequitur Domitiani Hierosolyma, et in Sinam montem persecutionem, et suam in idolorum delubro conversationem, et quod multitudo innumerabilis per eam accessit ad pietatem. Persequitur autem, non ut quæ se ostentaret, sed ut quæ teneretur veri dicendi desiderio. Est enim ostentatio aliena a modestia, et ab ea quæ nihil æque studuit atque latere. Alioqui autem accusari, quod ordines deseruisset, quæ semel ascripta fuerat in vitam monasticam, judicabat esse inutile, et quod posset occasionem præbere offensionis.
XXVIII. Marcellus autem singula statim aperit divino Bassiano. Ille vero auditione affectus tristitia, jussit eum quamprimum abire, et quietum aliquod et a tumultu alienum, non procul a monasterio, præstituere ei habitaculum, ut esset ei facile, si vellet, ad eam accedere. Ille autem cito effectum redditid id quod imperabatur : et cum aptam domum ei parasset, sanctam in ea collocat. Quem in locum cum magnus venisset Bassianus, eamque esset allocutus, et dixisset quæ oportebat, quin etiam ab ea didicisset de iis quæ ei obtigerant, et eam impertivisset iis quibus indigebat, eam rogavit, an sibi opus esset aliquo alio. Cum ea autem dixisset alias sorores esse Beryti, et eas velle ad ipsam venire, et una degere, hoc episcopo Beryti statim litteris significat, et quas nominatim quærebat Matrona, jussit ad eam quamprimum mittere. Eæ ergo emissæ, et cohabitantes cum Matrona, ei summam exhibebant obedientiam, illa seipsam præbente perfectissimum exemplar ad virtutem, quæ etiam multo magis, quam multa ora, eam prædicabat, et omnibus reddebat manifestam. Nani cum aliis Verina quoque imperatrix, uxor magni Leonis, ad ipsam ventitabat, nec tam illam, quam seipsam in hoc statuebat honorare. Quæ cum moderate et frugaliter ab ea esset accepta, et ut oportebat ipsam accipere, illa quæ erat remota a negotiorum sollicitudine, et dedita philosophiæ, recessit, admirans ejus virtutem, et quod nihil peteret ab ea quæ multa dare posset et vellet.
XXIX. Euphemia autem conjux Anthimi, qui fuerat præfectus urbis Romæ, cum fuisset sanctæ familiaris, et cujusmodi esset ipsa didicisset experientia, eam autem, quæ patricio Sporacio conjuncta erat matrimonio, in gravem morbum vidisset


[947] {#pg.116.947}
incidisse, et qui artem vincebat medicinam, et  nullam vitæ spem ostenderet; cum eam, inquam, vidisset sic affectam : Si me audias, inquit, jubens valere medicos, in præsentia trade te communi bono, quod venit in civitatem, sanctam, inquam, Matronam, quæ multos non solum a morbis corporis, sed etiam animæ liberavit, ab impietate traducens ad verum Dei cultum, et ipsam haud ita multo post admiraberis, et alios multos ad ipsam transmittes. Hæc cum dixisset, quænam esset, et qualem vitam transegisset, ei narravit ab initio usque ad finem. Die ergo sequenti ambæ ad eam veniunt, et cum in longum productus esset earum sermo, cum ea quæ morbo laborabat, manum dexteram sanctæ apprehendisset, et eam, quæ erat affecta, partem attulit : affectionem autem statim sensit leviorem. Matrona vero rogavit causam, cur eam tetigisset. Cum autem verum dixisset ea quæ laborabat, Dominus, inquit, te liberabit ab eo malo, quod te detinet; nos enim hoc non possumus efficere.
XXX. Quæ itaque laborabat, ab illo tempore remisse accedentem cogitans mali gravitatem, in animo habebat manere apud sanctam, exspectans perfectam liberationem ab eo quod ipsi afferebat molestiam. Propterea jubebat etiam manere animantia, quæ ei ad usum serviebant. Cum autem ei nuntiatum esset, non esse aliud, quod eam acciperet, habitaculum (unum enim esse, idque habitari mercede), cum beata conferens sermones, Facultates, inquit, satis magnas suppeditavit mihi splendor generis, multasque ædes exstructas ad pulchritudinem atque magnitudinem. Farum, inquit, quascunque volueris, quæ uno ambitu comprehenduntur, parata sum ego tibi largiri, animarum, quæ sunt servandæ, futuras habitacula, et principaliter ad meam ipsius utilitatem. Illa autem cum spiritu cognovisset, quam multis animabus ex hoc aperienda esset porta salutis, assensa est se esse accepturam, et cum diaconum accersivisset Marcellum, et ei rem exposuisset, mittit ad contemplandum ea quæ promissa fuerant. Qui postquam est contemplatus, reversus dixit esse quidem pulchrum loci situm, ut qui a dextera quidem mare, alia autem ex parte propinquum habeat Bassiani monasterium; non parva autem et levi, sed magna opus habere diligentia.
XXXI. Nondum hæc renuntiabantur, et promissio consecuta est effectum : et cum donum fuisset litteris insinuatum, statim ei quoque perfectum sanitatis donum a Deo fuit donatum. Postea autem et ædes exstruxit magnificentius, ut quæ eas, quibus opus erat, impensas ad corrigendum et exornandum munifica manus ei exhibuerit. Hinc fit, ut eas usque ad hodiernum diem vocent omnes Severianas. Μετὰ ταῦτα δὲ καὶ φιλοτιμότερον ἐξειργάσατο τὰς οἰκίας ὅσων ἐδέοντο τῶν ἀναλωμάτων πρὸς διόρθωσιν τε καὶ διακόσμησιν ἀφθόνῳ παρασχοῦσα χειρί, ἀφ᾿ οὗ μέχρι καὶ τήμερον ταύτας, τὰ Σευηριανὰ πάντες λέγουσιν. Ὧν καὶ δύο ἐτύγχανον ἀδελφαὶ μὲν τὴν φύσιν, τὴν δὲ τρόπον οὐκ ἀδελφὰ φρονοῦσαι, ἀλλὰ κομιδῇ διαλλάττουσαι, τὸ μέντοι γένος ἐπίσημοι καὶ περιφανεῖς. Ὡς δὲ πρὸς λόγους αὐτῇ συνῆλθον, καὶ τοῦτο μὲν ἐξ αὐτῶν, τοῦτο δὲ καὶ ἀπὸ τῆς τῶν ἠθῶν εὐκοσμίας πολλῆς ἀπέλαυσαν ὠφελείας, ἡ μία τούτων ἔρωτι ταύτης ἁλοῦσα πνευματικῷ, Σὺ μὲν ἄπιθι, ἔφη πρὸς τὴν ἑτέραν, ἐγὼ δὲ πρὸς ταύτῃ μενῶ· λήθη γάρ με καὶ ἀνδρός, καὶ οἴκου καὶ συγγενῶν ἔσχε καὶ κατὰ τὸν θεῖον φάναι Δαυῒδ, Ἐξελεξάμην παραῤῥιπτεῖσθαι ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ τοῦ Θεοῦ μου μᾶλλον ἢ τῆς κατὰ κόσμον ἀπολαύειν εὐημερίας. Ὢς δὲ ταῦτα ἡ Ἀθανασία, τοῦτο γὰρ αὐτῇ ὄνομα, πρὸς τὴν ἀδελφὴν εἶπεν, οὐκ ἔσχεν αὐτὴν συμφρονοῦσαν, ἀλλὰ καὶ φανερῶς ἀντιφθεγγομένην καὶ κουφότητος εἶναι τὸ  πρᾶγμα λέγουσαν καὶ ἀλόγου θερμότητος ἄξιον χωρούσης εἰς μεταμέλειαν. Ταῦτα δὲ ἔλεγε γνώμῃ φιλοκόσμῳ μᾶλλον ἢ φρονήματι στερροτέρῳ. Ἡ ὁσία δὲ τὸν μὲν κατὰ Θεὸν αὐτῆς ἐπῄνει σκοπὸν, πλὴν οὐδὲ αὐτὴν τὴν ἀθρόαν μετάθεσιν ἐδοκίμαζεν, ἀλλὰ παρήνει πρὸς τὸν ἄνδρα πρότερον ἀπελθεῖν καὶ κοινωνήσασθαι περὶ τούτου, βίου τε φησὶν ἐπιμεληθῆναι ὡς οἷόν τε τελειοτέρου, καὶ Θεοῦ δεῖσθαι διὰ παντὸς θεῖναί σοι εὔοδα τὰ κατὰ σκοπόν· καὶ τότε ἄφιξη μὲν παρ᾿ ἐμὲ, κατὰ νοῦν δέ σοι ἀπαντήσει: τὸ σπουδαζόμενον. Εἰ γὰρ καὶ πόθος θεῖος εἰσῆλθέ σου τὴν διάνοιαν, καὶ οὐκ ἀνήσει κατασχὼν, ἀλλὰ πολλὰ μετὰ τοῦ θαρρεῖν ἀπάγει αὐτό τε τῆς τῆς ἡλικίας νεάζον καὶ τὸ περὶ τοὺς πόνους ἀνάσκητον, ὑπηρετεῖσθαι μᾶλλον, οὐχ ὑπηρετεῖν πεφυκός· τὸ δὲ μεῖζον  ἢ παρὰ τοῦ ἀνδρὸς κώλυσις.

Τούτοις εἰρημένοις ἡ Ἀθανασία ἡσυχάζουσα ἦν ἀνιωμένη μὲν ὡς ἀπωθουμένη, μὴ βουλομένη δὲ ἀντιλέγειν καὶ τὸ ἑαυτῆς θέλημα συνιστᾶν· διὰ τοῦτο καὶ ἀνεχώρει τοῦ σκοποῦ τούτου καὶ τῆς προθέσεως ἰσχυρῶς ἐχομένη καὶ κατὰ τὴν τῆς ἁγίας παραίνεσιν σώφρονος βίου καὶ λιτότητος ἐπιμελουμένη καὶ οἷονεὶ προπαιδεύειν τοῦτο πρὸς τὸν ἀσκητικὸν ποιουμένη βίον. itum. Quo factum est, ut suum quoque gregem fecerit valde augeri, et ab inimicis conservarit illæsum, operibus habens adjutorem eum, qui est causa bonorum. Fit etiam tale quid aliud præter cætera, quod eam fecit majorem.
XXXII. Celebrabatur quidem publicum et venerandum festum Laurentii martyris. In ejus autem templum confluxerat maxima multitudo populi, et diem in eo transigebat. Ibi duæ erant quidem sorores natura, sed moribus minime, ut quæ valde inter se discreparent: genere autem erant claræ et insignes. Ex ergo peracto festo illine revertentes, cum fuissent in loco in quo erat sanctæ monasterium, numerosum quidam sonus hymnorum divinorum accessit ad aures earum. Quibus delectatæ, cum rescivissent, quænam ea esset, quæ præest monasterio, ad eam ingressæ sunt. Cum eam vero convenissent, et partim quidem ex verbis, partim autem ex modestia et morum honestate magnam accepissent utilitatem, una ex iis spiritali ejus amore capta: Tu quidem, inquit, abi, dixit alteri; ego autem manebo apud eam. Me enim cepit oblivio et mariti, et domus, et cognatorum; et, ut dicam cum divino Davide: «Elegi abjecta esse in domo Dei mei potius, quam in mundo frui rebus jucundis». Cum autem sorori hæc dixisset Athanasia (hoc enim erat ei nomen), ea non est illi assensa: sed aperte contradixit, dixitque rem esse levitatis et inconsiderati fervoris, qui facile tendit ad pœnitentiam. Hæc autem dicebat animo potius mundi cupido, quam gravi et constanti. Sancta vero ejus quidem laudabat ex Deo scopum, sed nec ipsa repentinam probat mutationem, sed suadebat ut prius rediret ad maritum, et eam rem communicaret marito: dicebatque oportere eam, quoad ejus fieri potest, curam gerere vitæ perfectioris, et Deum perpetuo rogare, ut scopum ejus dirigat. Tuncque, aiebat, venies ad me; ex animi autem tui sententia succedet id cujus teneris desiderio. Etsi enim divinum desiderium subiit tuum animum, nec te tenere intermittit, me tamen multa impediunt, nempe tua ætas tenera, et quod in suscipiendis laboribus non sis exercitata, et quod et sit natura magis ingeneratum, ut tibi serviat potius, quam ut servias, et quod est omnium maximum, mariti prohibitio.
XXXIII. His dictis, silebat Athanasia, animo quidem cruciata, ut quæ repelleretur, nollet autem contradicere, et suam confirmare voluntatem. Propterea recessit, hunc scopum et propositum acerrime persequens, et ut sancta ei suaserat, vitæ temperantis et frugalis curam gerens, et veluti præcedentem disciplinam, ad vitam et exercitationem id faciens monasticam. Porro autem cum venisset in quemdam ex suis agris, et sejuncta fuisset a conjuge, studebat ipsa se gerere ad sanctæ imitationem, eodem tempore cibum sumens, par-


[951] {#pg.116.951}
ceque et tenuiter, sicut illa, comedens, et preces  fundens ante cibum et post cibum, et alia omnia pro viribus imitans, quæ faciebat beata. Cum ea autem in hoc esset, et quæreret honestam a marito sejunctionem, tale quid accidit, valde honestam præbens liberationem. Nam cum ea ut prius diximus, abesset, maritus, qui erat sumptuosus et moribus intemperans, magnos faciebat sumptus. Cum itaque redactus esset ad inopiam, persuadet puero, at clam surripiat aliquam ex arcis dominæ, et ad eum afferat. Quod quidem cum factum esset, non latuit ancillam, quæ servabat res dominæ.  qua cum illa rursus id didicisset, de cætero tanquam honesta arrepta occasione, nulla non ratione Athanasia moliebatur separationem. Tandem autem cum marito persuasisset, et magnam partem facultatum, quæ ad ipsam pertinebant, secum tulisset, Matronæ fidei suam credit animam; accedensque, et ei suas offerens divitias, Hæc, inquit, mihi accipe, et repone in thesauris Christi, qui non possunt surripi; et ne despicias animam, quæ a sancta tua dependet anima, et omnia sua tibi dedicat.
XXXIV. Matrona autem administrationem quidem, ut molestam et negotiosam rejiciens, supplicis vero propositum admittens, dubitabat quidnam esset sibi faciendum. Et Bassiano quidem rem communicat, et audita ejus sententia, accipit quæ oblata fuerant, et ex pecuniis quidem quasdam consumit in monasterii faciendis operibus: ut quæ  monasterium quidem muro circumdederit, trium autem tabulatorum habitaculum exstruxerit. Primum quidem et terræ propinquum, quod est ad sepulcra definitum. Secundum autem et medium, quod est aptum ad hiemandum, et habet templum, quod est eo tempore aptum, ad quod conveniatur. Tertium vero et postremum, quod ipsum quoque templo ornatur pulcherrimo, et est pulchre accommodatum ad ver illic agendum. Quæ quidem perinanent usque in hodiernum diem, prædicantia sumptuosam et magnificam ædificationem. In his quidem, sicut dictum est, consumptæ sunt nonnullæ pecuniæ Athanasiæ: reliquas autem distribuit monasteriis, quæ erant Hierosolymis, et aliis, quæ rerum necessariarum laborabant inopia, bono  Marcello ad hæc pie inserviente. Et sic quidem Athanasia, amissis, quæ fluunt, divitiis, acquisivit divitias, in quas non cadit interitus.
XXXV. Cum quindecim autem annos fuisset superstes in hac vivendi ratione, a vita præsenti liberata, felix iter ad Deum iniit. Sic grex Matronæ fuit amplificatus, multis quotidie accedentibus, et volentibus pasci ab hac magistra. Cum autem instaret tempus ejus dissolutionis, plane ostendebat sancta, ne neque propter parata opera perturbari ad separationem. « Paratus enim fui, inquit divinus David, et non sum conturbatus ». Ἐν τούτῳ δὲ οὔσης αὐτῆς καὶ εὐπρεπῆ ζητούσης τὴν ἀπὸ τοῦ συνοικοῦντος διάζευξιν, τοιάνδε τι συνήνεχθη, λίαν ὑπρόσωπον παρέχοντα ἀπαλλαγήν. XIII. Not much time passed before rumor had it that a certain famous man named Pelagius had departed from the living, one who in his life had practiced great discipline and virtue. And immediately a multitude of monks was gathered, and there were also gathered those who were otherwise pious and gentle, both those dwelling near the Jordan and those who were citizens of Nicopolis and Jericho, and finally all who lived round about came together and offered their parting respects. And when they approached the body and set out to anoint it with oil, they saw a strange and alien spectacle: a woman who had hidden her sex and had behaved as a man with fortitude, and had reaped a greater reward from her labors. Then there was a struggle among them all, one seeking to precede another and striving to get to [the body], and [each] esteeming it a great thing to approach the body alone and touch some part of it. And thus, with many lamps and with great honor, the precious relics of Pelagia were carried out, escorted and borne by holy and pious men.

LIFE AND CONDUCT AND ASCETIC EXERCISE OF OUR HOLY MOTHER MATRONA, MARTYR OF CHIOS.
(The Latin is in Surius at the 8th of November; we have transcribed the Greek from codex ms. Reg. n. 1481, 11th century.)
1. To praise, according to one's ability, those who are temperate in life and lovers of virtue, is at once holy and beneficial.
For they themselves are worthy of praise, and the discourse concerning them is such that it touches upon similar things and kindles in others a desire for the same. But when that which is praised is a woman—which is to say, the weaker of human nature, and that which is most soft toward the harshness of labors—how is such praise not beneficial to men and women alike, being able to rouse both toward noble things: the women, that, partaking of the same nature, they might be seized by the same labors and rewards; the men, that they might not, being men, be seen as second to women and more ignoble in undertaking the same? Of these praised and virtue-loving lives, therefore, is Matrona, who vied with men in virtue and, by her accomplishments, even surpassed them, as the things to be stated hereafter will show.
II. There is a certain region subject to the Roman empire, lying on the borders of the Cilicians and the Isaurians, named Pamphylia, which obtained such an appellation because of its being nourishing and populous, and for having inhabitants from every city. One of the cities under it is called Perge, in which this blessed Matrona was brought up, who, having obtained a liberal education and training from her parents, upon reaching the age for marriage (for she was also remarkable for her beauty), is joined to a husband not of the common sort and of no random standing, by name Domitian; and she becomes the mother of one daughter, to whom, from the things that were to come, she straightway fixed the name, calling her Theodote, having given her to God from her very swaddling clothes. Now, as for how, after her marriage and cohabitation, she practiced moderation and humility and practiced not—in those superficial and cosmetic beauties, but rather in the inner man of the soul—the decorating [of herself], setting it as her study to live in temperance and with the gravity appropriate to life.
III. It is praiseworthy and otherwise grateful to pursue these subjects, but it would seem entirely superfluous were we, having passed over the greater and more perfect things, to choose to occupy the listeners with the memory of these. That, however, which she herself preferred above others, this it is fitting to set forth at the beginning of the narrative. For she judged that the word of the Apostle must be observed by her to the utmost. For he says that those who have wives ought to be as if they had them not. Wherefore, having of her own will departed from the land that bore her D — under divine providence, I say, which she also had as an assistant to her resolve—she makes her journey to the foremost of cities, Byzantium, along with her spouse, whom she had indeed accompanying her and not for a moment withdrawing, yet not cognizant of the things she contemplated, nor knowing what she held in her mind. Then, having entered the royal city, she would sit all day in the temples of the saints, not withdrawing from them by night or by day, concerning herself with fasting and prayer, and sharing her possessions with the needy, and at all times praying to God that the natural war might be quelled in her...
of the flesh. For she was at that time young in age, not having reached more than twenty-five years.
IV. She prayed, therefore, as has been said, to be released from the disturbances of the flesh, so that, being able to live without a husband, she might be free to occupy herself with spiritual and more divine things. There was a certain Eugenia who stirred her toward these virtuous things; she was what her name implied, noble in character, and one who spent her entire life in nightly vigils and prayers. Matrona strove against her in a good competition of discipline, diligently practicing those things in which monks are exercised. Domitian was annoyed by these daily practices and gave way to absurd reflections; he did not speak of her constant progress as something good, but, according to a mind leaning toward base desires, he began from that point to keep a strict watch, not even allowing her to look out of her chamber. A contest was established for both: for him, that he might not let her do what she was accustomed to do, and for her, that she might not be absent from the holy gatherings. But since he resisted strongly, she urged him with many prayers and at last with difficulty persuaded him to allow her to do what she wished.
V. With great fervor, therefore, she went to the temple of the holy Apostles. When she had arrived there, praying more intensely, she begged that nothing might be an impediment to her fleeing the world, but that they might offer her also to Christ, along with the community that had already been offered to Him by them beforehand. Having prayed in this way, and evening having already arrived and those who guarded the temple ordering everyone to depart, she found a dwelling in a certain portico among those who were there, belonging to a certain Susanna, who had long been known to her—a woman who from her earliest youth had chosen to preserve her virginity and to live her life toward God—and she spent the whole night with her. When day had already appeared, Matrona went to her teacher Eugenia to consult with her about the matters proposed. When many things had been said back and forth, the teacher said that this must be considered before all other things: how the situation regarding her daughter Theodote should be settled. But Matrona, who proposed to herself no B other goal than God, and neglected every other concern, said, "Let the solitary and monastic life receive me; but I entrust Theodote to Susanna and to God, and I am from now on released from all care regarding her." The deed soon followed her words.
VI. Again, seeking to learn how she ought to live in silence and please God, Matrona was instructed by the Lord through a manifest vision during sleep. This vision was truly marvelous. For it seemed that a certain man was pursuing her, but she, fleeing, was saved by certain monks; this indicated to her that it was necessary for her to undertake the life of the monks in a monastery. For in this way
it would be unknown to her husband and to others. Having therefore shorn her hair right to the skin and donned the habit of a eunuch, she came again with Eugenia to the aforementioned temple of the divine Apostles; and wishing to learn the meaning of the obscure things from those more divine, she opened the holy Gospel and immediately encountered such a saying: "Whoever wishes to come after me, let him deny himself, and take up his cross, and follow me." (Luc. ix, 23). Hence, having an indubitable knowledge of the things that please God, and having dedicated their own selves to Him, and having expected to have Him as their helper in the things that were being done, they separated from one another.
Matrona, however, as has been said, having feigned to be a eunuch and having called herself Babylas, approached the monastery of the holy Bassianus; and having been received by the monks, she immediately stripped herself for spiritual struggles, not by the pallor of her countenance and the glumness of her face feigning piety, but rather by genuinely and truly pursuing virtue, and by every means striving to remain hidden, so that in a short time she was elevated toward the summit of a life lived according to God, and was held in admiration by all, because a man who was burdened by the infirmity of a eunuch held out so strongly against labors, and vied to surpass all the monks, nobly restraining anger, despising glory as vain, and embracing every obedience with the utmost modesty.
VII. And indeed they could not envy her; for zeal according to virtue is free from all envy. But they wanted to pay attention to her all the more as a teacher, having been spurred on even before to imitation. And little would it have taken for what was hidden to be known, and for it to become manifest that she was a woman, had she not dispelled the suspicion which arose with spiritual wisdom. For at one time, as it happened concerning the gardening work that was taking place there, while she was toiling with the monks and was applying herself to the work more readily and eagerly, the monk who happened to be working with her, Barnabas by name—having cast his eyes upon her a little too curiously, as one who had come to the monastic life from the stage not long before, even though he later progressed in virtue and was entrusted with the prefecture of the monks—asked how it had come to pass that both her earlobes were pierced.
The blessed one gave a reply that was both brief and considered, saying: "You have suffered a human thing, brother, yet one alien to our calling; for it is necessary to pay attention to the earth and not to gaze curiously at human countenances. But nevertheless, learn the solution to the question: she who once held and raised me, possessing a certain fond affection for me, was munificent enough to put golden ornaments in my ears." Thus the blessed one by a wise invention warded off the monk from suspicion. But a great crowd of thoughts entered into her, and she came to remember the admonitions of Eugenia, who said: "It is a very difficult matter for a woman to associate with men, and to [endure]
appear as if she were a man, acting according to them; for it is impossible for the hypocrisy to remain hidden until the end." Nevertheless, being more attentive in her mind, and associating alone with God alone, she said, "By Your nod, O Master, I have undertaken the life of the monks, and I have followed without turning back the one who commanded me to follow; therefore, do not allow me to be deceived in what I have hoped for, nor to fall away from this good purpose. But do You Yourself, defending my weakness, grant that the end may correspond to my goal." Saying such things, she seemed to see in the purity of her soul a more favorable outcome.
VIII. Since she was in this state and was clinging to the spiritual way of life, her circumstances remained hidden for a long time from Bassianus, even though he was not devoid of the gift of discernment. Yet it was hidden from him by the wisdom of God and by judgments more inscrutable, just as some things were once hidden from Elisha by the Shunammite woman. But late and after a long time, the matters concerning her became manifest to him. And it happened in this way: The great Bassianus dreamed that he saw a man, honorable in his attire, decent in appearance, and seeming to be of a more divine nature, and this man said to him three times that Babylas the eunuch, who is one of the monks in your flock, is plainly a woman, but feigns to be a eunuch in order to remain hidden. At the same time, Acacius, a man illustrious for his piety, who happened to be the shepherd of the third monastery of the God-bearing Abramius, saw the same vision. In the morning, therefore, Bassianus sent for one of the monks, a certain John who was second after him in the monastery, to tell him the dream he had. While he was discussing these things, someone came sent by Acacius, reporting to Bassianus the vision just as he himself had seen it. Then, therefore, the great Bassianus, desiring to obtain greater certainty about this matter, took the holy Gospel into his hands and, opening it, chanced upon those words which stated that the kingdom of heaven is like leaven, which a woman took and hid in three measures of flour, till it was all leavened.
IX. Having therefore understood the meaning signified in the depths, he deliberated with himself how he might skillfully address what was proposed, and that this might not become manifest to the many. He sends for the blessed one, and glaring at her, he said, "What was the cause, O woman, that you have dared to undertake such an audacious task against us, and have inflicted upon us an unseemly temptation and reproach, while striving for so long a time to remain hidden?" But she, understanding what had been said and fearing the severity of his words, and stunned by the cloud of bitterness that had appeared upon his face, and especially having a conscience bearing witness to the things that were said, immediately casts herself upon the ground, and laying hold of the holy man's feet, [pouring out] words in great…
humility, though they were barely audible, she let them forth, saying: "I have not sought to bring temptation upon your flock, O master; rather, I have joined myself to it in order to flee the temptation from the adversary and to avoid the snares that are in the world." To this Bassianus replied again: "How is it that, being a woman, you approached the divine mysteries with your head uncovered, and in the kiss of peace you safely offered your mouth to the brethren?" She said: "Regarding the divine gifts, I feigned weakness and, without completely removing the covering that was on my head, I thus approached the communion of the mysteries; however, I never refused the sign of love towards the brethren. For I did not consider myself to be offering [myself] to the gaze of men, but to men who emulate angelic impassibility."
X. B Being amazed at this wise reply, Bassianus said: "And why, O woman, did you not betake yourself to another monastery, one where women are permitted to dwell?" She, having waited a little while and laid aside the greater part of her fear—having also found an occasion to use more words for her defense—unfolded her circumstances section by section: how she had been joined in lawful marriage to a husband and was called the mother of a single child; how she would spend entire nights lingering in the meeting places of the saints; and how, because the one who lived with her would sometimes threaten her and sometimes even inflict blows, she was hindered from such a zeal and purpose; and how she had sought out some mechanical pretext that would serve to escape the man's notice and enable her to carry out her own will. To these things she added the committal of her child to Sosanna, and finally she brought forward the vision she had in her sleep, in which, while fleeing her husband, it seemed she was saved by the hands of monks—which she understood, and so changed herself into this habit, changing her name as well. Having heard these things, and having been struck with wonder at the sharpness of her understanding and having considered her resolve as acceptable, Bassianus inquired if she would choose the monastic life for the future. When she said "Most certainly"—for it was for this reason that she had become [a stranger] to the world and to the things of the world—Bassianus said: "Be of good courage, my child, concerning the future." And having given her the counsels he knew would be profitable to her, he said: "For the rest, my child, it is fitting for you to place a ribbon upon your head because of the law of nature, and thus await the heavenly calling; and I will not neglect those things which pertain to bringing you aid."
XI. When she had thus departed from there, the Lord, who manages all things for what is profitable, caused the daughter of Matrona to depart from this life and took her to Himself, so that the wicked one might not have a pretext against her, and so that, since it was already known that she was a woman, he might not, under the pretext of taking care of the child, draw her away from her more divine concern and sow worldly thoughts in her mind. Wherefore she felt not grief, but pleasure at the event, deciding that what had happened was not the loss of a daughter, but
a deliverance from the cares surrounding her, and a laying aside of them. She was hidden, therefore, with Sosanna, or rather God was guarding her through Sosanna. Meanwhile, rumor was proclaiming everywhere what had been done by her, and the story reached the ears of everyone. Whence it happened that her husband Domitianus, hunting down the truth of what had occurred bit by bit, since he had not ceased to track down the affairs of his own wife, after having gone around to all the other monasteries, arrived also at that one in which the blessed woman had stayed. Emitting some indistinct and unseemly cries out of rage, and knocking importunately at the doors, and insulting [them], he said: "What is this violence that you have brought upon me? What is this great injury? It is surely a fine thing, O monks, very fine are the things that have been done by you. For why do you wish to divide badly those who were joined well? Give me my companion in life; give me her who is joined to me according to the laws."
XII. These and more things than these he shouted out, moved by desire and overcome by distress of spirit, his voice being choked by his passion. He heard, however, from them: "We have not received your wife, nor indeed is it permitted for women to be granted entrance into the monastery. But we know a certain eunuch monk, Babylas by name, who lived with us for a time, and then suddenly changed his mind and decided to set out for Jerusalem. We know that he, therefore, having such a motive for traveling, had departed from us not many days before these. Where he is now staying, however, is known only to Him to whose eyes there is nothing that cannot be seen. And that these things are true, the whole populace of the city would testify." Hearing these things, Domitianus, being frustrated in his intended inquiry and deprived of any plausible pretext, became all sorts of things: pained by his failure, enraged by zeal, and stirred to desire by his fondness for her. He departed thus, distracted by these agitations of mind.
XIII. A host of cares, however, beset Bassianus, lest the soul of Matrona, which had been committed to his faith, should truly have undergone destruction. Having called together those who were in a certain order of priority among the monks, he said: "We must consider further concerning the sister who has departed from us. For even if she was different in sex, yet since she was inscribed into our circle, we must consider the separation of her who has been [thus] divided from us as the amputation of a member. Let us therefore provide for her affairs, so that she may reach a good end, lest the evil one, who plots against us daily, overcome her steadfastness by using her husband as an instrument suited for his traps." Thus, then, did Bassianus speak, setting the deliberation in common. Marcellus, however, who was then a deacon, suggested an opinion of this sort, saying that in the city of Emesa—for he himself had originated from there—there was a monastery of women, in which his own sister was one of those who were practicing [the ascetic life]. "If therefore it pleases you, let her be sent thither; for thus it will be possible for you to remove from her the cares concerning
the word." Having assented to what had been said and permitted it, the deacon immediately found a ship that was setting sail from there, placed the holy woman in it, and she returned.
XIV. Having accepted what had come to pass with a cheerful spirit, as if it had not happened without God’s will, and having gathered a few things for food, she completed the voyage easily and well, with the Spirit urging the ship onward, and arrived at the monastic institution in Emesa. Being kindly received, she applied herself to the labors of virtue with such zeal that she was seen by the monastics as an example not easily attained by those competing in struggles equal to hers. Wherefore it seemed good to all, after the departure of the one who had just been presiding over them, to entrust to her the entire governance of the monastery. But by as much as Matrona strove to remain hidden, by so much did God care to make her manifest, to place her light upon the lampstand, and to make her virtue evident. At that time, therefore, a certain farmer working his small plot of land saw, for many days, a flame being emitted from the earth, and the emission was continuous and never-ceasing. This man, therefore, being a farmer and unable to surmise anything higher, approached the bishop of the city and reported it. He, however, understanding that something great was signified by what appeared, came to the place together with his clergy; and as was fitting, having offered a prayer, he commanded them to dig up the ground. When this was done, an urn was found, not hiding gold within or anything else of that sort which might entice a soul fond of finery, but a precious thing and one worthy of all, the truly venerable head of the Baptist.
XV. When this rumor had run everywhere, there was no one who remained at home, but all were flocking to the spectacle; by whom this venerable head is brought inside the church with all praise and hymns. Since the blessed Matrona was also present with all the monastics at the sight of what had appeared, she approached and drew from the ointment of the head, which had been set out; and communication of it was made, even against her will, to those who were present. But how and why this happened against her will, the narrative wishes you to attend and learn. For, having been cut off in the middle of the multitude and not being permitted to exit, as everyone was pushing themselves forward to receive it, she was compelled to share what she had gained of the ointment with the others. While she was distributing this, a certain man, blind from birth, passing by everyone (for there were also some priests then who were dispensing the divine ointment), came to her, being very insistent in his supplications. She, having anointed the sockets of his eyes with the precious ointment, immediately made him a spectator of light, who never before
had witnessed it. This proclaimed her great and illustrious, and caused her to be renowned among all, so that everyone said she was the one who had meditated to escape notice for so long a time, and had spent her life among men, performing the works of monks.
XVI. This illustrious report again summoned Domitian, and, as if touching him by the hand, made him come to the monastery. But when he heard that men were forbidden to have entrance, concealing what he desired, he feigned piety; and he requested through certain women that he might see Matrona for the sake of prayer and veneration. She, however, having inquired quite precisely, and having recognized by the tokens on his face and by what she heard that he was her husband, what did she contrive? She asked for a period of seven days, and after those, she promised that at her leisure she would come into his sight. When Domitian heard this from the women, he himself sat suspended in hope, revolving in his mind the appointed day, waiting to see her, imagining in his vision that he already held her in his hands. But Matrona, again seeking to remain hidden, set out for Jerusalem, wearing nothing other than a garment woven from hair, and carrying with her but a tiny, meager scrap of bread.
XVII. When seven days had passed, the women came as had been agreed; but they heard from the nuns, who were themselves also burdened by great grief, that from the time when they had last met her, they had not seen her anymore, nor did they know where on earth she might be. Those women, having quickly gone away after they learned this, told Domitian. He, wounded by a more vehement pain of heart, ceased not to inquire curiously, until he came to Jerusalem. And when he happened upon other women, describing to them the appearance of the one he was seeking, he moved every stone, wishing to know where she was dwelling. But they said that they had indeed seen a woman of such an appearance, but could not know clearly where she might be; for wherever she might be seen at an inn, she would lodge in one of the temples that were there, but would hasten to move on to another place again. "We, however," they said, "will serve you in finding her; for having divided ourselves individually, we shall hasten, and whichever of us might first see her will run to you most quickly and inform you." And they did as they had promised.
XVIII. Matrona, however, having met Domitian as it happened, and having recognized him, fixed her eyes upon the ground and, allegedly reaching out her hand to pick up a stone, passed him by as he approached, cleverly escaping his notice. But when she encountered those others, understanding for what reason they were diligently searching throughout that whole region and that she had become known by the characteristics of her form and was all but captured, with a wise reflection she warded them off, asking that three whole days be granted to her to go to Mount Sinai, and after those she would come most quickly and come into the sight of her husband. When Domitian learned this, thinking it to be a pretext and again
seeking her, pursued her with great speed. But Matrona, having learned this, and that her pursuer was present and that she was being hunted, was seized with great fear. She entered into a certain idol shrine which was in Berytus, judging it better to fall in with demons or wild beasts than to be taken by the hands of Domitian. For if those take her, they will only harm the body; but the husband, if he should seize her, will be more destructive than demons and wild beasts, inasmuch as he is able to harm the soul along with the body, dragging her back to the world and claiming her as his own wife.
XIX. Being thus situated and exerting herself in hymns and prayers to God, she heard demons chanting in opposition and emitting voices similar to her own; and this happened for many days. The thing that was happening brought great fear to her. Then, having fortified herself with the sign of the cross, she went out against the terrifying ones. But since she could see nothing, she prayed for divine grace to make manifest to her that which was in doubt. Immediately, then, she saw them manifest—who previously were not seen—emitting dark voices and bringing fire, but again, upon being rebuked by her, they would depart and vanish like smoke. Sometimes, when thirst had seized the noble woman, going a little way from the shrine, she would turn to the grass and bring dewy, green herbs to her mouth for a modest refreshment. And the Lord, who once miraculously rained down manna and fed the people to satiety—a people that was wicked and ungrateful—also displayed to her a drink and a table brought forth spontaneously. For in a parched place, receiving much sun, having found a small spot pregnant with a little moisture, and having dug it with sharp stones so as to be enough to drink with cupped hands, as night was already approaching, she herself went up to offer her accustomed prayers to the Lord. But on the following day, having arrived again, she finds the place full of water and a table of edible herbs, of a nature that did not at all fall short of the banquet being received, but rather gladdened her much more than sumptuous and costly feasts prepared for the wealthy.
XX. Since she was conducting herself in this way and was praying to God at all times, the wicked one judged this to be grave and unbearable. Wherefore, having assumed the shape of a beautiful woman, appearing to her, he said, "Why, my lady, have you chosen to dwell in this obscure and alien place? For the solitude is long and the penury of necessary things is great; and besides, you are still young in age and of distinguished beauty; and I am very fearful for you, lest this lovable face be an occasion of desire to lustful eyes, and because of the solitude of the place, and because there is no one present to bring you aid, you bring contumely upon your own body. But if you would obey me, I would lead you down to the city itself, where you will also find a house according to your will, and you will live as you please without any disturbance; nor will you lack those things which
are necessary." Having realized that this was an insidious design of the adversary, she regarded these words as trifles and passed beyond the snare. But again that malignant demon did not cease, attacking her by every artifice and setting upon Matrona. Indeed, having even taken on the form of an old wandering woman, and appearing to emit fire from her eyes, it threw itself at her feet, madly and boldly, frightening her and uttering absurd and foul things. When, however, the blessed one took no heed of it, nor turned herself toward it, as if raging and insulting, it cried out: "Even if I could not defeat you, noble woman, when you were young, yet in your old age I shall inflict upon you things that are the most difficult of all. For the present, however, I shall stir up against you those who are in Berytus, so that you, who hold their idol shrine in contempt and, as much as in you lies, cause it to be held in despite, may be seen by them." Such wicked wiles of the evil one were succeeded by more divine revelations and visions, in the way that God is accustomed to comfort in afflictions and to resolve sad things with more joyful ones. "For according to the multitude," says the divine David, "of my sorrows in my heart, thy comforts have gladdened my soul." And it happened as follows.
XXI. As she was offering her prayers in the evening, as was her custom, three men appeared, standing openly, and seemed to be chanting psalms with her; and for several hours a bending of the knees was performed by them behind Matrona. When she turned her face to see who they might be, they, having mildly and calmly said three times, "Pray for us," immediately departed. As therefore her virtue rendered her more and more manifest, the rumor spread everywhere that the spirits of malice fled from Matrona, and that the shrines of the idols were becoming empty of demons. Whence also many came to her, doing so many things just to commune with her in words. From this it came about that a certain woman named Sophrone, living her life temperately and in accordance with her name, but who was addicted to the false religion of the Gentiles—along with others who held the same opinion, having renounced parents and friends and all things of this world—surrendered themselves to Matrona and lent attentive ears to her, and indeed, within a short time, were deemed worthy of the saving baptism by her. D Among whom was also a certain Gentile virgin who was a priestess of the idols; having heard about the holy one, and having condemned her own gods, and being filled with divine zeal, she gave to the poor that which was offered by the Gentiles to the demons and which was to be consumed in impious sacrifices; and having departed from all, she approached Matrona with ardent zeal, and made herself one of those who had determined to follow her.
XXII. But when the day arrived on which it was necessary for the sacrifices to be performed, and the multitude of the people and those related to her by birth were assembled, not finding her, they knew not how they might perform things done according to custom, since the priestess was not present. And since they could not discern
what had happened, though with difficulty and late—the parents having learned this—they proceeded with haste to the idol temple where the holy woman happened to be. And seeing her staying close to the feet of the holy woman, they used more rash words, saying: "Why, O young girl, having held the greatest gods in contempt, have you left the sacrifices incomplete, and incited a multitude against us that does not bear the insult which is done to the gods, and you yourself, instead of the best and considered blessed life, have chosen this infamous one, which is clearly held in contempt? Therefore, having cast off the madness that suddenly oppressed you, be wise at last, and having laid aside this mournful habit, come out joyful to us who are joyful, lest the place of what seems to you salvation should become the tomb of your destruction. For if you do not do what is commanded, the present dwelling place will be consumed by fire tomorrow." When they had said such things, they heard nothing from her.
XXIII. But Matrona said to them in a friendly and peaceful manner: "Let her be," she said, "she is already a servant of God, the God of the universe, who was formerly a servant of your gods. There is nothing from now on in common between her and you." Having heard these things, they returned, threatening the burning of the temple and other most grievous things. But the maiden, lying at the feet of the blessed Matrona, prayed fervently to Christ that she might be sealed with His sign and be deemed worthy of divine baptism as quickly as possible. When Matrona had permitted the others to wait, she herself went out to collect dry material; and when she had collected a great deal and returned, "Let some of you," she said, "go with all haste and declare to those who came a short time ago, saying: 'Fire is prepared for you, and material in abundance. But for what reason does your arrival delay, so that the threats which were intended by you may receive an end?'" When they heard these things, and admired the intelligence of her plan and the nobility of her spirit, they knew not what they might answer against her, and they dared not return there afterward. Then, indeed, to those who had returned, the blessed woman gave another service again, to go and meet with the bishop and to bring with them one of the priests and a deacon. And this having been done with great speed, she handed over the priestess who had recently approached to the priests who had already arrived, and permitted them to catechize her, and to baptize her, and to bring her back to her.
XXIV. Therefore, having been brought back after baptism, Euche—for this is what she had been renamed—was led through every spiritual discipline, and was trained in divine things along with the others, who were eight in number. D The holy woman was so pleasant in discourse, and breathing the good fragrance of God, that many of those who conversed with her, after she had ceased to speak, though wishing to return home, would depart from her with difficulty, as if they were constrained by some bonds of love for her, or like those who, having satisfied themselves with beautiful water in their thirst, for that reason bear their departure grievously. But furthermore, the desire to meet Bassianus again took possession of her,
as he then was in the Queen of Cities, Constantinople, and her husband Domitianus was also present there; the fear of her husband, lest she fall into his hands if she should arrive in the Queen of Cities, repelled her yearning. Hence, being divided in her thoughts by these two things—the longing for her spiritual father and the fear of her lawful husband—she thought rather to reach Alexandria, or, if she failed in that, Antioch. For this reason she applied herself the more intently to prayers and awaited from the Lord the revelation of what would be profitable for her.
XXV. Having thus fallen into sleep, she thought she saw three men, and that each of them was contending vehemently and resisting strongly as to which of the three ought to be preferred to take her as his wife. And as she seemed to turn away her ear, thinking the affair altogether absurd (for a dream seen in opposition to the thoughts of the day is a cause of alarm), she appeared to say to them, "Who are you, and what are your names?" They replied that one was named Alexander, another Antiochus, and the third Constantinus, and that they were trying to settle the dispute by lot; the lot fell upon the one who was young in years; this one was Constantinus. Having awakened, and understanding what the revelation of the vision intended, she determined to abandon the way that led to Alexandria or Antioch; she was now entirely set upon the journey to Constantinople. For she said that this was pleasing to God, who clearly manifested the meaning of things that were obscure.
XXVI. Having communicated the dream to her sisters, she saw them resisting her will; not because they judged that what had been seen signified anything else, but because they loved her greatly and could in no wise endure to depart from her, as though they were newly planted shoots being watered by her words and in danger of withering through her absence. "Nevertheless," they said, "since divine providence has become an assistant to your counsel, we are in no wise able to block your resolve; but, they said, as a secondary matter, let it be your care to whom you will leave us after your departure." She then immediately made the matter known to the bishop and asked him to send to her two deaconesses whose virtue had been established by time. When they had arrived, the most blessed matron entrusted to their care those under her direction as a kind of deposit, and urged them to prize highly the souls entrusted to their faith, so that those whom God had caused to renounce the world they might not allow to return again to the vanity of the world and neglect their covenants.
XXVII. Having said these things and given them a final embrace, and having taken with her only one of them, Sophrone, she boarded a ship and, carried along by a favorable wind, she reached Constantinople. Then, having arrived at the church situated near the sea, which is called the church of Eirene from the name of the peace of God—
she called for the deacon Marcellus, and made herself known to him. This was the same man whom our narrative has already mentioned as having advised Bassianus to send her to the monastery in Emesa. He received her gladly, and asked what the cause of her long journey might be. She replied: "Nothing other—for what else could it be that would make one despise so much toil?—than an irresistible desire to meet you, and especially," she said, "the divine Bassianus, on account of which I have disregarded old age, the weakness of a woman, a quiet way of life, and everything else, and have become intent solely on the path leading to you." Then she briefly recounted the persecution by Domitian at Jerusalem and Mount Sinai, her stay in the idol temple, and how a countless multitude was brought to piety through her. She recounted this not with a desire for display, but with a love of truth; for such display is foreign to a humble mind and to one who strives for nothing so much as to remain hidden. Moreover, she judged that it would be improper, and could provide a pretext for scandal, to be branded as a deserter, having once enrolled herself in the monastic life.
XXVIII. Marcellus immediately reported each detail to the divine Bassianus. He, having heard this with sadness, ordered him to depart as quickly as possible and to set apart for her a quiet dwelling, free from noise and not far from the monastery, so that it might be easy for anyone wishing to visit her to do so. He quickly carried out what was commanded, and having prepared a suitable house, he placed the saint in it. When the great Bassianus came to that place, he held a conversation with her, and having said what was necessary, and also having learned from her of those things which had happened to her, and having provided her with what she needed, he asked her if she had need of anything else. Since she said that there were other sisters in Berytus who wished to come to her and live with her, he immediately signified this by letter to the bishop of Berytus, and ordered him to send those whom Matrona had requested by name as quickly as possible. These, having been sent and dwelling together with Matrona, rendered to her the utmost obedience, for she provided herself as the most perfect example of virtue, which proclaimed her and made her manifest to all much more than many voices. For, along with many others, the Empress Verina, wife of the great Leo, also visited her, and she judged that in this she was honoring not so much the saint as herself. And when she was received by her with moderation and frugality, and with the hospitality that it was fitting for that tranquil and philosophical woman to bestow, she departed admiring her virtue, and the fact that she sought nothing from one who was both able and willing to give much.
XXIX. Euphemia, the wife of Anthimus, who had once been prefect of the city of Rome, having become a friend of the holy one and having been convinced by experience of her character, when at that time she saw the woman who had been joined in marriage to the patrician Sporacius fall into a severe illness—
one who was surpassing in the medical arts yet showing no hope of life—she said: "If you will be persuaded by me, bid farewell to the physicians; for the present, commit yourself to the common good that has newly arrived in the city, I mean the holy Matrona, who has delivered many not only from bodily illnesses, but even from diseases of the soul, turning them from impiety to the true worship of God. And you yourself will marvel before long, and will send many others to her." Having said this, she recounted to her from beginning to end who she was and what manner of life she had led.
On the following day, therefore, both came to her, and when their conversation had been drawn out for a long time, the sufferer, having taken the right hand of the blessed one, applied it to the part that was aching, and immediately she felt the malady lighter. Matrona asked the reason why she had touched her. When the sufferer told the truth, she said: "The Lord will deliver you from the distress that grips you; for our own powers are not capable of such working."
XXX. The sufferer, therefore, from that time, considering that the gravity of the malady was subsiding, pondered remaining with the holy woman, awaiting complete deliverance from that which brought her discomfort. For this reason, she also gave orders for the animals that served her needs to remain there. But when it was reported to her that there was no other lodging to receive her—for there was only one, and this was occupied by rent—she immediately entered into discussion with the blessed one. "The splendor of my lineage," she said, "has provided me with considerable wealth, and indeed an abundance of houses constructed with beauty and scale. Of these, whichever you wish, provided they are within one precinct, I am ready to bestow upon you, being about to become a dwelling place for many souls destined to be saved, and primarily for my own benefit." As for her, having perceived through the Spirit for how many souls a door of salvation would be opened by this, she agreed to accept it, and having summoned the deacon Marcellus, and having entrusted the matter to him, she sent him to inspect what had been promised. When he had inspected it, having returned, he reported that the site was indeed well-situated, having the sea on the right, and on the other side providing the monastery of Bassianus as a neighbor, but that it was not in need of small or ordinary care, but rather of great diligence.
XXXI. These things were not yet fully reported, and the promise met its fulfillment: and when the gift had been confirmed in writing, straightway to her also was the perfect gift of health granted by God. After this, moreover, she built the houses more magnificently, in that her munificent hand provided whatever expenses were needed for their correction and decoration. Hence it is that all to this day call them the Severianae. From there, when the holy woman had migrated from her former dwelling, she did not at all migrate from her purpose which is toward God, and from her works; unless one should call this a migration—from lesser things to greater, from which also she would proceed to a great
er increase. And another such thing occurred besides the rest, which made her even greater.
XXXII.
public and venerable festival of the martyr Laurence was being celebrated. A great multitude of people had flocked together to his temple and were spending the day there. There were two sisters there by nature, though not by character, being very different from one another; they were, however, of noble and distinguished lineage. Now, when the festival was concluded and they were returning from it, when they happened to be near the place where the monastery of the holy woman was, a certain melodious sound of divine hymns struck their ears. Being delighted by this, when they had learned who it was that presided over the monastery, they entered to find her. And when they had met her in every way—having received great benefit both from her words and from the propriety of her manners—one of them, having been seized by a spiritual love for her, said to her sister: "You, indeed, go away; but I shall remain here with her. For forgetfulness of my husband, home, and relatives has taken hold of me, and as the divine David says: 'I have chosen to be cast away in the house of my God, rather than to enjoy the prosperity of this world' (Ps. LXXXIII, 11)." When Athanasia—for that was her name—heard these things, she did not merely disagree with her sister, but openly contradicted her, saying that the matter was one of levity and thoughtless fervor, which easily tends toward repentance. She said these things with a mind more worldly than firm and constant. The saint, indeed, praised her goal which was directed toward God, but she herself did not approve of such a sudden change. She advised her to return to her husband first and to share this matter with him; she said that it behooved her to take care of a more perfect life as much as possible, and to beseech God at all times: "And your goal will be made successful for you; and then you will come to me, and that which you desire will happen according to your mind. For although a divine desire has entered your soul and has taken hold of you without letting go, yet many things urge you to turn back from this: your youth, and the fact that you are unexercised in taking on labors, wishing to be served rather than being natured to serve; and, most important of all, the hindrance of your husband."
XXXIII.
Having said these things, Athanasia was silent, being pained in spirit as one who was being repelled, yet not wishing to contradict or to persist in her own will. Therefore she withdrew, yet persisting in this goal and purpose, and according to the holy woman's exhortation, she took care of a temperate and frugal life, making this, as it were, a preliminary training for the monastic life. And indeed, having gone to one of her farms and being separated from her spouse, she strove to conduct herself in imitation of the holy woman, taking food at the same time, spar-
and eating sparingly, just as she did, offering prayers before and after her meal, and, as far as her strength allowed, imitating all the things that the blessed woman did. While she was thus occupied and seeking an honorable separation from her husband, a certain event occurred that provided a very honorable release. For while she was away, as the account previously explained, her husband, who was extravagant and intemperate in his habits, was incurring great expenses. Being thus reduced to poverty, he persuaded a boy to steal something secretly from his wife’s chests and bring it to him. When this was done, it did not escape the notice of the maidservant who guarded the mistress's belongings. When Athanasia learned of this from her, she seized the remaining opportunity, not in just one way, but in every way, striving for a separation, and at last, having persuaded her husband, and having taken with her a large part of the property that belonged to her, she entrusted her soul to the faith of Matrona; and approaching her, she offered her wealth, saying, "Take these, I pray, and lay them up in the inviolable treasuries of Christ, which cannot be stolen; and do not despise a soul that depends upon your holy soul, and which dedicates everything it has to you."
XXXIV. Matrona, however, while rejecting administration as burdensome and distracting, yet accepting the suppliant’s volition, was nonetheless at a loss as to what she should do. She therefore shares the matter with Bassianus and, having received his advice, accepts what had been offered. Some of the money she spent on works for the monastery, surrounding the retreat with a wall and building a three-story housing structure. The first, near the ground, was set apart for tombs; the second, in the middle, was suitable for wintering and had a temple—at that time, it was also convenient for assembly; the third and final one was itself also adorned with a most beautiful temple and was well-situated for spending the spring. These remain even to this day, proclaiming the generosity and splendor of the building. In these works, as has been said, some of Athanasia's money was spent; the rest she distributed to the monasteries that were in Jerusalem and to others that were in need, and to those otherwise lacking in necessities, with the good Marcellus piously serving in these matters. Thus, indeed, Athanasia, having lost the wealth that flows away, acquired wealth into which decay cannot fall.
XXXV. After she had lived for fifteen years in this manner of life, she was released from her present life and began her blessed journey to God. Thus, Matrona’s flock was increased, with many coming daily and wishing to be fed by this teacher. But when the time of her dissolution drew near, the holy woman clearly showed that she was not unsettled by the preparation of these works at the prospect of her departure: "For I was prepared," says the divine David, "and I was not troubled." (Ps. CXVIII, 60.) And neither again
did she hasten her departure; the former, as has been said, because of her confidence, but the latter, because of her love for her flock. Therefore it was more necessary for her to remain in the flesh, according to Paul, and for the sake of reaping more fruits from having labored more. Wherefore, in the end, she exerted herself in greater labors, like good runners, and was given to more frequent and intense prayers.
XXXVI. But since God did not wish to deprive Matrona of even this consolation—that, before her departure from here, she should know the state that would receive her there—a sleep greater than usual took hold of her. In it, she seemed to be walking through a place exceptionally planted with trees, irrigated by the sweetest waters, and surrounded by an estate of the greatest delight; in this place, certain grave and honest women stood by, clearly showing in their countenances the marks of virtue. They seemed to show her by hand an inner dwelling, of such exceptional beauty that no human hand could fashion it, nor any tongue could describe it. They called to Matrona, saying that she should enter into it, for "this is yours, and has been especially set apart for you." Having awakened, she understood the direction of the dream, and it gave her faith regarding what things would await her after her departure from here. And so, her whole time of life having been measured out to one hundred years—of which only twenty-five were those in which she lived a worldly life, while she spent all the rest living according to the Spirit—she left the things here and departed to her longed-for Christ, leaving behind deeds that were indeed marvelled at by all, but which only a few were able to imitate, while she herself constantly besought the Christ to whom she had come to grant peace to the churches and salvation to all his people, for he is the one whom she glorified as of the undivided Trinity, to whom is due all glory, honor, and worship, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
