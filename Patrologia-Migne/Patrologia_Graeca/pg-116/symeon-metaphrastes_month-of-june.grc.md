I. Quando Constantius, Constantini Magni tertius filius, tenebat sceptra Romani imperii, Ariani persecutionem moverunt adversus pios, ut qui ad hæc a Constantio plurimum haberent adjumenti, et Athanasium quidem exterminatum accepit Occidens; divinus autem Alexander antistes Byzantii, cum vitæ fini appropinquasset, nullum quidem suæ Ecclesiæ constituit successorem : sed cum duos præcipuos elegisset ex aliis, populo hanc dedit optionem, ut si velint quidem doctorem et virtutibus resplendentem habere pontificem, Paulum eligerent, quem ipse ordinavit presbyterum. Sin autem habitu solum gravem, et vestitu extrinsecus honestum, Macedonium (1), qui fuerat jam olim ordinatus diaconus, et jam vergebat ad senectutem. Hæc cum eis dixisset,  transit ad sedes meliores, cum fuisset quidem pontifex viginti tres annos, vixisset autem nonaginta octo.
II. Circa electionem autem eorum qui dicti fuerant, exstitit contentio. Nam Ariani quidem magis aspiciebant ad Macedonium; orthodoxi vero eligebant Paulum (2). Vincit tamen suffragium orthodoxorum, et eligitur Paulus Byzantii episcopus, in ecclesia quæ appellatur sanctæ Irenes, quam tempus a nova separans, concedit ut vetus nominetur. III. Celebratur dedicatio ecclesiæ Antiochenæ, quam Constantinus quidem Magnus excitavit a fundamentis, Constantius autem studium adhibuit, ut consecraretur; et dedicationis prætextu congregantur episcopi nonaginta (4). Maximus vero antistes Hierosolymorum, vir pius, non aderat, neque Romæ Julius, nec per se, per aliquem ex subjectis. Congregatur ergo synodus, præsente etiam Constantio. Eusebiani autem student ante alia, ut deponatur Athanasius. Primam vero in eum intentant accusationem, quod fecerit præter regulam; regulam dicentes, quam tunc ipsi ediderant. Reversus enim, aiebant, ab exsilio, non communi sententia, sed ipse sibi tradidit episcopatum. Secundam autem, quod cum in ejus adventu exorta esset seditio, multi sint mortui. Tertiam, ea quæ facta sunt Tyri. Ex quibus condemnato pietatis athleta, ejus loco constituunt Gregorium, qui eadem quæ ipsi sentiebat. Et cum hæc ausi essent, audent etiam fidem transformare; nihil quidem aperte reprehendentes eorum quæ facta sunt Nicææ, sed improbo et malitioso consilio semina prius jacientes ad destruendum homousion. Et quæ Antiochiæ quidem habita fuit synodus, cum hæc et hujusmodi alia ausa esset, et quosdam fecisset canones, fuit soluta.
IV. Eusebius autem fungitur legatione (5) apud julium Romanum episcopum, machinans ut confirmaretur lata in Athanasium sententia. Sed nihil effecit eorum quæ sperabat. Paulus vero pontifex cum Romam venisset (6), et alii non pauci episcopi, quin etiam ipse quoque Athanasius, qui fuerant expulsi, comparatis ab Eusebio insidiis, simul Romæ degebant. Sanctus autem Julius cum intellexisset esse calumniam, quæ facta fuerant adversus pontifices, eos litteris munitos mittit ad Orientem, suam unicuique reddens sedem, et eos simul reprehendens, qui ipsos deposuerant. Atque suas quidem ecclesias ii rursus accipiunt, et mittunt litteras ad eos, ad quos scriptæ fuerant. Cum Eusebius itaque interiisset, Paulus in suam sedem restituitur. Qui autem partes Arii sequebantur, in ea quæ nunc ad Paulum dicitur, ecclesia eligunt Macedonium, qui blaspheme quidem Filium dicebat esse Patri similem, circumscribens homousium : sanctissimum autem Spiritum a divinitate omnino alienum.

V. Cum Athanasius autem suam recepisset ecclesiam, qui considebant rursus Antiochiæ (etenim rursus convenerant, conantes vicissim criminari Julium Romanum episcopum) protinus impium Gregorium, quem ipsi præter leges elegerant, mittunt Alexandriam. Et eum quidem reducebat prætor Alexandriæ, secum ducens septem millia militum. Quod cum rescivisset Athanasius, Romam iterum navigavit. Populus autem Alexandrinus non ferens rem tam nefariam, ecclesiam, quæ vocabatur Dionysii, non recte id faciens, exussit. Cum vero rescivisset Constantius, qui agebat Antiochiæ, Paulum præconem veritatis in suam sedem esse restitutum, jubet Hermogeni duci exercitus, qui mittebatur in partes Thraciæ, exturbare eum ex ecclesia. Et ille quidem cum venisset Constantinopolim, studebat facere, ut ei fuerat imperatum. Populus autem non est hoc passus, sed et domum ejus incendunt, et ipsum extractum interimunt. Quod quidem cum audisset Constantius, ægerrime tulit quod factum fuerat : et cum ipse venisset in civitatem, sanctum quidem Paulum ex ea expellit : et civitatem multat frumento, ablato ei dimidio doni, quod illius pater, benignus scilicet et clemens Constantinus, ei donaverat. Erat autem summa donationis octoginta millia panum quotidianorum. Irascebatur autem etiam Macedonio, non solum, quod præter ejus sententiam fuisset electus, sed etiam propter cædes, quas ipse, seditionem agitans, fuerat causa faciendi.
VI. Eo itaque dimisso, venit Romam (7), et  versabatur simul cum Athanasio. Refert ergo, quæ passus fuerat, Romano episcopo : et rursus munitus ejus litteris et Constantis imperatoris, statim revertitur, et suæ sedi restituitur, cum magno gaudio exceptus a civitate. Constantius autem rursus id factum ægre tulit, et rursus ab eo prodit jussus, qui justum mittat in exsilium. Itaque cum eum accepisset præfectus Philippus, sanctum quidem mittit in exsilium, ejus vero loco in ecclesia reponit sancti Spiritus hostem Macedonium. Processit autem missio in exsilium non aperte, sed ex insidiis. Cædes enim Hermogenis reddidit Philippum cautiorem ad malefaciendum : et honorifice quidem vocat Dei pontificem ad lavacrum, quod  appellatur Zeuxippus, tanquam propter quædam in communi utilia et arcana consilia : et illine mittit in aulam imperatoris, et per ipsam deinde Thessalonicam, quæ fuit patria ejus majoribus, et ei quidem est prohibitum ascendere ad partes Orientis, non autem in propinquas regiones.

Ἀθανασίου δὲ τὴν ἰδίαν ἀναλαβόντος ἐκκλησίαν, οἱ ἐν Ἀντιοχείᾳ πάλιν συνεδρεύοντες (καὶ γὰρ συνήεσαν, ἀντεγκαλεῖν ἐπιχειροῦντες Ἰουλίῳ τῷ Ῥώμης) τὸν δυσσεβῆ Γρηγόριον διὰ τάχους, ὃν αὐτοὶ παρανόμως ἐχειροτόνησαν, εἰς τὴν Ἀλεξάνδρου ἐξαποστέλλουσι, καὶ κατῆγεν αὐτὸν ὁ τῆς Ἀλεξανδρείας στρατηγός, ἑπτακισχιλίους στρατιώτας μεθ᾿ ἑαυτοῦ ἐπαγόμενος. Ὁ μαθὼν Ἀθανάσιος εἰς τὴν Ῥώμην πάλιν ἀπέπλευσεν. Ὁ δὲ τῶν Ἀλεξανδρέων λαὸς οὐκ ἐνεγκὼν τὸ ἔκθεσμον, τὴν καλουμένην Διονυσίου ἐκκλησίαν, οὐκ ὀρθῶς ποιοῦντες, ἐνέπρησαν. Μαθὼν δὲ Κωνστάντιος ἐν Ἀντιοχείᾳ διάγων ὡς Παῦλος ὁ τῆς ἀληθείας κῆρυξ εἰς τὸν ἴδιον ἀποκατέστη θρόνον, Ἑρμογένει τῷ στρατηλάτῃ ἐντέλλεται πρὸς τὰ τῆς Θράκης ἐκπεμπομένῳ μέρη, ἐξῶσαι τοῦτον τῆς Ἐκκλησίας. Καὶ ὁ μὲν τὴν Κωνσταντίνου φθάσας, ἐσπούδαζεν ᾗ προσέτακτο δρᾶν· τὸ πλῆθος δὲ οὐκ ἠνείχετο, ἀλλὰ τήν τε οἰκίαν αὐτοῦ κατακαίουσι, καὶ αὐτὸν σύραντες διαφθείρουσιν· ὅπερ ἀκούσας Κωνστάντιος δι᾿ ὀργῆς τὸ πραχθὲν ἐποιήσατο, καὶ αὐτός, τὴν πόλιν καταλαβὼν, τὸν μὲν ὅσιον Παῦλον ἀπελαύνει ταύτης, ζημιοῖ δὲ καὶ τὴν πόλιν τὸ σιτηρέσιον ἀφελὼν αὐτῆς τὸ ἥμισυ τῆς δωρεᾶς ἣν ὁ ἐκείνου πατὴρ, ὁ φιλάνθρωπος δηλαδὴ Κωνσταντῖνος, αὐτῇ ἐδωρήσατο. Ἦν δὲ τὸ ὅλον τῆς δωρεᾶς ἡμερήσιοι ἄρτοι μυριάδες ὀκτώ. Ὠργίζετο δὲ καὶ κατὰ Μακεδονίου οὐ μόνον ὅτι παρὰ τὴν αὐτοῦ κεχειροτόνητα γνώμην, ἀλλὰ καὶ διὰ τοὺς φόνους ὧν αὐτὸς στασιάζων γέγονε αἴτιος.
Ϛ΄. Ὅμως ἐάσας αὐτὸν ὁ μακάριος Παῦλος καταλαμβάνει τὴν Ῥώμην, καὶ Ἀθανασίῳ συνῆν. Ἀναφέρει γοῦν ἃ πεπόνθοι τῷ Ῥώμης, καὶ αὖθις τοῖς παρ᾿ αὐτοῦ, ἔτι δὲ καὶ τοῖς Κωνσταντος τοῦ βασιλέως κατοχυρωθεὶς γράμμασι, παραυτίκα τε ἐπάνεισι, καὶ τῷ οἰκείῳ θρόνῳ μετὰ πολλῆς αὐτὸν θυμηδίας τῆς πόλεως ὑποδεξάμενης ἐγκαθιδρύεται. Καὶ πάλιν ὁ Κωνστάντιος δι᾿ ὀργῆς τὸ γινόμενον ἐτίθετο, καὶ πάλιν πρόσταγμα εἰς ὑπερορίαν ἄγον τὸν δίκαιον· καὶ γὰρ τοῦτο δεξάμενος ὁ ὕπαρχος Φίλιππος, ἐξορίζει μὲν τὸν ὅσιον, ἀντικαθίστησι δὲ τῇ τοῦ θεοῦ Ἐκκλησίᾳ τὸν Πνευματομάχον Μακεδόνιον. Τὸ δὲ τῆς ὑπερορίας οὐκ ἐκ τοῦ ἐμφανοῦς, ἀλλ᾿ ἐξ ἐνέδρας προῆλθεν· ὁ γὰρ Ἑρμογένους φόνος σοφώτερόν τι κακουργῆσαι τὸν Φίλιππον ἀπειργάσατο· καὶ καλεῖ μὲν σὺν τιμῇ τοῦ θεοῦ ἀρχιερέα εἰς τὸ λουτρὸν, ὧν ἐπώνυμον Ζεύξιππος, ὡς ἐπὶ κοινωφελέσι δῆθεν καὶ τισι βουλεύμασι μυστικοῖς, κἀκεῖθεν εἰς τὰς βασιλείους αὐλάς, καὶ δι᾿ αὐτῶν εἰς Θεσσαλονίκην, ἣν καὶ οἱ αὐτοῦ πρόγονοι πατρίδα εἶχον, καὶ τοῖς μὲν τῆς ἀνατολῆς ἐπιβαίνειν μέρεσιν ἐκεκώλυτο, ταῖς δὲ πλησιαζούσαις οὐκ ἔτι.

τὴν τοῦ Θεοῦ Ἐκκλησίαν παρεισδύεται, τῆς διαφθορᾶς εἰς πεντήκοντα καὶ τρισχιλίους παραταθείσης. Ταύτης τῆς παροινίας καὶ Μαρκιανὸς συναπέλαυσε καὶ Μαρτύριος, ἀναγνῶσται καὶ τῷ θείῳ Παύλῳ ὑπογραφεῖς τυγχάνοντες, ἅτε δὴ καὶ αὐτῶν παρρησίᾳ συγκηρυττόντων τὸ ὁμοούσιον. Ὁ δὲ τὰ ἔκθεσμα ὧν ὑπέστησαν ἀκριβέστερον ἐκμαθών, γράφει τῷ ἀδελφῷ τρεῖς παρ᾿ αὐτὸν ἀπὸ τῆς ἀνατολῆς ἐπισκόπους ἀφικέσθαι, οἳ λόγον ποιήσονται τῶν περὶ ᾿Αθανάσιον καὶ Παῦλον τολμημάτων, ἅμα δὲ καὶ τὴν τῆς πίστεως ἔγγραφον ἔκθεσιν διακομίσονται πρὸς αὐτόν. Οὗτοι τὴν Ῥώμην καταλαβόντες τοῖς περὶ ᾿Αθανάσιον καὶ Παῦλον εἰς λόγους μὲν ἐλθεῖν οὐκ ἐθάρσησαν, τὴν δὲ ὑπ᾿ αὐτῶν ἐν τῇ ᾿Αντιόχῳ ἐκτεθεῖσαν πίστιν ἀποκρύψαντες, ἑτέραν τε συνθέντες κατὰ τὸ φανερὸν μὲν οὐδὲν βλάσφημον περιέχουσαν, τὸ μέντοι ὁμοούσιον οὐ πρεσβεύουσαν, ἐπιδιδόασι τῷ βασιλεῖ, καὶ πλέον οὐδὲν οὔτε δράσαντες οὔτε παθόντες αὖθις ἀνέστρεψαν.
Η΄. Τριετίας δὲ διαδραμούσης πάλιν οἱ τῆς ἀνατολῆς συναθροίζονται, καὶ πίστιν ἄλλην συντάττουσι, καὶ τοὺς ἐν Ἰταλίᾳ ἐκπέμπουσι. Διάκονοι δὲ ἦσαν τοῦ τοιούτου συντάγματος Εὐδόξιος ὁ τηνικαῦτα Γερμανικείας ἐπίσκοπος καὶ Μαρτύριος καὶ ὁ τῆς Μοψουεστίας Μακεδόνιος. Ἡ δὲ τῆς πίστεως αὐτῶν ἔκθεσις καὶ αὐτὴ τὰ μὲν ἄλλα τῇ προεκτεθείσῃ ραπλησίως εἶχε, προσθήκαις δέ τισι ταῖς κατὰ τὸ τέλος ἀλλοκότοις εἰς τὸ μακρότερον ἐξέτεινετο. Ὁ δὲ κατὰ τὴν Ῥώμην τῶν ἀρχιερέων σύλλογος οὐ προσεδέξατο ταύτην, ἀρκεῖσθαι φάμενοι τῷ συμβόλῳ τῆς πίστεως ὃ ἐν Νικαίᾳ προηγόρευσε σύνοδος. Οὕτω δὲ πάντα ταραχῆς κατεχούσης, καὶ συγχυτικῆς ἔριδος, κηρύσσεται σύνοδος οἰκουμενικὴ γνώμῃ τῶν δυοῖν βασιλέων μετὰ ἑνδέκατον ἔτος τοῦ εὐσεβεστάτου Κωνσταντίνου τοῦ πατρὸς αὐτῶν τελευτῆς. Συνῆλθον οὖν ἐν Σαρδικῇ ἐκ μὲν Ἑσπέρας ἐπίσκοποι πλείους ἢ τριακόσιοι, ἐκ δὲ τῆς ᾿Ανατολῆς ἑξ μόνοι καὶ ἑβδομήκοντα. Καὶ οἱ τῆς Ἕψας τοῖς ἐκ τῆς δύσεως εἰς ὁμιλίαν συνελθεῖν οὐκ ἐπένευον· τὸ δὲ αἴτιον ὅτι Παῦλος αὐτοῖς καὶ ᾿Αθανάσιος οἱ τῆς εὐσεβείας συνεδριάζον ἄθληται.

VIII. Cum triennium autem præteriisset, rursus congregantur episcopi Orientales, et aliam fidem componunt, et emittunt ad eos, qui sunt in Italia. JUNE. 883-884. LIFE OF OUR HOLY FATHER PAUL, ARCHBISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE THE CONFESSOR. VITA S. P. N. PAULI CONFESSORIS. (Latin at Surius for the 7th of June. Greek from Cod. Reg. 1481.)
I. When Constantius, the third son of Constantine the Great, held the sceptres of the Roman Empire, the followers of Arius raised a persecution against the pious, in order that they might have from Constantius the greatest assistance for these purposes, and they indeed accepted that Athanasius had been exiled to the West; but the divine Alexander, the high priest of Byzantium, when he was approaching the end of his life, did not leave behind a successor for his Church: but when he had singled out two of the others as the most excellent, he gave the people this choice: that if they wished to have a teacher who was resplendent in virtues as high priest, they should choose Paul, whom he himself had ordained a presbyter. But if they only wanted one who was grave in habit and externally honest, they should choose Macedonius, who had been ordained a deacon long ago, and was already verging upon old age. Having said these things to them, he departed to better dwellings, having been high priest for twenty-three years, and having lived in all ninety-eight years. B
II. But around the election of those who had been mentioned, a contention arose. For the Arians were looking more toward Macedonius; the orthodox, however, were choosing Paul. Yet the vote of the orthodox prevailed, and Paul was chosen as bishop of Byzantium, in the church which is called Saint Irene’s, which time, separating from the new, allows to be called the old. But when Constantius had returned to Constantinople from Antioch not long after, he was angry at the one elected, because it had proceeded not according to his own judgment
regarding the ordination, and having sat in a conclave of Arians, he condemned Paul to be removed, and declared Eusebius—who was moving to a third see—the bishop of the royal city from Nicomedia. But he who had impiously performed these acts returned again to Antioch. Eusebius, however, left no stone unturned in order to remove the homousion from the sacred symbol.
III.
The dedication of the church in Antioch is celebrated, which Constantine the Great had raised from its foundations, and Constantius had shown eagerness for it to be consecrated; and under the pretext of the dedication, bishops assemble to the number of ninety. But Maximus, the overseer of Jerusalem, a pious man, was not present, nor was Julius of Rome, either in person or through any of his subordinates. The synod is therefore convened in the presence of Constantius as well. Those around Eusebius show eagerness, before anything else, to dispose of Athanasius. They bring against him a first charge, saying that he acted contrary to a canon—a canon which they themselves had issued at that time. For he had returned, they claimed, from exile not by common judgment and vote, but he himself had entrusted the high-priesthood to himself. A second [charge] was that, upon his arrival, a riot had broken out in which many had died. A third was the events that took place in Tyre, from which they condemned the athlete of piety and installed in his place Gregory, who was of the same mind as they. And having dared these things, they further dared to transform the faith, in no way openly criticizing those things done at Nicaea, but with a wicked and malicious intent sowing seeds beforehand for the destruction of the [i] homousion [/i]. And the synod at Antioch, having decided these things and having dared other transgressions and enacted some canons, is dissolved.
IV.
Eusebius acts as an envoy to Julius of Rome, contriving to have the sentence against Athanasius confirmed. But he achieved nothing of what he hoped. Paul the high priest, having reached Rome, as well as other bishops, not a few—but also Athanasius himself, who had been expelled through the machinations of Eusebius—were living together in Rome. But the holy Julius, upon learning that the actions against the high priests were a slander, strengthens them with letters and sends them to the East, as well as restoring to each his own see and rebuking those who had deposed them. These men, therefore, take back their own churches, and the letters are sent to those to whom they had been written. For Eusebius having perished, Paul is restored to his own see; but those who Arianized in the church now called "of Paul" ordain Macedonius, who blasphemed that the Son was unlike the Father, setting aside the [i] homousion [/i], and [asserting] the all-holy Spirit to be entirely alien to the Godhead.
V. When Athanasius had recovered his own church, those who were residing again in Antioch—for they had gathered once more, attempting to bring charges in turn against Julius of Rome—speedily sent the impious Gregory, whom they had illegally ordained, to that of Alexander. The military commander of Alexandria escorted him, bringing seven thousand soldiers with him. Upon learning of this, Athanasius sailed back to Rome. The people of Alexandria, however, unable to bear such a lawless act, burned down the church called Dionysius’, though they did not act rightly in doing so. When Constantius, who was staying in Antioch, learned that Paul, the herald of the truth, had been restored to his own throne, he commanded Hermogenes, the commander of the army who was being sent to the regions of Thrace, to cast him out of the church. He, having arrived at Constantinople, strove to do as he had been ordered; but the multitude did not endure it, and instead they burned down his house, dragged him out, and destroyed him. When Constantius heard of this, he was very angry at what had occurred, and, having come to the city himself, he drove the holy Paul out of it, and punished the city by removing half of the grain allowance that his father, the humane Constantine—that is to say—had granted it. The total of this grant was eighty thousand daily loaves of bread. He was also angry at Macedonius, not only because he had been ordained against his will, but also because of the murders for which he had been the cause by stirring up sedition.
VI. Nevertheless, the blessed Paul, leaving him alone, arrived in Rome and remained with Athanasius. He therefore reported what he had suffered to the bishop of Rome, and, being fortified once again by his letters and those of the Emperor Constans, he immediately returned and was restored to his own throne, with the city receiving him back with great joy. Constantius again took this event ill, and once more issued an order for him to be sent into exile. Therefore, when the prefect Philip had received this order, he sent the holy man into exile and in his place installed the enemy of the Holy Spirit, Macedonius, in the Church of God. The mission into exile did not come about openly, but by way of an ambush; for the murder of Hermogenes had made Philip more cautious in his wrongdoing. He honorably summoned the high priest of God to the bath called Zeuxippus, as if for matters beneficial to the public and for some secret consultations, and from there he sent him to the imperial palace, and through it, then to Thessalonica, which his own ancestors had also held as their fatherland; and while he was forbidden to go to the regions of the East, he was no longer prevented from going to the nearby ones.
VII. Now the impious Macedonius, with a military force serving him, riding together with the prefect, [entered] the Church of God...
the slaughter having extended to three thousand and fifty. Marcian and Martyrius also suffered this outrage; they were readers and scribes for divine Paul, seeing that they themselves also openly preached the homousion. Thus, therefore, through the slaughter of Christians, the enemy of the Church is established in the ecclesiastical thrones. At which time Constantius also builds the Great Church, which is named for the Wisdom of God, and the divine Paul, a short while later, arrives for the third time in Italy and, taking Athanasius as his partner in the struggle, they explain what they had suffered to Constans, who was emperor of the West and was the brother of Constantius. And when he had learned more accurately the lawless acts they had endured, he writes to his brother that three bishops should come to him from the East to give an account of those things that were perpetrated against Athanasius and Paul, and at the same time to bring in writing a statement of faith. And indeed, four of the bishops are sent to Rome: Narcissus the Cilician, Theodore the Thracian, Marces of Chalcedon, and Marcus the Syrian. When they reached Rome, they did not dare to enter into discussions with those around Athanasius and Paul, but concealing the faith that had been set forth by them at Antioch, they composed another which openly contained nothing blasphemous, yet did not uphold the homousion; they handed this over to the emperor, and having neither done nor suffered anything further, they returned.
VIII. After three years had passed, the Easterners gather again and compose another creed, and send it to those in Italy. The deacons of this decree were Eudoxius, who was at that time bishop of Germanicia, and Martyrius, and Macedonius of Mopsuestia. Their exposition of faith was in other respects similar to that which had been previously set forth, but by some additions at the end it was extended to a greater length. The gathering of bishops at Rome, however, did not accept this, saying that they were satisfied with the symbol of faith which the synod in Nicaea had proclaimed. And as everything was thus held in turmoil and factious strife, an ecumenical synod is proclaimed by the counsel of the two emperors, in the eleventh year after the passing of the most pious Constantine, their father. There came together, therefore, to Sardica from the West more than three hundred bishops, but from the East only seventy-six. And those from the East did not consent to enter into conversation with those from the West; the reason was that Paul and Athanasius, the athletes of piety, were sitting with them.
IX. And since neither would the defenders of true religion remove Athanasius, nor would the heretics desist from...
the hostilities they waged against the athletes, they were divided among themselves. And when the Orientals reached Philippi, a city of Macedonia, they gathered together, and then they openly anathematized the *homousion*. But those who were at Sardica condemned them, though absent, and deposed them as calumniators; and they confirmed the faith that had been ratified at Nicaea. A Constans, the emperor of the West, having received knowledge of these things, instructed his brother Constantius by letter, and urged him to be clement and benign toward Paul and Athanasius, and not to be a hindrance to them so that they might be restored to their own sees, adding that if he would not willingly yield to those who possessed the right, he himself would arrive and, even against his will, render justice to those who were being treated with injury. Constantius, therefore, fearing his brother’s threats, did not prevent the holy Paul from being restored to his own episcopate: he summoned his fellow-contender Athanasius to him by diverse letters, and when he had arrived, he received him kindly and restored him to his own throne. Thus, that great turmoil was brought to tranquility, and the Church rejoiced to have the defenders of piety.
X. But when Magnentius had seized the tyranny and killed Constans the emperor of the West, the storm of Arian madness once more disturbed the churches of the inhabited world: and again Athanasius fled, and George seized his throne, and Paul, the champion of piety, was sent into exile in Cucusus of Armenia. There, by the strangulation of the impious who rushed in at night, he was brought to the completion of his martyrdom. Into Constantinople, in his place, the impious Macedonius was introduced, and the shepherds were driven out from the other churches, and wolves invaded them again; and the madness of Macedonius strove to obscure the cruelty of the Gentiles toward Christians. And the whole of the East was afflicted with evils, but especially Constantinople. At which time Angelios, the bishop of the Novatians, inasmuch as he also held to the *homousion*, was sent into exile; and it was a calamity that cannot be explained, not proceeding only as far as the chief priests and priests, but laying hold also of women and children, as many as rejected the communion of Macedonius. For the breasts of women—O the madness!—partly were inserted into the lids of chests and compressed and cut off, and partly with iron, and other heavy tortures were devised for others. For the things which modesty prevented even the Gentiles, who were strangers to God, from devising against common nature, these it then came into the minds of the impious to do.
XI. Macedonius extended his rage also against the region of Paphlagonia; for he had heard that many of the orthodox and of those from Novatus were staying in it; and he saw to it that four cohorts of armed soldiers were sent against them, and that they be compelled by arms to impiety.
And those who dwelt at Mantineum, having gathered in a multitude and in their zeal for piety having fashioned into weapons sickles and axes and whatever came to hand, drew up in battle array against the soldiers. Then, an engagement having occurred, many were slain—both of the Paphlagonians, and of the soldiers, all save for a few. This bloodthirsty and shameless deed, therefore, stirred up righteous hatred, not only among those who had been wronged, but even among those who seemed to be well-disposed towards Macedonius; and it provoked the emperor himself to enmity. Nor was this all, but also [the fact] that he took the body of Constantine—most pious emperor, I say (for the house in which it lay seemed to be in danger of collapse)—and without his permission moved it to the church of Saint Acacius. And since this translation did not meet with everyone's approval, but seemed to many an impious act—that the bodies of the departed should be disinterred, especially that of an emperor who was distinguished for his piety—a great slaughter took place, and for the many and incurable evils which Macedonius dared to commit, he paid a slight penalty: the deprivation of his seat.
XII. The cause of Arius, however, still prevailed throughout the entire reign of Constantius, and yet more during the times of the most impious Valens, until divine justice overtook the latter, and by the righteous sentence of Gratian, who administered the West, elevated the Spaniard Theodosius to the imperial throne of the East, and through him bestowed victory upon orthodoxy. But thus was the heresy of Arius, which had devastated the churches for forty years, banished by the piety of Theodosius, and the orthodox faith was adorned with its rightful honors. The same most pious emperor, therefore, convenes a synod, intending to bring the followers of Macedonius into concord with the orthodox and those who defended the *homousion*. On this account, he summons those who were leaders of their heresy as well. And there gathered of the orthodox, from Alexandria indeed Timothy, from Jerusalem Cyril, and Meletius from Antioch; and there was present Gregory the Theologian, and Ascholius of Thessalonica, and Gregory of Nyssa, and many others; for their entire assembly was one hundred and fifty [bishops]. The leader of the heretical crowd, however, was Eleusius of Cyzicus, and Marcian of Lampsacus, and Eudius of Ephesus, and some others, thirty-six in all.
XIII. But the heretics, taking no account of the righteous rebukes and gentle admonitions, did not abandon their own impiety.
