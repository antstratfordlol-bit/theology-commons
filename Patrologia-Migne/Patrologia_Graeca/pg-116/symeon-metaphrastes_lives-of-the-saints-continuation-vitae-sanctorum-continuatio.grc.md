IV. Cum sic essent precati, in nostra forma apparens Dominus, solvit quidem vincula : ignem autem irrorat, quomodo pueris olim in Assyria; et sic sancti illæsi et salvi producti sunt in conspectum regis. Ille autem cum esset valde crassus et insipiens, neque admirabili spectaculo obstupefactus, neque martyrum invicta motus patientia : Quid, inquit, vobis videretur, eos appellans nominatim, num hæc vobis parva persuaserunt tormenta ut a pretioso quidem vestro errore discedatis, nostris autem edictis cedatis? an adhuc vultus permanere in errore, vestris freti præstigiis? Respondit beatus Acindynus : Præstigias quidem neque scimus, neque unquam didicimus, neque unquam talibus usi sumus imposturis. Sed quoniam Dei vires insuperabiles, et quæ sunt supra naturam signa divina, putas esse præstigias, visioneque et non revera hæc fieri suspicaris, eris deinceps mutus et lingua impeditus, ut ex teipso testimonium feras Dei virtutis. Si potes ergo dicere, die, quibusnam diis nos jubes sacrificare? Non erat autem vox, neque auditio, sed erat mutus, non secus atque dii ejus, solo nutu indicans animi voluntatem. Cujus amentiam Christi servi arguentes et irridentes, dicebant : Recedimus, tu autem nihil nobis omnino dicis.
V. Cum autem finxissent sancti se recedere, erat rex mutus similiter, crebro defigens oculos in eos, qui circumsistebant, et nutu jubens, ut sancti comprehenderentur. Deinde cum ministri quod volebat, non possent intelligere, præ ira et dubitatione effectus semiinsanus, rupit chlamydem, et id quod fibulatorium dicitur, humi disjecit, et sic eos, qui aderant, vocabat ad misericordiam. Christi autem milites et ipsos et tyrannum miserati propter amentiam, O hominum cæcitatem! dixerunt, quod sua sponte claudentes oculos, intueri nolunt lucem veritatis. Sed aperi, Domine, oculos cordis eorum, ut illuminentur luce Dei cognitionis, et tua considerent miracula, et adorandum et sanctum tuum nomen significent. Cum hæc autem essent precati, eis apparet exercitus angelorum, quorum splendorem ferre non valentes, qui oculis animæ caligebant, humi ceciderunt. Καταμαθὼν οὖν τῆς αὐτοῦ γνώμης, τὸ ἀῤῥαγές τε καὶ ἀμετάθετον, εὐθὺς αὐτῷ τὸν διὰ ξίφους ἐπυηφίζετο θάνατον, ἐν τιμωρίας μέρει, τὰ εὐκταιότατα χαρισάμενος. Πρὸς ὃν οἱ ἅγιοι μάρτυρες, Εὐθυμῶν, εἶπον, ἀδελφὲ, τὴν μακαρίαν ὁδὸν πορεύου· μακάριος γὰρ εἶ τῆς ἀποδημίας, ἀνθ᾿ ὧν καὶ πρῶτος ἡμῶν τῇ ποθουμένῃ παραστήσῃ Τριάδι, καὶ τοῦ σοῦ καμάτου λήψῃ τὰς ἀμοιβὰς, φωτὸς αἰωνίου καὶ ἀϊδίου δόξης καταξιούμενος. Nos enim, qui tibi cedimus, videmus pati potius, quam Christi martyres. Tyrannus autem conturbatus repentina ejus mutatione, iram quidem apud se celat, sed mansuetudinem simulans apud Aphthonium (hoc enim erat nomen ei, qui crediderat): Jube eos, inquit, cito egredi ex lebetibus. Ille autem magna ejus audacia et fiducia obstupefactus, bonis verbis et honorum promissis cum conabatur potius inducere, dicens: Ego tibi causa fui hujus mutationis, qui te tamdiu reliqui in ea, qua fuisti collocatus, militia, et non sum te majoribus dignatus honoribus; sed si quid hucusque defecimus, id jam implere studebimus. Nihil ergo veritus, die libere, quasnam cupias dignitates, tuam enim petitionem nequaquam omnino renuemus. A Christo autem recens electus miles, Desidero, inquit, o rex, eam accipere militiam, quam ipse non potes praebere: sed solus rex immortalis, qui scit etiam dona largiri immortalia. Cum ergo ejus firmam et stabilem didicisset sententiam, eum statim condemnat ut ei caput feriatur gladio, pro supplicio ea largitus, quae erant ei maxime optanda. Qui cum lubenter eam accepisset sententiam: Ago tibi gratias, inquit, Domine, quod me servas, qui sum indignus, propter magnam tuam et ineffabilem misericordiam. XII. Deinde ad sanctos conversus: Ego quidem, inquit, o domini mei dilecti, ingredior vitam mihi propositam. Rogo autem vestram benignam et misericordem animam, ne recordemini eorum quae jussus in vos feci: potius vero pro me rogetis communem Dominum, ut sit mihi clemens et propitius, et ne me separet a vestra portione. Cui sancti martyres: Bono animo, dixerunt, o frater, beatam vitam ingredere. Beatus enim es propter tuam decessum, quod primus nostrum sisteris coram optata Trinitate, et tui laboris accipies remunerationes, aeternam lucem consequens et gloriam sempiternam. Quocirca tu potius memento tuorum sociorum, et precare, ut cum quod superest certaminis perfecerimus, absque ullo impedimento eadem, quae tu, praemia et eamdem adipiscamur haereditatem. Qui autem forte aderant Christiani, cum veluti ad quemdam thesaurum accurrissent, et mundis sindonibus pretiosas ejus reliquias involvissent, in loculo deposuerunt, consummationem sociorum ejus adhuc exspectantes, ut majores divitias sic sibi reconderent.
XIII. Post hæc sanctis dicit rex : Ne existimetis, inquit, me vobis æque levem ac facilem mortem allaturum, atque male perdendo Aphthonio. Nam paulatim acerbissimis tormentis carnes vestras consumens, miserabilem vobis mortem afferam. Hæc cum dixisset, bubulos jubet afferri culeos, et sanctos in eos injici martyres, deinde in aquam immiti. Hoc autem cum factum esset maxima celeritate, eis beatus apparet Aphthonius simul cum tribus, indutis candidis vestibus, quorum divina virtute disrumpuntur culei, et terræ illæsi redduntur ab eis martyres. Cum sic ergo conspecti fuissent a tyranno, arbitrans ille non fuisse factum, quod ipse jusserat, statim surrexit ad supplicium sumendum de lictoribus, et statim jussit eorum manus amputari gladio; erant autem quatuor numero. Deinde eos sic pro sanctis immitti in profundum maris. Quod quidem cum fieret, et mari traderentur, exclamabant : Domine, excipe nostros spiritus, et cum sanctis tuis martyribus nos, etsi simus indigni, connumera. Deo autem invisus et plane dæmon Saporius, cum statim beatos viros, Acindynum, inquam, et Pegasium, et Anempodistum jussisset includi in carcere, ipse seipsum gravissime cruciabat, anxius propter res præsentes, et magnis animi fluctibus agitatus et nec cibum quidem omnino sumere sustinens.

Iis ergo, qui primas partes apud eum obtinent, convocatis : Cur me solum, inquit, reliquistis in his, quæ sunt proposita, laborare : ipsi autem otiosum delegistis silentium, nec verbo, nec facto ullam opem mihi ferentes? Sed si aliquid quidem novi et insoliti velim afferre, vos statim habeo resistentes et reluctantes. Nunc autem cum nihil novi introducatur, sed patrium nostrum dogma despiciatur et ne cadat veniat in periculum, et nulli vestrum veniat in mentem, ne vultu quidem dolorem præ se ferre, de summa rei plane venio in dubium, et mærore enecor. Ἐπεὶ οὖν οὕτως ὤφθησαν τῷ τυράννῳ, δόξας ἐκεῖνος αὐτὸ κεκελευσμένον ὑπ᾿ αὐτοῦ τελεσθῆναι, πρὸς τὴν κατὰ τῶν δημίων εὐθὺς ἀνίστατο τιμωρίαν, καὶ ξίφει τὰς χεῖρας αὐτῶν ἐκκοπῆναι διεκελεύσατο· τέσσαρες δὲ τὸν ἀριθμὸν ἦσαν. III. That the Martyrdom of the Saints was Endured with Superhuman Strength
III. Then, therefore, he commands them to be stretched out by four men and beaten over the whole body with rods. But they, when they had lifted their whole mind to God, were so affected, as if, while they were being beaten with rods, they were present as spectators of others suffering. On that account, they neglected the blows; but they sang a fitting canticle to God, saying: "Thou hast seen, O Lord; do not remain silent, and do not depart from us." Then the lictors who were tormenting them, already exhausted, because the torments were produced for a longer time, others take their place. But when the tyrant saw this patience of the martyrs, which was above man, and that they bore the blows as if they were some vain thing without pain, he was led into the greatest admiration and seized by dizziness, and he falls backward, so that those present thought he was dead. Then, when he had recovered, even if not to that which was fitting and the truth, but when he ought to have been led to piety by the endurance of the athletes, which surpassed man, he appeared to be the more overcome by anger, and he orders the martyrs to be stretched with ropes in the air, and fire to be lit beneath them, so that by the double torment their souls might the more quickly break forth. But they, stronger than any iron and any adamant, as if showing a more recent and greater endurance toward the torment, and not in a living body and one feeling pain, but
rather in some inanimate object, endured the punishment; they cared nothing for it, but turned their tongue and mind toward prayer. "You who are our illumination," they said, "Christ our God, who for our sake bore insults and were stretched upon the wood, and before those who were killing You prayed, establishing for us the bounds of long-suffering and forbearance, look upon our humiliation and affliction, and deliver us from the machinations of this tyrant's wicked soul. Let all know that we have You as God in heaven, who can do all things, and Your Father who is without beginning, and Your adorable and most holy Spirit, forever. Amen."
IV. As they prayed these things, the Lord appeared to them in our own form, and loosed their bonds, and cooled the fire, just as He once did for the youths in Assyria; and thus the saints, unharmed and safe, stood before the king. But he, being utterly dull and senseless, was neither struck with awe by the paradox of the sight, nor moved by the unconquerable endurance of the martyrs. "What do you think," he said, calling them by name, "have these small torments persuaded you to depart from your ruinous deception, and to yield to our decrees? Or do you wish to persist still in your error, trusting in your sorceries?" The blessed Acindynus answered, "We neither know sorceries, nor have we ever learned them, nor have we ever used such deceptions. But since you suppose the insuperable power of God and the signs above nature to be sorcery, and imagine them to be delusions and not truth, you shall henceforth be speechless, and your tongue bound, that you may have the testimony of God's power from your own self. If, therefore, you are able to speak, tell us, to which gods do you command us to sacrifice?" And there was no voice and no hearing; but just like his own gods, he remained speechless, indicating the desire of his soul by a nod alone. The servants of Christ, reproving and mocking his folly, said, "We are departing; but you do not say anything to us at all."
V. When the saints pretended to withdraw, the king was likewise speechless, frequently staring at those standing around, and by nods commanding the seizure of the saints. Then, when the servants were unable to understand what he wanted, he became half-mad with perplexity and anger, and tore his cloak, and threw to the ground what is called the *biblatorium*, and thus appealed to those present for pity. But the soldiers of Christ, having pity on both them and the tyrant because of their mindlessness, said, "O blindness of men! Because, closing their eyes of their own will, they do not wish to gaze at the light of the truth. But open, Lord, the eyes of their heart, so that they may be enlightened by the light of Your knowledge, and may discern Your wonders, and may glorify Your adorable and most holy name." As they prayed these things, an angelic host appears to them, the splendor of which those who were dim-sighted in the eyes of the soul were unable to perceive.
But those holy [men], A perceiving the customary succor of God, addressed their thanksgiving to him, saying: "O God our refuge and strength, a helper in afflictions which have found us greatly; but stand up, and bring us help yet again, and deliver us from the surrounding distresses, for the sake of thy name."
VI. The son of perdition, however, understanding nothing from this, nor becoming any better himself, but as if bereft of his senses, began to send forth groans, and beat his face, and was like one who is perplexed and knows not what to do. When the lover of mankind, the disciple of the lover of mankind Christ, Acindynus, had beheld him thus affected, overcome by sorrow and shedding tears over the hard-heartedness of his heart, he turned to him and said: "Since, O man, neither the discipline of God hath made thee understand His power, but thou of thine own accord thus remainest wicked, rejoicing in the darkness of impiety and embracing ignorance, may it be that thou hast authority to speak without hindrance, and that there be nothing which obstructs thy tongue." And to him, the bonds of the tongue are immediately loosened, but the eyes of the soul are not at all loosened from the cloud which obstructed them, but, as if nothing new had happened, and he had suffered nothing, he continued to execute those things which he did before, and against those who were standing around, from the doubt of his mind, he wished to cast anger, being full of shame. Acindynus then said, "And he, and his companions, Pegasius and Anempodistus, will receive the end which is meet; but as for you, O vilest of all ministers, I will not at all allow [you to escape], but will treat you as is fitting; and [I demand] rightful satisfaction for the disobedience." VII. When they had said these things, a voice from heaven was heard, saying: "Since by your deeds you have confirmed your faith, your petitions shall lead to fulfillment." For the divine voice was immediately followed by the effects. For as many of those standing around as were deemed worthy to hear, exclaimed: "One alone is the true God whom the martyrs worship, one strong, one invincible, and besides him there is no God. Blessed, therefore, are you, who have become martyrs of his presence on earth, and by his
surrender of your souls to death, have betrothed yourselves to a death that wins eternal life. But we also beseech you to hold out a hand to us and draw us up from the abyss of ruin.
The martyrs, having considered their response to this prayer, and having lifted their eyes to the heavens, said: "O God most high, look down upon thy servants who call upon thy name in truth, and bestow a voluntary rain upon thy new inheritance—to those, I mean, who have now believed in thy name—so that this rain, which proceeds from thee, may be a medicine to them, purging away the diseased matter of sin, and may all know that thou alone art God, and that all things yield to thy power, O King."
At the end of the prayer, lightning flashed and thunder broke forth, and a torrential rain poured down, such that the adversaries, seized with fear, were scattered this way and that, leaving only those who had believed in Christ, who remained in the company of the holy martyrs. To these the saints said: "Do not fear, you who have dedicated yourselves to Christ, for it is even for your sake that these things have come to pass."
VIII.
When, therefore, they were sending up their doxology to God in one voice, a multitude of angels appeared, clothing them in white robes, which perhaps signified the cleansing of the soul through faith and water. The king, however, whose madness could not be contained, as was his wont, said to the saints: "The fact that I spared your youth and your prime has made you show contempt for my power. But you shall surely know through your actions that you are doing things that are useless and of no benefit to yourselves. Nevertheless, since we possess as much patience as we do power, we tolerate you and still advise you in your folly to be persuaded, and to gain this sweet sun, and not to draw upon yourselves an untimely and bitter death, but to draw near and sacrifice to the sovereign gods."
The holy Acindynus said: "We know, he said, one God who is in the heavens, and him alone we worship, who is able to cast both body and soul into eternal fire." To this the king said: "And I myself, he said, wish for you to worship one God." "And how," said the martyr, "do you command us to sacrifice to him?" "By following me," he said, "and whatever you see me doing, that you also do." "But you," the martyrs said, "will do as you know how; we, in turn, will act according to that which we have been taught."

Having said this, he stepped onto the chariot and invited the saints to sit with him; but they said: "Far be it from us, O King, to fall into such insolence as to dare to become your fellow-assessors, and so to insult your power." The Christians who happened to be present ran as if to some treasure, and having wrapped his precious remains in clean linen cloths, they placed them in a coffin, still expecting the completion of his fellow-athletes, that they might thus store up for themselves greater wealth.
XIII. After these things, the king said to the holy ones: "Do not suppose," he said, "that I will prepare for you a death as light and easy as that which occurred for the wretch Aphthonius. When they were thus seen by the tyrant, he, thinking that what he had ordered had been carried out by them, immediately rose up to enact punishment upon the executioners, and he ordered their hands to be cut off with a sword; there were four of them in number. When this had come to pass, and they were being delivered to the sea, they cried out: "Lord, receive our spirits, and count us worthy, though we are unworthy, to be numbered with your holy martyrs." Sapor, the God-hated man and a very demon, having ordered the blessed men—I mean Acindynus, Pegasius, and Anempodistus—to be shut up in prison, was himself the most grievous punisher of himself, fretting over current events and being tossed by many turmoils in his soul, and not even consenting to take any food at all.

XV. The Martyrdom of Elpidephorus and the Others
When these things had been spoken with suspicion to Sapor, the blessed Elpidephorus said: "Since I am the first of the senate, these men rightfully yield the place of speaking to me. But let them also say whatever occurs to each one, since you wish, O King, to hear the truth in everything." But one of the senators, having his soul illumined by divine grace, said: "And what is surprising to the truths, O King, if we yield to Elpidephorus, who is the first of the senate, and who answers nothing foolish or unseemly, the right to speak?" The king, having perceived the truth, said: "I see that your mind is not rightly disposed toward our power." Philologus, a man by name, younger in age, most sharp in intellect, and most ardent in zeal, said: "O King, the evil suspicion that has vainly attacked your soul concerning us will not end in a good outcome for us. For you are full of suspicion, intending to condemn all to destruction." The tyrant, filled with anger in his soul at the sudden boldness of Philologus, said: "Who even asked you, so that you should give so sharp an answer?" To this that noble man, not considering the anger of the tyrant, nor setting any store by his power, said: "But when someone speaks the truth, O King, will you need to be angry immediately?" Then looking also at the senators who were standing by: "By your salvation," he said, "one ought not to answer such a man. For where could a soul filled with madness receive a true word?" Upon these things, the tyrant, filled with anger after the likeness of the serpent, as the divine David says, orders soldiers to stand before the doors, so that they might dispatch with the sword those who contradicted him, and first of all Elpidephorus. But one Callistratus, a friend of the king, clinging to him by the neck, begged him to restrain his anger; and all who were present spoke boldly: "Most miserable of kings," they said, "you will know now precisely that we do not think as you do, nor do we strive equally for your impiety." And having said these things, they went out, thus leaving him. But he, being night and altogether a son of darkness, held to the night as a collaborator for his most evil deeds; and at dawn, sitting upon the tribunal, he orders three pits to be dug, and wild beasts and reptiles to be cast into them, and Saint Acindynus and his companions to be thrown in with them.
XVI. The Suffering of the Saints in the Pits
When therefore they had cast the saints into them, the martyrs were chanting: "O God, O our God, we watch for Thee from light; our soul has thirsted for Thee, has thirsted for Thee
for the living and mighty God. But do Thou act with us according to Thy gentleness, and lead us forth from the pit of misery; for Thy mercy is kind, and Thy goodness ineffable." And at the moment of their prayer, angels appeared shining, driving away the beasts as if with iron rods, and taking each of the saints by the hand, they led them forth from the pits unharmed. But the king, though he ought to have understood the power of Christ and ceased at length from attempting the impossible, instead ordered the saints to be suspended and flayed again. And when he saw their flesh being cut away for many hours, and that they were enduring the contests with a noble soul, he commanded them to be taken down from the wood and their heads to be cut off. For he himself grew more weary in inflicting the punishments than the martyrs did in enduring what was inflicted, and the excess of their nobility overcame the immeasurability of his cruelty. And as they were already being led to death, those who had been illumined by them unto piety, together with the senate, said, "Dismiss us, O teachers of piety, who [otherwise] sit on the seat of the pestilent in the midst of the assembly of the wicked, for we have not yet attained to the measure of the age in Christ." But the blessed ones answered them, "Christ our God, who teaches man knowledge, who is the illumination of our souls, He Himself shall illumine you and make you wise, and shall establish you on the foundation of His faith."
XVII. These things were reported to the king, and [it was told him] how a great multitude was following the saints, hindering their slaughter. He immediately sent a band of soldiers against those who were following the saints, with orders to drive them away. But those noble men, who had determined to resist sin unto the end, regarding this as nothing, chose rather to die with the saints. When the tyrant learned again of their steadfastness, he ordered all of them to be slain together with the saints, making their love of piety a pretext for his desire to kill men. And being incited by certain men who were zealous for the idols—as though the senate were the cause of so many evils—he ordered the blessed Elpidephorus to be brought, seeing that he held the first honors among them. He, turning to the unconquered martyrs who were not yet perfected, said, "Pray for me, Fathers in the spirit, my guides in the faith of Christ, that I too may not fall away from your company." To whom the saints said, "Take courage, brother, take courage and do not be downcast; for you shall precede us, being the first to be presented to the heavenly Master." Having therefore embraced them, he was led to the tyrant; and three others, sharing the same mind and intent, followed with him.

THE MONTH OF NOVEMBER.
THE CONTEST OF THE HOLY AND GLORIOUS MARTYRS OF CHRIST: ACINDYMUS, PEGASIUS, ANEMPODISTUS, APHTHONIUS, AND ELPIDEPHORUS.
I. Although the Persians were the first to worship Christ when He was born of the Virgin, and honored Him with gifts, yet afterward they inhumanely punished the servants of Christ because of Him. This is evident from many other things, and also from the subject now before us. For when that proud Sapor held the Persian scepter, and had inclined toward tyranny, and had been incited to persecute those Christians who were under his power, a great darkness covered the Church of Christ, and it was filled with turmoil and confusion. There was no Persian who held back from fiercely defending his own religion. Therefore, those who worshiped Christ were daily accused and delivered up to the royal hand; among whom they also denounced to the king the blessed Acindymus, Pegasius, and Anempodistus, who were hiding in a certain small house, as men who held the opinion of the Christians, and who were teaching the dogma of Christ to many of those who frequented them, and persuading them to despise the royal decrees. And he, being immediately highly enraged, ordered the saints to be seized and brought before him.
