ANALECTA
De unguento seu oleo e S. Demetrii tumulo prodigiose scaturiente aliisque adhuc ejusdem Sancti miraculis, partim e scriptoribus typorum beneficio jam vulgatis, partim e Joannis Stauracii ac Demetrii Chrysoloræ lucubrationibus nondum editis accepta.
§ I. Miracula, sæculo decimo posteriora, quæ Sanctus in Thessalonicæ favorem patrasse narratur.
1. Fuisse enimvero, Mauritio imperii Romani habenas moderante, Thessalonicam prodigioso prorsus modo adversus unitam Avarum aliarumque barbaricarum nationum vim a sancto Demetrio, patrono suo, defensam, expugnationisque simul ac direptionis periculo præsentissimo ereptam, Joannes, Thessalonicensis archiepiscopus, supra sæpissime laudatus, qui rei gestæ interfuit, miraculorum, a sancto Demetrio patratorum, lib. I jam dato, cap. 13 et binis seqq., indubitatum facit. Atque illud quidem, ut ex Adnotatis, dicto capiti 15 subnexis liquet, sæculo sexto ad finem inclinante seu anno 597 evenit; fuisse porro etiam eamdem civitatem sæculo septimo, cum idem, quem proxime laudavi, Joannes Thessalonicensem ecclesiam jam moderaretur, atque adhuc serius, sæculo octavo scilicet, modo pariter prorsus mirabili a Sancto nostro adversus Barbaros propugnatam, e patralorum abs hoc miraculorum lib. II, pariter jam dato, si non; quod hujus auctor (adi Commentarium prævium num. 24) fidem undequaque integram haud mereatur, omnino indubitatum, verosimillimum saltem apparet. Et vero, non tantum sæculo septimo ac sequenti, sed etiam ad annum usque 904 Thessalonicam inimicorum omnium impetu, patrocinante sancto Demetrio, superiorem permansisse, variisque belli periculis fuisse ereptam, Joannes Cameniata supra adhuc laudatus, qui tum floruit, in sua de Thessalonicæ per Saracenos excidio lucubratione, ac Nicolaus patriarcha, ecclesiam Constantinopolitanam tunc temporis moderatus, in sua de eodem luctuoso eventu Monodia non obscure indicant.

ANALECTA
Concerning the unguent or oil miraculously flowing from the tomb of St. Demetrius and other miracles of the same Saint, taken partly from writers already published through the benefit of the printing press, and partly from the works of John Stauracius and Demetrius Chrysoloras not yet published.
§ 1. Miracles later than the tenth century, which the Saint is said to have performed in Thessalonica.
1. That in truth, while Maurice held the reins of the Roman Empire, Thessalonica was defended against the united force of the Avars and other barbarian nations in a thoroughly miraculous manner by St. Demetrius, its patron, and simultaneously snatched from the most imminent danger of being stormed and plundered, is made indubitable by John, Archbishop of Thessalonica, cited very often above, who was present at the event, in the first book of the miracles performed by St. Demetrius, already provided, chap. 43 and the two following. And indeed, as is clear from the Annotations subjoined to the said 13th chapter, this happened toward the end of the sixth century, or in the year 597. Furthermore, that the same city was, in the seventh century, when the same John whom I have just praised was already governing the Thessalonian church, and even later still, namely in the eighth century, protected against the Barbarians by our Saint in a manner likewise absolutely miraculous, appears, if not absolutely indubitable—because the author of these (see the preliminary commentary, no. 24) does not merit complete faith in every respect—at least highly probable, from the second book of the miracles performed by him, also already cited. And truly, that not only in the seventh and following centuries, but even up to the year 904, Thessalonica remained superior to the attack of all enemies, with St. Demetrius patronizing it, and was snatched from various perils of war, John Cameniata, cited above, who flourished at that time, in his work on the destruction of Thessalonica by the Saracens, and Patriarch Nicholas, who then governed the Constantinopolitan church, in his Monody on the same mournful event, indicate not obscurely.
2. But that city, once very famous, as an eyewitness—the same one whom I shall praise again shortly, John Cameniata, published by Berthold Nihusius at Cologne in 1653, and later reprinted by Combefis in the *Byzantine History* after the writers of Theophanes in 1685 at Paris—attests that it was stormed, plundered, and devastated by the Saracens who were warring against Leo of the East, surnamed the Wise, under the leadership of the impious apostate Leo Tripolites, while a large number of inhabitants were led away into captivity. And indeed, given that up to that time Thessalonica had stood unshaken against all the machinations of its enemies under the patronage of St. Demetrius, as was believed everywhere by everyone, this event resulted in the greatest wonder. Hear how Nicholas the patriarch speaks, fittingly for our present purpose, in the place cited above, in Greek words, which I will join to the Greek text translated into the Latin language: "Where, for me, Demetrius martyr, is the invincible alliance in war? How did you disregard your city while it was being plundered? How, under you as its guardian, did it come to experience such evils, from the time the sun first shone upon it, when it had been inaccessible to enemies? How did you endure the brow of the impious as they began to attack your holy protection? How did you bear these things and endure to the very end?" Thus that illustrious man, also called the Mystic, patriarch of Constantinople, reveals his astonishment regarding the captured and devastated Thessalonica—which, up to his own time, had been safe under the powerful patronage of St. Demetrius—and there is no doubt that this was the common feeling of all men at that time, which Nicholas expresses in the given words.
3. But why Demetrius, who, as can be seen in the earlier books of his miracles, had once protected his city so often and in so many diverse and miraculous ways against the fiercest enemies, should have permitted it then to be stormed and overthrown by the Saracens, the enemies of Christ, are the deep judgments of God, which are also to be feared by us. Meanwhile the Saint, who at that time both on account of miracles of every kind performed in great number, and especially because Thessalonica had been miraculously saved from enemies more than once, had been adorned with the most glorious titles of a great martyr and wonder-worker by the Greeks, both in the work of John of Thessalonica mentioned above and in the work of the anonymous writer who continued John, protected that native city of his once again by his aid against its enemies after it had risen from the ruin brought about by the Saracens and had repaired the past disaster, although at length it was stormed by the Turks in the year 1429—which are again the deep and inscrutable judgments of God—and held in firm possession, remaining so up to this present day.
Since it suffices to have discussed, in whatever way, the miracle of our Saint regarding the relief of Thessalonica as it is reported by Cedrenus, let us turn our conversation to another matter.
8. Since Peter and Asan, the brothers already mentioned, through whose insistence the Bulgarians had shaken off the Greek yoke after the year 1185 in their native Bulgaria—a land they had labored to bring into freedom, and where, having met with successful results, they had reigned for some time—had been removed from the living, the Bulgarian kingdom passed to their brother John, who is called by others Calo-John, or Joannitza, or even Joannitius. This man, furthermore, after many wars, finally besieged Thessalonica in the year 1207 with a vast army; but in this military expedition, no less than with the aforementioned Alusianus, he had poor fortune, as George Acropolita, a writer of the thirteenth century, testifies, having found Saint Demetrios himself to be his enemy. As to this final point, there is in our manuscript—in the long narrative taken from the manuscript of Leo Allatius and noted there, and in the annotations to the history of Acropolita compiled by Allatius himself and appended to the thirteenth chapter—the account of John Stauracius, who has been cited here more than once. I shall quote some of the words of this writer, which are most relevant here as published by Du Cange in the place soon to be cited, having first brought forward some words of George Acropolita which likewise pertain to the matter. Thus this writer, in his *History* published in Paris in the year 1651, provides the following words, which almost all appear later again in the *Chronicon Compendiarium* published in Paris in the same year on page 115, in the chapter concerning the aforementioned John or Joannitius or even Joannitza, king of the Bulgarians: "He razed to the foundations Philippopolis, a very illustrious and eminent city which lies near the Hebrus, and subsequently all the others—Heraclea, Panium, Rhaedestus, Chariupolis, Trajanupolis, Macre, Claudiupolis, Mosynupolis, Peritheorion, and very many others besides, which it is not worth the labor to enumerate. He settled them with his own colonies, compensating, as he himself used to say, for the losses with which the Emperor Basil had afflicted the Bulgarians; and he confessed that he was called the Bulgar-slayer, while he himself styled himself the Roman-slayer. Continuing as far as Thessalonica, he lost his life to a side-ailment or, as others asserted, was executed by the wrath of the Divinity. For he dreamed that he was struck in the side by a spear by an armed man; for no one—and I do not lie—ever afflicted the Romans with as many calamities in human memory as this man, so much so that the name 'dog' was imposed upon him, and he was called Scylo-John by everyone." Thus far Acropolita. That the death of John, king of the Bulgarians, while he was besieging Thessalonica, did not occur by natural causes, but was inflicted by divine power because the Divinity was angered by the crimes perpetrated by him, is here quite openly taught; and that this divine power, by which the Bulgarian king John, while besieging Thessalonica, perished in a miserable manner, proceeded from Saint Demetrios according to the opinion of some, John Stauracius recounts in the aforementioned place in Leo Allatius in Greek words. So that I may keep my promise, I transcribe them here now together with a Latin version added at the foot.
10. Therefore, they have it thus: "Then, yes, then at the dead of night, God finally polished the sword of His own vengeance, and set it on fire with the zeal of the martyr. And suddenly the truly vigilant guardian and patron of us, the great soldier of the great King, Demetrius, is seen by the Bulgarian king on a white horse, and he immediately casts a mortal blow upon the wretch. And he, crying out with a mighty voice, called out to the high commander of his army, Manastras, as if he had been wounded by him; for he seemed to see him riding on a noble white horse, entering the inside of the tent, and plunging the spear which he was carrying into his heart. And because of this, and many times over, he was rattled by the very shouts, and he leaped from his bed—for his tent was in the neighborhood—and he came in, and he saw, and he appeared, and he heard: 'Did you not, just a little while ago, appearing here on your white horse, slaughter me with a spear?' And he was terrified and said, 'Not I, O King, have compassion, have pity; it is not this, it is not this. A phantom has come to you, not the truth.' The other, having received the wound deep within, was writhing terribly, and he cried out and wailed. A great amount of the purest blood flowed from beneath, so that your foot might be bathed, Demetrius, in the blood of your enemies for the sake of this city of yours. And he already began to lose consciousness. Seeing this, the high commander of his forces, Manastras, having quickly lifted him onto his back, flees headlong, and stealing his safety, retreats with all the speed he has, and with him and in his company is all that myriad-fold army, so quickly, as if some Bulgarians were pursuing these boasted ones. For fear and trembling came upon them by some strange countermanding force; but indeed, he was not to live long, but on that very day toward sunset, according to the living creature one-day-living in the histories..."
He dies violently; thus the wretch, having reaped the fruits of his own wickedness and vanity, having drained the empty dregs of wrath and poured out the righteous recompense of his entrails, full of much unjust blood, he was later called, he whom the great Demetrius clearly slew when he marched against Thessalonica, as is recorded in his miracles.
14. Does John, or Joannitius, King of the Bulgarians—as the account handed down in these words, recited here in both Greek and Latin, reports—when he was besieging Thessalonica, truly and in fact perish, having been slain by Saint Demetrius, who brought aid to this his native city, through the mediation of some strange turn of events? The author, as I showed in preliminary commentary no. 26, flourished most probably in the thirteenth century, and therefore it happened in the same century in which John, King of the Bulgarians, was killed at the siege of Thessalonica, as mentioned above. But since, as I shall show below, our manuscript no. 193, page 93, not obscurely indicates that the event that occurred to the Emperor Emmanuel Comnenus (who reigned from 1143 to 1180) at the hands of Saint Demetrius happened long before his time, and it seems that he flourished no earlier than late in the same thirteenth century, so from the death of John, King of the Bulgarians, which occurred in 1207 according to the texts, he was much further removed than he could win certain and unquestionable credit for by attributing it to Saint Demetrius while he was alive. Nor, even if he had said it was at the beginning of the said century, would it be necessary to speak otherwise about it in this matter. For that John, King of the Bulgarians, was killed by Saint Demetrius rested only on the rumor of the common people, which prevailed in his age—so that I might say no more—and he committed it to writing. This is especially clear from the fact that George Acropolita, who also flourished in the thirteenth century, records in the words transcribed above that the death of the aforementioned Bulgarian king was attributed by some to at least divine power, and thus he seems to indicate that the rumor was born from the people who did this.
15. But everyone knows how often rumor is wont to be false, especially if it is not common; I would wish it to be observed from the words of Acropolita that that rumor was not common, and that it may easily have happened that, from the dream of King John, which the same Acropolita mentions there, that sinister rumor regarding the death inflicted upon that prince by Saint Demetrius was spread, and was soon admitted by the overly credulous too easily, even though meanwhile John had finished his life not by a violent, but by a natural death. Moreover, Acropolita, whom some might perhaps think supports Stauracius, makes mention only generally of the wrath of the divine Power, by which John, King of the Bulgarians, was killed, but he does not expressly mention Saint Demetrius, nor have I been able to find as yet any writer of known faith or authority who agrees with John Stauracius. Allatius indeed adds to the words of John Stauracius which he transcribed at the place cited above: "In the Vatican manuscript CLXIII, in the margin of the History of Choniates, it is read: This is the impious Scylo-John, who was later called that, whom the great Demetrius clearly killed when he marched against Thessalonica, as is recorded in the miracles of the same."
