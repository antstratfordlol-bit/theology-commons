II. Ea patriam quidem et civitatem habuit Antiochiam. Cum esset autem et divitiis insignis, et eximia pulchritudine, non recte usa est divitiis, neque pulchritudinem corporis ita possedit, ut non læderetur pulchritudo animæ. Sed cum illa quidem illi naturaliter valde floreret, plurimo etiam uteretur artificio, pulchritudo animæ marcescebat et flaccescebat. Erat vero ei multum quidem auri, quod corpus exornabat, multum autem etiam, quod corpus serviebat usibus, et multitudo comitum, qui partim quidem præcedebant, partim autem sequebantur. Quibus quidem rebus animi perturbationem habens ignem maxime accensum, illiciensque et urens amatores, inter meretrices (proh dolor!) vitam agebat et agnoscebatur prima Mænadam, quæ erant in civitate. Sed enim non sivit Deus generosam animam et naturam a maligni opprimi tyrannide, futurum videns tanquam quod jam adesset, et præsciens qualis ex quali esset evasura, et volens iis, qui ipsum colunt, dare aliquod exemplum, quod solum velle alicui sufficiat ad hoc, ut fiat honestum (1).
III. Cum itaque si tunc Antiochiæ administrabat sedem pontificalem, usu aliquo ita postulante, eos qui sub se erant, congregasset episcopos, inter quos erant, qui multis præstabant, et erat etiam Nonnus vir et in cæteris admirabilis, et moribus angelicus. Cum ii autem jam essent congregati et sederent pro foribus templi cujusdam ex iis quæ erant in civitate, id autem erat Juliani martyris, philosopharentur autem de iis quæ sunt pia et sursum ferunt, et sacrosanctus Nonnus quamdam animæ utilem apud eos persequeretur doctrinam, accessit Pelagia utens consueto apparatu et habitu, sedensque in quodam vehiculo.  multis autem deducebatur et propinquum aerem colorabat et perfundebat tum odore unguentorum, tum splendore margaritarum. Hanc ergo tam inverecunde, et tam impudenter accedentem videns pastorum chorus, alii quidem, ut qui nihil possent intueri præter ea quæ cernebant, damnabant aspectum ejus meretricium, et ne sustinebant quidem omnino aspicere, sed erubescebant et eam aversabantur. Nonnus autem qui sciebat ex contrariis sæpe colligere contraria, per quod alius sed sequatur gratiam Dei, sine qua nec ipsum veile bonum habere possumus.

Τούτου δὴ γενομένου καὶ τῶν ἐκπεμφθέντων ἐπανελθόντων καὶ τὸ προσταχθὲν ἀγγειλάντων, βουλὴν βουλεύεται γενναίαν καὶ ἀνδρικήν καὶ πρὸς τὴν ἱερὸν παρρησιάζεται Νόννον, καὶ δὴ καὶ λαβοῦσα χάρτην διεχάραξεν οὕτως· Ἤκουσά σου τῶν Χριστιανῶν, Χριστοῦ μαθητὰ, ὡς φησί που τῶν ἑαυτοῦ λόγων ὁ σὸς Δεσπότης· « Οὐκ ἦλθον καλέσαι δικαίους, ἀλλὰ ἁμαρτωλοὺς εἰς μετάνοιαν » καὶ ὡς δὴ καὶ τελώναις συναυλισθῇ, καὶ πόρναις συναυλεστράφη, καὶ Σαμαρείτιδι πρὸς τὸ φρέαρ ὡμίλησε, καὶ λόγου μετέσχε τε καὶ μετέδωκεν ὃν τρέμει τὰ χερουβίμ, καὶ οὐδὲ βλέπειν στέγει τὰ σεραφίμ. Ἀλλ᾿ εἴ τι σὺ τοιούτου μὲν δοῦλος δεσπότου, τοιούτου δὲ μαθητὴς διδασκάλου, δεῖξον τοῖς ἔργοις, καὶ μὴ βδελύξῃ πόρνην ὁμογενῆ καὶ ὁμόδουλον, ἀλλὰ καὶ δεομένην τῆς σῆς ἱερᾶς ἀξίωσον θέας, καὶ ἐξομολογουμένην δέξαι, καὶ σῶσον ἀπολλυμένην, καὶ σοῦ μόνου ἐξεχομένην καὶ σοὶ τὰς ἐλπίδας σαλεύουσαν.
Η΄. Ταῦτα δεξάμενος ὁ ἱερὸς ᾐδέσθη ἀνὴρ, καὶ μόνῳ μὲν ἀντεπιστέλλει μὴ προσελθεῖν, ἤδη δέ τε καὶ τῶν λοιπῶν συμπαρόντων ἀρχιερέων φανεροῦν ἑαυτὴν καὶ ὃ βούλοιτο λέγειν καὶ ἐκκαλύπτειν τὰ ἑαυτῆς. Ἡ δὲ τοῦτου τυχούσα τὸν καιρὸν ἁρπάζει, καὶ διώκει τὸν σώζοντα. Καὶ καταλαβοῦσα τὴν ἐκκλησίαν, πόρνη τις ἦν ἄλλη κατὰ γῆς ἐῤῥιμμένη καὶ κλίνουσα τὸ γόνυ καὶ τὴν ψυχὴν καὶ πόδας βρέχουσα κἂν μὴ δεσποτικούς, ἀλλ᾿ οὖν ἱερατικοὺς καὶ ὡραίους καὶ ψυχῆς εἰρήνην ἀληθῶς εἰπεῖν εὐαγγελιζομένους, καὶ οὐδ᾿ ἂν εἴ τι γένοιτο μεθήσειν πρότερον ἔφασκε πρὶν ἢ τὴν δι᾿ ὕδατος λάβῃ καὶ Πνεύματος ἀναγέννησιν. Καὶ ἦν ἀγὼν ἀμφοτέροις τῷ μὲν πεῖσαι ταύτην τῶν ποδῶν ἀφεμένην ἐξαναστῆναι, τῇ δὲ τούτων ἔχεσθαι μᾶλλον. Καὶ πρὸ τοῦ λουτροῦ κάθαρσις ἦν ἄλλη καὶ δακρύων λουτρῶν, τοῦ πνευματικωτέρου δηλαδὴ τὸ ἐπιπονώτερον. Ταῦτα καὶ παρὰ τῶν ὁρώντων δάκρουν ἐξεκαλεῖτο, καὶ ἦν αὐτοῖς λύπη τις, ἐκπλήξει καὶ ἡδονῇ σύγκρατος, τῶν δακρύων μὲν αὐτῇ κοινωνούντων καὶ συναλγούντων, καὶ τὴν πίστιν ἐκπληττομένων, χαιρόντων δὲ καὶ ἡδομένων τῇ σωτηρίᾳ.

VII. Cum hoc autem factum esset et reversi es- sent, qui missi fuerant, et renuntiassent id quod fuerat imperatum, generosum et virile capit consilium, et cum accepisset chartam, sacrosancto Nonno libere sic scribit : Audivi, o discipule Dei Christianorum, quod alicubi in iis quæ de eo scripta sunt, dicit tuus Dominus : « Non veni vocare justos, sed peccatores ad pœnitentiam », et quod in mensa accubuit una cum publicanis, et versatus fuit simul cum meretricibus, et cum Samaritana locutus est ad puteum, et verba dedit vicissim et accepit, quem timet Cherubin, et nec aspicere quidem sustinent Seraphin. Itaque si tu es servus talis Domini, et discipulus talis magistri, id osterde factis, et nec abhorreas ejusdem tecum generis meretricem et conservam, sed etiam rogantem dignare sacro tuo aspectu, et suscipe confitentem, et serva pereuntem, et a te solo pendentem, et spem in te collocantem.
VIII. Hæc cum accepisset vir sacrosanctus, est revertus, et rescribit ne ad solum quidem accedat, sed cum jam simul reliqui affuerint pontifices, se sistat et dicat quod velit, et ea quæ spectant ad ipsam aperiat. Illa autem cum hoc impetrasset, arripit occasionem et sequitur eum qui dat salutem. Et cum venisset ad ecclesiam, erat quædam alia meretrix humi jacens, et genu flectens et animam, et pedes rigans, si non Dominicos, at sacerdotales quidem certe, et speciosos et pacem animæ (ut vere dicam) evangelizantes. Quæ dicebat, nulla omnino fore ratione, ut aliquid disceret, priusquam regenerationem accepisset per aquam et Spiritum. Et erat utrisque certamen, illi quidem ut ei persuaderet surgere dimissis eorum pedibus, huic autem ut eis magis hæreret. Et ante lavacrum erat alia purgatio, et lavacrum lacrymarum spiritaliori scilicet laboriosius. Hæc eos qui videbant provocabant ad lacrymas, eratque eis quædam tristitia, stupore et voluptate temperata, ut qui cum flente quidem suas lacrymas conjungerent, et cum dolente dolerent, fidemque ejus stuperent, salute autem ejus gauderent.

X. Sed enim solet nescio quomodo superatus inimicus fieri impudentior. Quamobrem pati non potuit tantum suum dedecus, neque tantam fidelium spiritualem voluptatem. Deinde etiam impudenter accedens ad Pelagiam, eam proditricem vocabat et infidelem. Et, Cur, inquit, tuum abnegasti et es assensa alieno? Illa autem extendit manum et malignum pulsat cruce. Rursus autem feritur cruce, et statim evanescit. Illa vero cum surrexisset, significat quidem ei, quæ ipsam e sancto susceperat baptismo cui nomen erat Romana, luctam adversarii. Confirmatur autem verbis illius, et adversus hostem dirigit aciem, et imponit maligno hanc secundam et certam plagam. LIFE AND CONDUCT OF SAINT PELAGIA OF ANTIOCH
(Surius ad Octobr., cod. Reg. n. 1512.)
I. It is useful and beneficial for all to recount feminine virtue that is in no way inferior to the fortitude of men, and therefore,
all the more so, because a corresponding vice had been introduced beforehand. For in this way, the masculine race would be ambitious not to have its nature surpassed by that of the female; and women, for their part, would strive not to be left behind by the virtue of their kin. Thus, the habit of the evil one—which, for the most part, is hard to wash away—would be removed from both. Knowing this better than others in the case of this blessed Pelagia, who revealed to us the sea of God’s philanthropy, I shall tell her story from the beginning.
II. She had for her homeland and city Antioch. Being illustrious in wealth and distinguished in beauty, she did not use her wealth for good, nor did she so possess the beauty of her body that the beauty of her soul was not harmed. For while the former flourished in her naturally—and she used much artifice upon it—the beauty of her soul was withering and fading. She had much gold that adorned her person, much that served her needs, and a multitude of attendants, some going before and others following after. By these things she especially kindled the fire of the passions, leading on and inflaming her lovers; she lived, alas! among harlots, and was known as the first of the Maenads in the city. But the Lord did not suffer such a noble soul and nature to be oppressed by the tyranny of the evil one; seeing the future as if it were already present, foreknowing what sort she would become, and wishing to give to those who worship Him an example that, for one to become good, the mere will is sufficient.
III. Once, when the one then administering the priestly throne at Antioch had gathered the bishops under him for some need—among whom were many who excelled the crowd, and indeed Nonnus, a man admirable in other respects and angelic in his manner—they were gathered and were sitting before the gates of a certain church in the city. This was the church of the martyr Julian, and they were philosophizing on matters that are pious and lead upward. While the holy Nonnus was pursuing some soul-beneficial teaching before them, Pelagia passed by, using her customary appearance and dress, sitting in a carriage, and escorted by many, staining and perfuming the nearby air with the scent of perfumes and the splendor of her gems. When the choir of pastors saw her moving along so shamelessly and brazenly, the others—for they were unable to see anything beyond what was visible—condemned her meretricious appearance and could not even bear to look at her at all, but blushed and turned away. But Nonnus, who knew how to gather the contrary from the contraries, and by which another might perhaps have received a goad of pleasure into his soul, this man had
a spark for virtue, for which cause he did not turn his eyes away from the harlot; but even when she was present he would look at her intently, and when she departed, he still continued to hold her in his view.
IV. Then, having sighed deeply and being filled with tears, the blessed man wept for himself and called the adornment of that woman his own condemnation; for if she, loving indeed bodies that are subject to corruption—and that too to the destruction of herself—and having a certain genuine beauty blooming in her members, nevertheless does not hesitate to build upon and add artifice to this natural beauty, and to make it more splendid with certain contrivances and painting, so as to steal for herself a bitter pleasure; we, however, to whom the one who is loved is immortal—let me add also that the Lover and the bridal chamber are immortal, and the grace to those who are truly adorned never grows old—are so far from adorning the soul that we are not even ashamed to insult its natural beauty with disgrace. But we inflict injury upon ourselves and miserably exclude ourselves from that ineffable and admirable pleasure, and this even when we are not ignorant of the punishment that hangs over us and the torments that are there. Thus he, not only looking, as I believe, at what was present, but already foreseeing the future with his mind, and knowing that not long after they would be surpassed even by the beauty of her soul, and that she would be seen to yield the harlot to spiritual struggles. This was indeed what this divine and admirable vision from God signified to him.
V. It seemed that while the divine liturgy was being performed, Nonnus himself was standing at the left horn of the altar, and a certain mud-stained dove was flying around him, and would not easily depart, but would fly nearby and trouble him and cause the greatest distress with its foul odor, and this until the catechumens were called by the priest. But what followed after this? It did, indeed, become invisible as long as the time of the mysteries and the whole ministry lasted. But when it was already finished, as he was going out of the church and had reached the threshold, the same dove appeared again, being and doing just as before, until, having stretched out his right hand and taken hold of it, he lowered it into a basin full of water. And that creature, having then cast off the filth from its wings, stood in the air and was borne to such a height that not even he could reach it with his eyes. This portrayed beforehand what was about to happen, and what was done in the night was a prefiguration of what was to happen in the day.
VI. When he had risen in the morning and had shared the vision with some, he arrived at the church together with the other pastors; and when he had taken the New Testament into his hands—the divine Gospel, that is—at the command of the chief pastor, because of the grace of the Spirit that was in him, he spoke to the people. And so there happened what had not happened before, and a matter of things not expected nor
customary: Pelagia, listener to the sacred words, heard of things present and things to come; she heard what the soul is, what its bond is, and how it is separated from the body, and how it rises again and is judged, and at what things it is ashamed. O the wonder! She recognizes her own state, and condemns all her previous ways, and having turned in upon herself, she becomes wholly given over to the good and to repentance, shedding tears that were a match for her former laughter; then, she puts to the use of salvation those things which she had previously used for destruction. She instructs some of her own people, who had come out of the church with the holy Nonnus and followed him, to observe where he dwells, to report it to her as quickly as possible, and not to delay in this commission.
VII. When this had been done, and those who had been sent out had returned and reported what was commanded, she forms a noble and manly resolution, and speaks freely to the holy Nonnus; and having taken paper, she wrote as follows: "I have heard of you, follower of the Christians, a disciple of Christ, as your Master says somewhere in His own words: 'I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance' (Matt. IX, 13); and how He reclined at table with tax collectors, and associated with harlots, and spoke to the Samaritan woman at the well (Joan. IV, 1 sq.), and participated in words and also gave them—He whom the Cherubim fear, and whom the Seraphim do not even sustain their gaze upon. But if you are a servant of such a Master, and a disciple of such a Teacher, show it by your deeds, and do not abhor a harlot of the same race and fellow servant, but deign to grant your sacred sight to one who is asking for it, and receive one who confesses, and save one who is perishing, who clings to you alone and places her hopes in you."
VIII. The holy man, having received this, was struck with awe, and writes back to her that she should not approach him in private, but rather that when the rest of the bishops were present together, she should present herself and say what she wished, and reveal her own affairs. She, having obtained this, seizes the opportunity and follows him who gives salvation. And having reached the church, there she was, another harlot, cast upon the ground, bending the knee and soul, and watering the feet—if not the Lord's, yet certainly the priestly and beautiful ones, and those who truly evangelize the peace of the soul—and she said that there was no way at all that she would let go before she received the regeneration through water and Spirit. And there was a struggle for both: for him, to persuade her to leave the feet and stand up; for her, to cling to them all the more. And before the bath, there was another purification and a bath of tears, more laborious than the more spiritual one, that is to say. These things called forth tears even from those who were watching, and there was among them a certain grief, tempered with astonishment and pleasure, those who were weeping with her and suffering along with her and standing amazed at her faith, rejoicing and finding delight in her salvation.
IX. Then, with her having been persuaded with difficulty and having stood up, and with the
sacred Nonnus seeking a guarantee and delaying the expiation, she again fell at his feet and uttered the same words. "You will render an account for those things I have done," she said, "O high priest of God, and the multitude of my iniquities will be ascribed to you if you delay the illumination and postpone the baptism, and do not hasten and exert yourself for my salvation, and spiritually labor and regenerate me, and present me today as a pure bride to the pure Bridegroom, and set me beside Christ." In the end she persuades him, and having arisen, she confesses and invokes the divine philanthropy. And what was her name from youth? Being asked by the bishop, she said that it was Pelagia from the beginning for all to call and name her. But the Antiochenes, having marveled at the multitude and beauty of the gems and pearls upon her, called her Margarita, devising the appellation from the name of the things with which she was adorned. Here she takes up her former name, and she is called Pelagia, and she receives the bath and is purified, and is anointed with myrrh, and becomes a partaker of the immaculate and unbloody sacrifice. And there was then a public and common joy among the Antiochenes, both because a common enemy had now fallen and been defeated, and because the believers were rejoicing, with each one saying and thinking her salvation to be his own.
X. But the evil one, for he is accustomed in some way to become more shameless when defeated, could not endure such great disgrace for himself, nor such great spiritual pleasure for the believers. Therefore, the enemy of mankind was transformed into a human being, and appearing in the midst of everyone, tearing out his hair and striking and lacerating himself before the eyes of all, he first began to cry out against the holy Nonnus, maintaining that he had been greatly injured by him and deprived of the powers that were in him. Then, also approaching Pelagia shamelessly, he called her a traitor and an unbeliever, and said, "Why did you deny your own and assent to a stranger?" But she extends her hand and strikes the evil one with the cross, and the enemy immediately vanished. And even so he does not cease from his shamelessness and the undertaking, but attempts and tries again; and coming to her about midnight as she was sleeping, he stands there and wakes her, and says the same things, but is again struck by the cross and immediately vanishes. Having arisen, she informs the one who had received her from the holy baptism—whose name was Romana— C of the struggle with the adversary, but she is encouraged by her words, and she stands in opposition for the battle and strikes the evil one with this second and timely blow. For having called the most well-disposed and obedient of her own servants, she orders all the property that was in her house to be inventoried, how much there was and what kind, in gold, in silver, in stones, in pearls, in vessels, in garments, and that nothing at all should be left out that was not inventoried, and that the inventory be brought to her as quickly as possible. This was done according to her intent. And having called for the holy Non D
nus, she yielded all her property to him, saying that the wealth she possessed from virtue and from her Bridegroom and Lord Christ was sufficient for her; and she released her own servants from the yoke of slavery. But so that they might feel her care for them even when she was not present, and that their means of livelihood might not be straitened, the benevolent woman also gave them money.
XI. On the blessed Pelagia (or Pelagius)
Then, when everything had proceeded according to her intention, upon the Lord’s day she took off her bright garment, and putting on a haircloth and tattered tunic, she hastened to Jerusalem, and reached the Mount of Olives, which was also the mount of virtues, having shared her purpose with no one, nor disclosing the mystery. And her affairs were thus hidden, known only to Him who sees the things that are secret. Now when the bishops who had been gathered at Antioch, among whom was Nonnus, had returned to their own churches, and she had remained unknown in the holy places—and since three years had passed for her there—a divine longing came upon one of those deacons who had been with Nonnus (this was Jacobus), and he was enamored of the holy places, wishing to be both a spectator and a worshiper of them. And having approached the high priest, he requests this journey to Jerusalem, and asks him to grant this to him. And he (for the man was prophetic and full of the Spirit, and able to see things far off and future), "You shall go," he said; "but if you seek, you will find a certain eunuch, the monk Pelagius," calling the blessed Pelagia by this name; "approach him, and converse with him, and you will truly partake, brother, of much spiritual benefit." Jacobus, then, as he was ready, hastened to the holy places, and having become a worshiper of those things, he set out on the search for Pelagius. And having arrived at Pelagia's cell, he knocked and called. And when she came out, he sees Pelagius, not knowing who it was, nor, even if someone had told him beforehand, being able from the things that were seen to understand or guess this. For her color was changed, and her eyes were sunken, and everything, through her abstinence, was altered and transformed. But neither did any of the others who were in Jerusalem, looking at her, think they were looking at a woman.
XII. On the same.
He, then, as having received a blessing from a man, stood astonished and, as it were, beside himself at the sight of her abstinence. But she, having known whence and who he was who stood there, asked if he was of the high priest Nonnus, asking willingly that which was known to her even before the question. And when he affirmed this, "The man," she said, "is an apostle; may he pray for my iniquities." And having extended her discourse thus far, she closes the door again, and began to sing her accustomed songs. Jacobus, however, having gained much profit in a short time, and having been taught the measure of speech that is sufficient and becoming for monks, departed and proceeded to the next holy places and thinking
gathered much spiritual benefit from many others.
XIII. Not much time passed before rumor had it that a certain famous man named Pelagius had departed from the living, one who in his life had practiced great discipline and virtue. And immediately a multitude of monks was gathered, and there were also gathered those who were otherwise pious and gentle, both those dwelling near the Jordan and those who were citizens of Nicopolis and Jericho, and finally all who lived round about came together and offered their parting respects. And when they approached the body and set out to anoint it with oil, they saw a strange and alien spectacle: a woman who had hidden her sex and had behaved as a man with fortitude, and had reaped a greater reward from her labors. Then there was a struggle among them all, one seeking to precede another and striving to get to [the body], and [each] esteeming it a great thing to approach the body alone and touch some part of it. And thus, with many lamps and with great honor, the precious relics of Pelagia were carried out, escorted and borne by holy and pious men.
XIV. Such is the life of the harlot; such are the strategies of this desperate woman against the enemy; such was her change for the better, and so easy is this for us, too, for the guidance towards something more excellent. Wherefore let anyone be of good courage, even if he is conscious that he did not act honestly at first, provided only that he is willing with his final deeds to wash away his former ones. Together with whom may the Lord grant us also to receive the ineffable pleasure that is there, and to be deemed worthy of the crowns that are there. For to Him be the glory, with the Father and the holy and life-giving Spirit, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.

Therefore, the enemy of mankind was transformed into a human being, and appearing in the midst of everyone, tearing out his hair and striking and lacerating himself before the eyes of all, he first began to cry out against the holy Nonnus, maintaining that he had been greatly injured by him and deprived of the powers that were in him. Then, also approaching Pelagia shamelessly, he called her a traitor and an unbeliever, and said, "Why did you deny your own and assent to a stranger?" But she extends her hand and strikes the evil one with the cross, and the enemy immediately vanished. And even so he does not cease from his shamelessness and the undertaking, but attempts and tries again; and coming to her about midnight as she was sleeping, he stands there and wakes her, and says the same things, but is again struck by the cross and immediately vanishes. Having arisen, she informs the one who had received her from the holy baptism—whose name was Romana—   of the struggle with the adversary, but she is encouraged by her words, and she stands in opposition for the battle and strikes the evil one with this second and timely blow. For having called the most well-disposed and obedient of her own servants, she orders all the property that was in her house to be inventoried, how much there was and what kind, in gold, in silver, in stones, in pearls, in vessels, in garments, and that nothing at all should be left out that was not inventoried, and that the inventory be brought to her as quickly as possible. This was done according to her intent. And having called for the holy Non


[917] {#pg.116.917}
A nus, she yielded all her property to him, saying that the wealth she possessed from virtue and from her Bridegroom and Lord Christ was sufficient for her; and she released her own servants from the yoke of slavery. But so that they might feel her care for them even when she was not present, and that their means of livelihood might not be straitened, the benevolent woman also gave them money.
XI. On the blessed Pelagia (or Pelagius)
Then, when everything had proceeded according to her intention, upon the Lord’s day she took off her bright garment, and putting on a haircloth and tattered tunic, she hastened to Jerusalem, and reached the Mount of Olives, which was also the mount of virtues, having shared her purpose with no one, nor disclosing the mystery. And her affairs were thus hidden, known only to Him who sees the things that are secret. Now when the bishops who had been gathered at Antioch, among whom was Nonnus, had returned to their own churches, and she had remained unknown in the holy places—and since three years had passed for her there—a divine longing came upon one of those deacons who had been with Nonnus (this was Jacobus), and he was enamored of the holy places, wishing to be both a spectator and a worshiper of them. And having approached the high priest, he requests this journey to Jerusalem, and asks him to grant this to him. And he (for the man was prophetic and full of the Spirit, and able to see things far off and future), "You shall go," he said; "but if you seek, you will find a certain eunuch, the monk Pelagius," calling the blessed Pelagia by this name; "approach him, and converse with him, and you will truly partake, brother, of much spiritual benefit."   Jacobus, then, as he was ready, hastened to the holy places, and having become a worshiper of those things, he set out on the search for Pelagius. And having arrived at Pelagia's cell, he knocked and called. And when she came out, he sees Pelagius, not knowing who it was, nor, even if someone had told him beforehand, being able from the things that were seen to understand or guess this. For her color was changed, and her eyes were sunken, and everything, through her abstinence, was altered and transformed.   But neither did any of the others who were in Jerusalem, looking at her, think they were looking at a woman.
XII. On the same.
He, then, as having received a blessing from a man, stood astonished and, as it were, beside himself at the sight of her abstinence. But she, having known whence and who he was who stood there, asked if he was of the high priest Nonnus, asking willingly that which was known to her even before the question. And when he affirmed this, "The man," she said, "is an apostle; may he pray for my iniquities." And having extended her discourse thus far, she closes the door again, and began to sing her accustomed songs.   Jacobus, however, having gained much profit in a short time, and having been taught the measure of speech that is sufficient and becoming for monks, departed and proceeded to the next holy places and thinking
gathered much spiritual benefit from many others.
XIII. Not much time passed before rumor had it that a certain famous man named Pelagius   had departed from the living, one who in his life had practiced great discipline and virtue. And immediately a multitude of monks was gathered, and there were also gathered those who were otherwise pious and gentle, both those dwelling near the Jordan and those who were citizens of Nicopolis and Jericho, and finally all who lived round about came together and offered their parting respects. And when they approached the body and set out to anoint it with oil, they saw a strange and alien spectacle: a woman who had hidden her sex and had behaved as a man with fortitude, and had reaped a greater reward from her labors. Then there was a struggle among them all, one seeking to precede another and striving to get to [the body], and [each] esteeming it a great thing to approach the body alone and touch some part of it. And thus, with many lamps and with great honor, the precious relics of Pelagia were carried out, escorted and borne by holy and pious men.
XIV. Such is the life of the harlot; such are the strategies of this desperate woman against the enemy; such was her change for the better, and so easy is this for us, too, for the guidance towards something more excellent. Wherefore let anyone be of good courage, even if he is conscious that he did not act honestly at first, provided only that he is willing with his final deeds to wash away his former ones. Together with whom may the Lord grant us also to receive   the ineffable pleasure that is there, and to be deemed worthy of the crowns that are there. For to Him be the glory, with the Father and the holy and life-giving Spirit, now and ever, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
