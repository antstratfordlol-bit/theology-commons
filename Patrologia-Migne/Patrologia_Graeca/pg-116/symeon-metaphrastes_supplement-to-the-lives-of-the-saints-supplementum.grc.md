ON SAINT DEMETRIUS THE MARTYR
THESSALONICE IN MACEDONIA
(ANNO CIRCITER CCCVI)
CORNELIUS BYE'S PRELIMINARY COMMENTARY
(Acta SS. Bolland. ad diem 8 Octobris, tom. IV.)
§ I. Multiplicia, quæ de S. Demetrio reperiuntur scripta, præcipue Græca, recensentur.
1. That this holy martyr, whom they call Megalomartyr, that is, the great martyr, and whom the Greeks everywhere decorate with many most honorable titles, which are to be mentioned below in their proper place, was plainly enjoyed among them both in singular veneration and in the fame of his name, is shown more than enough by the mere multiplicity of the ancient writings of various kinds in Greek — namely, homilies, panegyrical orations, and other little works — which are found today in the small chests of libraries, and some of which have even reached our hands, in which either St. Demetrius himself is celebrated, or the martyrdom which he underwent is set forth, or finally, the miracles which he performed in great number are related. To make this clear, and that I may at once inform the studious reader, inside this commentary (in which not a few of these will have to be weighed or at least put to some use), what and what sort of literary monuments concerning the Saint survive today, I shall review in this present § all those scripts, each and every one, of which I have been able to acquire any knowledge whatever, intending then, in the following §, to set forth which of these ought to be published, and also to add which ones either openly show, or at least make it easy to collect, how great and—especially those which I shall say should be committed to print—how powerful they are in either fidelity or authority. From the little works or lucubrations D
of which the saint himself or the miracles which he performed are celebrated, I take my beginning.
2. Leo Allatius enumerates such ten diverse writings, of which the first has as author Philotheus, the Patriarch of Constantinople; the second, Demetrius Chrysoloras; the third, fourth, and seventh, Leo the Emperor, surnamed the Wise; the fifth, an anonymous author; the sixth, John Stauracius, the chartophylax of Thessalonica; the eighth, John, Archbishop of Thessalonica; the ninth, Gregory the deacon; and finally the tenth, Nicolaus Cabasilas; see pages 85, 94, 96, 97, 102, 103, 105, and 107. Fabricius, in his *Bibliotheca Graeca*, adds to these yet other lucubrations of the same kind, besides those which he himself enumerated in the same place (vol. VI, p. 521 et seq., and vol. IX, p. 71 et seq.), the first of which was composed by Gregory Palamas, Archbishop of Thessalonica, the second by Nicetas, likewise Archbishop of Thessalonica, and the third and fourth by anonymous authors, from the latter of the two volumes just mentioned, page likewise just cited et seq. And truly, Leo Allatius and Fabricius agree well enough in assigning the authors of the lucubrations which both enumerate; but then both incorrectly ascribe to an anonymous author the third of the last four lucubrations, which Fabricius alone lists, beginning with *Μαριανός τις ἀνὴρ ὢν τῷ γένει*, as well as the fifth of the previous ten lucubrations, which are mentioned by both him and Allatius, beginning *Καιρὸς δὲ λοιπόν*, because each constitutes only a partial fragment of the eighth of those same ten lucubrations, which has John of Thessalonica as its author, beginning with the words *Παντὸς ἔργου τε καὶ λόγου*.
3. Perhaps it will be of importance, regarding the one which begins with the words, *Ἔφη ὁ σωτήριος λόγος*, the third of those same ten preceding lucubrations, which both lauded writers attribute to Emperor Leo the Wise—whether it truly has this author—to call into doubt the MS 4517, which occurs in the second volume of the collection of manuscript codices of the Royal Library of Paris, published in the year 1740. This is entirely regarding Saint Demetrius, a parchment codex of, as it appears, the XII century, or rather the index provided therein of the lucubrations contained in this volume, formerly the Mazarin codex; for here it has it thus: “In which (the parchment Greek codex, formerly Mazarin) are contained: 1. Martyrdom of Demetrius. It begins: *Μαξιμιανὸς, ὁ καὶ Ἑρκούλιος*. 2. His Life and miracles, by John, Archbishop of Thessalonica. 3. Homily of the same John, when the house in the palace was consecrated to Saint Demetrius. It begins: *Ἔφη ὁ σωτήριος λόγος*. 4. History of the miracles performed by Saint Demetrius. It begins: *Ἄλλοι μὲν ἄλλα τῶν τοῦ μεγαλομάρτυρος*. 5. Homily of John, Archbishop of Thessalonica, in praise of Saint Demetrius. It begins: *Μνήμην δικαίων (read δικαίων) μετ’ ἐγκωμίων*. 6. Homily by Joseph, Archbishop of Thessalonica, in praise of Demetrius, unpublished. It begins: *Φαιδρὰ μὲν οὖν ἡ πανήγυρις*. The end is desired.” But these items, which from these words, *Ἔφη ὁ σωτήριος λόγος*, begins, although ascribed by Allatius and Fabricius to the Emperor Leo, this lucubration is clearly attributed here to John, Archbishop of Thessalonica.
4. However, there is more than one error in the provided list; for the lucubration which in this codex immediately follows the martyrdom of the Saint, and which is mentioned in the second place, is none other than the eighth of the ten lucubrations listed above. It does not contain the life of Saint Demetrius together with his miracles, as is indicated in the list, but instead—as I learned by examining the aforementioned codex, formerly Mazarin, now Royal—contains only a panegyric which takes the place of a preface, before the miracles, in praise of God and Saint Demetrius; with absolutely nothing brought forward regarding details or facts or incidents pertaining to his life and martyrdom, thus forming a eulogy. Then, however, the same lucubration, which bears this title at the head: *Τοῦ ἁγίου Ἰωάννου ἀρχιεπισκόπου Θεσσαλονίκης ὕμνος εἰς Θεὸν καὶ τὴν πανένδοξον ἀθλοφόρον Δημήτριον ἐν μερικῇ διηγήσει τῶν θαυμάτων* (“Laudation of Saint John, Archbishop of Thessalonica, to God and the most glorious victor Demetrius, with a particular narration of miracles”), covers the entire space in the cited codex, formerly Mazarin, where a leaf or two has subsequently been excised—of pages, however, which did not happen to be numbered until this was done—the series is not interrupted. It exists between the beginning with these words, *Μαξιμιανὸς, ὁ καὶ Ἑρκούλιος* (the martyrdom of Saint Demetrius and the homily remembered in the third place in the quoted index), and runs from page 12 to page 206; most certainly, contrary to what the said index requires, it does not occupy that space, or, which comes to the same thing, does not extend itself to that homily.
5. For, as will be clear to the one examining the codex itself, from page 135, where it terminates, up to page 206, it still has two other subjects: one, stripped of both title and name of author at the beginning, exposes six diverse miracles of Saint Demetrius from page 135 up to 205; the other, stripped not only of title and author's name, but also of what pertains to an exhortation to celebrate the feast of Saint Demetrius with spiritual rather than bodily joy, is contained from page 205 up to page 206, and then finally follows the homily regarding whose author we are disputing here. But now, since it is so, and it has the following title: *Τοῦ αὐτοῦ ὁμιλία, ὅτε τῷ ἀοιδίμῳ τῷ εὐσεβείας ἀγωνιστῇ, καὶ τὰ οὐράνια περιπολούντι ἀνάκτορα Δημητρίῳ ὁ οἶκος ἐν τοῖς βασιλείοις ἐνεφάνθη* (“Homily of the same, when the house was consecrated in the palace to the celebrated champion of piety Demetrius, who traverses the heavenly palaces”), is prefixed to it, there was certainly no reason why he, who in the list of the little works—
which runs only from page 206 to page 208—compiled the index, should ascribe that brief little work to John, Archbishop of Thessalonica. Indeed, since the same little work, as is clear from the title it bears, is undoubtedly composed by the same author by whom the little work that precedes it was also composed, and there is nothing at all to prevent one from attributing it to Leo the Wise, being as it is devoid of the author’s name in the aforementioned codex, it seems to me highly probable, if not certain, that it was also adorned by this illustrious writer, to whom it is ascribed by Allatius and Fabricius—so much so that in the list already mentioned several times, it is erroneously marked under the name of John, Archbishop of Thessalonica.
6. But even if this be so, in that same list, the piece which follows it, beginning with the words “’Αλλοι μὲν ἄλλα” and extending from page 208 to page 226, is a lucubration of an anonymous author concerning the miracles of Saint Demetrius; it is immediately followed by a homily, or rather, as it is inscribed in the title it carries, a context in praise of Saint Demetrius, which runs from page 226 to the verso of page 247, and is correctly ascribed to John, Archbishop of Thessalonica, as is that other encomium of the Saint by Joseph, also Archbishop of Thessalonica, which is subjoined to the aforementioned encomium and which, being mutilated at the end, extends only from the verso of page 247 to the verso of page 253 of the codex—which has been mentioned very often, and from which some pages have been excised—that we have here mentioned at the last. And thus we have to add to those listed above three other works from this same codex, which celebrate either the Saint himself or the miracles performed by him, and which remained to be enumerated here. It is the next step that we should now also review the little works in which the life or martyrdom of Saint Demetrius is set forth. Allatius, in his *De Simeonum scriptis*, lists two different ones, both of which he titles the martyrdom of Saint Demetrius, at pages 123 and 126. The first, which he refers to as being among the Lives of the saints not written by the Metaphrast, has these words for its beginning: “Ὅτε βασιλεὺς ἐν τῇ Θεσσαλονικέων διῆγε πόλει”; the second, however, which he classifies among the Acts or Lives composed by the Metaphrast, has as its beginning the following: “Εἶχε μὲν τὰ Ῥωμαίων σκῆπτρα Μαξιμιανὸς ὁ Ἑρκούλιος.”
7. And this latter work, which was first translated into Latin by Lipomanus and inserted into the sixth volume of the Lives he donated in Latinity, for the day of October 26, sacred to Saint Demetrius among the Greeks, was later brought by Surius into the collection of Lives he compiled—with no change at all introduced to the interpretation of Lipomanus—for the day of October 8th, dedicated to the same Saint among the Latins, and was finally reviewed against the Greek exemplar of the Medicean library of His Most Christian Majesty, and published in both Greek and Latin, set apart in a separate volume, which one copy exists with me, which our Bollandus, at the request and expense of Laurentius Cocchus, had printed by the Meursiani in 1635. But as regards the former, I think it is that very one which, rendered into Latin by Anastasius the Librarian and subsequently inserted into many manuscripts and other books, begins with these words: *Cum imperator Maximianus in Thessalonicensium degeret civitate.* Such is the opinion, that this is the one and same beginning for both. Nor does it matter that it is written in Greek as *Maximianus* in Allatius, and in Latin as *Maximianus* in Anastasius. For besides the fact that in Greek *Maximianus* is often written for *Maximianus* or vice-versa, Fabricius in *Bibliotheca Graeca* vol. IX, page 72, praises a manuscript existing in the Coislinian library, which was rendered back to the Greek exemplar—which was produced for Anastasius the Librarian—in which it is not *Maximianus* (in Greek), but *Maximianus* that is uttered. Nor is this name written otherwise in the Greek text, which agrees in substance and in almost all circumstances with the Anastasian lucubration, although the words are different, as is the other *Passion of Demetrius*, exhibited by Photius in codex 255 of his *Bibliotheca*. Moreover, in all the Latin versions that I have been able to see, the copy of the *Life* translated by Anastasius is written as *Maximianus*, not *Maximinus*.
8. But enough of this matter. Besides the two, or rather three, Greek Lives or Passions already numbered—namely, the one I just mentioned as being exhibited by Photius—another Greek one, likewise beginning with these words, “Μαξιμιανὸς ὁ καὶ Ἑρκούλιος ὑποτάξας Γότθους,” is praised in the *Bibliotheca Graeca*, vol. IX, by Fabricius, who in that same place also reviews the two former ones already mentioned from Allatius (at page 72), and the same is exhibited entire in the formerly Mazarin codex, cited often above, from page 1 to the verso of page 10, as well as in our codex signed ms. 193 from page 38 to page 42. Furthermore, in order to spare brevity, I pass over in silence the shorter eulogies of the Saint, which are only in the course of the commentary, if they contain anything worthy of note, whether they be to be transcribed or at least to be commemorated. To the Greek Lives of Demetrius already enumerated, one must still add a longer Latin Life, or rather a paraphrase of the Greek Life (the former) and of another (the latter), which I reviewed in the second place. It was introduced into the *Legenda* of the patron saints of Poland, Hungary, Bohemia, Moravia, Prussia, and Silesia, compiled around the year 1470 by John Longinus (alias Duglossius), as a manuscript annotation prefixed to it shows, and finally printed at Cracow in the year 1511. It was also exhibited in summary by Pelbartus of Temeswar, who flourished in the order of the Friars Minor as the fifteenth century was waning, in the second part of his *Pomerium Sermonum de Sanctis*, in sermon 79. And thus I have now reviewed the writings concerning Saint Demetrius that I had determined to review here; but what credence each deserves, and at the same time which of them are to be published, I shall set forth in the following section, which takes its beginning from the Lives or Passions.
