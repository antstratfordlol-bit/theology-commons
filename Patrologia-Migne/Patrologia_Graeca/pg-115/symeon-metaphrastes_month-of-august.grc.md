I. Postquam fuisset salutaris prædicatio ubique annuntiata, et omnia essent repleta cognitione Domini, veluti quædam sceleris reliquiæ Adrianus Romanorum administrans imperium, in falsos deos, qui nominantur, ostendebat plurimam, si ullus alius, superstitionem, alios quidem alliciens blanditiis, alios autem etiam minis territans. Illo autem tempore mulier quædam pia et religiosa, nomine Sophia, clara genere, Itala natione, cum esset viduata marito, cum tribus filiabus solis vitam agitabat. Ea cum simul cum virginibus, quæ erant valde honestæ, et elegantes animo et corpore, Romæ versata esset, non fuit errore seducta, neque ad impii imperatoris reclinavit religionem, factis ipsis pulchre suam ostendens appellationem quæ nomen vere sumpsisset a superna sapientia, et diceretur Sophia. Quæ est enim desuper Sapientia, inquit divinus Jacobus, primum quidem pudica est, deinde pacifica, modesta, obediens, plena misericordia et fructibus bonis. Eo enim, quod virtutis fructus apud eam agnoscebatur, adaptavit filiabus suis appellationes : et illæ rursus in se ostendebant esse veras, neque habebant aliquid rami, quod discrepare aut esset alienum a radice. XII. Iniquus autem judex cum ad extremam venisset dubitationem, et non posset apertam ferre ignominiam, in extrema tamen sorore adhuc spe tenebatur. Eum enim movebat valde tenera illius ætas, quæ ei existimabatur fore expugnatu facilis. Postquam autem adhibuit interrogationes, et movit verba, quæ poterant persuadere : Ne te decipiat, inquit Charitas, ætas mea tenera, ut quæ facile possit decipi, et a tuis verbis allici. Te docebit mox experientia, me esse ramum ejusdem radicis, et fetum illius unius uteri, cujus jam duorum germinum et filiarum fecisti periculum. Non enim quantum in me erit, priores pudore afficiam neque degenerabo ab eadem nobilitate : sed tantum superabo fortitudine, quanto majorem habeo experientiam, ut quæ Christi auxilium in eis didicerim ! Hanc libertatem non tulit sceleratus, sed jubet suspendi Charitatem, et omni ex parte extendi loris adeo ut membrorum compages vehementi illa contentione divellerentur a naturali sua compositione ; sed Dei virtus, quæ aliarum sororum curam gesserat, cum ipsa quoque erat, illam illæsam servans a malis. Accenditur ergo ei quoque fornax ex omnis generis materia, cum is qui jusserat, prius vehementi ira fuisset incensus : Vides, dicit ille martyri, ignem qui contra te movetur. Non poteris aliter minas ejus effugere, nisi quod studueris. Ego autem te utar etiam benignius. Dic enim solum : Magna Diana, et statim ab omni criminatione liberaberis. Illa autem : Absit, inquit, ut vel solo verbo faciam aliquid impium et indecorum, et linguam tali polluam locutione, quæ est aliena a pietate in eum, qui creavit.

MONTH OF AUGUST
MARTYRDOM OF THE HOLY WOMEN SOPHIA AND HER DAUGHTERS, FAITH, HOPE, AND LOVE
I. After the saving preaching had been announced everywhere, and all things had been filled with the knowledge of the Lord, Hadrian, administering the empire of the Romans, like some remnant of wickedness, showed the greatest superstition toward those who are called false gods, if ever any other did, wheedling some with flatteries, and frightening others with threats. At that time, therefore, a certain pious and God-loving woman, Sophia by name, of illustrious lineage and from the land of the Italians, having undergone the widowhood of her husband, was passing her life with three daughters alone. Having come to the renowned city of Rome, and being with these maidens—who were quite honorable and elegant in soul and body—she was not seduced by error, nor did she incline toward the religion of the impious emperor, showing her name to be true by her very deeds; for she had truly taken this name from the wisdom from above, and was called Sophia. For the wisdom that is from above, says the divine James, is first chaste, then peaceable, compliant, full of mercy and good fruits. Since the fruit of virtue was recognized in her, she adapted their names to her daughters; and they, in turn, showed them to be true in themselves, nor did the branches have anything that was discordant or alien from the root.
II. But when the prudence and virtue of these, and their devotion to Christ, had become noised abroad among all, the father of envy, not bearing it, through the envious eyes of a certain man, Antiochus, to whom the prefecture of the city had been entrusted, signifies their piety to the emperor, and reports them
His tongue a wicked man, and with words of hatred; the impious one forthwith, moved by thoughtless anger, sends those whom they are wont to call apparitors, to bring them in. But they, when they had learned the cause of their arrival, and since they were inseparable in body and even more closely united in spirit, showed nothing in the daughters that did not harmonize with the mother, as they were found to agree fraternally among themselves, and were as it were a crown equal to itself, which was completed by them and the mother, went forward with alacrity. After they had sat before the tribunal of the prefect of the city, having invoked the Lord, who said that those who kill the body are not to be feared, and who promised that wisdom would be given by Him, and having fortified themselves with the sign of the cross, all fear was cast aside, and they stood with a brave and noble spirit. But the judge, casting his eyes upon them, when he had seen their liberal countenance and a mind great and illustrious, to which the modesty of their eyes and demeanor bore witness, ceasing to act the part of a judge, turned to admiration.
III. But when he had caused the mother to stand near, and had separated the others, he said: "These are no small matters, O woman, which are brought against you. For it is no small thing, the denial of customs and religions handed down from parents. Why, therefore, have you filled the region of the Romans with tumult and dissension, asserting that there are no gods at all, but that they are merely names lacking the things themselves? Tell me, therefore, as quickly as possible, your country, race, name, and religion." But she said: "First indeed, I say, my chief name is to be called from Christ. But Sophia is the name imposed upon me by my parents. I trace my lineage from those who held the first magistracies in Italy, in which I was also born. But above all things, as has been said by me, I glory in the invocation of my Christ and Lord, to whom I was offered continuously from my birth, and to whom I have offered the fruit of my womb: for whose sake we also came into this city of the Romans, so that when we have preserved the right faith even unto death, bringing a beautiful provision, the struggle undertaken for Christ, and passing from death into life, we may be heirs of the goods which are promised there."
IV. The impious man admired the boldness of these words, and with that boldness, the wisdom. But as one who had already been overcome by the first danger presented, he then put off the interrogation of the virgins; but he orders them to be kept with their mother at the house of a certain woman of the senatorial order, by the name of Palladia, commanding that she produce them for examination on the third day. But they, using the leisure that had been given to them well, applied their mind to their mother, who was persuading them of the things they needed. For she said to them: "I indeed, O beloved daughters, when I had brought you forth according to the flesh, trained you in all doctrine and in the holy Scriptures. Since, therefore, the time calls you to the contest, let the fruit of my words appear in you; and do not let the
mischief that is soon to befall you bring any disgrace upon the virtue you have practiced for so long, nor let the powerlessness of error prevail over the truth which is stronger than all. Your youth is a cause of anxiety to me, but do not fear the danger; for you have as your helper the invincible alliance of Christ. Grant it, therefore, to my old age, grant it to your own struggles, to leap forward more youthfully, preserving until the end, unyielding, the confession in Christ, who will also crown you with an immortal hand and provide you with unfading crowns, with whom you shall dance in the ages to come, and enjoy incorruptible delight, and possess wealth undisturbed, and taste of a pleasure that never ceases. It is the part of the wise, therefore, to exchange the small for the greatest, the unstable for the permanent, the fleeting for that which does not pass away; for this is the most precious of all things: to purchase the kingdom of heaven with a little blood. And this, indeed, was Sophia.
V. But the daughters, acting toward their mother as was proper, said: "Do you, O venerable mother, accompany us with your maternal prayers, providing them as a good protection for us. But the Lord, who said, 'Do not be anxious how or what you are to answer when you are brought before kings and governors' [*] (Matt. x, 19), He, being truthful, will not deceive, and being powerful, will fulfill His promise, providing us with wisdom, through which we shall be able to conquer the truly foolish and passing wisdom, and openly to triumph over impiety." When, therefore, the three appointed days had now passed, the holy ones were led to their questioning. Then the judge, being cunning and having considered what it was that might relax the resolve of the tender young girls, approached them with much flattery; and he said: "Beholding your beauty, O children, and the pleasure flowing from this form, I do not believe you to have come from human nature, but to have been sent from some more divine source for the wonder of those who see you. For this reason, also, I show a fatherly affection, and I beseech you, most beloved, not to wish to be altogether disobedient to a loving father, but first consider the age of your mother, whose old age must needs be scourged if you appear disobedient. Then, also, take into your heart the youth of your own age, the flower of beauty and the ineffable comeliness, which, as much as I admire it, I will to that extent punish, even against my will, if you, by not obeying, provoke me to anger by causing me grief. And then, alas! you will perish miserably, when you ought rather to be rejoicing, faring well, enjoying riches and glory and the king’s favor, and delighting fully in the goods of this time and age." But these things, indeed, the judge [said].
VI. But the virgins, the offspring of Sophia, said: "We, indeed, desire neither your promises, as you have spoken them, nor do we fear threats and tortures; for we love incorruptible goods and an incorruptible Bridegroom, despising all human things. But because you threaten to punish our mother,
you are unaware that you are announcing the perfection of good things both to us and to her. For what is more pleasant to Christians than to suffer for Christ? And even if we did not have such rewards to hope for, what excellence of glory could exceed suffering for the One who formed us? But when the kingdom of heaven is also prepared for us, and incorruptible wealth, and the enjoyment of eternal goods, then to attempt altogether to lead us away by promises of temporal goods is manifest madness. Do not be deceived, then, judge. For you will neither draw us by flattery, nor will you change us by punishing; but rather you will cause us greater distress if, by sparing this youth and beauty which you esteem so highly, you do not bring down upon us grievous tortures. For you will be depriving us of the reward of greater goods.
When the judge heard this firm and constant defense, he decided to separate them from one another and to bring them forward for examination one by one, so that he might afflict them with greater distress by making use of those who were weaker. When he had asked about the age and names of each one, and had learned from her who gave them birth that one was called Faith and was already in her twelfth year; that the second was two years younger than the elder, whose name was Hope; and that the third was named Charity, being one year younger than the second; he ordered the first to be brought forward, and said, “Sacrifice, young woman, to Artemis; we are not commanding you to do something new, but what has long been done by us as well.”
VII. But she, as if wishing to demonstrate right then that they had separated them in vain, wishing to break the chain woven from the three, while ignorant that their boldness and the instruction on how one must answer came from above, said, “Oh, the blindness that penetrates to the depths of you! You who are so miserably blind, yet wish to be guides of the way to others, and force us to proceed with you on the same road to destruction! For what sensible man would be persuaded to depart from the true God—whose handiwork are the heavens and the earth and all that is in them—to approach the works of human hands as though they were gods, and choose to offer worship to things devoid of mind and hearing? This is a terrible madness, terrible, and it is the folly both of those who order it and those who are persuaded. Do, therefore, what you will, and hand us over to whatever evils you wish; for it is better to suffer all things first, while we have our senses, than to be persuaded to do such things.” These words filled the judge with rage, and since he could not persuade her, he turned to applying force. He ordered her garment to be removed, and that she be bound with her hands behind her back and beaten with the heaviest rods. But when the experience showed all the more the steadfastness of her resolve, and the martyr appeared not as one being beaten with rods, but rather as one being strewn with roses, with not even a stripe appearing on her body, the judge was incited all the more to cruelty, and both
commanded her breasts to be cut off. And it was possible to see in this a wondrous and strange thing. For instead of blood, streams of milk flowed down; which even more kindled the rage of the judge, and he ordered her to be placed upon a red-hot gridiron. But just as he, being wicked, was not at a loss for contriving means of torture, so the Good God, in turn, did not cease to glorify the saint through the things being inflicted; and this is what happened here also. For the fire, having taken hold of her, kept her unharmed, having lost its own power; then, having taken her up from there, he commanded her to be cast into a frying pan burning with pitch and bitumen. Standing in the middle of this, with an unshaken and untroubled countenance, she called for help from above; and immediately the fire was no longer fire, and the heat was changed into coldness, so that the saint seemed to be resting in a dewy meadow and upon some soft grass.
Ⅷ. Thus, therefore, as none of the punishments being inflicted harmed the martyr of Christ, but rather moved those who were looking on to wonder, the wicked minister, not knowing what else he could do, pronounced the sentence of death by the sword against her. When this was announced to her, it was clear that the martyr was full of joy. She brought a request to her mother to pray for her daughter, and exhorted her sisters not to slacken in their zeal for the prize of the upward calling. “You know,” she said, “to whom we have plighted ourselves, and under whose seal we have been marked. Let us not, through cowardice, doubt, but remain in the confession of Him until the end. One mother bore us, the same nourished all three; by one were we reared in both bodily and spiritual food. Let there be one end for the three; let there be a sisterly and common purpose among you. Let the first be a domestic example to those who are to follow her.” Strengthened by these words, the sisters, when they had spoken their last farewells to the martyr, requested her to pray to their common Master on their behalf, that they might run the same race without stumbling, so that they too might be deemed worthy of the same crowns.
Ⅸ. The noble mother, undergoing nothing ignoble or faint-hearted or womanish—but as if ashamed to do or say anything that was abject and unworthy of such a daughter—was altogether of a great and lofty spirit, taking this alone as a grief, lest she should see the others fail in any small way to follow the example of the first, and lest she herself should run the risk of being seen as the true mother of only one, and not of three such. Wherefore she sent the child on her way with worthy words: “I, indeed, O daughter, bore you,” she said, “and endured the pains of labor for your sake, and nourished you even to this age. Yet, I now receive the prize for the nourishment, I receive the recompense for my labors, and those multiplied. For even if no one can return equal thanks to parents (for how can one, from whom one has received the very fact of being?), yet you have returned everything to me magnificently, by showing me to be the mother of such a daughter, having thus wrestled for Christ so magnanimously. Depart to Him,
depart, dyed with the blood shed for Him, with which you shall appear before your Bridegroom, and with which you shall stand before Him, adorned more beautifully than any rouge or flower." Having heard such words from her sisters and from her mother, she immediately stretched out her neck, and her head was cut off, and she was lifted up to Christ, who is the Head of all.
X. The impious judge, however, unable to bear the ignominy, thought to repair the loss of the victory he had thought won by the others, but proved yet more defeated by them as well. He immediately summoned the one who was second in age and said: "Believe me, my child, and when you have worshipped the greatest goddess, Diana, depart in joy." But she, truly Hope, who cannot be affected by shame, said: "As you believed me to be the sister of the one who came before—of whom you have already had trial—so let it be persuaded in your mind that I shall also be seen to be her sister in constancy, and that there is absolutely nothing, whether joyful or painful, that could lead me away from this constancy." When he heard this, he turned immediately to punishment, judging it vain and superfluous to prolong the interrogation. Therefore, he ordered her to be stripped of her clothes, and he commanded that she too, just like the first, be scourged with raw ox-hide. But when she endured the same punishment and displayed a fortitude equal to the other, even greater anger was stirred up in the judge; and he ordered that a furnace, greatly heated, should receive the venerable martyr. But God likewise cared for her as He had for her sister. And the same thing happened now as in the Babylonian flame; for the flame did not touch the martyr at all. Therefore, Hope sent forth voices, both giving thanks and supplicating at once. She was giving thanks to God that she had been preserved unharmed in the midst of evils; she supplicated, however, for the sake of what was to come, so that in all things His name might be glorified and the impious might be put to shame.
XI. Therefore, the impious man, not fearing her, whom even the fire itself seemed to fear, ordered her to be suspended on a stake and torn with iron claws. She, however, even then displayed the same greatness of spirit that she had shown in the preceding evils. For this reason, a noble grace illuminated her eyes, and a certain sweet odor was present with her torn limbs. And smiling softly with the tips of her lips, she said: "You indeed, O bloodthirsty man, think to weaken my strength with tortures. I, however, strengthened by my Christ, trust that I shall render you weak and feeble, and make your efforts vain, having set out to wage war with a woman, and that too, while I am a young girl, and have nothing prepared to bring me aid except that I trust in the true God." Provoked to fury by these words, he ordered a cauldron filled with pitch and resin, and vehemently heated, to be placed under the holy one. But as God did as was His custom, the cauldron was indeed dissolved like wax before the face of the fire; and what it contained, burning, suddenly poured out and destroyed not a few of the unbelievers who were standing around. But not even thus could he, whose mind was utterly blinded, discern who the power was that was working these wonders. Whence also to all
of those things he had done, having borne away defeat and being at a loss, as if performing according to her judgment, he pronounced against her the same death by the sword as he had for her sister. Again, she too invoked her mother’s prayer as her departure, and she stirred up her last sister after her into the contests, offering herself as a safe example and providing trustworthy words from what she had suffered. Then, clinging to the lying remains of her sister, she embraced them, kissed them, and clasped them; this she did partly out of reverence for her as a holy one, and partly because she was encouraged as a sister to weep by the pulling of kinship, and was again drawn toward joy by the knowledge that the one lying there was a martyr. For how would she have wept for one to whom she was already moving by the same manner of death? Therefore, she too receives death by the sword, and departs upon the beautiful path, the truly blessed way, desirable to all Christians, but passable for few.
XII. The lawless judge, then, having come to the last extremity of doubt and being unable to bear the obvious disgrace, was nevertheless still holding onto his hopes regarding the last of the sisters. For the very young age of the martyr bit at him, as it was expected that she would be easily overcome by him. When he brought forward his questions and initiated words that had the power to persuade: "Let not the tenderness of my age deceive you," says Agape, "as if it would be easily led astray and cheaply ensnared by your words; for experience will teach you shortly that I am a branch from the same root, and the offspring of that same womb, of which you have already had the trial of two sprouts and children. For the matters of those who preceded me will not be put to shame on my account, nor shall I belie their nobility, but I will surpass them all the more in fortitude by as much as I have an advantage in experience, having been taught the help of Christ in their cases." The impious one could not bear this boldness, but orders Agape to be suspended from a rack and to be stretched on all sides with leather thongs, so that the joints of her limbs were torn from their natural composition by that violent tension. But the power of God, which had stood over the other sisters, was also with her, keeping her free from harm. Therefore, a furnace of all kinds of material is ignited for her too, the one who ordered it having first been inflamed with strong anger. "Do you see, then," he says to the martyr, "the fire burning before you? You could not otherwise escape its threat, unless you hasten to perform what is commanded without delay; but I will deal with you even more kindly; say only, as a pretext with a word alone, 'Great is Artemis,' and immediately you shall be released from all charges." But she, "God forbid," she says, "that I should, even by a word alone, do something unholy and improper, and defile my tongue with such an utterance, which is alien to piety toward Him who created me."
XIII. What then? Abandoning words, he did what his anger commanded, and ordered her to be thrown into the fire. And she, not only those who were to have apprehended
her, but almost forestalling the command itself, leaped into the midst of the flame, as if into warm waters and most pleasant baths. Straightway, therefore, the flame was diffused, and seized upon those who were standing around. The fire touched the judge himself as well, both that it might avenge impiety, and that he himself might have no doubt, but might use himself as a witness of the power of God. Thus, then, the affairs of the impious were affected with disgrace, while God was glorified through His martyr. But because that part of the body which the flame had touched fared ill for the judge, he sent certain men to bring the holy one. Those who had been sent saw certain persons clad in white garments, of elegant and noble appearance, who could, by their mere form, induce admiration; and who were also often seen to be with the blessed one in the furnace, and to pour out prayers together with her. But when their hands were paralyzed as they attempted to seize the holy one, being unable to do anything more: "Come forth," they cried, "handmaid of God; the judge calls you." Although she went forth from the fire unharmed, the fire having not injured her at all, and the impious man still had a semi-ustulated body, and could have understood from that very fact; yet, bearing a thick cloud upon the eyes of his soul, he was plainly devoid of understanding. On that account he again brought upon her other torments, and ordered her limbs to be pierced with augers. But when the martyr, having her mind entirely raised to God, cared little or nothing for the torments, he condemned her to death by the sword. When the young girl heard this—she whose spirit was not small or womanly, and who had reckoned this to be, as it were, the seal and firmness of her confession in God—she gave great thanks to God: "I give thanks to Thee," she said, "Divine Trinity, one divinity, one glory, one power, that Thou hast rendered me, who am most vile, worthy of Thy bridal chamber and of Thy martyrs, my sisters." "Grant, however, that my mother may survive in this miserable life after my departure, for as long as it takes to perform the customary rites for Thy handmaids."
XIV. Thus having prayed, the blessed Charitas, the admirable mother of an admirable daughter, prayed for her—not that she might see her alive, and that she might live for a long time, escaping death, and that she might have her alone as a consolation and hope of her old age, being deserted by her other daughters; she prayed for none of these things, nor did she wish for them. For she judged these to be signs of mothers who were ungenerous and of small mind, and who look only at that which is present and which is seen; but she prayed that she might receive a consummation consistent with her purpose, and that a good end might follow upon good deeds. Wherefore, adding spirit to her for death, and confirming her for the end of her course: "Well done," she said, "O daughter; O truly happy plant of my womb! O you who have honored your parents! O you who have glorified God in your members! Who will not praise your fortitude? Who will not praise your strength of soul and constancy? Go to the common Lord, to the bridal chamber into which decay does not fall, to a truly blessed rest, D
the rewards of the struggles. How I imagine your crown being offered by the Lordly right hand! How I see the angels rejoicing and awaiting your consummation! Oh, with what three daughters have I honored the Trinity! What gifts, what sacrifices I have brought to Him! Such prayers and such words did the noble mother provide as viaticum for her daughter, sending her forth upon the desired journey. B The blessed one, therefore, yielding her neck, gladly underwent death for Christ. Her body the mother embraced with great affection and piety, doing what reason and nature demanded; and when she had perfumed it and wrapped it, as was fitting for one who was both a mother and a lover of martyrs, and had performed with magnificence and generosity all things that are the custom for such relics to receive, she joined it to those of her sisters and deposited them in the temple, which she had formerly built with great beauty—a great treasure and an inexhaustible wealth that the truly Christ-loving woman bestowed upon Christians, a healing for the soul, a healing for the body, an unhindered course of life, a reunion with God, able to provide a store of great goods. Then, after that one’s consummation, when three whole days had passed, this mother of noble children went forth to perform the commemoration for her holy children, and approaching the casket of her relics, she said: "O precious sacrifices before God, consummated and beautifully offered offerings, receive your mother as a tent-mate in the tabernacles in which you dwell." She said these things, and the noble woman was carried away with these words, and she fell into the sleep that is fitting for the just, the mother being added to her daughters and once again, in common, to the choir of the saints. When the pious women had gathered, they performed the customary rites for her, and deposited her in the common casket of the daughters. For it was fitting that, just as in the case of their souls, so also in the case of their bodies, there should be one dwelling-place for them unto the glory of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, of the one Godhead, to Whom is due all glory, honor, and adoration, now and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
