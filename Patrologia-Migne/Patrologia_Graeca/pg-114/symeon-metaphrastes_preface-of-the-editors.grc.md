Hinc non male a Niceta et Cinnamo observatur, a cancellarios dici logothetas a Græcis. De cancellario multa Casaubonus in Flavium Vopiscum, et multo plura Salmasius. Logothetam præterea jusjurandum a novo imperatore accipere in templo Blachernarum, ex Niceta in Manuele Comneno lib. 3, eademque dignitate plures eodem tempore fuisse exornatos, ex Cantacuzeno lib. 1, cap. 14, observavit Meursius. Theodorum itaque Metochilam ærarii publici logothetam cum imperator Andronicus ex Palæologis hoc nomine primus honorare constituisset, magnum logothetam fecit, qui et priores magno stratopedarcha tenebat, sed non protostrator. Vide Gregoram Histor. lib. VIII, cap. 19.
Sed cum, ut jam vidimus, logothetæ varii ac diversi fuerint, dubium esse poterit, Synecrem hunc nostrum logothetam ad quemnam ex illis referre debeamus. Non dubito hominem prudentissimum pro opportunitate rerum in senatu Constantinopolitano diversas functiones subiisse, et optime administrata provincia a minoribus ad majora officia aditum sibi aperuisse; quod tradit etiam Psellus in ejusdem encomio. Hinc estimo eum tantopere seriptione valere a logotheta cursus, quod in orationibus ab ipso ex operibus divi Basilii concinnatis præfixum legi ad magni logothetæ dignitatem ascendisse. Adde, ut plurimum logothetæ [33] nomen sine alia additione prolatum, magnum nobis logothetam indicare. Ne in exemplis producendis nimius sim, legimus passim Georgii Acropolitæ logothetæ chronicum : in eo licet Dousa hæreat, sibique haud satis compertum esse fateatur, quo munere ex istis omnibus, quæ supra commemoravimus, logothetas Georgius functus sit, magni tamen logothetæ functum ex altera ejusdemmet Historiæ additione hactenus inelita, certum est; illi namque præfigitur : Ἡ παροῦσα χρονικὴ συγγραφὴ τοῦ μεγάλου λογοθέτου τοῦ ᾿Ακροπολίτου ἐστὶν, ὃς ἔκτισε τὸ μοναστήριον τῆς ῾Αγίας τοῦ Χριστοῦ ᾿Ανατάσεως· περιέχει δὲ τὰ μετὰ τὴν ἅλωσιν. The third is the *Logothete of the Oikeiakon* (Logothete of Domestic Affairs). The *Incertus* says: "Let the Logothete of the Oikeiakon lead." Matthew says: "Praetor of the people, and at the same time Logothete of the Oikeiakon." The *phakeolion* and *epilorikion* are appropriate for him, but not the *skaranikon*. Codinus says: "The vestments of the Logothete of the Oikeiakon are the *phakeolis* and *epilorikion*, but not the *skaranikon*." He lacks a function: the same author says, "The current Logothete of the Oikeiakon has no service." The fourth is the *Logothete of the Stratiotikon* (Logothete of Military Affairs). The *Incertus* says: "Consider the Logothete also among the soldiers." Matthew says: "The Logothete of the military register." The vestments of the Logothete of the Army are the *phakeolion* and *epilorikion*, but not the *skaranikon*. Codinus says: "The vestments of the Logothete of the Stratiotikon are the *phakeolis* and *epilorikion*, but not the *skaranikon*," and he recognizes no ministry for him: "The Logothete of the Stratiotikon has no service." The fifth is the *Logothete of the Agelai* (Logothete of the Herds). The *Incertus* says: "And let the Logothete of the Herds enter." Matthew says: "Logothete of the Herds, and the *Dikaiophylax* (Keeper of Justice)." Codinus describes his habit: "The vestments of the Logothete of the Herds are the *phakeolis* and *epilorikion*, like those of the Logothete of the Army, but he does not use the *skaranikon*." Besides these, they add the section of the *Sekreta* (the Arcana). Anna Comnena mentions him in Book II. Nicetas, in *Life of Manuel Comnenus*, Book VII, says: "As the voice of the Latins expresses 'Chancellor,' so the Greeks would say 'Logothete'." John Cinnamus, in *Roman History*, Book IV [31], speaking of Richard, Lord of Andria, says: "When the gates of the city were guarded with little caution, he surrendered himself into the faith of Juvenatius. Hence, for the sake of occupying the others, he entered into counsel with other counts, and indeed with the Chancellor of William—whom a man speaking Greek would call a Logothete—and he proceeded with them as far as Trani, as if he were about to seize the city in a first assault." Before all these was the Great Logothete: for ambition, when it meets with the worst of morals, is such that unless it is adorned with great and distinguished offices, the offices themselves seem base; and unless you hold the leading parts in the dignity itself, and appear as a giant among the smallest pygmies or a whale among tiny fish, the dignity itself is valued at nothing. Hence, among the offices of the palace—would that this evil had not also had a place in ecclesiastical duties—the name "Great" was granted to the superior, so to speak, in that function. Not to recount individual details, the Great Logothete was distinguished not only by authority but also by the insignia of his garments. Codinus says: "The garment of the Great Logothete, that is, the *skiadion*, the *kabbadion*, and the *skaranikon*, are such as those of the Protostrator, but he carries no *dikaionikion* (scepter)." The vestments of the Great Logothete, that is, the hat, the *cabbadium*, and the *skaranikon*, do not differ from those of the Protostrator; nor does he carry a scepter. The hat is gold and scarlet in color, studded without a boss; the *skaranikon* is gold and scarlet, and fringed, containing on the front part the emperor standing, and on the back, sitting on a throne; the *cabbadium* is purple, or whatever he chooses by custom. Matthew the Monk says: "Of the second rank are the Great Duke and the Great Domestic, As the Protostrator is, so is the Great Logothete, And the Great Stratopedarch, whose hats are red, Showing gold only on the upper part." The *Incertus* reports that a gold and scarlet hat was given as a gift to the Logothete by the Emperor Andronicus: "And after him, the noble Metochites, The greatest Logothete, having attained wisdom, Wearing the red head-covering, Which the brilliant King Andronicus provided as a gift To him who brought power to him." Nicephorus Gregoras, *History*, Book VI, regarding the hat of the Great Logothete Muzalon: "For which reasons he held this honor, unique among those who had previously held a similar dignity, to wear on his head a *calyptra* covered with gold and purple cloth, differing in this one respect from that of the emperor's grandsons—insofar as it turns upward and flows toward the pyramid of the surface—that it did not also have the lower and hollow surface decorated with golden circles, but was completely smooth." For these reasons, this honor was held by him alone among the ancients who had performed the same duty. Furthermore, we learn the office of the Logothete from Codinus: "The Great Logothete arranges the mandates and *chrysobulls*, or golden letters, sent by the emperor to kings, sultans, and toparchs, and this is the proper ministry of the Great Logothete." So that it may be seen, as Gretser also noted, that they express exactly the Chancellors among the Latins.
From this, it is not incorrectly observed by Nicetas and Cinnamus—and let others judge whether he could be a "trivial" one—that the Greeks call chancellors "logothetes." Concerning this chancellorship, Casaubon has written much in *Flavius Vopiscus*, and Salmasius much more. Furthermore, Meursius observed that logothetes received an oath from the new emperor in the Church of Blachernae (from Nicetas, *Life of Manuel Comnenus*, Book 3), and that several were adorned with the same dignity at the same time (from Cantacuzene, Book 1, ch. 14). We ourselves also note that there was sometimes an advancement from the office of minor logothetes to that of the major, which Codinus recounts regarding Theodore Metochita. When the Emperor Andronicus Palaiologos decided to honor Theodore Metochita—logothete of the public treasury—with this title for the first time, he made him Great Logothete, a rank which held precedence over the previous Great Stratopedarch, though not the Protostrator. See Gregoras, *History*, lib. viii, cap. 19.
And in what sense could he have been a "trivial" schoolmaster (Ludimagister), whose authority and fame were so great that his writings were read daily in the churches and in public to confirm dogmas and stabilize the order of events, with the Fathers in Council praising them, and believing that a weight of truth was added to their own opinions if they could demonstrate that he had asserted the same things they had? I shall mention a few out of very many, and to pass over what Psellus expounds at length in his *Encomium* of him, I shall bring forward a few things from the context of his Troparia.
But since, as we have now observed, logothetes were various and diverse, it might be doubtful to which of them we ought to refer this our own logothete. I do not doubt that, as the situation required in the Constantinopolitan senate, this man of most prudent character performed various functions, and having administered his province most excellently, opened for himself a path from minor to major offices; which Psellus also relates in the same *Encomium*. Hence, I think that he is so highly valued for his writing—being called the Logothete of the Course, which I read on the title page of orations he himself compiled from the works of the divine Basil—because he ascended to the dignity of Great Logothete. Add that, for the most part, the name "logothete" [33] expressed without other addition signifies the Great Logothete to us. Not to be too lengthy in producing examples, we read in the chronicle of George Acropolites—though Dousa may be perplexed by it, and may confess that he does not know for certain which of those offices we have commemorated above the logothete George held—that he held the office of Great Logothete is certain from another hitherto unpublished addition to that same history; for this is prefaced to it: "The present chronological writing belongs to the Great Logothete Acropolites, who built the monastery of the Holy Resurrection of Christ; it contains everything after the capture [of the city], etc." Indeed, I had noted these things before the most clear and learned commentaries of Jacques Goar on George Codinus concerning the offices of the Palace had been published. But now that those have been edited, I seek from them whatever is stored in antiquity regarding this matter. They possess that which slakes the thirst and fulfills the desire. Adorned, then, with so many and such great dignities, how could our Symeon be a schoolmaster, and a trivial one at that? Let others judge, but those who perceive the heart of wisdom know that those who bark inconsiderately with a maddened mind speak folly.

NOTICE OF THE WRITERS REGARDING SYMEON METAPHRASTES (From Allatius, *On the writings of the Symeons*. Paris 1664, 4º.) 24 But now Symeon Metaphrastes, for whose sake alone this our Diatribe has been instituted, calls me to himself—a man admirable beyond human comprehension both in virtue and in his writings, by whose resource alone the structures of the Church, which are crumbling under the frauds of adversaries, might be solidified and sustained. The piety of such a man, his most elegant writings, his most ingenious labors, his vigilantly useful works, his sincere, truthful spirit that knew not how to lie, have stirred up the bile of heretics; they cannot endure their own falsehoods being so openly refuted by so great a writer. What then? Is he to be cast down from his rank? Is he to be removed from his dignity? Is he to be brought into low repute? Is he to be made one of the common crowd? Is he to be accused of inconsistency, falsehood, negligence, ignorance? Is his talent, his industry, his capability, his judgment to be denied? Thus, faith is taken away from man. Where there is no uncorrupted faith, there is credulity, inconsideration, and temerity. Lest they be said to believe rashly, or to trust wrongly, the statement must be examined as if it were doubtful, and judged; and in an examination so varied and so little fair, there will be no lack of contrivances for refuting—at least they will find room for frauds and deceits, and will render the reader’s mind suspended and anxious. Hence, all the slander of the heretics against Metaphrastes has flowed; but to what gain? Let Gesner be considered in his *Bibliotheca*. “Symeon the Constantinopolitan, a schoolmaster, composed in Greek about two hundred years ago Metaphrases, that is, Lives of the saints which are properly read in each month.” Hospinian, in *On Temples*, writes that he was a vulgar schoolmaster at Constantinople, that he wrote Legends of falsehoods, and that he did not live more than two hundred twenty years ago. Casaubon, in his *Exercises on Baronius* 16, no. 95: “To Metaphrastes, who wrote the life and deeds of that Longinus, we attribute as much as is owed to him, who spent his time in that collection with excellent diligence but with no judgment”—and Baronius judges no differently, whenever he applies his mind to it, which I wish he had always done. 25 Not to mention other passages of his, in which Metaphrastes, while he severely castsigates the "carter-talk" of old wives’ fables, is not consistent with himself in this history. And no. 96: “For the first, as far as is known today, Metaphrastes, a very recent writer and very often most vain, and a great compiler of old wives’ fables, mentions the matter.” And another: “We have the Lives of the saints translated into Latin by Lipomanus and Surius; but very many things are written in them fabulously: namely, Metaphrastes, as Bellarmine most truly says, added much from his own invention—not as the deeds happened, but as they could have happened.” Let each point be weighed, but with the judgment of prudent men. *Magistros*, Master, is a name of dignity and office in the Court of the emperors, and among others, one of the chief, as one who would claim for himself titles, *perifanos*, and *peri-kleipon*, who would hold a place among the patricians, nay, would be held above them, who would sit in councils, be chosen to support the sons of emperors at their baptism, and be joined to them by consanguinity—yet, through ignorance of Greek affairs, this is turned into "School-Master," and if it pleases the gods and Hospinian, a "vulgar" one. But to be brief, I shall report what Constantine Porphyrogenitus, who could have known this well, wrote of that dignity in the *Life of Basil the Macedonian*, which is not so well known. For he narrates concerning Basil: "The emperor, leading Basil skillfully toward what he wished, immediately after his return from the campaign 26 adopts him as his son. For he happened to be entirely lacking in his own offspring, and deemed him worthy of the most brilliant honor of the Magisters. Upon which the logothete Symbatius, bursting with envy, etc." Verily, by the providence of God bringing Basil to that which He wishes, immediately after that military training and return, the emperor adopts him as his son, because he himself had no children, and adorns him with the shining dignity of the Magisters. By this, the envy of the logothete Symbatius is broken, etc. The dignity of the Magisters is such that it was not neglected even by the emperor's sons, but the sons of emperors would move from a lower rank to it as to something more sublime. Those who say these things, why do they not produce their author? Among whom did they find them? And it is necessary that they have found them, unless they are mandating to writing their own meditations and not historical facts. See what Codinus, other historians, and the Greco-Barbarian Glossators say about the dignity of Master. And so that they may rave more bitterly, let them hear and learn, and only then rage, that Metaphrastes, born at Constantinople of an illustrious family and sprung from the highest lineage, abounding in wealth, and adorned by the emperors with various dignities, could not have been a man of the lowest bench, much less a "vulgar" schoolmaster. Michael Psellus in his *Encomium* on the same man: "And what is more wonderful, and for which one would admire him all the more, is that he possessed from his home life both good lineage, and the gifts of glory, and great wealth..."
