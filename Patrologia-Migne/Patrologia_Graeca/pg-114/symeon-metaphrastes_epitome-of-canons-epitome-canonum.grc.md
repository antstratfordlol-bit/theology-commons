Mutare stylum, mutare rerum et personarum faciem, membra etiam ipsa in meliorem statum, ut videtur componere, posteaque pro iisdem primis divendere ac publicare, astutia est, fallacia est, fraus est, auctoribus piis sursum versum os offucia sublinere est, ut nihil de sententia dicam, quam an mutato stylo, firmam in omnibus ille tenuerit, judicent alii. Ni forte apud nonnullos veniam sibi inveniet fraus ab ipso, qui eam comminiscitur professa. Sed quis minimum excusabit eam apertam fraudem non esse, cum Lector Suriana ab auctoris geminis verbis dignoscere non valeat? Poterat ille sua characterum alio atque alio genere in ipsa editione ab aliis distinguere, ut fecere in Interpretatione Thucydidis a Valla conscripta Stephanus et Portus. Tum Surio gratias ageremus, quod illius opera, et auctorem veterem [140] haberemus, et illius studio elegantiorem, et ictu oculi et Suriana, et Auctoris verba, sententiasque conferemus. Mihi sane æque ac tibi simplex, minimeque fucatum dicendi genus, quod tantopere admirata est Christiana antiquitas summopere perplacet, et veritas ab omni ornatu denudata animum adhibet stimulis ad pietatem excitat: non ita tamen ut res illæ elegantiori stylo expositæ, et amenioribus colorum flosculis instinctæ rejiciantur. Complector summe elegantes, et Latinas dicendi formulas cum veritate conjunctas, quibus doctior factus una pietatem addisco. Sed mihi sermo est de mutato in aliorum scriptis stylo, posteaque illis Auctoris primi nomine sine ulla mutationis nota divenditis. Videndum est, An et Metaphrastes quid simile fecerit. Videamus. Psellus, qui omnia Metaphrastæ studia in scribendis Vitis sanctorum accuratissime prosecutus est, quamvis dixerat, male comptas, inurbane conscriptas elegantiore stylo enarrasse, nunquam tamen dixit, in aliquibus stylum immutasse; in aliquibus ita ut erat reliquisse: postmodum Vitam illam sub primi scriptoris nomine publicasset. Quod et verum est; nam Symeon Vitas, quas sibi scribere proposuerat, ita in alium sermonem extulit, ut prope jam essent elocutione a primis illis diversæ, ideoque suas Μεταφράσεις, Metaphrases, in aliam nempe linguam conversas vocavit, et sub suo nomine, abolito vetere scriptore, edi curavit. Et licet in antiquis scriptoris fieret mentio, Metaphrastes tamen illius suppresso nomine, ne tempora confunderentur, reliqua sibi probata scripto consignavit. Ubi vero scriptoris Antiquioris memoriam facere voluit, id ita fecit, ut non videretur illius Vitæ, quam commentabatur Symeon, auctor, sed alterius, quæ ante ipsum circumferebatur. Μέγας τοίνυν οὗτος ὤν, καὶ πολὺς τὴν φήμην γενόμενος, πολλῶν ἔτυχε καὶ τῶν συγγραφέων· ἀλλ᾿ οὐδεὶς οὐδέπω κατὰ μέρος τὰ κατ᾿ αὐτὸν διεξῆλθεν, οὐδὲ εἰς ἅπαντα καθῆκεν ἑαυτόν, οὐδὲ ἀκριβῶς, ὅπως ἕκαστα εἶχον, ἀνέγραψεν. Ἀλλὰ τοὺς ἄνω χρόνους ἐν κεφαλαιώδεσιν ἐπιτομαῖς εἰπόντες, ἐνιά τε τῶν μέσῳ παρέδραμον, καὶ οὐδὲ πρὸς αὐτὸ τὸ τέλος τῶν τούτου πράξεων ἦλθον· ἡμῖν δὲ χρήσιμος ὁ περὶ αὐτοῦ ἔσται λόγος, ἄνωθεν τε ἀρχομένος, καὶ ἐπὶ τὰ τελευταῖα τοῦ ἀνδρὸς καταδιδάξουσι τὴν διήγησιν. Magnum Symeonem, ingens orbis terrarum miraculum, et ex iis, qui terræ regiones alias atque alias incoluerunt, quidam res gestas illius oculis, quidam auditu percipientes, virum linguae suæ delicias fecere. Magnus itaque hic cum esset, et fama illius esset undique pervulgata, multos habuit suarum rerum scriptores; sed nemo unquam ad hæc tempora omnes persecutus est, nec universas colligendi studium insumpset, neque accurate, ut singula accidere, scripto signavit; sed superiores annos capitibus compendio digestis describentes, nonnulla, quæ media fuere, silentio involuerunt [141], nec ad suarum actionum exitum pervenerunt. Nobis vero erit sermo de eo proficuus, qui ab anteactis incipimus, et ad viri postremas actiones narrationem dirigimus. Vitam sancti Joannis Eleemonis scripsisse Sophronium et Joannem habeo ex Leontio Cyprio in Supplemento Vitæ illius: "Ἤδη μὲν οὖν καὶ ἕτεροι φθάσαντες πρὸ ἡμῶν κάλλιστά τε καὶ ὑψηλότερα περὶ τούτου τοῦ ἀρχιερέως καὶ θαυμασίου ἀνδρὸς ἐφιλοσόφησαν, δυνατοὶ ὄντες ἔργῳ καὶ λόγῳ, λέγω δή, Ἰωάννης καὶ Σωφρόνιος οἱ θεοσεβεῖς καὶ φιλάρετοι, καὶ τῆς εὐσεβείας ὄντες ὑπέρμαχοι. Ἀλλ᾿ ὅμως καὶ τοιοῦτοι τυγχάνοντες τῆς ἀξίας, καὶ αὐτοὶ τῆς τοῦ ἀνδρὸς ἀρετῆς ἀπελείφθησαν, καὶ ταὐτὸν πεπόνθασιν, οἱονεί τινες φιλόπονοι γεωργοὶ ἄμπελον εὐθαλῆ καὶ κατάκαρπον τρυγῶντες, ὅτι καταλείψουσι πάντως ἐκ τῆς τοῦ καρποῦ εὐλογίας, καὶ μὴ βουλόμενοι τοῖς κατόπισθεν ἀκολουθοῦσι πτωχοῖς ἐπιρωλογουμένοις, τὴν ἄμπελον, ἐξ ὧν ἐσμεν καὶ ἡμεῖς ἐλάχιστοι. Εἰ γὰρ καὶ πάσῃ αὐτῶν δυνάμει οἱ πανόσιοι οὗτοι τὴν ἔγκαρπον ταύτην ἐλαίαν ἀληθῶς διὰ τῶν ἐν αὐτῇ πλουσίων ἐλαίων, τὴν ἐν τῷ οἴκῳ τοῦ θεοῦ, ὡς φησιν ὁ ὑμνῳδὸς Δαυῒδ πεφυτευμένην τρυγᾶν ζήλῳ θεοῦ ἐπετήδευσαν· ἀλλ᾿ ὅμως διέλαθεν αὐτοὺς ὁ πολὺς τῆς ἐλαίας καρπός, καὶ τὴν ἡμετέραν εὐτελῆ καὶ ψυχρὰν προθυμίαν προσδέξασθαι, ὡς τὰ δύο τῆς χήρας λεπτά. Οὐ γὰρ ἐκείνους διαβάλλοντες, οὐδὲ ὡς δυνάμενοι τὴν αὐτῶν θεοδώρητον σοφίαν μιμεῖσθαι, τὰ τοῦ δικαίου κατορθώματα συγγραφῇ παραδοῦναι ἐπεσύσαμεν. Jam enim et alii ante nos optima quæque sublimiora de hoc præsule, et admirando viro philosophati sunt, qui opere et sermone mirum in modum præstabant, Joannes et Sophronius pii et studiosi, pietatisque propugnatores. Conferri possunt Vitæ sanctorum ante Metaphrastem ab auctoribus Synchronis scriptæ, quæ certe adhuc supersunt plurimæ, cum descriptis a Metaphraste. Nihil ibi commune invenies. Sua de se narrat Metaphrastes, aliorum ut aliena et Scriptorum antiquorum propria, non facta, neque personas cum suis confundens, ut aliena prosequitur. Quare cessat dubitatio de illis Vitis, quæ sub Metaphrastæ nomine veniunt, ex ipsis tamen colligitur alios, non Metaphrastem, fuisse illarum scriptores. Adde etiam, si lubet, Vitam sancti Eustathii et sociorum ante Metaphrastem scriptam, cujus illud est principium: Πρόκειται τοῖς ἀνθρώποις καὶ ἐκ φύσεως. Scriptor in Proœmio refert, sua ætate tantum illuxisse virtutem: Οὐδὲν οὖν ἧττον προσεῖναι τοῖς ἐγγράφοις τῶν ἀρχαίων ἀνδρῶν ἀνδραγαθήμασιν, καὶ τὸ νῦν ἐν τοῖς καιροῖς ἡμῶν κατορθωθέντα πρὸς εὐεργεσίαν μὲν τῶν ἀκουόντων, ἐπιστροφὴν δὲ τῶν φασκόντων μὴ δύνασθαι νῦν τοιαῦτα κατορθοῦσθαι, οἷα ἐν τοῖς παλαιοτέροις χρόνοις. Non minus itaque mihi proposui aliis antiquorum præclare gestis, scriptoque mandatis, adde, et quæ nunc hac nostra ætate summa cum laude patrata sunt, tum ad utilitatem audientium, tum eorum conversionem, qui dicunt non posse hoc ævo talia absolvi, qualia antiquitus facta sunt. Cum suam ex eodem Metaphrastes conscriberet, nihil simile suis interseruit, sed alio proœmio appicto, cætera narrat de suo, ut frustra Scriptoris ætatem in Metaphraste quæras. [143] Scripseram in Diatriba mea alia quoque Metaphrastæ scripta egisse, quæ tamen in Diatriba ante transmissa non habebantur. Ita est. Et plane in Diatriba egi abunde de illis. Josephus the Bishop of Methone calls the Ephesian Metaphrastes a "younger theologian," from which one might rightly say that Symeon the younger theologian, whose twenty-three orations and other works James Pontanus published in Latin, is the same as Symeon Metaphrastes, who finally became a monk and superior of the monastery of Saint Mamas. And indeed, the Greeks express this Symeon as they usually do by the name of Logothete, because he was indeed once a great Logothete. Why then could he not elsewhere, omitting the name of Metaphrastes, be called only the younger theologian, for the sake of distinction from the divine Nazianzen, who had claimed the name of Theologian for himself before him? Since I have dealt with this more fully in the *Diatribe* about the Symeons, I will explain myself briefly here. It is true that Symeon the younger theologian was a monk. It is true that Metaphrastes is understood only under the name of Logothete: yet it is wrong for Methonensis to confuse the younger theologian with Metaphrastes, deceived by the authority of certain manuscript codices, which note the treatises of Symeon, superior of Saint Mamas, under this title: *Of Metaphrastes Lord Symeon and the Younger Theologian concerning Attention or Prayer*. And, *Other chapters of Symeon Metaphrastes and the younger Theologian*. To omit the rest, that Symeon Metaphrastes and Symeon, superior of Saint Mamas, were one and the same person, the times themselves do not suggest. Metaphrastes lived under Leo the Wise, son of Basil; the superior of Saint Mamas under Alexius Comnenus, who, having ejected Botaniates, seized the empire, [148] Metaphrastes the younger almost seven years and two hundred. This is confirmed by the testimony of the same Symeon, who says in his Oration: "He himself, [the enemy] of men, that is, the devil," and so on. For six thousand six hundred and more years he has remained the enemy of men. He calculates, according to the Greek custom, from the creation of the world: wherefore this year will be 1092 of Christ. And it is described in the Palatine codex with the syllables *hexakosis*, six hundred. But if we read with others *diakosis*, two hundred, as Pontanus represented, those Symeons are still distinct in age, and Symeon Metaphrastes will be younger than the Theologian by at least 194 years, if Symeon at Xerocercus wrote in the year of the world 6200, which corresponds to the year 692 of Christ, under Justinian Rhinotmetus, about thirty years before the heresy of the Iconoclasts. But if he mentions Stephen the younger killed by Copronymus for the defense of the holy images in the year of Christ 767, then he will also be much older than Metaphrastes, perhaps by a hundred years. But how could someone living in the year 602 of Christ mention Stephen the younger, who suffered martyrdom in the year 767, being a hundred years older? Hence, from this second number, an error is manifest, and consequently the first number is more probable, by which this superior at Xerocercus is later than Metaphrastes. Whatever, however, the case may be, it is entirely concluded that he is another
Symeon Metaphrastes, and the other a superior at Xerocercus, and the argument collapses: for since these two are far different, it is wrongly inferred from the fact that a superior at Xerocercus was a monk, that Symeon Metaphrastes was a monk. But as I have said, he was such in the Diatriba.
But that Metaphrastes was not a monk, when even those things which are told about the superior at Xerocercus and others are not true, whence does Leo have this? He has it from the fact that he was bound to a wife in his already-confirmed age: nor did he ever marry again afterward, when she had died, or bind himself to another kind of life when released from the marriage bond. But he could, with his wife consenting, have committed himself to a monastery, or even have donned the monastic habit when near to death according to the custom of the Greeks, and especially of the Magnates. He could, I do not deny: therefore, he did. But this must be proven. I myself have the opposite view: until the contrary is established by authority, I will never give my hand. But, infected by the opinion of the Greeks regarding the notion that all sins were wiped away by the habit, he assumed the habit at the very end of life. This also must be proven for those who assert the contrary. Psellus, when he pursues his other praises, would not have been silent about such a prime monument of piety. Turn to the words.
How many crowns of excellence would not this wonderful man be crowned with? Or rather, with how many is he not already crowned, when he has ascended to God and leads the choirs of those whose morals and lives he transcribed? For even in the end he showed what kind of life he had lived: for those who saw him did not say that he seemed cut off or divided, but he seemed as if released from a certain bond, and with a joyful countenance he reached out to the angels leading him, in such a way as to commit himself into their hands, that he might quickly depart from the body. And he had died, indeed, in this way; but he immediately filled all things with the abundance of the highest fragrance. Joannes Caminiata, a cleric and Cubuclesius of Thessalonica, flourished in the times of Leo the Wise, Emperor of Constantinople. M. Baillet does not speak more advantageously of Metaphrastes: "One can be sure," he says (*Disc. sur la Vie des Saints*, p. 59), "that it is for having known too well how to lie that some heretics have looked upon this author as an impostor and a forger, born to produce monsters, exaggerated beyond what pagan Greece imagined in fables in favor of its gods." Following this, he has Casaubon say that he found much vanity and little judgment in the work of Metaphrastes, and that he remarked there all the faults of poets and declaimers, in that excessive itch to feign and to overwhelm the truth under fables. After having reported the judgment of some other Protestants, he has not forgotten that of several other Catholic authors who do not approve of his falsifications or his infidelities.
His authority seems so slight, and his name so hateful to M. Baillet, that he would make it a point of conscience to cite him, and even to name him in his work; and this is what he thought he should warn the public about, in these terms (*Avertiss.*, p. 14): "I have refrained much more still from using and even from naming authors deemed disreputable, such as Metaphrastes and Nicephorus among the Greeks." Finally, M. Baillet and M. de Tillemont have such disgust for everything that comes from this legendary author, that when they want to mark that a fact or a history is false or fabulous, they content themselves to say that it has passed through Metaphrastic hands; or else that it is in the style of Metaphrastes.
However just this censure may seem, it is nonetheless not approved of by everyone. There are many learned men who have testified to much respect for Metaphrastes, who have praised his work and the services he rendered to the Church, and who have even undertaken his defense. But as this is not what is at issue here, I leave it to those who may wish to take upon themselves the task of examining the reasons on both sides, to decide if the complaints made against Metaphrastes are just, or if they are exaggerated.
What concerns us, therefore, is to make some reflections on what M. de Tillemont and M. Baillet tell us about Metaphrastes. It is certain that these scholars almost never cite this author, except to discover and censure his faults: and if one excludes four or five places, one will find that they have never cited him to support a fact, nor for what concerns the history of the life of a saint who lived outside of his country and before his century.

After the city was subsequently handed over to the enemy by the just judgment of God, that transcription also came to an end after a few pages, interrupted either by the storming of the city or by the misfortune—would that it were not the slaughter—of the copyist. Be that as it may, the author is elegant, if anyone is; he frequently uses foreign turns of phrase and figures from the storehouse of the Sophists. His periods are concise and full, except when they are rendered harsh and obscure by the novelty of their composition. To put it in a word, he models himself entirely on Procopius. And it is a wonder that, among so many other writers of a later age who rave in their speaking and writing in a tumultuous fashion, he alone should have been wise. Someone might say that he indulged himself too much in narrating the affairs of the Greeks, making them greater than they were. To these, however, Thucydides will reply: "Up to this point, praises spoken of others are tolerable, insofar as each man thinks himself capable of doing the things he has heard of. But when they exceed this, men are already envious and disbelieve." To that extent are the praises of others tolerable, as long as each man considers himself equal to achieving any of them: but those to whom he considers himself unequal, he envies, and does not believe. Regarding the Roman pontiffs, when the occasion has presented itself, he is always and sharply intemperate. But others might examine these things more equitably.
I have received the first complete part of the Marian piety of the Greeks. I give immortal thanks; I shall rouse myself to it with such sweet and learned reading, and with most erudite notes, surely sprinkled like gem-like pebbles. Do not succumb to the burden, I pray, in such a great calamity of our times. Marian piety itself will suffice for your efforts in this most laborious task with the greatest of aids. It is reported to me that my treatise on the perpetual agreement of the Western and Eastern Church has now reached its end, along with my dissertations—one on the Sundays and Weeks of the Greeks, the other on the Mass of the Presanctified—published in Amsterdam or Cologne. It has not yet been seen in Rome. Farewell, most excellent man, and love me, your most obedient servant, as you do. Rome, September 25, 1648.

You do not approve of Surius’s *Factum*, nor do I praise it: nor do I think that others will, unless men who praise things from the dregs. To change the style, to change the appearance of things and persons, to assemble even the very limbs into a better state, as it seems, and afterwards to sell and publish them as if they were those very same originals, is cunning, it is fallacy, it is fraud; it is to daub the faces of pious authors, so that—to say nothing of the meaning—others may judge whether he has kept it firm in all respects, even with a changed style. Unless perhaps the fraud he himself invented may find favor with some by being openly avowed. But who will excuse the fact that it is not an open fraud, when the reader of the Surian edition is unable to distinguish it from the original words of the author? He could have distinguished his own characters in the edition itself by another type, as Stephanus and Portus did in the interpretation of Thucydides written by Valla. Then we would give thanks to Surius, because we would have the original author and, through his study, a more elegant one, and we would compare, at a glance, both the Surian and the Author’s words and sentiments. To me, indeed, as well as to you, that simple and in no way adulterated manner of speaking, which Christian antiquity has so greatly admired, is most pleasing, and truth, stripped of all ornament, excites the soul to piety with sharper stings: not, however, in such a way that those things explained in a more elegant style and distinguished by more pleasant flowers of color are to be rejected. I embrace elegant formulas of speech, and those joined with truth, from which, having become more learned, I simultaneously learn piety. But my discourse is about the style changed in the writings of others, and then sold as theirs, without any note of the change, under the name of the original Author. One must see whether Metaphrastes did something similar. Let us see. Psellus, who most accurately followed all of Metaphrastes’s studies in writing the *Lives* of the saints, although he said he recounted those that were poorly compiled and unurbane in a more elegant style, yet he never says that he changed the style in some, and in others left it as it was: and afterwards published that *Life* under the name of the first writer. Which is also true; for Symeon brought the *Lives* which he had proposed to write for himself into another style in such a way that they were already nearly different in elocution from those first ones, and therefore he called his own *Metaphraseis* (Metaphrases), that is, converted into another tongue, and he published them under his own name, with the old writer abolished. And although mention of the writer was made in antiquities, Metaphrastes, with his name suppressed—lest the times be confused—consigned the rest that were approved by him to writing. But when he wished to make mention of the older writer, he did it in such a way that it did not seem that the author of the *Life* which Symeon was commenting upon was the author, but someone else, who was circulated before him. For he admits that this or that writer had written that *Life* before him. This will be made clear by an example. In the *Life of Symeon Stylites* in the *Mandra*: of Symeon the great, the great marvel of the universe, and all those who happened to obtain some other part of the earth. M. de Tillemont, speaking of the testimony of the Fathers of the Church (*Avertissement*, p. 13): "One has not," he says, "been obliged to have the same respect for the Menaia of the Greeks, nor for the Acts that are of Metaphrastes, or of other newcomers in times when the truth of history was altered by popular traditions, and often by fictions invented on purpose. It has been thought, I say, that no regard should be paid to histories of this kind." And in note 3 on Papias (T. II, p. 663, col. We have," this learned critic says further (Ib. not. 3, on Saint Ignatius, p. 620, col. 1), "the Acts of Saint Ignatius, which are read in Metaphrastes: their author is sufficient to show that one cannot see much credibility there."
M. Baillet does not speak more advantageously of Metaphrastes: "One can be sure," he says (*Disc. sur la Vie des Saints*, p. 59), "that it is for having known too well how to lie that some heretics have looked upon this author as an impostor and a forger, born to produce monsters, exaggerated beyond what pagan Greece imagined in fables in favor of its gods." Following this, he has Casaubon say that he found much vanity and little judgment in the work of Metaphrastes, and that he remarked there all the faults of poets and declaimers, in that excessive itch to feign and to overwhelm the truth under fables. After having reported the judgment of some other Protestants, he has not forgotten that of several other Catholic authors who do not approve of his falsifications or his infidelities.
His authority seems so slight, and his name so hateful to M. Baillet, that he would make it a point of conscience to cite him, and even to name him in his work; and this is what he thought he should warn the public about, in these terms (*Avertiss.*, p. 14): "I have refrained much more still from using and even from naming authors deemed disreputable, such as Metaphrastes and Nicephorus among the Greeks." Finally, M. Baillet and M. de Tillemont have such disgust for everything that comes from this legendary author, that when they want to mark that a fact or a history is false or fabulous, they content themselves to say that it has passed through Metaphrastic hands; or else that it is in the style of Metaphrastes.
However just this censure may seem, it is nonetheless not approved of by everyone. There are many learned men who have testified to much respect for Metaphrastes, who have praised his work and the services he rendered to the Church, and who have even undertaken his defense. But as this is not what is at issue here, I leave it to those who may wish to take upon themselves the task of examining the reasons on both sides, to decide if the complaints made against Metaphrastes are just, or if they are exaggerated.
What concerns us, therefore, is to make some reflections on what M. de Tillemont and M. Baillet tell us about Metaphrastes. It is certain that these scholars almost never cite this author, except to discover and censure his faults: and if one excludes four or five places, one will find that they have never cited him to support a fact, nor for what concerns the history of the life of a saint who lived outside of his country and before his century.
§ II. — The learned critics have derived several things from Metaphrastes.
One cannot deny that there were great gaps in the collection of the Lives of the saints given by M. Arnaud d'Andilly, in the *Actes sincères et choisis* of Dom Thierry Ruinart, in the *Mémoires* of M. Tillemont, in the *Vie des Saints* of M. Baillet, and in the works of other scholars who have worked on the same subject, and who show so little esteem for Metaphrastes, if one were to remove from them all that comes from this writer: for if one goes back up to the source of several Acts of the martyrs and Lives of the saints, with which these scholars have filled their works, one will see that a very large number of these pieces were given by the Greeks to Metaphrastes. Michael Psellus made a very beautiful panegyric in his honor. Theodore Balsamon praised his collection of the Lives of the Saints highly, in the Council of Constantinople, can. 63. Leo Allatius maintains that Metaphrastes was very sincere, and that it is for having been too upright that the heretics rose up against him. Roland, whom one places among the most learned critics of the last century, undertook his apology against Bellarmine. He endeavors to show by several reasons that the excesses of Metaphrastes are not as enormous as one has wished to publish, and to justify the conduct he held in his Collection of the Lives of the Saints.

But if one had asked these two legendaries what is the source from which they drew them, they would both have confessed that they come to us from Metaphrastes, who composed them or at least preserved them: Auctore Symeone Metaphraste, or ex Metaphraste, says Surius.
Although there are several Acts of the martyrs in the collection of Dom Thierry (Acta selecta Martyrum) which have not passed through the hands of Metaphrastes and which he did not even know; there are nevertheless some that he has preserved for us and for which we are indebted to him. It is through this channel that we have received the Acts of Saint Justin the Philosopher, as M. de Tillemont acknowledges; the Acts of Saint Agape, Saint Chionia, and Saint Irene, sisters, and their martyr companions; those of Saint Didymus and Saint Theodora the virgin, martyrs; those of Saint Pherbutha or Tarbula, virgin and martyr, and other martyrs of Persia under Sapor; those of Saint Sadoth, bishop of Seleucia and Ctesiphon in Persia, martyr; those of Saint Sabas, a Goth by nation, martyr; those of Saint Bademus, abbot in Persia, martyr. One does not undertake to gather here everything that M. Baillet borrowed from Metaphrastes in his work on the Lives of the Saints; that would take us too far. It suffices, therefore, for my purpose to remark that this learned critic collected all the Lives of the Saints provided by M. Arnauld, and which we have noted as having been drawn from Metaphrastes. One must add to them the Acts of the martyrs from the collection of Dom Thierry Ruinart, which we have reported and which come from the same source. But these are not the only Metaphrastic histories that M. Baillet inserted into his work: here are others that are found in full in this Greek author, if one excepts six or seven from which M. Baillet borrowed only some rather considerable extracts. Such are the Lives of Saint Marcian of Constantinople, of Saint John the Almsgiver; of Saint Cyrus and Saint John, martyrs in Egypt; of Saint Polyeuctus the martyr; of Saint Theophanes surnamed the Confessor; of Saint Carpus, bishop of Thyatira in Asia, and his companions; of Saint Marina, solitary virgin; of Saint Arsenius, tutor and governor of the emperor Arcadius, then a solitary in Egypt; of Saint Pantaleon the physician and martyr, and his companions; of the holy Faith, Hope, and Charity, sisters, virgins, martyrs, and their mother Saint Sophia; of Saint Anastasia the widow, abbess of Timia in Greece; of Saint Andrew, tribune or colonel, martyr, and his companions; of Saint Cyriacus or Quiriacus, anchorite in Palestine; of Saint Arethas and his companions, martyrs in Arabia; of Saint Joannicius, hermit, abbot in Bithynia; of Saint Theodore of Amasea in Pontus, martyr; of Saint Menas, martyr in Phrygia; of Saint Nicholas, bishop of Myra in Syria; of Saint Daniel, surnamed the Stylite, solitary; and of several others that are omitted so as not to be too long.
It must be remembered that when it is asserted that M. Arnaud, M. de Tillemont, and M. Baillet have reported at length some of the Lives of the Saints attributed to Metaphrastes, this means that they have preserved the substance of the history and have only removed from it some miraculous facts, some prodigies that were not to their taste, or some minor circumstances that they deemed appropriate to omit. But as for the Acts of the martyrs that come from Metaphrastes, and which Dom Thierry Ruinart placed in his collection, they are there without any alteration, and in the same manner as he found them in the manuscripts and in the printed editions: for although these Acts passed through the channel of Metaphrastes, they received no stain from him.
It seems, after this, that one must agree on two things: the first, that these learned critics drew many things from this Greek legendary, although M. de Tillemont had said that he did not believe himself obliged to have any regard for the histories of this au-
teur; and that M. Baillet had promised the public that he would refrain from employing in his work, and even from naming, discredited authors such as Metaphrastes. The second is that they have been persuaded that the name of this writer could hardly authorize the facts they might have supported with his testimony, nor do much honor to their works. It is undoubtedly for this reason that they do not cite him, and that they believe themselves dispensed even from naming him in matters that come from him. One sees, in fact, that they content themselves with saying that a story is found in Lipoman, Mombrice, Surius, Baronius, Bollandus, without making any mention of Metaphrastes. But if one consults these legendaries, one will see that they do nothing but report these facts, and that they attribute them to this Greek author: Auctore Simeone Metaphraste, or ex Metaphraste, as one finds in a hundred places in Surius, Bollandus, and the others.
I do not know if this manner of citing has appeared sufficiently exact to these scholars: but there is every appearance that they would not approve the conduct of a writer who, having put into French the history of the martyrs of Lyon, would cite for that Surius, apud Surium, or would content himself with saying that it is reported by Surius (Tom. III, 2 Jun.). For although it is true that this learned Carthusian inserted into his collection the letter of the Churches of Lyon and Vienne concerning their martyrs, nevertheless, as he admits that Eusebius has preserved this piece for us and that he inserted it into his History, it seems that the ordinary manner of citing this letter would be to give it first in our language and to put in the margin as a note, apud Eusebium, and not apud Surium, as these scholars practice: why then should there be any exception for Metaphrastes?
§ III. — Examination of the reasons for which critics claim to be authorized not to cite Metaphrastes, even in matters they have drawn from him.
Although we have, these scholars will perhaps say (Baill. Disc. pag. 58), a quantity of Lives of the Saints that are attributed to Metaphrastes, nevertheless Leo Allatius, making the distinction of what belongs to this legendary from what is foreign to him, has recognized only one hundred and twenty-two Lives of his making, and five hundred and thirty-nine, both Lives and Panegyrics or historical Eulogies, which falsely bear his name. Does one not find, in fact, that the Life of Saint Porphyry, Bishop of Gaza, is written by Mark his disciple; that of Saint Auxentius by a contemporary author; that of Saint Sabas, superior of several monasteries in Palestine, and of Saint John the Silent, by Cyril, a monk of Saint Sabas? Does one not know that Eustathius, a priest, composed that of Saint Eutyches, Patriarch of Constantinople; George Eleuse, that of Saint Theodore, abbot; Ignatius, Bishop of Nicaea, that of Saint Tarasius, Patriarch of Constantinople; Theophanes, a priest, the treatise on the exile and translation of the relics of Saint Nicephorus; finally Theosterictus, that of Nicetas the abbot, his master? One is further persuaded, these critics will add, that all the Lives of the Saints written in Greek that remain to us are not by Metaphrastes: one may put in this number the history of Saint John the Calybite, that of Saint John the Almsgiver, that of Saint Xene or Eusebia, whose memory the Church celebrates on January 15, 23, and 24; and several others that it would be useless to report here.
Moreover, these scholars will further say, even if we had some Lives of the saints of the making of Metaphrastes, and even if several had passed through his hands, one could only grant him the title of glossator, or interpolator, and if one wishes, that of author, but of a pitiful author: for one believes oneself entitled to hold, with regard to his collection, the same conduct that two great cardinals held with regard to the Golden Legend (5). The first, who is Bellarmine, did not judge its author worthy of being named in any place (Baill. Disc. sur la vie des saints), and Baronius, who is the second, fearing to diminish the price of his notes on the Roman Martyrology if one saw there a name that the monstrous fictions of the Golden Legend had dishonored, preferred to name another less known who gave a new edition of it (6). The excesses, the impostures, and the lies of Metaphrastes have so discredited him that he has lost all the reputation that his labors might have earned him: so that scholars do not believe themselves obliged to name him in their works, fearing to diminish their price, and to repel the reader, if he saw a name that his monstrous fictions have dishonored. Thus they prefer to name Surius, Baronius, Bollandus, and the others who have collected some of the Lives of the saints, even though they come from this corrupted source.

Finally, the more these critics strive to expose the untrustworthiness of Metaphrastes and to portray him as an impostor and a forger, born to produce monstrosities, an author whose extravagance surpasses all the fables pagan Greece devised in honor of its gods, the more the public must be surprised that men so enlightened, who had led it to expect that they would advance nothing that did not come from the purest sources of antiquity, should have borrowed so many Acts and Lives of saints, and so many substantial extracts, from an author so discredited, and should have supported so great a number of facts with testimony so unworthy of belief. For whatever efforts they make to conceal the source of some of these accounts and the channel through which others reached them, under the names of those who compiled collections from Metaphrastes, it will nevertheless always remain true that he preserved for us numerous Acts of martyrs and several Lives of saints of which nothing is found elsewhere, and that no manuscript or work by any author can be produced that mentions them before him. Thus all who followed him received these Acts only through his mediation.
