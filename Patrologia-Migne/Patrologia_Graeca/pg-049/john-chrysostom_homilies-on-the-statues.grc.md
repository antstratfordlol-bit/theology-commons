Ἐπεὶ οὖν ἅπαντα ἐπελθεῖν ἀδύνατον, τί βούλεσθε τῶν εἰρημένων προχειρι-σάμενοι πρὸς τὴν ὑμετέραν ἀγάπην εἴπωμεν; Καθάπερ γὰρ ἐν λειμῶνι πολλὰ καὶ ποικίλα ὁρῶ τῆς ἀναγνώσεως τὰ ἄνθη, καὶ πολλὴν μὲν τὴν ῥοδωνιὰν, πολλὰ δὲ τὰ ἴα, καὶ οὐκ ἐλάττω τὰ κρίνα, ἀλλὰ ποικίλον πανταχοῦ καὶ δαψιλῆ τοῦ πνεύματος διεσπαρμένον τὸν καρπὸν, καὶ πολλὴν τὴν εὐωδίαν· μᾶλλον δὲ οὐχὶ λειμὼν μόνον, ἀλλὰ καὶ παράδεισός ἐστι τῶν θείων Γραφῶν ἡ ἀνάγνωσις· οὐ γὰρ εὐωδίαν ψιλὴν ἔχει ταῦτα τὰ ἄνθη μόνον, ἀλλὰ καὶ καρπὸν ψυχὴν τρέφειν δυνάμενον. Τί τοίνυν βούλεσθε τῶν εἰρημένων εἰς μέσον ἀγάγωμεν τήμερον; βούλεσθε ὃ δο-κεῖ πάντων εὐτελέστερον εἶναι, καὶ τοῖς τυχοῦσιν εὐσύν-οπτον, τοῦτο μεταχειρισώμεθα νῦν; Ἔμοιγε δοκεῖ, καὶ ὑμῖν εὖ οἶδ’ ὅτι συνδόξει. Τί ποτ’ οὖν ἐστι τὸ τῶν ἄλλων εὐκολώτερον; τί δὲ ἕτερον, ἀλλ’ ἢ ὃ καὶ τῷ τυχόντι δοκεῖ ῥᾴδιον εἶναι, καὶ εὐσύνοπτον εἰπεῖν; Ποῖον δὴ τοῦτο; Οἴνῳ ὀλίγῳ χρῶ διὰ τὸν στόμαχόν σου καὶ τὰς πυκνάς σου ἀσθενείας. Μὴ τοίνυν παρέργως ἀκούσωμεν, ἐπεὶ καὶ οἱ τὴν μεταλλικὴν ἕψοντες γῆν, ἐπειδὰν εἰς τὸ χωνευτήριον αὐτὴν ἐμβάλωσιν, οὐχὶ τὰς μάζας τοῦ χρυσοῦ μόνον ἀναιροῦνται, ἀλλὰ καὶ τὰ μικρὰ ψήγματα μετὰ πολλῆς συλλέγουσι τῆς ἀκριβείας. THE WORKS OF OUR HOLY FATHER JOHN CHRYSOSTOM, ARCHBISHOP OF CONSTANTINOPLE.
HOMILIES XXI ON THE STATUES, DELIVERED TO THE PEOPLE OF ANTIOCH. HOMILY ONE, DELIVERED AT ANTIOCH IN THE PALÆA OR OLD CHURCH, WHILE HE WAS STILL A PRESBYTER, ON THAT SAYING OF THE APOSTLE: USE A LITTLE WINE FOR THY STOMACH’S SAKE AND THINE OFTEN INFIRMITIES (1 TIM. 5:23).
Did you hear the apostolic voice, the trumpet sounding from heaven, the spiritual lyre? And just as a trumpet, sounding a terrible and martial note, strikes down the enemies but rouses the drooping spirits of its own, so too... C
does it impart confidence, rendering those who attend to it invincible to the devil. And like a lyre, giving delight again with much spiritual joy, it calms the passions of strange thoughts, and brings us much profit along with the pleasure. A Since you have heard, then, about many and necessary matters being discussed today with Timothy—for even regarding ordinations he sent word to him, saying: *Lay hands suddenly on no man, neither be partaker of other men’s sins* (1 Tim. 5:22), and he displayed the intolerable danger of such a transgression, showing that others will endure the punishment for what was failed by others along with them, because they provide the authority for wickedness through ordinations. Then again he says: *Use a little wine for your stomach and your frequent infirmities* (Ibid. v. 23). And he discussed with us today about the subjection of servants, and about the madness of the greedy, and about the arrogance of the wealthy, and about many other things. Since, therefore, it is impossible to go through everything, what would you have us select from what has been said to speak to your love? For just as in a meadow I see many and various flowers of reading, and much rose-bed, and many violets, and no fewer lilies—but various and abundant fruit of the Spirit sown everywhere, and much fragrance; or rather, the reading of the divine Scriptures is not just a meadow, but a paradise. For these flowers have not only bare fragrance, but also fruit capable of nourishing the soul. What, then, would you have us bring into the midst today? Would you have us handle now that which seems the most trivial of all, and easily understood by anyone who chances upon it? It pleases me, and I know that it will also please you. What, then, is that which is easier than the others? What else, but that which seems simple and easy to speak about to anyone at all? What is this, then? *Use a little wine for your stomach and your frequent infirmities.* Come, then, let us spend the whole discourse on this one saying. We do this not for the sake of ambition, nor striving to provide a display of power in speaking (for the things said are not ours, but whatever the grace of the Spirit might inspire), but that we might rouse the more indolent of the hearers and persuade them how great the treasure of the Scriptures is, and how it is unsafe and fraught with danger to pass it by. For if this bare saying, which is easy to understand and seems to the many to contain nothing necessary, should appear to provide us with motives and grounds for the highest philosophy, much more will those things that demonstrate their own wealth from the very outset fill those who attend to them with countless treasures. Let us not, therefore, pass by even those thoughts of the Scriptures that are thought to be bare; for these also are of the grace that comes from the Spirit. And the grace of the Spirit is never small and trivial, but great and wonderful and worthy of the magnificence of the Provider. Let us not, therefore, listen carelessly, since even those who refine metallic earth, when they cast it into the furnace, gain not only the masses of gold, but also collect the small particles with great care. Since, therefore, we also refine gold taken from the apostolic mines—not casting it into a furnace, but letting it down into the understanding of your soul—not kindling a flame, but lighting the fire of the Spirit, let us collect even the small particles with great care. For even if the saying is brief, the power is great; for pearls do not hold their inherent wealth in the bulk of the body, but in the beauty of the nature of the thing. So it is with the reading of the divine Scriptures. For secular education, unrolling much nonsense and pouring much garrulity upon the hearers, sends the hearers away with empty hands, having harvested nothing good, neither great nor small. But the grace of the Spirit is not so, but on the contrary, it inserts philosophy into all who attend through small words; and it often suffices to have taken one saying from here to have provisions for one’s whole life.
2. Since, therefore, the wealth is so great, let us rouse ourselves and receive what is said with a sober mind; for I am preparing to let the discourse down into a great depth. For to many this exhortation seemed to be superfluous and to be something redundant; and they say such words as: Was Timothy not able to discern for himself, or know what he should use, but did he wait to learn from the teacher? Then the teacher not only commanded it, but also committed it to writing, as if upon a bronze tablet, having engraved it in the letter to him; and was he not ashamed to send word about such things publicly, writing to the disciple? In order, therefore, that you may learn that the exhortation not only is not redundant, but has become necessary and most useful, and that this is not of Paul, but of the grace of the Spirit—I do not mean only that it was spoken, but that it was also put into writing, and handed down to all who are to come through this letter—I will proceed at last to the proof itself. For along with the things mentioned, some also raise this other question, no less important, asking among themselves why God permitted a man having such boldness, whose bones and relics cast out demons, to fall into such great and continual and frequent and constant illnesses; for he did not simply live in illness, but in illnesses, and those not giving him even a little to breathe. Whence is this evident? From Paul’s own words; for he did not say, *Because of the infirmity*, but *Because of the infirmities*, and not even simply infirmities, but signifying their continuity he said: *Your frequent infirmities*. Let them hear, whoever are handed over to long-term disease and are indignant and despair. For this is not the only question, that being a saint he was sick, and so was sick continually, but that he was also entrusted with the common affairs of the inhabited world.
