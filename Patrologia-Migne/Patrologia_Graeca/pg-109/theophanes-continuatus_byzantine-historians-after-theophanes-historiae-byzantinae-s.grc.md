NOTICE
ON THE BYZANTINE HISTORIANS AFTER THEOPHANES
(Fabric. Biblioth. Gr. VI, 348.)
We now proceed to review the volume of the *Scriptores post Theophanem* [Writers after Theophanes], or those who extended the Byzantine history from Leo the Armenian, the successor of Emperor Michael Curopalates—where the *Chronicon* of Theophanes concludes—down to Emperor Nicephorus Phocas. These were reviewed from manuscript codices and donated with a translation by François Combefis, of the Order of Preachers of the Congregation of Saint Louis, though they finally saw the light of day only after his death, Paris, 1685, folio; hence the annotations which that learned man prepared for these writers are also missed. The writers themselves are read in this order:
1. A ANONYMOUS (whom, based on an uncertain conjecture, see Labbe in *Protrepticon*, p. 14 and 46, I see called Leontius the Byzantine): A *Chronicle*, written by the command of Constantine Porphyrogennetus continuing Theophanes, in four books, concerning as many emperors: Book I, on Leo the Armenian, who began to reign in the year of Christ 813; Book II, on Michael Balbus; Book III, on Theophilus, son of Michael; and Book IV, on Michael, son of Theophilus, whom he writes was removed by slaughter on September 24 of the year of the Greeks 6376, that is, of Christ 868. Combefis published the Greek from a Barberini copy of a Vatican manuscript codex, which Holstenius had sent from Rome. Words seem to have been inserted by Constantine Porphyrogennetus, or in his name, in Book II, where there is a discussion about the liberation of Crete from the Saracens, p. 51.
2. CONSTANTINE PORPHYROGENNETUS, Emperor, deceased A.C. 959, from the life of his grandfather, Basil the Macedonian, who succeeded Michael son of Theophilus in A.C. 868, whom he notes to have held the reins of empire for one year with that predecessor of his, Michael, but afterwards to have administered the sum of affairs alone for nineteen other years, namely until the year 888. This *Life* had already seen the light of day, with the translation of Leo Allatius, in his *Συμμικτοῖς* [Symmikta], 1653, 8vo, divided into 70 sections or chapters. But Combefis revised the Greek according to the aforementioned Barberini copy, added a new translation, and divided it into 101 sections, differently than Allatius did; this diverse distinction of chapters is accustomed to fix a cross upon those citing the writer or desiring to search for cited passages. Regarding what is read in the title: *Ιστορική διήγησις τοῦ βίου καὶ τῶν πράξεων Βασιλείου τοῦ ἀοιδίμου βασιλέως, ἣν Κωνσταντίνος βασιλεὺς ἐν Θεῷ Ρωμαίων ὁ τούτου υἱωνός, φιλοπόνως ἀπὸ διαφόρων ἀθροίσας διηγημάτων τῷ γράφοντι προσανέθετο* [Historical account of the life and deeds of the celebrated King Basil, which King Constantine, in God, of the Romans, his grandson, having painstakingly collected from various narratives, assigned to the writer], by *τὸν γράφοντα* [the writer] understand the author of the four preceding books, to whom he subjoined this *Life* with the emperor’s polite permission, and by which he testifies that he was deterred from writing the *Life of Basil* himself, for that anonymous writer of the *Chronicle* thus writes in the preface to the same Constantine, by whose command he professed to have come to continue Theophanes: *Εἰ καὶ τοῦ ἀοιδίμου σου πάππου, τοῦ ἐν βασιλεῦσι εὐκλεοῦς Βασιλείου τὰ παρὰ σοῦ ἱστορούμενα, καὶ μετὰ πάσης σαφηνείας καὶ λόγου εὐκοσμίας κατὰ τὸ τῶν γενομένων ἀληθὲς καὶ εἰλικρινὲς εἰς τὸ φανερὸν παρὰ τῆς σῆς πολυμαθεστάτης εἰδήσεως ἐξαγορευθέντα, περιττὸν ἂν εἴη οὐδοτιοῦν διηγήσασθαι* [If indeed what is being historicized by you concerning your celebrated grandfather, the glorious among kings, Basil, and expressed with all clarity and propriety of speech according to the truth and sincerity of what occurred, having been brought to light by your most learned knowledge, it would be superfluous to relate anything whatsoever]. Labbe, who suspected that this *Life* was not written by Constantine himself but by another at his command, was refuted by Martin Hanckius, *De Hist. Byz. scriptoribus*, p. 473; and he alone (the passage of the anonymous writer not having been seen by Hanckius, for it had not yet appeared) suffices to refute the conjecture of Labbe and of Cave, who follows him (where he discusses Leontius the Byzantine chronographer at the year A.C. 920).
3. ANONYMOUS (who appears to be different, and more concise than the author of the previous four books): On the affairs of Leo the Philosopher, who was the son and successor of Basil, pp. 217-232. On Alexander, brother of Leo, p. 233; he reigned for seven years under Patriarch Nicholas and other tutors, and mother Zoe; for twenty-six years with Romanus Lacapenus, his father-in-law; and finally he ruled alone for fifteen years, and thus lived and held the dignity of emperor for 55 years: pp. 255-292. And on Romanus the Younger, son of Constantine Porphyrogennetus, pp. 293-300.

Regarding what is read in the title: *Ιστορική διήγησις τοῦ βίου καὶ τῶν πράξεων Βασιλείου τοῦ ἀοιδίμου βασιλέως, ἣν Κωνσταντίνος βασιλεὺς ἐν Θεῷ Ρωμαίων ὁ τούτου υἱωνός, φιλοπόνως ἀπὸ διαφόρων ἀθροίσας διηγημάτων τῷ γράφοντι προσανέθετο* [Historical account of the life and deeds of the celebrated King Basil, which King Constantine, in God, of the Romans, his grandson, having painstakingly collected from various narratives, assigned to the writer], by *τὸν γράφοντα* [the writer] understand the author of the four preceding books, to whom he subjoined this *Life* with the emperor’s polite permission, and by which he testifies that he was deterred from writing the *Life of Basil* himself, for that anonymous writer of the *Chronicle* thus writes in the preface to the same Constantine, by whose command he professed to have come to continue Theophanes: *Εἰ καὶ τοῦ ἀοιδίμου σου πάππου, τοῦ ἐν βασιλεῦσι εὐκλεοῦς Βασιλείου τὰ παρὰ σοῦ ἱστορούμενα, καὶ μετὰ πάσης σαφηνείας καὶ λόγου εὐκοσμίας κατὰ τὸ τῶν γενομένων ἀληθὲς καὶ εἰλικρινὲς εἰς τὸ φανερὸν παρὰ τῆς σῆς πολυμαθεστάτης εἰδήσεως ἐξαγορευθέντα, περιττὸν ἂν εἴη οὐδοτιοῦν διηγήσασθαι* [If indeed what is being historicized by you concerning your celebrated grandfather, the glorious among kings, Basil, and expressed with all clarity and propriety of speech according to the truth and sincerity of what occurred, having been brought to light by your most learned knowledge, it would be superfluous to relate anything whatsoever]. Labbe, who suspected that this *Life* was not written by Constantine himself but by another at his command, was refuted by Martin Hanckius, *De Hist. Byz. scriptoribus*, p. 473; and he alone (the passage of the anonymous writer not having been seen by Hanckius, for it had not yet appeared) suffices to refute the conjecture of Labbe and of Cave, who follows him (where he discusses Leontius the Byzantine chronographer at the year A.C. 920).

This codex holds Leontius’s *De sectis*, far more emended than those already edited, and richer in authorities which are desired in those versions. Psellus’s *History* may receive much light from the reading of his letters to emperors, Caesars, and the highest princes of the age and magistrates, to patriarchs, metropolitans, prelates of churches and governors—not to speak of his accusation against Michael Cerularius before the synod, and the very brilliant eulogies of his, composed by Constantine Lichudes, who succeeded him, and also by Xiphilinus, on those men of course who shone brilliantly in both military and civil service of Constantinople, and regarding his own statements—he who was himself a partner in public concerns with them, and almost the greatest of those who participated in civil administration—especially under the Emperor Constantine Monomachus, to whom he directed tracts on policy that are not to be despised.
