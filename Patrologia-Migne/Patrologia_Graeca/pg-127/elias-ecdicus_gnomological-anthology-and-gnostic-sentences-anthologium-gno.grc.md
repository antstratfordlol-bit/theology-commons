ANNO DOMINI MCXXI
ELIAS ECDICUS SEU DEFENSOR ECCLESIÆ CRETENSIS
NOTITIA.
(Ex libro inter rariores rarissimo cui titulus : Φιλοκαλία τῶν ἱερῶν Νηπτικῶν, etc., Venetiis, 1782, fol.)
Elias divus ille Pater, qui etiam dicitur Defensor,  utrum sit Elias Cretensis, theologicorum Gregorii scriptorum commentator, an quispiam alius, nobis incertum est. Quantum quidem ex suis eum capitibus, tanquam ex quibusdam operibus opifecem, cognoscimus, vir erat, si quis alius, perfectus in sapientia, tum profana, tum multo magis in ea quæ nostra est et excellens. Ejus enim eloquii vim et eminentem virtutis laudem vir peritus facile in illis agnoscet. Quæ quidem meditati, uberemque in eis moralis mysticæque scientiæ fructum admirati, ac delectati quasi quibusdam frondibus tegmen fructui aspectumque præbentibus amœnum, id est, figurarum et verborum leporibus quibus auctor illa optime concinnavit : ipsa lectionis amantibus largimur, eosque amice hortamur ut ingrediantur floridum hoc odoriferumque pratum (hoc enim titulo ornantur), densis altisque spiritalium oraculorum arboribus consitum, ac dulcissimis divinæ sapientiæ fluentis irriguum, utque sub umbra illius sedeant : ita ut et ipsi, non solum practicæ fluminibus inebrientur, sed etiam alborum theoriæ fructibus (ut ad cantilenæ modum loquar) ædituum more efferciantur.
Ἠλίας ὁ θεῖος οὗτος Πατὴρ, ὁ καὶ Ἔκδικος ἐπονομαζόμενος, πότερον Ἠλίας ὁ Κρήτης, ὁ καὶ τῶν τοῦ Γρηγορίου θεολογικῶν λόγων σχολιαστής, οὗτός ἐστιν, ἢ ἕτερός τις, ἡμῖν ἄδηλον. Ὅσον γε μὴν ἐκ τῶν παρόντων αὐτοῦ κεφαλαίων, ὡς ἔκ τινων ἀποτελεσμάτων τὸν ποιητὴν, ἐπιγνῶμαι, ἀνὴρ ἦν, εἴ τις καὶ ἄλλος, σοφίας εἰς ἄκρον ἐληλακώς, τῆς τε θύραθεν, καὶ πολλῷ μᾶλλον τῆς καθ᾿ ἡμᾶς τε, καὶ κρείττονος. Τήν τε γὰρ ἐν λόγοις δύναμιν, καὶ τὸ περὶν αὐτῷ τῆς ἀρετῆς κλέος ῥᾳδίως ἂν ἐν τούτοις, κριτικὸς ἀνὴρ καταμάθοι. Ἃ δὴ καὶ ἡμεῖς κατιδόντες, καὶ τὸν ἐν αὐτοῖς πολύχουν καρπὸν τῆς ἠθικῆς, καὶ γνωστικῆς διδασκαλίας θαυμάσαντες, ναὶ μὴν, καὶ ἡσθέντες οἷόν τισι φύλλοις σκέπην τε τῷ καρπῷ, καὶ ὄψιν παρεχομένοις οὐκ ἄωρον, ταῖς τῶν μεταφορῶν φημὶ, καὶ λέξεων χάρισιν, αἷς εἰς τὸ ἄριστον ὁ Πατὴρ αὐτῶν ἐχαρίτωσε, τούτοις τοὺς φιλαναγνώστας φιλοφρονούμεθα, καὶ εἰσελθεῖν αὐτοὺς παρακαλοῦμεν ἐν ἀγάπῃ, τὸν εὐανθῆ τουτονὶ καὶ εὐώδη λειμῶνα, ὡς οὕτως ἐπιγράφεται, πολλαῖς μὲν καὶ ὑψηλαῖς ἀναδενδράσι τῶν πνευματικῶν λογίων πεπυκασμένον, ῥείθροις δὲ θείας σοφίας ποτιμωτάτοις κατάῤῥυτον· καὶ τῇ σκιᾷ τούτου ὑποκαθίσαι. Ὡς ἂν καὶ αὐταὶ, οὐ μόνον τῶν τῆς πρακτικῆς ῥοῶν ἀπολαύσωσιν, ἀλλὰ καὶ τῶν ἀκροδρύων τῆς θεωρίας, φσματικῶς εἰπεῖν, ζακόρως κατατρύφήσωσιν.

Potest unusquisque Christianus semper sollicitus ac suspensus tentationem exspectare, ut cum venerit, neve miretur neve terreatur; sed grato animo tribulationis laborem molestiamque sustineat, et mente psallens cum Propheta dicat: « Proba me, Domine, et tenta me ». Nec enim actio citra contemplationem est laudabilis; nec contemplatio ab actione deserta, probata est et accepta. 4. Actio est, quoad corpus, jejunium et vigiliæ; quoad os, psalmodia, precatio et silentium, sermone melius; manuum actio, quod ex ipsis sine murmuratione acquiritur; pedum, quod ex primo impulsu fit. 5. ANNO DOMINI MCXXI
ELIAS ECDICUS OR DEFENDER OF THE CRETAN CHURCH
NOTICE
(From a book among the most rare, with the title: *Philokalia of the Sacred Niptic Fathers*, etc., Venice, 1782, fol.)
That divine Father, Elias, who is also called the Defender—whether he is Elias the Cretan, the commentator on the theological discourses of Gregory, or someone else—is uncertain to us. Yet, as much as we know of him from his present chapters as if from certain masterworks, he was a man who, if any other, had attained the pinnacle of wisdom, both secular, and much more so in that which is our own and excellent. For a skilled man will easily recognize in them the power of his eloquence and the eminent praise of his virtue. Having meditated upon these, and having admired the abundant fruit of moral and mystical knowledge within them, and having been delighted as if by certain leaves that offer shade and a pleasant aspect to the fruit—that is, the ornaments of figures and words with which the author has beautifully arrayed them—we bestow these upon those who love reading, and we urge them affectionately to enter this flowering and fragrant meadow (for thus are they titled), planted with dense and lofty trees of spiritual oracles, and irrigated by the sweetest streams of divine wisdom, and to sit under its shade; so that they themselves may be inebriated not only by the rivers of practical [living], but may also, to speak in the manner of a canticle, be abundantly satisfied by the fruits of the trees of contemplation.

You will find here a fountain flowing with the dew of moral words, if you seek with sincerity.
A α΄. Every Christian who believes rightly in God may not live without care, but must always look forward and await temptation, so that when it comes, he is not astonished nor troubled, but with a grateful heart endures the labor of tribulation; and he understands what he sings with the Prophet, saying: "Prove me, O Lord, and try me." And he did not say, "Your discipline has overturned me," but, "It corrected me unto the end."
β΄. The beginning of good things is the fear of God. But the end is love for Him.
γ΄. The beginning of every good thing is word made manifest in action, and action that is discerning. For neither is action good without the word, nor is any word that comes forth good without action.
δ΄. Action consists, as regards the body, in fasting and vigil; as regards the mouth, in psalmody and prayer, and silence which is more precious than speech; regarding the hands, in what is acquired by them without complaint; and regarding the feet, in what is accomplished by them from the first impulse.
ε΄. Mercy and truth take precedence over all these. Their offspring is humility, and the discernment which, according to the Fathers, is born from this; without which, neither of the others can contemplate its own end. For action, when it scorns the yoke of the word, is found wandering here and there about useless things like a heifer; and the word, when it casts aside the honorable tunic of practice, is not seemly, even if it feigns to be so in appearance.
ς΄. The courageous soul, like a woman throughout her life, keeping two lamps burning—the practical and the contemplative—does what is required. Conversely, the soul that is lax and remiss in pleasures does what it ought not.
