Of brilliant deeds and things bravely done by noble emperors—which is to say, by heroic men, excelling in nobility of birth, valiant and magnanimous—the paths are impossible for the tongue even to navigate, as if by ship. For according to these, the most valiant Comnenid emperors present themselves to be looked upon, who crossed, as it were, entire seas of victories and trophies, which that keen Hercules could scarcely have measured. Wherefore, let us by all means tie up the cable here, with oars restrained and sails furled. For things lying beyond Gades, as the proverb is wont to say, are not to be approached by sailing.

ANNO DOMINI 1084.
NICOLAUS, PATRIARCH OF CONSTANTINOPLE, SURNAMED GRAMMATICUS.
NOTICE.
(CAVE, *Script. eccles.* for the year 1084, p. 538.)
Nicolaus, called Grammaticus, was elevated from monk to Patriarch of Constantinople in the year 1084, or, as Baronius has it, in the year 1089. A man not unacquainted with letters, says Zonaras, though not altogether learned.
In another Vatican codex, number 1989, from approximately the eleventh century, formerly of Grottaferrata, I found a previously unpublished Greek panegyric on Saint Bartholomew, delivered by an anonymous monk, a contemporary and intimate of the holy man, as is apparent from the seventh and subsequent chapters, and indeed, [it was delivered] in the presence of the abbot who was Bartholomew's primary disciple. Wherefore, I believe that Sciommarus, in the aforementioned work, is speaking of an entirely different panegyric, the author of which he claims to be Pancratius, abbot of Grottaferrata, who lived around the middle of the thirteenth century, that is, almost two hundred years after Bartholomew; and indeed, [he claims] that the entire life of the holy man was recounted in it in order; a fact which is not recognized in our own [manuscript]. Finally, in the same codex 1989, folio 194b, I also read the history of Saint Phantinus with his twenty miracles; this holy man is often mentioned as a contemporary in the Life of Nilus the Younger. The author is Peter, bishop of the Western [region]. However, when I discovered that this Life had been published in Latin among the Sicilian Saints by Octavius Cajetanus, Vol. I, p. 552, I voluntarily set aside the study I had prepared. Indeed, Cajetanus most correctly observes in his annotations (p. 137) that there were two Phantinuses: one a Syracusan, far more ancient; the other a younger Calabrian (who was a contemporary of Saints Nilus and Bartholomew). It is clearly the history of the latter, not the former ancient one, that Peter, the Western bishop—that is, of the city of Tauriana in Calabria—wrote, as the same Cajetanus probably affirms. The title and beginning of the work is: "Peter, bishop of the Western [region], on the life and miracles of the holy and glorious servant of God, Phantinus. It is good to conceal the mystery of a king, but glorious to proclaim the works of God. This celebrated servant of God, Phantinus, was a servant to a man living in the world, Balsamius by name; but because of his natural sagacity, or rather, because of the shining of divine light bestowed upon him by the foreknowing power of God, he had arrived at piety and extreme love of God..." But let us proceed to the biography of our own Bartholomew, who is distinct from the other Bartholomew, the founder of the monastery of the Holy Savior at Messina, whose history the same Cajetanus recounts (op. cit. Vol. II, p. 136).
LIFE AND CONVERSATION OF OUR HOLY FATHER BARTHOLOMEW THE YOUNGER OF GROTTAFERRATA
1. Nothing is so well suited to provoke imitation as the memory of the Fathers, when it is recounted purely, studied with diligence, and handed down to the ears of worthy sons. For by inserting a kind of goad of zeal into the souls of those who hear, it inflames them with love, stirs them to imitation, and invites them to an equal contest; especially if it concerns those who have shone like beacons in our own generation, and who, by the splendor of their virtuous deeds, call us to tread the same path. For this reason, to transmit these things in writing and to honor them with these yearly processions and panegyrics is not, by heaven, an indifferent work or effort, but one that is most just.
