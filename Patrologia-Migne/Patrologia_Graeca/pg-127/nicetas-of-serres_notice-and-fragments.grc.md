admonitum, eum, qui non Olympiodorum Alexandrinæ Ecclesiæ diaconum, sed Nicetam Heracleæ episcopum catenæ hujus auctorem existimabat, sensisse se captiosa probabilitate deceptum, ideoque semel et iterum datis litteris errati sui veniam deprecatum, et in sententiam suam manibus pedibusqu[e] venisse, erratum professum. Licet enim, ut ille asserebat, a Joanne Parisiensis Ecclesiæ canonico didicisset, tum in suo, tum in regis Gallorum (et Cæsaris, et in ottob.) exemplaribus et non paucis quæ Constantinopoli visuntur, summo cum consensu, Nicetæ nomen præfixum habeatur, et stylus una cum reliquis Nicetæ operibus plane consentiat, suo tamen judicio Comitolus, et aliorum eximiorum virorum, diversorum exemplarium diversos esse auctores censet, et Nicetæ nominis hujusmodi libris affixi repellit antiquitatem : tum demum quia Græcæ nationi, quo præsertim tempore ab Romana Ecclesia secessit, solemue fuit, adulterinis notis aspergere : et varios esse auctores suadent, vel omnibus in libris non cumdem esse explanatorum numerum, vel explanationum auctorum non esse eamdem dispositionem et ordinem, vel ad eosdem locos sacræ Scripturæ non eorumdem doctorum explicationes adhiberi, vel eorumdem doctorum explicationes in nonnullis libris concisas, in aliis magis diffusas esse, vel nullam præfixi nominis Nicetæ notam vetustioribus codicibus videri. Adde in ipsa inscriptione, quæ Nicetam præfert, nihil recinere purum, nihil veteris linguæ Græcæ resonare domesticum. Ἐξήγησις εἰς τὸν μακάριον Ἰὼβ, συγγραφεῖσα παρὰ τοῦ ἱερωτάτου μητροπολίτου Ἡρακλείας, κυρίου Νικήτα. Νικήτας enim, si regulis grammaticorum insistere voluisset, dicendum erat. Græcos porro non suorum sed alienorum nomina assuesse præfligere, probant Commentarii in Joannem, qui Cyrilli nomen præferebant (c), cum essent Chrysostomi; Commentarii Origenis in Job, qui prorsus Origenis non sunt (d). Nee potest dissimulari, multa Hieronymo non Hieronymi, Augustino multa ascripta esse non Augustini (e). De stylo quoque Nicetæ præter rem esse videtur, cum nullum in hoc toto Commentario Nicetæ verbum reperiatu[r], sed omnia aliorum verbis exposita, et licet omnes aliæ catenæ, quæ cum Nicetæ nomine conjunguntur, Nicetæ sint, quam est interpretatus Comitolus, nullo modo Nicetæ tribuendam esse. Et hæc quidem sunt Comitoli argumenta adversus eos, qui hujus catenæ in Jobum auctorem Nicetam existimant.
Olympiodorum vero diaconum hujus opificii esse auctorem necessario probat, quod catenæ auctor in extrema præfatione, cum Chrysostomi oratione suam connectens, Nunc ad ipsam verborum, inquit, interpretationem veniendum est; quæcunque vero historiæ congruunt, atque allegoria, etc. Atqui verba hæc prorsus consequitur nomen Olympiodori, ejusque protheoria. Si superior oratio est catenæ auctoris, tum historiam, tum theoriam, cum res feret, in singula capita pollicentis, cum in utriusque sensus et historici et theorici pervestigatione fuerit Olympiodorus perdiligenter versatus, cum illius duntaxat sint omnes illæ protheoriæ, quis vocabit in dubium, quin idem sit interpres? Olympiodori interpretatio cum reliquorum doctorum explanationibus ita cohæret, ut non multorum expositio, sed unius tantummodo videatur esse continuatio. Sententiæ illius ex aliorum sententiis efforescunt, et pleræque illius commentationes in aliena junctione verborum sæpe considunt, aliorum verba et sententiæ Olympiodori comprehensionibus astringuntur : aliorum interpretum infracta ab Olympiodoro sanantur, et vitiosa quæque complentur : quæ variis ex eadem catena adductis exemplis Comitolus confirmat. Tandemque cardinalis Gulielmi Sirleti, Francisci Turriani, et Petri Galesini protonotarii apostolici sententias, suæ comites allegat, et Vincentii Pinelli codicem pervetustum, in cujus primis paginis est Olympiodori, tanquam legitimi auctoris, litteris Latinis nomen ascriptum. Tandemque Parisiensium Patrum et sodalium suorum patrocinium adnectit, qui cum sua causa regis reginæque bibliothecas acri studio perlustrassent, singulosque libros ac librorum indices diligentius inspexissent, responderunt : Non autem quòd rev. patrem Comitolum pigeat conjecturæ, catenam istam Olympiodoro tribuentis. Nam et in Epitome bibliothecæ Gesneri, et in Catalogo librorum edito a Sinlero anno 1586, catena in Job Olympiodoro tribuitur quædam, quæ dicitur in bibliotheca Sambuci servari : itemque ejusdem Olympiodori diaconi hypotheses in Job. His concludit esse vero quam simillimum hujus catenæ scriptorem Olympiodorum, Alexandrinæ Ecclesiæ diaconum, exstitisse. Pro qua sententia defendenda, ingentes Comitoli conatus, et concitatam animi ardorem conspexeris. Ad Comitoli quoque sententiam accedit. Possevinus in apparato, in Olympiodoro : Accedo enim lubens ad sententiam Pauli Comitoli, qui eamdem catenam, ut alibi diximus, e Græca Latinam fecit eleganter. Qui item solidis rationibus in editione Veneta, quæ prodiit apud Iolitos anno 1587, evincit eam esse non Nicetæ Heracleensis episcopi, sed ipsius Olympiodori.

Quid interim nos? In tanta conjecturarum copia, et eximiorum hominum consensu acquiescentus? Etiam si producta sententia quam longissime abesset a vero, tanta dicendi copia praestructa, nos quoque etiam invitos ad se pellexisset. Sed veritatis studiam, quo semper incontinenter exarsimus compellit, ut dictas conjecturas conjecturis aliis (quae enim in rebus hujusmodi demonstratio fuerit?) ponderatas, sapientiorum examini committamus. Qui semel oppositum sentiebat, rationibus Comitoli persuasus, in sententiam Comitoli venit. Optime factum, laudo ingenium, id quoque suasus ipse faciam; et si peccatum est, talibus in rebus diversa ab aliis sentire, veniam deprecabor; et rem agam. Joannis Parisiensis canonici codex Nicetam praeferebat auctorem hujus catenae, et regis Galliae exemplar, et non pauca alia, quae Constantinopoli visuntur, quamvis Comitolus nullam exstare in bibliotheca regia catenam affirmat; sed Constantinopoli exstitisse tradit grammaticus in Catalogo exhibito in bibliotheca patriarchae Constantinopolitani, ut ex Antonii Verderli supplemento colligo; in Anglia duo Bodleiani codices, ex quibus suam eruit, ediditque Patricius Junius. Olympiodorum unus solummodo qui fuit Sambuci. Quibus fides adhibenda est? unine tantummodo Sambuci, relato ab epitomatoribus Gesneri, an tot aliis, qui nobis Nicetam praefigunt? Et si attentius verba epitomatorum considerentur, non nobis Olympiodorum auctorem catenae dant, sed unum ex iis, ex quibus interpretationes illae collectae sunt. En verba: Olympiodori et aliorum piissima catena in Job, in bibliotheca Sambuci. Ni alios quoque auctores catenae auctores substituere velimus. Et cum Comitolus dicat, diversorum exemplarium auctores esse diversos, undenam ille habuit in Sambuciano illo codice catenam a se versam, et quam Olympiodore ipse vindicat, et non aliam atque diversam, contineri? Quare in hujusmodi conjectura, rei dubiae majorem fidem faciunt plures codices, pluresque testes firmioris auctoritatis reputantur. Quid si Olympiodorum nullos alius testis, nullus hujus commentationis foetus alius, Nicetam autem ha et in alios sacrae Scripturae libros, perplures collectiones acusant? At Nicetae stylus cum aliis operibus Nicetae non consentit. Cum nullus hic cernatur, verum est; sunt enim aliorum scriptorum sententiae eorundem verbis expositae, vel quandoque etiam decurtatae, in quo nulla certa styli ratio formari potest. Quamvis in aliis suis scriptis, in quibus λογοειδὲς dicendi genus sectatur, non inelegans sit, sed gravis, facundus, et plenus sententiis. At hic in inscriptione nihil purum retinet, nihil Graecae linguae domesticum resonat. Quis id ait? Res ipsa, si Comitolo credimus viro in Graecis litteris veratissimo. Νικήτα enim illud pro Νικητοῦ non videtur e grammaticorum schola. Et tamen purum hellenismum sonat. Nomen enim illud fere in omnibus etiam antiquioribus ita enuntiatur. Hinc legitur: Συναγωγὴ ἐξηγήσεων εἰς τὸ κατὰ Λουκᾶν παρὰ Νικήτα διακόνου. Νικήτα Σερρῶν εἰς Γρηγόριον τὸν Θεολόγον. Νικήτα μονάζοντος τοῦ Στηθάτου εἰς τὸ βιβλίον τῶν θείων ὕμνων, Νικήτα τοῦ φιλοσόφου. Et in editis conciliis Roman's, de Vita sancti Ignatii: Συναγράφη δὲ παρὰ Νικήτα δούλου Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ. Sic etiam vulgatum est, Νικήτα τοῦ Χωνιάτου. Et in expositione Graeca carminum Gregorii Nazianzeni, Νικήτα φιλοσόφου, τοῦ καὶ Δαυὶδ ἑρμηνεία; Possem etiam infinita ex historicis referre, qui Nicetae nomen in secundo casu ita efferunt, neque propterea praeter puritatem Graeci sermonis locutos esse fuisse aliquis criminatus est. An ergo tot auctores ob unius syllabae male scriptae in ipso titulo, suis Commentariis spoliandi sunt? De Niceta Heracleensi, et de hac catena tautummodo id decernere, de aliis non item, λῆρος plane esset, καὶ οὐδὲν ὑγιές. Quod si Nicetas ideo ex hac catena exterminandus est, quod in escribendo in declinatione peccatum est, fortasse etiam Olympiodorus ejiciendus ex hoc eodem opere, cuius nomen in emendatissimo exemplari, quod asserit Patricius Junius, constanter et per omnia ita scriptum reperitur, lege compositionis, ut videtur, aliter postulante, per ω in antepenukima exprimitur Ὀλυμπιωδώρου. Sed nimius ipse sum in similibus referendis.
Reliqua Comitoli videantur. Solemne fuit Graecae nationi adulterinis notis libros aspergere. Sit certum atque indubitatum, neque enim nunc ego Graecae nationis causam ago. Quid ergo inde sequitur? Ergo et hoc quoque nomen, eraso vero auctore, catenae praefixerunt. Hoc vero probandum est, ut in exemplis allatis de Cyrillo et Origene probatur. Neque propterea licebit Comitolo, pulso Niceta, alium substituere. Tum enim et in libris quoque Latinis id facere pro libidine uniuscujusque concederetur; cum, ut ille ait, dissimulari non possit, multa Hieronymo non Hieronymi, Augustino multa ascripta esse non Augustini. Hinc concludet nescio quis plane non dissimili argumento: Ergo catena quae prostat sub nomine divi Thomae Aquinatis, divi Thomae non est, cum Latini sueverint nonnullis libris ac voluminibus non suorum auctorum, sed alienorum nomina, titulosque affligere. Imo permulta in libris suae Societatis, si hunc eumdem ratiocinandi modum sequemur, decerni possunt. Audiatur Comitolus: Haereamus in nostris. NICETAS SERRONIUS: NOTICE
(Leo Allatius, *De Nicetis*, ap. Mai, *Bibl. nov. Patr.*, tom. VI.)
Nicetas Seron is mistakenly divided into two by Possevinus in his *Apparatus*, namely into Nicetas, bishop of Heraclea, and Nicetas Seron. But he is most poorly treated by the Gesnerian Epitomators as Nicetas Heradius, Nicetas Serronius, Nicetas of Serres [τοῦ Σερρῶν], and Nicetas, metropolitan of Heraclea. For he is one and the same author who composed all that is ascribed to the aforementioned. In my diatribe on Psellus, when I was debating against heretics regarding the age of Michael Psellus, in order to refute them, I brought forward many things from manuscripts about Nicetas David and Nicetas Seron, which even they had to concede, and subsequently I concluded that Nicetas Seron and Nicetas David were distinct individuals; and I held the opinion that he flourished in the age of Theophylact, archbishop of Bulgaria, to whom the ninth letter of Theophylact among those published by Meursius is addressed. Regarding the diversity of the Nicetas, I have with me the most learned Vossius in his new edition on Greek writers: yet it is the opinion of Leo Allatius, in his diatribe on Psellus, that these Nicetas are distinct persons, and I subscribe rather to him in this part. He was first a deacon and master or doctor of the Great Church, then metropolitan of Serres, then translated to Heraclea. He was a man most learned and pious. He wrote many and various things. But his primary and not inglorious efforts were in explaining the Bible according to the mind of the holy Fathers.
What has reached our hands are: his grammatical canons chanted according to musical mode. Grammatical rules which are harmonized to musical chant, as well as troparia. The title prefixed to the work: "This is the labor of Nicetas of Serres." P. "The epithets of Zeus: Dodonaeus, Idaeus, Hercius, Cenaeus, Meilichius." By the same, "Canon on grammatical rules." P. "On the oxytone endings in *os*." By the same, "Another canon on various grammatical matters." P. "The *alpha* at the beginning *aspazetai*." And other things in the libraries: Vatican, Vallicellian, Royal at Paris, Altempsian. Iambic poems on the same work. P. "A beloved locust, a tender floor." Ibid. Other iambic poems. P. "It is the time for sleep, and it was proper to sleep." Royal [Library] at Paris.
Others add: "On the heretical and Christian emperors of Constantinople by the same." I have not yet seen what this is.
On the sixteen orations of Gregory Nazianzen are Enarrations and Commentaries, namely: on the holy Pascha, and on Tardiness [the second oration]. On another oration on the holy Pascha. On New Sunday. On Pentecost. On the Nativity of Christ. On the Epitaph of the divine Basil. On the Holy Lights. On the holy Baptism. On the love of the poor. On the father who was silent because of the hail storm. On the Maccabees. On St. Cyprian the martyr. On Julian the tax collector. On the presence of many bishops. On Gregory Nyssen, brother of the Great Basil. On St. Athanasius: which were printed in Latin by the interpreter Joannes Leunclavius together with the works of Gregory Nazianzen at Basel in 1571. Commentaries by the same on Nyssen, an Ambrosian manuscript index. It is without a doubt Nicetas who also labored on Nazianzen. I wish, however, that the index or title does not mislead! And I suspect these are the things which Nicetas himself had written on the oration of Nazianzen, which he delivered on Gregory Nyssen, the brother of Basil.
He also applied his industry to explaining the books of sacred Scripture; not from his own [thoughts], but culling from the holy and other most excellent men, and indeed even from heretics, provided they are not separated from the understanding and sense of the catholics, setting them in order and arranging them most fittingly, so that they do not appear disconnected and separate, patched together from scraps of superior cloth, but woven in one continuous text by one author. The Greeks called these *seirae*, the Latins *catenae*: for just as diverse and disconnected
rings, when linked to one another, are drawn together into one and form a continuous whole, so too those opinions scattered throughout the writings of the Fathers, having been collected and gathered in due order, are continued as if in a single series. Comitolus contends that the *Catena on Job* is not by Nicetas, but by Olympiodorus, and Possevinus reports that he proves it with solid arguments. Regarding the style of Nicetas, it seems also to be beside the point, since not one word of Nicetas is found in this whole Commentary, but everything is set forth in the words of others, and although all other catenae which are joined with the name of Nicetas may be Nicetas’s, the one which Comitolus interpreted is in no way to be attributed to Nicetas. And these indeed are the arguments of Comitolus against those who believe that Nicetas is the author of this catena on Job.
That Olympiodorus the deacon is the author of this work is proven necessarily by the fact that the author of the catena, in the final preface, when connecting his own work with the oration of Chrysostom, says: "Now one must come to the very interpretation of the words; but whatever things are congruent to history, and allegory, etc." And indeed, after these words, the name of Olympiodorus follows, as does his protheoria. If the prior oration belongs to the author of the catena, promising both history and theory—as the subject matter provides—in individual chapters, and since Olympiodorus has been very diligently engaged in the investigation of both historical and theoretical senses, and since all those protheoriae are his, who would call into doubt that he is the same person who is the interpreter? The interpretation of Olympiodorus coheres with the explanations of the remaining doctors in such a way that it does not appear to be the exposition of many, but the continuation of one only. His thoughts flourish out of the thoughts of others, and most of his commentaries often consist in the joining of others' words, and the words and thoughts of others are tightened by the comprehensions of Olympiodorus: the broken parts of other interpreters are healed by Olympiodorus, and all faulty parts are completed: which Comitolus confirms with various examples adduced from the same catena. And finally, he cites the opinions of Cardinal Gulielmus Sirletus, Franciscus Turrianus, and Petrus Galesinus, the apostolic protonotary, as his allies, and the very ancient codex of Vincentius Pinellus, on the first pages of which the name of Olympiodorus, as the legitimate author, is inscribed in Latin letters. And finally, he appends the patronage of the Parisian Fathers and his associates, who, having thoroughly examined the libraries of the king and queen for his cause with sharp study, and having inspected individual books and their indices most diligently, answered: "There is no reason, however, why the reverend father Comitolus should repent of his conjecture in attributing this catena to Olympiodorus. For in the *Epitome* of the library of Gesner, and in the *Catalogus librorum* edited by Simler in the year 1586, a certain catena on Job is attributed to Olympiodorus, which is said to be kept in the Sambucus library: and likewise the hypotheses on Job of the same Olympiodorus the deacon." From these he concludes that it is truly most likely that the writer of this catena was Olympiodorus, deacon of the Alexandrian Church. You might see the immense efforts and the stirred-up ardor of mind of Comitolus for the defense of this opinion. Possevinus also agrees with the opinion of Comitolus in his *Apparatus*, under "Olympiodorus": "For I willingly accede to the opinion of Paulus Comitolus, who, as we have said elsewhere, elegantly translated that same catena from Greek into Latin." He who also, by solid reasoning in the Venetian edition, which was produced by the Ioliti in the year 1587, proves that it is not by Nicetas, bishop of Heraclea, but by Olympiodorus himself.
What shall we do in the meantime? Should we acquiesce in such a store of conjectures and the consensus of distinguished men? Even if the argument brought forth were as far as possible from the truth, such a wealth of discourse having been constructed, it would have drawn us to itself even against our will. But the love of truth, with which we have always burned incessantly, compels us to submit these stated conjectures—after weighing them against other conjectures (for what demonstration has there ever been in matters of this kind?)—to the examination of wiser men. Whoever once felt the contrary, once persuaded by the arguments of Comitolus, came over to the opinion of Comitolus. Well done; I praise his ingenuity, and being persuaded of it, I shall do the same; and if it be a fault in such matters to think differently from others, I shall beg forgiveness; and I shall proceed with the matter. The codex of Jean of Paris, a canon, preferred Nicetas as the author of this catena, as did the exemplar of the king of France, and not a few others which are seen at Constantinople, although Comitolus asserts that no such catena exists in the royal library; but a grammarian reports that it existed in Constantinople in a catalogue exhibited in the library of the patriarch of Constantinople, as I gather from the supplement of Antoine Verderius; in England there are two Bodleian codices, from which Patricius Junius extracted and published his own. One only, which belonged to Sambucus, was of Olympiodorus. To which should faith be given? To the one of Sambucus alone, as reported by the epitomizers of Gesner, or to the many others who place Nicetas before us? And if the words of the epitomizers are considered more attentively, they do not give us Olympiodorus as the author of the catena, but one from among those from whom those interpretations were collected. Here are the words: "A most pious catena on Job by Olympiodorus and others, in the library of Sambucus." Unless we wish to substitute other authors as authors of the catena. And since Comitolus says that the authors of different "exemplars" are different, from where did he get the catena that he translated from that Sambucian codex, which he claims for Olympiodorus himself, and that it contains no other and different one? Therefore, in a conjecture of this kind, many codices provide greater assurance for a doubtful matter, and many witnesses are reckoned as having firmer authority. What if no other witness, no other product of this commentary, points to Olympiodorus, whereas these and very many collections on other books of holy Scripture accuse Nicetas? But the style of Nicetas does not agree with the other works of Nicetas. If none is discerned here, it is true; for the opinions of other writers are set forth in their own words, or sometimes even shortened, in which case no certain account of style can be formed. Although in his other writings, in which he follows a highly eloquent style, he is not inelegant, but grave, eloquent, and full of sententious thoughts. But here, in the inscription, he retains nothing pure, nothing that sounds indigenous to the Greek language. Who says this? The thing itself, if we believe Comitolus, a man most versed in Greek letters. For that *Nikēta* instead of *Nikētou* does not seem to come from the school of the grammarians. And yet it sounds like pure Hellenism. For that name is pronounced in this way in almost all, even more ancient, texts. Hence we read: *Collection of interpretations according to Luke by Nicetas the deacon.* *Nicetas of Serres on Gregory the Theologian.* *Nicetas the monk of Stethatos on the book of divine hymns.* *Nicetas of Byzantium.* *Nicetas the philosopher.* And in the published Roman councils, concerning the Life of Saint Ignatius: *Written by Nicetas, a servant of Jesus Christ.* So also it is common usage: *Nicetas of Chonae.* And in the Greek exposition of the poems of Gregory Nazianzen: *Interpretation of Nicetas the philosopher, also called David.* I could also cite infinite others from historians who express the name "Nicetas" in the second case in this way, and no one has accused them for that reason of having spoken outside the purity of the Greek language. Are so many authors, therefore, to be stripped of their Commentaries because one syllable in the title itself is written incorrectly? To decide that this is solely the case regarding Nicetas of Heraclea and this catena, and not likewise of others, would be clearly absurd and devoid of any truth. But if Nicetas must be cast out from this catena for the reason that a mistake was made in the declension while writing it, perhaps Olympiodorus should also be ejected from this same work, whose name in the most emended exemplar, which Patricius Junius asserts, is found written constantly and through everything in this way, as the law of composition seems to demand, expressed with an omega in the antepenult: *Olympiodōrou*. But I myself am excessive in citing similar things.
Let the rest of Comitolus be seen. It was habitual for the Greek nation to sprinkle books with forged notes. Let this be certain and undoubted, for I am not now arguing the cause of the Greek nation. What, therefore, follows from that? Therefore, they prefixed this name also to the catena, having erased the true author. But this must be proven, as it is proven in the examples brought forward concerning Cyril and Origen. Nor for that reason will it be permitted to Comitolus, having driven out Nicetas, to substitute another. For then it would be allowed in Latin books also to do so at the whim of anyone; since, as he says, it cannot be hidden that many things are ascribed to Jerome that are not Jerome's, and many to Augustine that are not Augustine's. From this, someone or other will conclude with a clearly not dissimilar argument: Therefore, the catena which is on sale under the name of Saint Thomas Aquinas is not Saint Thomas's, since the Latins have been accustomed to affix the names and titles of others, not their own authors, to some books and volumes. Indeed, very many things in the books of his own Society, if we follow this same mode of reasoning, could be condemned. Let Comitolus be heard: "We stick to our own." In this very catena, which was printed last year at Lyons, does not an Epistle to the most ample Cardinal Antonius Carafa exist? When the pressure of time and the prayers of those who were urging the edition were wringing it more sharply from the moderator of our whole order, and he saw that their desire could not be satisfied if the Epistle were to be awaited from me at Rome, the task of writing it was given to a learned companion: nor does the Epistle lie in that matter, because it did not find Paul as its author; for it came forth itself as a rich witness of my own will, and I acknowledged it, as it were, as my own offspring. As if the most upright and most holy Fathers, and especially the moderator of so great an order, would distribute writings composed by this or that companion at his own command, by his own nod, to others of his own, and substitute the labors of others to whomever it pleases him. But let us examine the force of the argument. Greeks likewise, and Latins, bestow many things upon an author not their own through false subscriptions, and sometimes
they were accustomed to be at the divine offices; they were silent in the churches, although they were worn down by the individual daily thoughts of the learned, and by their reading conveyed all the best things into the minds of the readers. This is what John Zonaras had also indicated before me in his commentary on Canon 4 of the I Constantinople Council: “The Theologian likewise makes mention of him (Maximus the Cynic) in one of those discourses of his which are not read in the churches.” Indeed, it was done unworthily that discourses so worthy of the light should be branded with such a term. John, Bishop of Euchaita, was also indignant at this, and inveighed sharply in iambic verse against those who prefaced the discourses with such a name. And so that I myself may show that I bear such things with difficulty, I shall bring that poem against them, hidden and obscure as it is, into the public light.
Who was the first so bold as to affirm that these discourses should by no means be read?
Who hides the light shining over the whole world in a corner?
Who covers the shining stars with a cloud?
Who buries clear pearls beneath the earth?
Alas for the envious and false naming; if it were put into practice, it would bring detriment to the whole world.
But in truth it is in no way put into practice, but the rumor is merely empty.

EDITOR’S NOTICE ON THE PATROLOGIA
Of the catenae which Nicetas collected, these are the ones that have seen the light in print:
1. *Catena in Job*, published in London in the year 1658 by the care and study of Patrick Young, royal librarian.
2. *Catena duplex in Evangelium Matthæi*, which P. Petrus Possinus, S.J., edited and published in two folio volumes in Toulouse in 1647.
3. *Catena in Evangelium Lucæ*, mentioned above by Allatius, which Cardinal Angelo Mai had copied from a Vatican codex and inserted into volume IX of his *Scriptorum veterum*.
4. *Catena in Epistolam I ad Corinthios*, published under the editorship of Joannes Lamio in a book entitled *Deliciae Eruditorum*, Florence 1738.
It does not escape the studious reader that the fragments taken from the catenae listed here have been restored to their own respective authors in the course of the *Patrologia*, or omitted if, after accurate collation, they occurred among other published writings. For he would err not a little who believed all these interpretations were taken from a special commentary; for it is the custom of those who compile catenae to adapt passages to their own purpose, culled from writings of various arguments, and to link them into the rings of a catena. Since, therefore, the materials which Nicetas had derived have returned to their proper place, who would blame us for not wishing—without any benefit—to burden our collection, already so weighty from such dense works, once again?
