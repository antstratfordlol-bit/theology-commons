Ibid. D. "God, and He was always, and is, and will be," etc. These words are from Plato's *Timaeus*. "Was" and "will be" are species of time, which we do not rightly attribute to the eternal essence. For to Him, "Is" alone truly belongs. Therefore, having borrowed these from Him, the Theologian says: "Was" and "will be" are segments of this flux and caducous nature of ours. For since human affairs are in motion and flux, it is clear that their time is of the same kind. One part of it has passed away, another is in the future. For the present of it can in no way be apprehended; for before it is grasped by knowledge, it flies away and slips by. For the "now" is momentary, and without division. The account of divine things is different; for since they are devoid of all motion, surely it is necessary that their nature also be firm and constant and of one mode. For *aion* takes the etymology of its name *apo tou aiei*, that is, from that which always is, just as *chronos*, that is time, [is so named] *apo tou rheein*, that is, from that which flows. Wherefore, to those things which are in time and have had a beginning, "was" and "will be" are appropriate. For example: my father was yesterday, I am today, and my son will be tomorrow. But God always is, and, as I might say with the divinely-speaking Dionysius, He embraces the entire essence within Himself. For Scripture says: "I am the first, and I am hereafter, and before me there is no other God, nor will there be after me" (Isa. XLIV, 6). For the whole essence of Him, having neither beginning nor ever to have an end, is like a vast sea of essence, immense indeed and infinite, as lacking beginning and end; yet it is interminable, as not being circumscribed by place nor by any definition declaring His nature. For God exceeds all thought: of time, indeed, as being infinite; of nature, however, as being included by no limits. Yet the mind, not without reason, as the image of God, receives a certain thin and very obscure contemplation. B God is shadowed forth by the mind, not from those things which are in Him, but from those things which are around Him—that is, not from His proper nature and essence (for that cannot in any way be comprehended or perceived by the intellect), but rather from His effects—that is, from creatures, and from the government of things and His providence. For, carried aloft and rapt on high through the actions and works of God, and conceiving the Creator in our mind through creatures, we arrive at the knowledge of His goodness and wisdom (for this can be known of God, which God has revealed to all mortals), and we gather another species and imagination from another thing into one image and likeness of truth. Since the presence of spiritual light and divine splendor, as the great Dionysius speaks of divine matters, converts the mind of those who have been deemed worthy of such glory and contemplation from many and various opinions and imaginations to that which truly is—that is, to God—and gathers the multiplied visions, or, to use a more apt word, the imaginations, into one true and pure and uniform knowledge. Moreover, this meager image of truth, not only when it has been understood, cannot be held, but not even fully and perfectly comprehended; for as soon as it begins to be known, it immediately snatches itself away, no differently than a lightning bolt, which, before it has been fully beheld, flies away and escapes the eyes. But for what reason is God thus understood, but yet cannot be fully perceived and comprehended? In my judgment, it is so that through that which is seen to some extent, and obscurely known, He may draw us to Himself; but through that which can be seen to some extent, yet not perfectly comprehended, He may draw us into admiration of Himself, and by exciting admiration for Himself (from this, of course, that He escapes the grasp of our intellect), He may be more ardently loved and desired; and, being desired, may cleanse those who desire Him, and make them god-like; and when one has arrived at that point, God, true and natural, now talks familiarly with those who are called gods by grace, bestowing upon us, as each has been cleansed, the divine splendors of His knowledge, and showing Himself as much knowing and to be imagined as He himself also knows those who are known through the purity of their soul.

Now, the world, according to Aristotle, is a structure composed of heaven and earth, and those natures that are contained within their embrace. And it is called a structure and cementing because it consists of these and is completed; yet it is a *sygkrima* [composition] and as it were a *symmigma*, that is, a commixture, because of the elements, which not only have their own places but are also mixed among themselves.
