« Βασιλικῶν ἐκλογήν, sive synopsin (x) Joan. Sambucus vir Pannonius Tarenti apud Calabros reperit, et cum versione Latina Leunclavius ipsam edidit Basileæ 1575. A ...But because these have no connection with sacred history nor do they pertain to religion, they had to be omitted here. The treatises *On the Themes* and *On the Provinces of the Byzantine Empire*, as well as *On the Administration of the Empire*, contribute not a little to the definition of sacred geography. The writings on medicine, on veterinary science, and on agriculture, which Constantine either composed himself or ordered learned men to compose, are not of this place. However, they will become known to our readers from the *Notitia* of the authors of this writer, which J. A. JOANNES ALBERTUS FABRICIUS ON THE WRITINGS OF CONSTANTINE PORPHYROGENITUS
(Bibliotheca Graeca ed. Harles, Vol. VIII, p. 1.)
I have spoken of the *Life of Basil the Macedonian*, which his grandson, the Emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus, son of Leo the Wise—that most wealthy cultivator and restorer of all learning—wrote, in this volume, chapter p. 349. [Vol. VI of the old edition; Vol. VII, p. 681 sq. of this edition]. Now it is helpful to review his other writings; for whatever must be said about the emperor himself—who reigned from 6 June 912 under regents, or with his mother Zoe, or with Romanus his father-in-law, and from 946 to 959 alone—the reader will more rightly seek from the chroniclers of the tenth century, whether those be the ancients, such as Zonaras, Cedrenus, Glycas, and others, as well as the continuators of Theophanes, Simeon the Master, George the Monk, Leo the Grammarian, Joannes Scylitzes, etc., or the more modern ones, Baronius, Spondanus, Antonius Pagi, etc., and those who have published extensive commentaries on the emperors, Joannes Cuspinianus, etc., and he who composed the *Life* of this Constantine individually, Martinus Hanckius, *De scriptoribus hist. Byz.*, lib. 1, cap. 25, p. 461-478. FABR.

The book *On the Administration of the Empire* was written (at least in part) in the year 6457 (Christ 949), the seventh indiction, and in the year 6460 (Christ 952), the tenth indiction. Joannes Meursius was the first to edit this from a Palatine codex, which formerly belonged to Baptista Egnatius, with his own translation and corrections, at Leiden, 1610, 8°; then it was placed before other writings of this Constantine at Leiden, 1617, 8° C. Most recently, Anselmus Banduri had the same book reprinted in the first volume of his *Imperium Orientale*, with a translation added, which he interpolated and emended here and there, and filled in the gaps.

Luitprand relates, *Legat.* p. 485, that Nicephorus Phocas had prepared forty Hungarians adorned in most precious garments as his patrons and defenders. Which is the same as saying that he had enrolled them in his Hetaeria. Patrons themselves are helpers, auxiliaries, as the Arabs call the praetorian soldiers the assistants of some city or town, and the *praetorium* the house of aid, because truly the aid—*boetheia* in new Greek, or the praetorian militia—sleeps there. Codinus gives the etymology of the name in *De Officiis*, p. 65, not indeed proven, yet not at all to be scorned: “the *hetairiarches* is he who receives the *hetairoi*, that is, friends.” Certainly the new Greeks did not call foreigners coming to Constantinople, who were not of their own race, *xenoi* (strangers), as the ancient Greeks were accustomed, but *hetairoi*, as I demonstrate in another place. I thought at one time that the appellation of *hetairoi* (comrades) was born either from the Roman term *commilitones* (fellow-soldiers), or handed down from the ancient kings of Macedonia, who also had *hetairoi* in their militia, as attested by Polybius, p. 534, ed. Gronov. Then it seemed simpler to me that the Byzantines, without any imitation, had so affectionately called foreigners moving to their camps. But all these things are foreign, and the reason for this appellation must be sought from our own ancestors, from those Saxons and old Franks. They were accustomed—and we still are by the example of our fathers, and the Batavians also, the offspring of the old Germans, are accustomed—to call free men serving for hire, soldiers, craftsmen, and others, *Gesellen, Maten, Folger*, that is, comrades, followers. See what I shall say below regarding the Acolouthos of the Varangians. The Greeks, having rejected the German word, called those foreigners whom they received into their cities and pay, by a Greek word signifying the same as the German. Namely *hetairoi* and *akolouthoi*, of which the former expressed *Maten* or *Gesellen* (whence *Vasalli* was born, for *Vasallus* is the same as *Guesell*), and the latter *Folgher*. The word *Mat* (comrade) is still in use today among the Batavians. From there you may understand what the *Frangomates* are in the *Assizes of Jerusalem*, in Du Cange, v. *Franchire*: “Of those who avow themselves as Frangomates or free slaves.” The word is composed of *Franc* and *mat*, comrade, a free servant, or one given into liberty. REISKII COMMENTARY I conclude that it was the custom to put aside the *melancia* [black cowl] from the place in the second Invective against the Armenians by Isaac, if it is permissible to determine this with sufficient certainty from a passage taken out of context. They also used to put aside their omophoria—as a sign of the episcopate, just as the infula is a sign of imperial authority—at the reading of the Gospel, as is evident from Isidore of Pelusium, book 1, epistle 136, where, explaining the nature of the omophorion mystically, he alleges the reason why the bishop puts it aside at that time: "When the true Shepherd Himself arrives through the unfolding of the worshipped Gospels, the bishop lays aside the emblem of imitation, indicating that the Lord Himself, the Leader and God of the pastoral office, is present." Consult Du Cange, v. *Cervellerium* and *Almucia*. Since our author begins to describe those rites which emperors in solemn processions were required to observe in the celebration of divine worship—and this matter cannot be understood without knowledge of the sacred rites that constitute divine worship among the Greeks—I have decided here at the beginning of this treatise to submit a general overview of the Greek liturgy and Greek churches, from which subsequent matters can be better perceived. I will therefore follow the entire order of the Greek liturgy according to the account of La Croix and Stephan Gerlach, who was present at the sacred rites of the Greeks not once, and who diligently recorded in his itinerary what he saw and heard. There will subsequently be a place for discussion. The regular churches are divided into three parts, called *narthex* (vestibule or *pronaos*, the before-nave), *naos* (the nave), and *bēma* (the sanctuary). The vestibule... serves at present for baptizing children and for exposing the bodies of laymen during funerals... It also serves the congregation of the faithful, who do not enter into the *naos* before the arrival of the patriarch, or the priest, and the emperor, or the principal personage in any given communion, and before the *eisodos* [entry]. The nave serves to receive the faithful, who assemble to sing the praises of God and place themselves according to their rank without confusion. The ministers of the church and distinguished persons occupy the part most advanced near the sanctuary, which is surrounded by stalls, as in our choirs, and is not separated from the other part where the people stand. The sanctuary is at the back of the church, separated from the nave by a large painted entablature, with paintings of Jesus Christ, the Virgin, and the Patron Saint. One enters the sanctuary by three doors, two smaller ones and one large one named by excellence *pylē hagia kai basilikē*, the holy and royal door. [I indeed believe it is called this in the new manner; but in the time of our Constantine, that door was only called *hai hagiai thyrai* (the holy doors), and *hai basilikai* was the door through which one entered from the Narthex into the *Naos*.] There are three enclosures in the interior of the sanctuary destined for different uses; that of the middle, which is made of a single stone marked with a cross, is called the sacred, divine, and mystical table, on account of the mystery, and it is adorned only with a cross and the book of the Gospels. The altar at the right is intended to receive the presents of the faithful and to deposit the elements of the sacrifice. That to the left serves to place the ornaments and sacred vessels, and the deacons, subdeacons, and minor officers take their places there during the sacrifice to light the candles and to heat the water for the chalice. In patriarchal and metropolitan churches, there is at the back of the sanctuary a throne at the side of the great altar for the patriarch or the metropolitan during the celebration, which is named *hagion synthronon*, the holy throne; to distinguish it from the ordinary chair, which they occupy among their clergy during the rest of the office. Women do not mingle with the men; they withdraw into the galleries around the church, or into a tribune closed with lattices, where one does not see them, in order to prevent the distraction, the scandal, and the bad usage that is made frequently of our churches, which is not found among the Greeks, who are very attentive to the office, which is chanted almost entirely in the vulgar Greek. It is not permitted to the laity to enter the sanctuary, the door of which opens three times on the days of solemn feasts; the first before the consecration, and it closes at the same time; the second at the Communion; and the third at the beginning of vespers; and on ordinary days one opens it only twice for the sacrifice. One lights during the office a quantity of candles and lamps in the interior, and before the three doors of the sanctuary, but one places none on the altars. After these things have been brought forward, which contain the parts of the Greek churches and a certain order of the liturgy, I judge it necessary to transcribe the whole of the same booklet, which treats of the manner in which the feast of Easter is celebrated in Wallachia and Moldavia. It excellently illustrates this place of ours. The feast of Easter is celebrated much more solemnly in Wallachia and Moldavia, where there are only Greeks, than in all other places, because of the liberty. The prince and the *domna* [princess] go in ceremonial habit, and followed by a very nimble court, three hours before the day to the Church, in the courtyard of which there are two thrones, one for him and his spouse, and the other for the metropolitan, who receives them in pontifical habits followed by his clergy. The officers of the prince’s court are behind him; namely, the principal lords and the nobility to the right; and the officers of justice and corps of merchants to his left; and the militia makes an enclosure to prevent the people, who come in a crowd to see the ceremony, from incommoding the assistants. There is in the middle of this place an altar, on which the Mass is celebrated; and after the Gospel, the prince and the metropolitan depart at the same time from their thrones, and meet at the altar, where they salute each other face to face, give each other to kiss mutually a small cross adorned with jewels that they hold in their hand, while saying *Christos anesti* [Christ is risen], and return to their places. Afterwards, all the assistants, regular and secular, go in their order to kiss the hand of the metropolitan and of the princess to the sound of drums, trumpets, timbales, and oboes, at the noise of multiple discharges of cannons and musket fire, and at the acclamations of the people, who make the air resound with these words of the Resurrection, while embracing one another. This ceremony is followed by a public feast of the prince in his palace, for the principals of the clergy and the nobility; one serves the clergy with fish, as *caloyers* [monks], religious, and all sorts of meats to the others. There are three tables; one small one on a dais under a canopy for the prince, who eats alone, and two very long ones to the right and left of that one, one for the metropolitan and the clergy, and the other for the officers of the nobility and the nobles. The metropolitan makes the blessing of the table; during the repast, the prince sends plates loaded with the best dishes to those whom he wishes to favor, and drinks the health of the *domna* to the brim, on his knees and bareheaded. This same repast is reiterated two other times in the course of the year, at the feasts of Christmas and the Epiphany; but that of Easter is accompanied by presents of garments, which one distributes to the clergy and to the nobility; namely, three, of velvet, satin, and cloth.
