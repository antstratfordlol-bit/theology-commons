PREFACE OF MEURSIUS TO THE BOOK ON THE ADMINISTRATION OF THE EMPIRE Kind Reader, I shall address you here at the threshold, but in few words. Accept Constantine, and for this favor, if you do not render gratitude, at least render goodwill. I ask for nothing more than this; nor is there anything easier for you to give. You should know, however, where I obtained it. Four years ago, I transcribed it from a manuscript which is in the Palatine Library and is said to have once belonged to Joannes Baptista Egnatius. But I should not here conceal how much, on this very account, I owe to that most distinguished man, Georg Lingelsheim, councillor to the most serene Elector, who, because he is excellently disposed toward letters and all learned men, and toward myself privately, procured for me from his prince the favor of handling that entire well-stocked library at my discretion. Moreover, the kindness of that most distinguished man Janus Gruterus, its prefect, was added to this, through whom the library was opened to me for daily access. By this kindness, both have so bound me to themselves that I have thought it necessary to declare it here publicly. I therefore offer the greatest thanks I can; and you, too, may offer yours, if indeed I have earned any gratitude from you through the edition of this work. I wanted you not to be ignorant of this. Farewell, and whoever you are, contemplate eternity. FROM BANDURI'S PREFACE TO HIS ORIENTAL EMPIRE The first part, in which is contained the division of the provinces of the Roman Empire into *themata*, as well as the provinces and cities once subject to the Constantinopolitan Emperor, comprises Constantine Porphyrogenitus' one book on the *themata* of the East and another on the *themata* of the West, as well as Hierocles the Grammarian's *Synecdemus*. Porphyrogenitus describes the *themata* in these two books in such a way (for anciently, indeed, *thema* meant the same as legion; later, the provinces in which the legions were stationed in garrisons were called *themata*) that he commemorates the lands in which they were stationed—and consequently the peoples to whom they partly owed their names—by inserting various antiquities and narratives. The former book, on the East—that is, on Asia both Minor and Greater—and the latter on the West—that is, on the provinces in Europe, Thrace, Macedonia, Hellas, the Peloponnese, and several other places subject to the Constantinopolitan Empire—comprise the *themata*. Vulcanius first brought the former to the public domain from his library, illustrated with a Latin version and brief notes, at Leiden, by Franciscus Raphelengius, A.D. 1588, in 8vo. The same book appeared a second time with various readings, emendations, and supplements from the Royal manuscript, by the study and work of Fredericus Morellus, in the works of Constantine edited in the same place, from the Elzevir press, A.D. 1617, in 8vo. The latter book of Porphyrogenitus, however—namely, on the *themata* of the West—the same Morellus first edited from a manuscript in the Royal library with a Latin interpretation and notes, in the works of Constantine, in the aforementioned place. We, however, again present both of these books of Porphyrogenitus, which are absolutely necessary for illustrating Byzantine affairs, having compared them with the Royal vellum manuscript no. 2451, written about five hundred years ago, emended and illustrated here with our Latin interpretation and observations, hoping that some light will thereby accrue to both historians and geographers. In the second part, one finds primarily the book of Constantine Porphyrogenitus to his son Romanus; which is entitled by Meursius *De administrando imperio*. In this work, while Porphyrogenitus prescribes the method of prudently administering the empire, he describes the lands, origins, customs, and deeds of very many nations that were formidable to the Constantinopolitan Empire—the Patzinaks, Russians, Bulgarians, Turks, Saracens, Dalmatians, Croatians, Slavs, Franks, Italians, and others—such that he has explained therein the summary of the whole empire, the alliances of all allies, and the strengths, plans, and councils of enemies; and so the subject matter of this work is both historical and political, as Constantine himself teaches in the prologue. This work first appeared in Greek and Latin, translated by Jo. Meursius and with his brief notes, at Leiden, by the types of Jo. Balduinus, at the expense of Lud. Elzevirius, A.D. 1617, in 8vo. But the task I myself performed for Constantine in this new edition, although it may be plain to one who compares both editions, I shall briefly explain. First, I compared the Greek text with an excellent Royal vellum manuscript, no. 2661, which they say was written about five hundred years ago. We removed the innumerable errors with which the Meursian edition was infested everywhere; we repaired from the same Royal manuscript the corrupt and mutilated passages, which were very numerous in the edited Greek text. Furthermore, chapters 23 on Iberia and 24 on Hispania, which Meursius, as he himself says in the notes, had left without a Latin interpretation because they were too corrupt, I have rendered into Latin. Finally, we have emended the translation of the whole book in many places, expanded it, and illustrated the whole work with observations. How much this *opus* of Porphyrogenitus—in which there are very many things either unknown to historians and geographers or passed over by them in silence—is to be valued, no one even moderately learned is ignorant.
BONAVENTURA VULCANIUS'S DEDICATION OF THE FIRST BOOK ON THE THEMES.
TO JANUS DOUSA OF NOORTWYCK, PAULUS BUSIUS, AND ABRAHAM VAN ALMONDE,
Most distinguished and most eminent men, a monstrously destructive storm of war has for fully twenty years now shaken, tossed, and torn apart our Belgium; nor has any favorable star yet shone upon us to give us at last hope of seeing the land: for we wretched men rather wish for than hope for an end to such great labors. But out of all the evils that this truly savage war has dragged along with it in troops, at the recollection of which the mind rightly shudders, I am accustomed to judge almost the most grievous to be the dispersal of libraries; in splendor and multitude of which Belgium, before these upheavals, yielded to no province of the whole world. For what else is to be expected from the destruction of libraries than barbarism and ignorance, from ignorance the confusion of all things, from confusion the shipwreck of the state? Prudently, therefore, and in a manner befitting your office, you act, guardians, who, while war is still blazing and public upheavals are raging, whatever leisure the helm of the state, at which you constantly and bravely keep watch, leaves to you, you spend all of it on promoting the academy and restoring the public library in it, and the torn records from the shipwrecks of earlier years, cast out even to the farthest corners of the world, you seek out at your greatest expense, and once collected, you publish. I am speaking of the book of ancient inscriptions throughout all Europe, which you have arranged to be published for public use at your own expense. A truly excellent work, if ever any has come forth from the printing presses; whose fame, having spread through the whole world long before its publication, held it suspended with an incredible expectation of itself, since it is now at last, with old age cast off, to recover by your kindness its former glory of youth: for which reason it will owe you immortal thanks as long as it shall stand. And that I may for the present say nothing of the usefulness that will come from this work to all students of ancient and recondite learning, which is indeed very great, you will stir up, indeed inflame, others by this illustrious torch that you have held forth, so that whatever torn records from this common shipwreck of the Belgian libraries has come to them, they may contribute with equal eagerness to public use. In their number I gladly profess my own name. For as soon as I learned the reason for your plan concerning the publication of this work, I could not contain myself, for the sake of testifying to my private joy and zeal for literature, from taking up Constantine Porphyrogennetus [On the Themes], an author never before published, first of many, and making him public. But there were three reasons by which I was led to judge him most worthy that at one and the same time as the delights of the world were reborn, that is, your inscriptions, he should come forth into the most illustrious light of this most learned age under the auspices of your most eminent name. The first, that he is an emperor, the son of that Leo; then, that he deserved very well of literary studies, since he revived and rescued from destruction the good arts and sciences that were almost extinguished and half-buried in his age; the third, that he was captured by nearly the same fortune as your inscriptions, experienced military fury, and was redeemed. For first at Mechelen, in the house of a certain English physician, Thomas Clement, he was kept kindly as a small book and lay hidden for many years in the squalor and dust of a library, or rather of a *bibliotheca*; afterward in

There is no need to institute here a lengthy eulogy of this emperor and excellent writer before you, since I have gathered at the end of this book several illustrious testimonies from the historians of that age regarding him, and you yourselves, by your singular erudition and highest keenness of judgement, will most easily recognize them—as they say, recognizing the lion by his claw—and will judge this work to be extremely useful to the republic of letters, since it contains an accurate description of almost the whole of Asia and the military forces distributed throughout it, and besides the fact that it contains much that is either unknown to other geographers or passed over by them in silence, it holds not a few things conducive to the knowledge of antiquity and history; add also the skill of the military art. The gravity of these matters he mixes with pleasant digressions and various authorities of ancient poets, as if with certain condiments, so that his reading, even when repeated thrice, never wearies. He uses at times the names of some military offices that are remote from the custom of the speech of the ancient Greeks; for which reason notes necessarily had to be added by me, through which I might bring some light to these very terms. If perhaps I have wandered from the mark in these, I both beg and deserve pardon, as they cause no small trouble even to the most learned men of our age. Receive then, most noble and most ample men, Constantine, brought back from Orcus by me and given over equally to the light and to Latium; receive him, I say, as a witness of the immense joy which I conceived in my inmost heart from the great eagerness of your mind to aid studies, which you have most clearly declared by this most sumptuously produced edition of these inscriptions. And at the same time persuade yourselves that I, encouraged primarily by your example, shall henceforth make every effort so that neither the will nor (far be the envious envy of the word) the faculty for promoting literary affairs and adorning your academy may seem to have been wanting to me. Farewell, most noble and most ample men, and while you happily curate the state and its seminary, the academy, persevere through the fretting and raging of envy. Your most devoted,
BONAVENTURE VULCANIUS.

DEDICATION BY FEDERICO MOREL OF THE SECOND BOOK OF THEMATES
TO THE MOST ILLUSTRIOUS AND MOST REVEREND CARDINAL PERRON, ARCHBISHOP OF SENS, PRIMATE OF GERMANY, ALMONER TO THE MOST CHRISTIAN KING OF FRANCE AND NAVARRE, FREDERIC MOREL, ROYAL PROFESSOR, SENDS GREETINGS.
Most illustrious Cardinal, this *commentarius* (commentary) on the *themata*, or administrative prefectures of the eastern part of the Roman Empire, of Constantine Porphyrogenitus—*holoporphyrōn antaxios pollōn* (all-purple, worthy of many)—hitherto desired and sought with all prayers by those *historiarchaiologophilos* (lovers of the history of the archives) who are students of Roman lore; now, if less than perfect, *holoscherēs* (complete), at least as much as it was found recently by me, with Mercury as my propitious guide, in the single ancient manuscript codex of the Royal Library, transcribed, revised, edited in the language of both emperors, and illustrated with notes, it desires to come forth and appear under the excellent brightness of your celestial genius and the splendor of your purple. And why, indeed, should an emperor *philomousotatos kai theosebestatos* (most music-loving and most god-fearing), born in the Porphyra palace and immediately vested in purple, not seek the honor and protection of your cardinalitial purple? Nay, why should the Emperor Porphyrogenitus, whose majesty is decorated more by his *philologia* (love of learning) and *architektonikē* (architectonic) science—which he magnified in so many royal volumes—than by his purple cloak, not desire this offspring of the royal muses to be included in your ethereal Minerva’s *peplos*, that is, *egkyklopaideias* (encyclopedia), the circle of sciences and virtues? Especially since it was the same vow for this prince as for Alexander the Great: *tais peri ta arista empeiriais mallon ē tais dynamei diapherein* (to excel more in experience concerning the best things than in forces). Hence, with the best of cares for the safety of the fatherland, he always exercised his mind and his pen, the best teacher of speaking, for he wrote a gem-like book for his Roman son, who succeeded to the paternal kingdom, and who took as his wife Berta, daughter of Hugh, King of Italy, later called Eudoxia, in which book the sum of the entire Greek empire was described, all covenants of allies, and the strengths of enemies; which the Venetians, *philokaloi* (lovers of beauty), as Egnatius relates, diligently preserve in their library. He also knew, in the Roman manner, how to spare the subject and war down the proud. And as he cultivated justice above all and broke the silence of the laws, so he enjoyed and possessed most happily the *Hesychia*, or quiet born of it, as it is celebrated by Pindar, *philophrōn Dikas thygatēr* (kind daughter of Justice), having experienced also how truly Pausanias said, *to ek Theou eumenes epi panti ergazesthai rhastōnen* (the favor of God makes ease in every work). There is no reason, therefore, for me to doubt that the *politikon syngramma* (political treatise) of such an Emperor might be *katathymion* (to your heart’s content), grateful, and acceptable; whose divine genius...
