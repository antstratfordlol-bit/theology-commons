Leudefred, Bishop of Córdoba, discusses more extensively. This office in the earlier centuries of the Church belonged only to presbyters, as is manifest from various councils. For in the Council of Ephesus, act VI, occurs Charisius, presbyter and oikonomos of the holy church of the city of Philadelphia. Likewise, in the Council of Chalcedon, act III, Proterius is "presbyter and oikonomos of the great city of Alexandria." As Greece grew older, this office was transferred also to deacons, and it was the first dignity among those five held by those who were known as the *exokatakoiloi*; these were the great oikonomos, the great sakellarios, the great chartophylax, the chartophylax, and the sakellarios. Their rights and privileges are reviewed by Du Cange in the *Glossarium mediae et infimae Graecitatis*, Vol. I, p. 409 sqq., who also records there various opinions of writers concerning the etymology of this name, p. 411.
The name of Polycarp, too, the prelate of the church of Hadrianopolis, by whose command the poem was composed, is as unknown to writers as that of Joannes Diaconus, and was omitted in Michael Le Quien’s *Oriens Christianus*, Vol. I, p. 1171 sqq., where he enumerates the bishops of Hadrianopolis. Perhaps, however, he is the twenty-second bishop of that church, and indeed the very person on whose behalf George, Bishop of Synnada, is read to have subscribed at the Synod of Constantinople held in the temple of St. Sophia around the year 1440, in which the Greeks abjured the union sanctioned at Florence with the Latins. For Allatius, in his *Addenda* to the third book *De Consensu*, col. 1387—where he reports the *hypographai* of the individual patriarchs, metropolitans, and others who had been present at that synod—has this to say concerning our subject: "George of Synnada, holding also the opinion of the blessed Bishop of Rhodes, and of Hadrianopolis, and of Menas of Mitylene." This conjecture might not seem entirely alien to reason to anyone, if the time of the aforementioned abjuration by the Greeks is compared with the poem completed at that time, in which the poet points to the return of John Palaiologos to the Orient.
Regarding the city of Hadrianopolis, it should be noted that many cities are called by this name, as can be seen among geographers, who place one in Paphlagonia of the Pontic diocese, another in Caria of the Asian diocese, a third in Pamphylia, and a fourth in Pisidia of the same diocese. Ours, however, is distinct from these, since it pertains to the Thracian diocese and is the capital of the province of Haemimontus. This city, once called Orestias after Orestes, its founder as they say, afterwards took the name Hadrianopolis from its restorer, Hadrian Augustus. It lies on the river Hebrus at 53° 50' longitude, 43° 20' latitude; and before the capture of Constantinople—from which it is distant 130 miles—it was the royal city of the Turks, as Leunclavius testifies. Regarding this, Phil. Cluverius in his *Introductio Geographica*, lib. IV, cap. 15, p. 459 (Amstelod. edition), has this: "Hadrianopolis holds the second place among the Thracian cities after Constantinople; it is called Endrem by the Turks; it is indeed great and vast, yet not surrounded by walls, and built with common buildings." POLITICAL POEM OF JOHN THE DEACON
Of that humble oikonomos of Orestias, A 
And deacon of that same blessed church,
Named John, I call to mind his title,
A work of great longing for the divine crowned one,
Palaiologos the steadfast and the grace-named,
Who by his graces stirred the currents of the sea,
Arranged by the commands of the shepherd
Of Orestias, the brilliant and noble Polycarp.
If I had steadfast lungs, a chest of bronze, B 
And mouths with many tongues, and piercing bores,
Or rather, if in truth every part were a mouth,
Truly, both this vast axis, the moist earth, and the rest
Which of existing things has achieved structure from the Creator,
Would I wish to weave together for you, singing praises,
