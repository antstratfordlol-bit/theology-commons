GEORGII CODINI
EXCERPTA
DE ANTIQUITATIBUS CPOLITANIS.
—
EMINENTISSIMO ET REVERENDISSIMO PRINCIPI
FRANCISCO BARBERINO, S. R. E. CARDINALI,
DOMINO MEO SUMMA OBSERVANTIA COLENDO,
PETRUS LAMBECIUS S.
Multa sunt, Eminentissime Princeps, quæ homini ad immortalem gloriam aspiranti viam præbent; verum aut me amor propriæ opinionis fallit, aut ulla nec honestior nec compendiosior potest reperiri quam vel ipsa eruditio vel quæ eruditis exhibetur benevolentia et benignitas. Harum altera quibuscunque egenis et obscuris terræ filiis ad summam laudem aditus patet, dummodo natura aptam iis non denegaret indolem, nec ipsi profectui suo debita diligentia, famæ autem legitima non desint ambitione. Altera illius duntaxat ad claritudinem pervenire licet, quibus eam generis potentiæ et fortunarum amplitudinem largiri Deus voluit, ut protegere alios possint et sustentare. Illi ergo hos colunt, ut necessariis ad studia adminiculis potiantur: hi contra illos fovent, ut monumentis litterarum nominis et virtutis suæ memoria apud posteros celebretur; ita utrique per se indigentes alteri alterorum vigent auxilio. Quare beatissimos ego semper existimavi qui hanc velut omnibus numeris perfectam felicitatem consecuti sunt, ut pariter splendidissimo vitæ statu et merito haud vulgaris eruditionis venerabiles sint atque conspicui. His te numero, eminentissime princeps, qui cum majestate sacræ purpuræ et pontificalis familiæ prærogativa satis sine ullo aliunde petito splendore fulgeas, adeo tamen litterarum studio inter publica tantæ dignitatis munera et fuisti semper et etiamnunc es deditus, ut cum iis qui tota vita aliud nihil egerunt jure et audacter de multifaria scientiæ palma possis contendere. Testatur enim hoc ditissima tua lectissimaque bibliotheca, qua post Vaticanam nihil Roma perfectius habet aut magnificentius; testatur haud exiguus præstantium ingeniorum numerus, quibus aula tua nunquam non fuit exornata; testatur publicum doctissimorum virorum de Te judicium, qui sæpe gravissimarum quæstionum arbitrium tibi detulerunt, quod cum ita sit abunde, ni falior, apparet quibus ego nunc causis impulsus volumen hoc Originum Cpolitanarum eminentisimo nomini tuo dedicare audeam. Nempe jam primum minime dubito quin pro singulari tuo erga litteras affectu, quidquid hoc munusculi litterarii est, lætissimo vultu sis excepturus: deinde præcavebo hac dedicandi ratione ne exprobrari mihi possit, quod olim Anacharsin philosophum in Græcorum ludis improbasse refert Laertius, ἀγωνίζεσθαι μὲν τοὺς τεχνίτας, κρίνειν δὲ τοὺς μὴ τεχνίτας. Certum denique habeo obtensam nominis tui auctoritatem non honori tantum laboribus meis fore, sed et præsidio adversus malevolorum improbas detretationes; quod videlicet nemo sanus ea lacessere ausurus quæ tibi optimo acerrimoque scriptorum censori probantur. Et hæc quidem præcipuæ ex iis sunt quas cum omnibus eruditis communes habeo causas, cur tibi potissimum, eminentissime princeps, quidquid hoc et qualecunque est libri, merito ac lubens consecrem: insuper vero alias multas privitim habeo, quas domesticas appellare lubet; easque tanto cæteris graviores existimo quanto vehementius ἀχαριστίας crimen subire vereor. Etenim repelenti mihi memoriam omnis ejus temporis quod studiorum causa exteris regionibus perlustrandis impendi, vix uilus occurrit dies qui singulari aliquo tuo in me collato beneficio non sit notabilis. Advenientem me primum Lutetiam humanissime per eos qui tum rebus tuis agendis ibi præerant excipi curasti. Mox ipse Lugduno reversus participem me fecisti mensæ, admisisti inter intimos; et ut paucis omnia complectar, tanto me prosecutus es affectu ut majorem ab avunculo non potuissem desiderare. Eamdem benevolentiam Romæ postea expertus sum, ubi integrum biennium in aula tua commoranti mihi ea videndi multa et discendi fuit occasio, ut vere gloriari possim plus me in luce illa et strepitu aulico quam in ullo Academiæ recessu et umbra profecisse. Reversus deinde in Galliam incredibile memoratu est quantam ex commendatione tua cum aliis multis locis utilitatem perceperim, tum vero inprimis Tolosæ, cujus urbis illustrissimus archiepiscopus Dn. Carolus de Montchal, vir sanctitatis simul et prudentiæ et eruditionis rarissimæ, continuos octo menses in palatio suo plus quam paterna


[431] {#pg.157.431}
liberalitate et cura me tractavit. Plura ejus generis addere inconveniens esse credo et superfluum, tum quod ipsa hæc beneficiorum enumeratio nescio quid videatur habere levitatis et arrogantiæ, tum quia vel ex minimo eorum quæ jam dicta sunt superat elucet, quanta obligationis necessitate tibi sim devinctus. Accipe igitur, eminentissime princeps, et boni consule qualemcunque hunc juvenilis diligentiæ meæ fructum, quo pariter et gratitudinem meam tibi studeo declarare, et quanti faciam judicii tui acumen et auctoritatem nominis. Quodsi forte nonnunquam editionem minus feliciter curatam deprehendes, ignoscas velim, et benigne reputes, ex quo hæc Lutetiæ absoluta reliqui, triennium jam præteriisse, ideoque quidquid sub prælo commissum est erroris, absentiæ meæ esse tribuendum. LAMBECII PRÆFATIO AD LECTOREM.
Cum ante biennium Lutetiæ Parisiorum agerem, tantum apud me potuerunt innatum publicæ utilitatis studium et amicorum cohortationes et exempla complurium doctissimorum, ut summum nefas esse crederem inceptæ editioni corporis historiæ Byzantinæ quaticunque mea opera deesse. Quæsivi igitur cui potissimum parti laborem impenderem, et cum fere omnia ab aliis præoccupata esse viderem, tandem mecum cogitare cœpi operæ pretium si hactenus inedita Codini excerpta de antiquitatibus Cpolitanis in publicum producerem. Præterquam enim quod pertineant ad rem et multum lucis afferant historiæ Byzantinæ, hoc etiam nomine edi merentur, quod a gravissimis hujus et superioris sæculi scriptoribus sæpius laudata et ad confirmationem dubiarum opinionum adhibita sint. Etenim Joannes Leunclavius in Pandectis historiæ Turciæ libri de ædificiis frequentissime mentionem facit; et quæcunque Nicolaus Alemanus notis suis in Procopium ἐκ τῶν ἀνεκδότων πατρίων CP inseruit, inde desumpta sunt. Item Petrus Gillius ex supradicto libro de ædificiis et altero de statuis integras transscripsit paginas. Certum quoque est ineditum illud Chronicon Cpolitanum, quod Joannes Meursius in Glossario Græco-barbaro et alibi creberrime citat, nihil aliud fuisse quam ineditam partem excerptorum Codini; quæ nunc publicatur. Testantur hoc citata loca, et testabitur integrum Chronicon, si quando in lucem veniet. Similiter etiam per antiqua illa collectanea θεαμάτων urbis CP, quæ doctissimus Jacobus Sirmondus in notis ad Sidonium Apollinarem citat et in multis bibliothecis occurrere ait, hæc alias non evulgata Codini excerpta intelligenda sunt. Addere plures coætaneos auctores, in quorum scriptis ea citantur, nisi longum esset enumerare et supervacaneum. Cum itaque jam omnino decrevissem edere Codini illa ἀνέκδοτα, nihil mihi antiquius fuit quam ut propositum hoc avunculo meo per litteras significarem, qui illud non tantum non improbat, verum ut cœptum urgerem monuit, atque insuper duos mihi submisit codices manu scriptos, quibus continebantur hæc Georgii Codini opuscula :
Περὶ τῶν πατρίων τῆς Κωνσταντινουπόλεως, Περὶ τῆς σχηματογραφίας τῆς Κωνσταντινουπόλεως, Περὶ ἀγαλμάτων, στηλῶν καὶ θεαμάτων, Περὶ κτισμάτων, Περὶ τῆς οἰκοδομῆς τοῦ ναοῦ τῆς ἁγίας Σοφίας, Περὶ τῶν βασιλευσάντων ἐν τῇ Κωνσταντινουπόλει μέχρι τῆς παρὰ τῶν Ἀγαρηνῶν ταύτης ἁλώσεως, Περὶ τῆς τάξεως τῶν ἀξιωμάτων καὶ ὀφφικίων τοῦ παλατίου καὶ τῆς μεγάλης ἐκκλησίας.

Illas vero tres maxime ob causas conscripsi, nempe ut varias lectiones, quæ sensum auctoris immutare posse videbantur, aliorum ingeniis dijudicandas proponerem; tum ut loca mendosa, dubia, obscura atque minus recte ab aliis intellecta corrigerem et explicarem, et ubi opus esset, correctionis atque explicationis meæ rationem redderem. Verum utrum ego hoc quod mihi proposueram bene præstiterim necne, res ipsa indicabit. Interim tamen, amice lector, velim tibi persuadeas sedulo me operam dedisse, ut utilitati tuæ pro viribus meis quam fidelissime servirem, neque præter modum diligens atque non necessaria multa aut multoties ab aliis antea dicta compilans patientia tua abuterer.

LAMBECII
DE GEORGII CODINI VITA ET SCRIPTIS
BREVIS DISSERTATIO.
—
Georgium Codinum non sub ultimis tantum imperatoribus Cpolitanis vixisse, sed aliquandiu etiam superfuisse post ipsam Cpoleos expugnationem, ex ipsius scriptis liquido apparet. Curopalatæ vero cognomen, quod ei ab iis tribuitur qui librum ejus de Officiis palatii CP ediderunt, in illis quos ego vidi codicibus manu scriptis non reperi; et facilius multo credam impositum ei fuisse Curopalatæ cognomen, quia de Officiis palatii scripsit, quam quod ipse curopalatæ dignitatem obtinuerit. Cæterum quod ad scripta ejus attinet, non propterea duntaxat vano quodam præjudicio improbanda sunt, quoniam Codinus ducentis tantum abhinc annis floruit; quippe qui, quæcunque edidit, ex antiquioribus et magis probatis auctoribus excerpsit et collegit. Nam quod primum inter opuscula ejus locum obtinet et inscribitur περὶ τῶν πατρίων sive de originibus CP, ejus major pars verbotenus descripta est ex Hesychio Milesio, reliqua vero ex Glycæ Annalibus, Chronico Alexandrino et alio quodam hactenus non edito, cujus auctor est quidam Julius Pollux, antiquo illo, qui Commodo imperatori Onomasticum suum dedicavit, aliquot seculis recentior. Libri vero de statuis prima pars de simulacris gentilium ex Phurnuto desumpta est et ex Joannis Lydi Philadelphiensis libro nondum edito de Mensibus *), unde etiam Suidas hausit eadem. Altera autem pars, quæ tractat περὶ στηλῶν καὶ θεαμάτων, quod est, ut ego interpretor, de statuis aliisque rebus spectatu dignis Cpoli, ea tota verbotenus descripta est ex antiqui anonymi auctoris collectaneis antiquitatum Cpoleos, quæ inscripsit παραστάσεις συντόμους χρονικάς, et compilavit ex Theodoro lectore, Papia, Eusebio, Socrate, Marcello lectore et compluribus aliis. Hæc vero collectanea nunc primum ex antiquissimo codice bibliothecæ Regiæ in lucem prodeunt, qui, ut omnes, quotquot eum viderunt, testantur, ad minimum ante quadringentos annos scriptus fuit. Est autem inter codices ejus bibliothecæ numero millesimus vigesimus septimus. Quisquis igitur auctor est illorum collectaneorum, ducentis saltem vel trecentis annis Codino est antiquior. Ex iisdem quoque collectaneis libri περὶ κτισμάτων satis magna pars excerpta est: cætera desumpta sunt ex Joanne Antiocheno, Panodoro Ægyptio et aliis, qui in eo libro citantur. Totus denique catalogus imperatorum Cpoleos usque ad imperium primi Alexii Comneni, auctoris est multo antiquioris Codino: quippe qui eum catalogum conscripsit sub Manuele Comneno Alexii nepote, ut ipse testatur his verbis: ὁ κύριος ᾿Αλέξιος ὁ Κομνηνός, ὁ πάππος τοῦ κραταιοῦ καὶ ἁγίου ἡμῶν βασιλέως. Reliqua porro catalogi pars, a Theodoro Lascare ejus nominis primo usque ad ultimam Cpoleos expugnationem, ab ipso Codino superioribus adjecta est, cum ille, ut principio dixi, aliquantulum illi urbis excidio superstes fuerit.
GEORGE CODINUS, EXCERPTS CONCERNING THE ANTIQUITIES OF CONSTANTINOPLE
TO THE MOST EMINENT AND REVEREND PRINCE, FRANCESCO BARBERINI, CARDINAL OF THE HOLY ROMAN CHURCH, MY LORD, TO BE CULTIVATED WITH THE HIGHEST RESPECT, PETER LAMBECIUS SENDS GREETINGS.
There are many things, most eminent Prince, that offer a path to a man aspiring to immortal glory; but either I am deceived by a love of my own opinion, or there can be found nothing more honest or more brief than either learning itself or the benevolence and kindness shown to the learned. To the former, the entrance to the highest praise is open to any needy and obscure sons of the earth, provided nature has not denied them a suitable talent, nor have they themselves lacking the diligence due for their own progress, and the legitimate ambition for fame. The latter, however, may be attained only by those to whom God has willed to grant such an amplitude of family power and fortune, so that they might be able to protect and support others. Therefore, the poor honor the rich so that they may obtain the necessary resources for their studies; the rich, in turn, foster them so that the memory of their name and virtue may be celebrated by posterity in the monuments of literature; thus, each, being needy in itself, thrives by the aid of the other. Therefore, I have always considered those to be the most blessed who have attained this happiness, as it were, complete in every respect, so that they are venerable and conspicuous both for a most splendid station in life and for merit of no ordinary erudition. Among these, I number you, most eminent Prince, who, while you shine with the majesty of the sacred purple and the prerogative of a pontifical family, sufficiently without any splendor sought from elsewhere, are yet so devoted to the study of letters—amid the public duties of such great dignity—both always and even now, that you could rightfully and boldly contend for the palm of multifarious science with those who have spent their whole lives doing nothing else. For your most rich and choice library testifies to this—than which, after the Vatican, Rome has nothing more perfect or magnificent; the not inconsiderable number of excellent minds with which your court has never failed to be adorned testifies to it; the public judgment of the most learned men regarding you testifies to it, who have often brought to you the arbitration of the most serious questions. Since this is so in abundance, if I am not mistaken, it is apparent for what reasons I am now impelled to dare to dedicate this volume of the *Antiquities of Constantinople* to your most eminent name. For, in the first place, I have no doubt at all that, because of your singular affection for letters, whatever this little literary gift may be, you will receive it with a most joyful countenance; then, by this method of dedication, I will take precautions so that I may not be reproached for what Laertius reports the philosopher Anacharsis once criticized in the games of the Greeks, that the unskilled judge the skilled. Finally, I am certain that the protection of your esteemed name will not only be an honor to my labors, but also a safeguard against the base detractions of the malevolent; for no sane person would dare to attack those things which are approved by you, the best and most keen critic of writers. And these, indeed, are the chief of the reasons which I hold in common with all learned men for why I should merit and willingly consecrate to you, most eminent Prince, whatever this book—of whatever kind it may be—is. Yet, I have many other private reasons as well, which I would like to call domestic, and I consider these all the more important to the rest, the more vehemently I fear to incur the crime of ingratitude. For, as I recall the memory of all that time which I spent traveling through foreign regions for the sake of studies, no day occurs to me that is not notable for some singular benefit of yours bestowed upon me. Upon my first arrival in Paris, you saw to it that I was most humanely received by those who at that time were in charge of your affairs there. Soon, having returned to Lyon, you made me a participant at your table and admitted me among your intimates; and, to comprise everything in a few words, you pursued me with such affection that I could not have desired more from an uncle. I experienced the same benevolence afterwards at Rome, where, while staying in your court for two whole years, I had such an opportunity for seeing and learning many things that I can truly boast that I have profited more in that light and courtly bustle than in any retreat and shade of an academy. Returning then to France, it is incredible to recount how much utility I derived from your recommendation in many other places, but especially at Toulouse, whose most illustrious archbishop, Lord Charles de Montchal—a man of holiness and of most rare prudence and erudition—treated me for eight continuous months in his palace with more than paternal
liberality and care. I believe it is inconvenient and superfluous to add more of this kind, both because this enumeration of benefits themselves seems to have I know not what of levity and arrogance, and because, even from the least of those things which have already been said, it shines forth sufficiently and more than sufficiently, under what necessity of obligation I am bound to you. Receive, therefore, most eminent prince, and take in good part this fruit, such as it is, of my youthful diligence, by which I strive to declare both my gratitude to you and how much I value the sharpness of your judgment and the authority of your name. If, by chance, you sometimes find the edition to have been less fortunately curated, I would wish that you forgive me, and kindly take into account that three years have now passed since I left this work completed in Paris, and therefore whatever error has been committed under the press is to be attributed to my absence. Farewell. Given at Hamburg, the day before the Ides of December, 1653.
LAMBECIUS’ PREFACE TO THE READER
When I was living in Paris two years ago, the innate desire for public utility and the exhortations and examples of many most learned men had such power over me that I believed it to be a supreme disgrace to be lacking, through any work of mine, in the undertaking of the edition of the corpus of Byzantine history. I sought, therefore, at which part I might best employ my labor, and when I saw that almost everything had been preoccupied by others, I finally began to consider with myself that it would be worth the effort if I were to bring to light the hitherto unedited A Excerpta of Codinus regarding the antiquities of Constantinople. For, besides the fact that they are relevant to the matter and bring much light to Byzantine history, they deserve to be edited also on this account: that they have been praised very often by the most serious writers of this and the previous century and have been used for the confirmation of doubtful opinions. Indeed, Joannes Leunclavius makes very frequent mention of the books on the buildings in his *Pandects of Turkish History*; and whatever Nicolaus Alemannus inserted into his notes on Procopius from the unedited *Patria* of Constantinople was taken from there. Likewise, Petrus Gillius transcribed entire pages from the aforementioned book on the buildings and another on the statues. It is also certain that that unedited *Chronicon Constantinopolitanum*, which Joannes Meursius cites very frequently in his *Glossarium Græco-barbarum* and elsewhere, was nothing other than an unedited part of the extracts of Codinus, which is now being published. The cited passages testify to this, and the complete *Chronicon* will testify to it, if ever it comes to light. Similarly, those ancient collections regarding the *Patria* of the city of Constantinople, which the most learned Jacobus Sirmondus cites in his notes to Sidonius Apollinaris and says are to be met with in many libraries, are to be understood as these previously unpublished extracts of Codinus. I would add many other contemporary authors in whose writings these are cited, were it not long and superfluous to enumerate them. Since, therefore, I had now completely decided to edit those unedited writings of Codinus, nothing was more important to me than to signify this plan by letter to my uncle, who not only did not disapprove of it but warned me to urge on the endeavor, and furthermore sent me two manuscripts containing these little works of Georgius Codinus:
On the *Patria* of Constantinople,
On the Topography of Constantinople,
On the Statues, Columns, and Sights,
On the Buildings,
On the construction of the church of Hagia Sophia,
On those who reigned in Constantinople until the capture of this city by the Hagarenes,
On the order of offices and dignities of the palace and the Great Church.
Of these, the first, fifth, seventh, and the beginning of the third had already been edited long ago by Georgius Dousa, Franciscus Junius, and Jacobus Gretserus; the rest, however, are now published by me for the first time. Moreover, besides the two codices of my uncle, one of which he had received as a gift from the most learned Peiresc, and the other he had ensured was transcribed from a Vatican exemplar, I have used four other codices in preparing the edition, the first of which, described from a British exemplar, was given to me by the most learned Isaac Vossius, when I was still in Amsterdam; the second, the R. P. Jacobus Sirmondus shared with me; the third, the Royal library provided to me by the permission of the brothers Petrus and Jacobus Puteanus; the fourth, I myself found in Rome in the Vallicellian library.
So far I have said whence the unedited works of Codinus have emerged: it follows that I should speak briefly about my own notes. I wrote them for three main reasons, namely, that I might propose for the judgment of others’ intellects various readings that seemed capable of changing the author’s meaning; then, that I might correct and explain passages that were faulty, doubtful, obscure, and less correctly understood by others, and, where it was needed, to provide the rationale for my correction and explanation. But whether I have performed this task that I proposed for myself well or not, the matter itself will indicate. In the meantime, however, friendly reader, I would have you believe that I have applied myself diligently to serve your utility as faithfully as possible to the best of my strength, and that I would not abuse your patience by being excessively diligent and compiling many unnecessary things or things said by others many times before.
Furthermore, so that you may more easily forgive me if I should have stumbled, as humans do, either in a translation or in the observations, I have avoided every single occasion that presented itself to me to contradict those who have published something of Codinus before me or understood it otherwise than they ought. And if at any time I was forced by necessity to expose their errors, I used such moderation in that matter that it is unclear against whom I am writing; yet it is manifest that I did this not to seek glory from the errors of others, but solely to take care that I not appear to have omitted anything necessary through ignorance. It remains, therefore, that you accept these my labors kindly, and if you find in them anything that will less please you, that you indulge somewhat in my youth and in my excessive zeal for you and in the heavier occupations, through which I could not follow up and examine everything most exactly. Thus you will show yourself gracious, and you will render me more eager to perform greater things. Farewell. Rome, May 1st, 1649.
A BRIEF DISSERTATION BY LAMBECIUS ON THE LIFE AND WRITINGS OF GEORGIUS CODINUS
That Georgius Codinus lived not merely under the last emperors of Constantinople, but also survived for some time after the conquest of Constantinople itself, appears clearly from his own writings. However, the surname "Curopalata," which is attributed to him by those who published his book *On the Offices of the Palace of Constantinople*, I have not found in those manuscripts which I have seen; and I would much more easily believe that the surname "Curopalata" was imposed upon him because he wrote about the "Offices of the Palace," than that he himself attained the dignity of Curopalata. But as for his writings, they are not to be condemned for that reason alone by some sort of vain prejudice, since Codinus flourished only two hundred years ago; seeing that he excerpted and collected whatever he published from more ancient and more approved authors. For as concerns his first work, which holds the place among his opuscula and is inscribed *Concerning the Origins of Constantinople* (Περὶ τῶν πατρίων), its major part is transcribed word-for-word from Hesychius of Miletus, and the rest from the Annals of Glycas, the Alexandrian Chronicle, and another work hitherto unpublished, whose author is a certain Julius Pollux, who lived some centuries later than that ancient Julius Pollux who dedicated his *Onomasticon* to the Emperor Commodus. B The first part of his book *On the Statues*, concerning the idols of the pagan gods, is taken from Cornutus and from the as yet unpublished book *On the Months* by Joannes Lydus of Philadelphia, from whence Suidas also drew the same things. The second part, which treats *concerning the statues and sights* (περὶ στηλῶν καὶ θεαμάτων), which is, as I interpret, of statues and other sights worthy of being seen at Constantinople, is entirely transcribed word-for-word from the collections of the antiquities of Constantinople of an ancient anonymous author, which he inscribed *Short Historical Excerpts* (παραστάσεις σύντομοι χρονικαὶ), and compiled from Theodorus Lector, Papias, Eusebius, Socrates, Marcellus Lector, and many others. These collections are now for the first time coming to light from a most ancient manuscript of the Royal Library, which, as all who have seen it testify, was written at least four hundred years ago. And it is among the manuscripts of that library numbered one thousand and twenty-seven. Whoever, therefore, the author of those collections is, he is at least two or three hundred years older than Codinus. From the same collections a fairly large part of the book *On the Monuments* (περὶ κτισμάτων) is excerpted; the rest is taken from Joannes Antiochenus, Panodorus the Egyptian, and others who are cited in that book. Finally, the whole catalog of the emperors of Constantinople up to the reign of the first Alexius Comnenus is from an author much older than Codinus: for he wrote the catalog under Manuel Comnenus, the grandson of Alexius, as he himself testifies in these words: "The lord Alexius Comnenus, the grandfather of our powerful and holy emperor." The remaining part of the catalog, from Theodore Lascaris of that name, the first of that name, up to the final conquest of Constantinople, was added by Codinus himself to the former parts, since, as I said in the beginning, he survived that fall of the city for some time.
