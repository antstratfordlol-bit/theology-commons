Moreover, what Codinus narrates here about the tokens or images of the pagans, he took from Cornutus and John Lydus the Philadelphian, in his book *On Months*, from which Suidas also, as Lambecius observed, drew the same things. The rest concerning the statues and things worthy of note in the city, he described almost word for word from the anonymous collection of antiquities of Constantinople, which bears the title *Brief Historical Collections*, which Lambecius first published in Greek from a code of the King of France with his own notes at the end of Codinus, pp. 85-105, and observes that they were excerpted from Theodore the Reader, Papias, Eusebius, Socrates, Marcellus the Reader, and many other writers. The same Anonynymous Collections, far more corrected, were given by Francis Combefis from that time, in Greek and Latin with his own version and notes, in his *Fasciculus of Constantinopolitan Affairs*, at the end of Allatius' *On Simeon*, Paris, 1664, 4to. And since he frequently chides Lambecius in those notes, Lambecius wished to reply to him in a new edition of Codinus which he had promised, which he wanted to insert by equipping it primarily from the Imperial library as a supplement to the *Corpus of Byzantine History*. See Book III of his *Commentaries on the Library of Vienna*, p. 426. [Book VIII, p. 970 Koll.] But that Supplement never saw the light of day. There follows in that first and last Lambecian edition of Codinus the work of the same writer *On the Buildings of Constantinople*, *On the monasteries and other buildings of Constantinople*, pp. 37-64; or *On the structure of the church of St. Sophia and the Holy Apostles*, pp. 64-74. Which part is also read in the edition of Georgius Dousa, and finally *On the years from the creation of the world down to the empire of the great Constantine, and on those who reigned in the same queen of cities, down to its very capture by the Hagarenes*, pp. 75-83. [Concerning the codices, we have already spoken above.] There is subjoined at the end of the volume *An explanation of the offices of the Holy and Great Church, and they were interpreted according to their order*. An anonymous explanation of the offices of the Holy and Great Church of Constantinople according to their order: which Bernard Medonius of Toulouse first edited from a manuscript code belonging to Carolus Monchallus, Archbishop of Toulouse, and added his own Latin version and Glossary, and brief notes in the margin.

THE BOOK OF GEORGIOS CODINUS CONCERNING THE OFFICES OF THE GREAT CHURCH AND THE IMPERIAL PALACE OF CONSTANTINOPLE
TO THE MOST EMINENT PRINCE OF THE HOLY ROMAN CHURCH, MICHAEL MAZARINO, ARCHBISHOP OF AIX-EN-PROVENCE.
To the extent that Gaul is more of an emulator of Greek letters, most eminent Cardinal, not inferior in ancient piety, and now more powerful in the dignity of empire, so much the more does Greece, mindful of its pristine erudition and religious majesty, seek more ardently the protection of Gaul; and with greater fervor the Gallic nobles court the Athens of wisdom, with so much greater a wealth of joys does Codinus, having attained the name of *sophōtatos* (most wise), desire that this work of his on ecclesiastical and palatine offices be dedicated by the Gauls for eternal consecration. It would seem to have wandered outside its fatherland, though not without fame, had it not received its native ecclesiastical dignitaries into your sanctuary and brought its courtiers into your palace, in which he might prepare for them a seat fitting for ancestral religion and royal majesty at once. Upon perceiving the whiteness of the vestment beneath your purple, he conjectured the innermost whiteness of your soul, nor a nobility unequal to your eminence; and—the patriarch along with the dignitaries of the Great Church—now, when it neither mourns nor fasts, shining with a white color, and now, if it should ordain a period of mourning or fasting, with purple.
