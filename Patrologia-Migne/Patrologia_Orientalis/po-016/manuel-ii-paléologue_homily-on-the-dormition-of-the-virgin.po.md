The present oration was written by the Emperor in praise of the most glorious Mother of God, because she delivered him from a very serious illness. But since all those who have celebrated her in sermons dwell more conveniently on narratives about her admirable life and linger on these, as is usually the case, he judged that they should be omitted here entirely, so as not to say the same things as such great men. And they indeed propose her praises as their end, and strive for these praises; he, however, having them as if omitted, extols the most venerable Virgin throughout the whole oration, so to speak: He shows indeed that that sadness, which commonly happens to all men from the separation of soul and body, was so light in her and vanished so quickly and as if in a moment because of her most dear passing, that it is not even permitted to call it sadness. And devising this as a kind of subject matter, he divides it, and shows the ways in which death becomes terrible to men, and finally persuades us not to be sad but to rejoice with the Mother of God concerning her migration to the Son.

It is not new, O Mother of God, nor discordant, but rather very decent and suitable that the migration from this life, which nature dreads as future and cannot bear when it supervenes because of pain, should have been for you, on the contrary, a matter of a certain joy and pleasure. For it was fitting that it should be consonant with your character and life and your entirely stupendous birth-giving, so that whatever pertained to you would be full of miracle. For this reason, to speak more openly, the separation of the soul from the body, which it is our custom to call death, and at whose very name we are troubled, was deservedly for you a celebration full of hilarity and an occasion of delight more sublime than earthly joys. Since all yield to you to such an extent, even those who have attained the highest praise for those things for which a generous soul is famously eager to be commended, that indeed it is not permitted to find a distance of excellence. Therefore, that most new thing, namely to die with joy, can belong to no one else among all others; but that this has happened to you alone, O purest one, higher than the heavens, Mother of God, all who think rightly confess. That this is rightly affirmed, the oration will demonstrate at an opportune time, taking the occasion from the present day, on which you migrated from earth to heaven, which would have been a place clearly suitable for you even while living, just as it is to live and reign together with the Son and King.

For you, O immaculate one, when we called you Mother of God, we offered you everything that pertains to weaving praises. For since you abound in many and very ample titles, which called you Mother of God, it placed a crown on the eulogy. For this is truly the sum of encomia, whatever not only the tongue of men, but even of the angels themselves might utter about you. For what is of such value for praise as to assert that you yourself received the flame of Divinity in your womb, and having been made partaker of that purest light above all creatures, yet suffered no burning from it? What, finally, could there be that is not far inferior? Whence, having sufficiently declared the zeal and purpose of our mind with this appellation as a kind of general title, we judge that special ones should not be abused.

For just as it would be foolish to think that splendor is added to solar rays by countless lit lamps, or that this immense sea is increased by the addition of one drop, similarly to attempt to extol you with praises, Virgin, a vessel full of all divine gifts and elevated with such dignity that no mind could even imagine it. For he who is impassible was captured, so to speak, by the beauty of your soul, and appeared enclosed in you, who is enclosed by no limits, and he made you the treasure and dispenser of his graces.

However, all our things are mixed, nor is it possible to find pleasure in any thing without sadness, nor again sadness without pleasure, as a certain wise man of the ancients said: It is not possible to find in anyone a life without sadness. He would have said the opposite, too, if there had been need: It is not possible to find in anyone a life without joy. But if that which conquers prevails, it is altogether necessary that the lower and weaker immediately yield to the stronger and greater. Since these things are so, why should it not be said that pain was then absorbed by that vehement joy which her migration from then on brought to the Mother of God? For if the hope of enjoying those things which were stored up for her by the Son in heaven produced exultation and delights for her perpetually, how would she not have preferred above all things that which would bring her the possession of these for all ages? This one thing was dear to her heart, to depart hence from human affairs. But this desire, innate from the beginning, flourished throughout her whole life, incessantly nourished by the sentiments of true and genuine and sublime philosophy. This was for her a festival, to be freed from mortal bonds; this alone was desirable, which could grant her pure enjoyment of spiritual things, the mass of the body no longer hindering contemplation. Her spirit was so consumed by this desire that it could not be abolished. Moreover, it arose at the time when her Offspring had ascended to the Father, and it always flourished in that heavenly soul. This was also a bridge to immortal life and incorruption for her, who became a bridge for those who were freed from corruption. This was a ladder to the Son for her, which is the ladder of those who are saved to him, who has heaven as his seat, whom the Virgin bore, and to whom she always adhered in spirit, the Son himself doing the same unceasingly.
