XI. TEN ARGUMENTS OF MARCUS OF EPHESUS AGAINST PURGATORY FIRE. TEN SYLLOGISMS, BY WHICH IT IS SHOWN THAT PURGATORY FIRE DOES NOT EXIST. 1. Those who see the glory of God, one sees more perfectly than another, and he sees more perfectly who has been more purified. But that anyone is more purified, by that very fact it is inferred that another is less purified, namely he who enjoys the vision less: for if a greater thing is consequent to a greater, also a lesser is consequent to a lesser. Whence it happens that he who carries with him some, though light, faults, he also sees God, without needing purgatory fire, the place of which is supplied by the mercy of God according to the most learned Dionysius, who asserts that "to those who have lived holily, according to the merits of each, the divine and most clear life is repaid by those most just scales, the divine clemency indeed, for its goodness, dissimulating the stains contracted from human frailty, because no one, as the sacred scriptures hand down, is clean from filth." 2. Likewise, if things that are the same according to one and the same thing are the same in turn, they will not differ among themselves according to that; but things that do not differ among themselves according to something, will not differ among themselves according to that efficiency by which they are the same, and therefore neither will the end proposed to that efficiency be different and different, but one and the same. But to that efficiency of being purified, which consists both in aversion from sin and in conversion to virtue and the exercise of this, the vision of God responds. But between pure and pure there is no difference. But if all who, having spurned sin, have performed many good works, or have done nothing good, prevented by death, have died nevertheless with a right will,

3. Likewise, in purified souls, that evil on account of which fire was needed is no longer considered, since purgation necessarily effects liberation from evil. But evil and good are opposed to each other as privation and form. But in those in which no privation is considered, in them a perfect form must be held, since imperfection does not arise except from privation. Therefore, the purified soul will attain the perfect good, as the reason of beatitude demands. But if many perfect things occur in the same species, they will not differ among themselves in good except according to number; and because the proposed end, that is, beatitude, is not given except according to the reason of purgation, it follows that all the souls of the deceased are equally participants in the vision of God, in which beatitude consists. But that is false: for the Lord says in the Gospels that there are many mansions in the house of the Father. By which words not a few saints, whose testimony it is not right to deny, have judged that the degrees of that blessed life are signified. Therefore it follows.

Likewise, for the attainment of beatitude, there is required both the rectitude of the will, by which one is properly carried toward the universal good, and that which elicits good and reward-worthy works. But the exercise of both will and works is necessarily included in this life, as you yourselves also think: for you assert that the will of souls detained in purgatory is immutable. But where no movement of the will is carried toward any thing at all, there also one is by no means held to be more worthy than another. Therefore, souls, even if they have not been purged by purgatorial fire, are no less worthy of the vision of God, provided that they have departed with a right will, which is required for beatitude principally and most powerfully. For if through purgatory the will is not rendered right from wrong, and the rectitude of the will is required for beatitude, then purgatory contributes nothing to it. But that which has no effect at all is posited in vain; but God does nothing at all in vain. Therefore, it is not so. 6. Likewise, the equity of eternal punishment is shown most of all in this, that those who have sinned have an immutable evil will: for to a perpetually evil will, a perpetual punishment is also owed. And conversely, in the very same way, if he who remains immutable in the commission of evil is punished with perpetual punishment, he who is not punished perpetually will not have an immutable will either. For he who has the same immutable will, if it is indeed in evil, is reserved for eternal punishment; but if in good, what need has he of punishment, to whom crowns are owed? But you yourselves contend that those who are purged by such fire have an immutable will. Therefore, they are not purged by fire.

9. Likewise, it is more expedient for the divine goodness not to despise a small good than to avenge a small fault. But a small good in those who have sinned gravely obtains no reward because of the preponderant wickedness. Nor, therefore, does it befit a small evil in those who have exercised outstanding virtues to be punished, because those things which are more excellent prevail. For if that which is seen more is not, surely that which is seen less will not be. Nor, therefore, is it to be thought that there is a purgatorial fire. 10. Likewise, as is the case with some small good in those who are otherwise wicked, so is a small evil in those who are otherwise good. But a small good in the former cannot effect a retribution of goods, but only a difference of punishment; nor, therefore, will a small evil in the latter effect punishment, but only a difference of fruition. It is not, therefore, to be believed that there is a purgatorial fire.
