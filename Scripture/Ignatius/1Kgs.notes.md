# 1 Kings — Notes

## Doctrinal notes

### 1Kgs 1:1 {#nt.1Kgs.1.1}

The First and Second Books of the Kings (the Third and Fourth Books of the Kings): the period covered in these books extends from the death of David to after the fall of Jerusalem in the year 586 B.C. The main theme is the steady decline of Israel dragged down by the monarchy, and her punishment by God for the worship of false gods, introduced by Solomon and actively promoted by many of his successors, especially in the northern kingdom.

### 1 Kings 1:1 {#nt.1Kgs.1.1b}

The story continues from 2 Sam 20:26.

### 1 Kings 1:5 {#nt.1Kgs.1.5}

There was as yet no natural right of succession and David had already given the right to Solomon, a younger son; cf. verse 13.

### 1 Kings 3:1 {#nt.1Kgs.3.1}

Chapters 3–11 give the history of Solomon. The *Pharaoh* of 3:1 and 9:16 (?) was of the twenty-first dynasty, possibly Psusennes II.

### 1 Kings 3:5 {#nt.1Kgs.3.5}

*a dream:* A common means of divine communication, especially before the age of the prophets.

### 1 Kings 4:32 {#nt.1Kgs.4.32}

*proverbs*:Doubtless some of those of the book of Proverbs are to be ascribed to Solomon. The book of Wisdom however (called in Greek The Wisdom of Solomon), is ascribed to him only because of his reputation for wisdom. It was actually written in the first century B.C.

### 1 Kings 6:1 {#nt.1Kgs.6.1}

The temple was built on the high ground to the north of Ophel, David’s city.

### 1 Kings 8:10 {#nt.1Kgs.8.10}

*cloud:* The visible sign of the presence of Yahweh taking possession of his sanctuary; later called the shekinah by the rabbis.

### 1 Kings 8:23–53 {#nt.1Kgs.8.23}

This eloquent prayer admirably sums up the relation of God to his people.

### 1 Kings 8:27 {#nt.1Kgs.8.27}

A reminder that Yahweh was very different from the gods of other nations, who dwelt in their temples.

### 1 Kings 9:3–9 {#nt.1Kgs.9.3}

God’s response to Solomon’s prayer.

### 1 Kings 10:1 {#nt.1Kgs.10.1}

*Sheba:* A kingdom in southwestern Arabia.

### 1 Kings 10:22 {#nt.1Kgs.10.22}

*ships of Tarshish:* i.e., ships that could sail to Tarshish (usually located in Spain). The word then came to be applied to ocean-going vessels.

### 1 Kings 11:1–8 {#nt.1Kgs.11.1}

This account of Solomon’s moral decline stresses the connection between apostasy and sexual excess—a theme often met with in the Old Testament.

### 1 Kings 11:14 {#nt.1Kgs.11.14}

Because of Solomon’s idolatry, God raised up enemies, to the south (Edom), to the north (Damascus), and within the kingdom (Jeroboam).

### 1 Kings 12:1 {#nt.1Kgs.12.1}

*Shechem* was the old religious center of the northern tribes. The alliance between these and Judah was never very strong, even during the united monarchy.

### 1 Kings 12:28 {#nt.1Kgs.12.28}

*Behold your gods*: Jeroboam seems to have had no intention of introducing false gods. These were to be images of Yahweh. But in doing this he debased the whole idea of true worship and made it more like pagan religion, to which it was bound to lead in the end; cf. 16:31. Judah, with all its advantages of temple and dynasty, was no better at this time, but reforms were instituted at intervals.

### 1 Kings 16:24 {#nt.1Kgs.16.24}

*hill of Samaria:* One of the most splendid sites in the Middle East for a capital city. But even so, the northern kingdom never achieved stability.

### 1 Kings 17:1 {#nt.1Kgs.17.1}

With this description of the drought begins the prophetic career of Elijah, the great opponent of the nature religion that flourished in the northern kingdom and that Ahab and his Phoenician wife, Jezebel, were so active in promoting.

### 1 Kings 18:19 {#nt.1Kgs.18.19}

*prophets of Baal:* These had been brought from Phoenicia by Jezebel.

### 1 Kings 19:8 {#nt.1Kgs.19.8}

*Horeb:* i.e., Mount Sinai, where the law was given to Moses and God made a covenant with his people.

### 1 Kings 21:10 {#nt.1Kgs.21.10}

Two witnesses were required for a legal charge involving the death penalty; cf. Susanna and the elders; cf. Num 35:30; Deut 19:15; Dan 13:40; Mt 18:16.

### 1 Kings 21:29 {#nt.1Kgs.21.29}

God has mercy on the repentant king, as before in the case of David. But he does not say here that the dynasty will be preserved.
