# Matthew — Notes

## Doctrinal notes

### Mt 1:1 {#nt.Matt.1.1}

The genealogy is given to show that Jesus had the descent required for Messiahship, i.e., from Abraham and, in particular, from David the King.

### Mt 1:16 {#nt.Matt.1.16}

Joseph’s, not Mary’s, descent is given here, as the Jews did not usually reckon descent through the mother. Joseph was the legal and presumed father, and it was this fact that conferred rights of inheritance, in this case, the fulfilment of the Messianic promises.

### Mt 1:25 {#nt.Matt.1.25}

This means only that Joseph had nothing to do with the conception of Jesus. It implies nothing as to what happened afterward.

### Mt 3:2 {#nt.Matt.3.2}

*Repent* implies an internal change of heart.

### Mt 3:6 {#nt.Matt.3.6}

Not a Christian baptism but a preparation for it.

### Mt 3:15 {#nt.Matt.3.15}

Though without sin, Jesus wished to be baptized by John, as this was the final preparation for his mission as Messiah.

### Mt 5:17 {#nt.Matt.5.17}

Jesus came to bring the old law to its natural fulfilment in the new, while discarding what had become obsolete; cf. Jn 4:21.

### Mt 5:29 {#nt.Matt.5.29}

An exaggeration to emphasize the need to avoid occasions of sin.

### Mt 5:32 {#nt.Matt.5.32}

*unchastity*: The Greek word used here appears to refer to marriages that were not legally marriages because they were either within the forbidden degrees of consanguinity (Lev 18:6–16) or contracted with a Gentile. The phrase *except on the ground of unchastity* does not occur in the parallel passage in Lk 16:18. See also Mt 19:9 (Mk 10:11–12), and especially 1 Cor 7:10–11, which shows that the prohibition is unconditional.

### Mt 6:6 {#nt.Matt.6.6}

This does not, of course, exclude public worship but ostentatious prayer.

### Mt 6:24 {#nt.Matt.6.24}

*mammon*: i.e., riches.

### Mt 8:3 {#nt.Matt.8.3}

The miracles of Jesus were never performed to amaze people and shock them into belief. They were worked with a view to a real strengthening of faith in the recipient or beholder, from whom the proper dispositions were required.

### Mt 8:29 {#nt.Matt.8.29}

*before the time*: Before the day of judgment the demons are permitted by God to tempt men and even to possess them.

### Mt 10:5 {#nt.Matt.10.5}

The gospel, the Messianic salvation, had first to be preached and offered to the chosen people, Israel. Later it would be offered to the Gentiles.

### Mt 11:3 {#nt.Matt.11.3}

The Baptist expected more obvious signs of the Messiah. By quoting the prophet Isaiah, Jesus showed that he was indeed inaugurating the Messianic kingdom—but by doing good rather than by glorious manifestations or sudden punishments.

### Mt 11:27 {#nt.Matt.11.27}

This shows a profound relationship between the Son and the Father, far superior to adoptive sonship.

### Mt 12:14 {#nt.Matt.12.14}

The Pharisees regarded healing as work and so forbade it on the sabbath.

### Mt 12:24 {#nt.Matt.12.24}

*Beel-zebul*: Name of a Canaanite god meaning “the Prince-god.” The Jews interpreted this name as “Prince of demons,” because for them all false gods were demons. The form “Beel-zebub” is a contemptuous adaptation meaning “Lord of the flies.”

### Mt 12:31 {#nt.Matt.12.31}

To attribute to the devil the works of the Holy Spirit seems to imply a hardness of heart that precludes repentance.

### Mt 12:46 {#nt.Matt.12.46}

*brethren*: The Greek word or its Semitic equivalent was used for varying degrees of blood relationship; cf. Gen 14:14; 29:12; Lev 10:4.

### Mt 12:48 {#nt.Matt.12.48}

Jesus puts the work of salvation before family relationships. It is not said, however, that he refused to see them.

### Mt 13:12 {#nt.Matt.13.12}

To those well-disposed Jews who have made good use of the old covenant will now be given the perfection of the new. On the other hand, from those who have rejected God’s advances will now be taken away even that which they have, because the old covenant is passing away.

### Mt 13:52 {#nt.Matt.13.52}

This is Matthew’s ideal: that the learned Jew should become the disciple of Jesus and so add the riches of the new covenant to those of the old, which he already possesses; cf. verse 12.

### Mt 13:55 {#nt.Matt.13.55}

See note on Mt 12:46.

### Mt 14:33 {#nt.Matt.14.33}

Their realization of his Godhead was the prelude to Peter’s confession of faith at Caesarea Philippi (Mt 16:16).

### Mt 15:5 {#nt.Matt.15.5}

By dedicating his property to God, i.e., to the temple, a man could avoid having to help his parents, without actually giving up what he had. The scribes held such a vow to be valid without necessarily approving it.

### Mt 15:24 {#nt.Matt.15.24}

See note on Mt 10:5.

### Mt 16:14 {#nt.Matt.16.14}

The title of prophet had a Messianic significance because the gift of prophecy, which had been extinct since Malachi, was expected to return at the beginning of the Messianic era, especially by an outpouring of the Spirit as foretold by the prophet Joel and as realized in Acts 2:16.

### Mt 16:16 {#nt.Matt.16.16}

The context shows that Peter recognizes the sonship of Jesus as divine and not adoptive like ours. Mark and Luke in the parallel passages mention only the confession of the Messiahship.

### Mt 16:18 {#nt.Matt.16.18}

The name “Peter” comes from the Greek word for “rock.” Jesus makes him the foundation on which the church is to be built. The word “church” means “assembly” or “society” of believers. The Hebrew equivalent is used in the Old Testament to indicate the chosen people. In applying it to the church, Jesus shows it to be the Messianic community foretold by the prophets.

### Mt 16:19 {#nt.Matt.16.19}

*the kingdom of heaven*: Peter has the key to the gates of the city of God. This power is exercised through the church. “Binding” and “loosing” are rabbinic terms referring to excommunication, then later to forbidding or allowing something. Not only can Peter admit to the kingdom; he also has power to make authoritative decisions in matters of faith or morals.

### Mt 16:26 {#nt.Matt.16.26}

*life* (both times): A play on the word “life”—natural and supernatural; cf. Mk 8:35–36.

### Mt 17:4 {#nt.Matt.17.4}

Peter thought the glorious Messianic kingdom had come. In fact, Jesus allowed this glimpse of his glory to strengthen them for the coming passion.

### b {#nt.Matt.18.9}

Mt 18:9: Gehenna (see footnote b) was the name of a valley south of Jerusalem where human sacrifice had once been practiced; cf. 2 Chron 33:6. Later it became a cursed place and a refuse dump, and the name came to symbolize the Christian place of punishment.

### Mt 18:18 {#nt.Matt.18.18}

To the other apostles is given a share in the authority given to Peter.

### Mt 19:9 {#nt.Matt.19.9}

This appears to refer to the case in Mt 5:32, though the Greek word for “except” is different.

### Mt 19:11–12 {#nt.Matt.19.12}

Jesus means that a life of continence is to be chosen only by those who are called to it for the sake of the kingdom of God.

### Mt 21:9 {#nt.Matt.21.9}

The crowd openly recognizes Jesus as the Messiah and he allows it for the first time.

### Mt 21:23 {#nt.Matt.21.23}

They object to the assumption of authority implicit in the manner of his entry into the city and in his expulsion of the sellers from the temple.

### Mt 21:33–44 {#nt.Matt.21.33}

This parable is really an allegory in which almost every detail represents something in God’s dealings with Israel.

### Mt 22:11 {#nt.Matt.22.11}

The wedding garment represents the dispositions necessary for admission to the kingdom.

### Mt 23:5 {#nt.Matt.23.5}

*phylacteries*: Little leather boxes containing, on a very small scroll, the principal words of the law; cf. Deut 6:4–9. Taking the command literally, they fastened these to their arms and their foreheads.

### Mt 23:9 {#nt.Matt.23.9}

i.e., “Do not use the title without reference to God’s universal fatherhood.” He cannot mean that the title is never to be used by a son to his father.

### Mt 24:1—25:46 {#nt.Matt.24.1}

The “Eschatological Discourse,” as it is called, deals with the fall of Jerusalem and the end of the world. The two themes seem to be inextricably intermingled in the Gospel as we now have it, but it is possible that originally they were in separate discourses. However, the fusion of the two does bring out their connection. The one prefigures the other. Moreover, in the reverse direction, so to speak, the language used to describe the day of the Lord in Joel and elsewhere is here applied to the fall of Jerusalem, the details of which must therefore not be taken too literally (24:29).

### Mt 25:29 {#nt.Matt.25.29}

See note on Mt 13:12.

### Mt 26:17 {#nt.Matt.26.17}

The passover supper was eaten this year on the Friday evening (Jn 18:28). Jesus must have anticipated the passover meal because he would be dead the following day and because the meal prefigured his death.

### Mt 26:26 {#nt.Matt.26.26}

The details of the Eucharist are superimposed on the ritual of the passover.

### Mt 26:51 {#nt.Matt.26.51}

It was Peter, as John in his later Gospel tells us (Jn 18:10), though Matthew is reluctant to say so.

### Mt 26:59 {#nt.Matt.26.59}

They sought evidence against him and this was necessarily false.

### Mt 26:64–65 {#nt.Matt.26.63}

For the first time Jesus speaks clearly of his own identity. Caiaphas evidently understands him to claim divinity.

### Mt 27:46 {#nt.Matt.27.46}

Jesus applies Psalm 22 (Vulgate 21) to himself.

### Mt 27:66 {#nt.Matt.27.66}

The sealing and guarding only helped to make the subsequent resurrection more obvious.

### Mt 28:1–20 {#nt.Matt.28.1}

The resurrection appearances. There are divergent traditions in the gospels, Galilean and Judean. Paul adds his own record (1 Cor 15). The accounts do not easily fit together, but this is surely evidence of their genuineness. There is no attempt to produce an artificial conformity.
