# Luke — Notes

## Doctrinal notes

### Lk 1:3 {#nt.Luke.1.3}

Theophilus is again referred to in Acts 1:1, but nothing is known of him.

### Lk 1:5—2:52 {#nt.Luke.1.5}

The “Infancy Gospel,” as it is called, is written in a markedly Semitic style, which differs from that of the rest of the Gospel. It appears to be based on the reminiscences of Mary.

### Lk 1:30 {#nt.Luke.1.30}

The words of the angel are drawn from Messianic passages in the Old Testament.

### Lk 1:34 {#nt.Luke.1.34}

*How can this be*: alternate reading is *How will this be*.

### Lk 1:46–55 {#nt.Luke.1.55}

The Magnificat is based on the Song of Hannah (1 Sam 2:1–10), and other Old Testament passages that describe God’s favor toward Israel and especially toward the poor and lowly.

### Lk 1:69 {#nt.Luke.1.69}

*a horn of salvation*: i.e., a mighty savior.

### Lk 2:7 {#nt.Luke.2.7}

*first-born*: The term connotes possession of certain rights, privileges, and obligations; cf. Ex 13:1–2, 11–16. The word is used even in modern times without necessarily implying subsequent births.

### Lk 2:34 {#nt.Luke.2.34}

*for the fall*: i.e., in the sense that by rejecting his claims many would sin grievously.

### Lk 2:49 {#nt.Luke.2.49}

Jesus stresses the priority of his duty to his Father, which involves a high degree of independence of earthly ties.

### Lk 3:2 {#nt.Luke.3.2}

See note on Jn 18:13.

### Lk 3:7 {#nt.Luke.3.7}

*brood of vipers*: This epithet seems to have been directed mainly at the Pharisees; cf. Mt 3:7.

### Lk 3:23 {#nt.Luke.3.23}

This genealogy is more universalist than that of Matthew. Like Matthew, however, it gives the genealogy of Joseph, though Mary may well have been of the family of David.

### Lk 4:16–30 {#nt.Luke.4.16}

This account of the visit to the synagogue seems to be composed of the details of more than one visit. Luke is trying here to underline the contrast between Christ’s offer of salvation and the people’s refusal of it.

### Lk 6:20–49 {#nt.Luke.6.20}

Luke’s discourse is shorter than that of Matthew because it does not contain Matthew’s additional material collected from other occasions, or his details that would interest only Jews.

### Lk 7:28 {#nt.Luke.7.28}

John, by virtue of his office, belonged to the old dispensation, the time of preparation for the kingdom. In terms of spiritual status, even the humbler members of the kingdom were superior to him.

### Lk 7:47 {#nt.Luke.7.47}

The preceding parable suggests that she loved much because she had been forgiven much. Jesus now implies that her love is a sign rather than a cause of forgiveness, thus confirming the point of the parable.

### Lk 8:19 {#nt.Luke.8.19}

*brethren*: See note on Mt 12:46.

### Lk 8:39 {#nt.Luke.8.39}

There was no reason for secrecy (to avoid popular disturbance) in a non-Jewish area.

### Lk 9:51 {#nt.Luke.9.51}

Here begins the “Travel Narrative” of Luke, which continues up to the passion.

### Lk 9:51 {#nt.Luke.9.51b}

*received up*: i.e., into heaven; cf. 2 Kings 2:9–11; Acts 1:2, 11. The term here includes his passion, death, resurrection, and ascension.

### Lk 9:51, 53 {#nt.Luke.9.51c}

The Samaritans worshiped on Mount Gerizim, while orthodox Jews, of course, went to Jerusalem, and to Jerusalem only, for sacrifice.

### Lk 10:18 {#nt.Luke.10.18}

Jesus refers to the fall of the angels (cf. Rev 12:9), while he speaks of his conquest of the forces of evil.

### Lk 14:26 {#nt.Luke.14.26}

Christ’s disciples must be prepared to part from any one who prevents them from serving him.

### Lk 16:8 {#nt.Luke.16.8}

The master commended his foresight without approving what he actually did.

### Lk 17:20 {#nt.Luke.17.20}

At that time many persons were expecting to see the kingdom inaugurated with striking manifestations; cf. 19:11.

### Lk 19:41–44 {#nt.Luke.19.44}

These moving words spoken over the city are full of scriptural allusions. Moreover, the details given could apply as well to the siege of 587 B.C. as to that of A.D. 70. It is not safe, therefore, to argue from this passage that the fall of the city had already taken place when Luke wrote his Gospel.

### Lk 20:37 {#nt.Luke.20.37}

As elsewhere (1 Cor 15:13–19), survival after death is linked with the resurrection of the body.

### Lk 21:24 {#nt.Luke.21.24}

*the times of the Gentiles*: i.e., those during which the Gentiles will take the place of the unbelieving people of Israel. Evidently, therefore, the end of the world does not coincide with the fall of Jerusalem. St. Paul says that the Jews will be converted before the end (Rom 11:26).

### Lk 22:52 {#nt.Luke.22.52}

Matthew and Mark describe the arrest first, before Christ’s words. Luke and John both put his address to the soldiers and officials before the arrest, doubtless to stress his command over events.

### Lk 23:2 {#nt.Luke.23.2}

They purposely produce political charges, as these alone would interest Pilate.

### Lk 23:14 {#nt.Luke.23.14}

Luke, writing for Gentiles, makes it clear that Pilate wanted to release Jesus.

### Lk 23:31 {#nt.Luke.23.31}

One does not burn green wood. The meaning is that, if an innocent man is thus punished, what must the guilty (dry wood) expect?

### Lk 24:38 {#nt.Luke.24.38}

Luke stresses this episode for the benefit of his Greek readers, for whom the resurrection of the body was both impossible and absurd; cf. Acts 17:32.
