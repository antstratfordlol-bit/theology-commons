THE THIRD PASSION OF ST. DEMETRIUS THE MARTYR, BY SYMEON METAPHRASTES

### CHAPTER I.
The saint’s homeland, lineage, upbringing, dignity, preaching of Christ, arrest, and imprisonment.
1. Maximian Herculius was wielding the Roman scepter, breathing heavily against the Christians and driven by fury; a cloud of idolatrous error covered the entire earth under the heavens, even if those who suffered for Christ shone forth, scattered amidst it like bright stars. Among these, at that time, the admirable Demetrius was acknowledged—Demetrius, who was renowned for his faith in Christ and famous for the wonders he performed in Christ. He was brought forth by the great city of Thessalonica; as for his lineage, he was prominent, and intracing his descent back to the ancient Macedonians, he was most illustrious.
2. But he was not shining so much by the splendor of his ancestors as by his own ornament of virtue. For he surpassed those who were famous for nobility of soul just as much as he surpassed in bodily beauty those who prided themselves on their appearance. By nature, he was capable of grasping instruction and good at gathering together and blending his character toward a more gracious disposition; his very charming countenance also revealed the nobility of his soul. From his boyhood even until the age of puberty, he was superior to all his peers; and there was nothing among those things that are truly good in which he did not excel the others by a wide margin; it was clear from the beginning what a most delightful tree he was, and how abundant and good a fruit he was going to bring forth.
3. When he reached manhood, he demonstrated the strength of his nature; he practiced courage and vigor, and he labored at learning the arts of war, adorned with good character and that modesty and moderation which blossomed in him most sweetly; the very fact that his fame was noised abroad to all made the man well-known even to the emperor. The emperor, therefore, having observed Demetrius, possessing such a brilliant and noble character, immediately raised him to great glory, and appointed him proconsul of Greece, as also, at the same time, giving him consular
insignia. But he (for he was most capable of perceiving what was necessary, and most worthy of dignity in choosing what was better) paid little attention to these things, but was exceptionally occupied with those things that lead to the service of God. And for this reason, he despised all the pleasures here equally, but was possessed by love for this one thing alone: piety, and he thirsted for death for Christ’s sake, and the blessedness that proceeds from it.
4. He preached, therefore, not with any fear or hesitation, but most boldly and freely, the saving name of God; and there was nothing on his lips but Christ. The bread of life, which we know to be the Word of truth, he distributed religiously to all the needy who were in want of piety, bringing forward proofs from the divine Scriptures, and teaching that for man, who had fallen from that blessed way of life in paradise and had been reduced to the servitude of sin, the Word Himself, who is co-eternal with the Father—neither departing from His divinity, nor having taken flesh from a Virgin, and having voluntarily assumed the form of a servant—made him worthy of a more divine way of living, and granted him his former freedom. Teaching these and other things, and interpreting them, he explained them to them, and as it were provided opportunities and seeds for them to both become and be called sons of God, and to be deemed worthy of the life that is hidden in Christ.
5. From this, many who enjoyed that sweetest teaching of the martyr withdrew from the bitter darkness of deceit and attached themselves to the sweet light of truth. But he, conceiving a great love for Christ and insatiably desiring the salvation of men, considered it a very great loss if he were unable to rescue all from the hands of the enemy, and he did not think he was accomplishing anything at all. The matter, therefore, proceeded according to his desires, and fame sent the man everywhere, preaching the Trinity, not only to those in Greece and Thessalonica, but even to those who lived far away who heard of him. While he was in this state, and pouring out that beautiful stream of teaching, there came to him certain men whose task it was to search for Christians and to acquire for themselves the favor of the emperor through their betrayal.
6. At that time he had with him a man who was great in the war against the Sarmatians and Scythians, but who was known to be far greater still in his cruelty toward the pious. When the emperor straightway heard these things, he was affected in every way at once: he was struck with astonishment, grief, fear, and anger. He was struck by the novelty of what he heard; he grieved for the loss of such a man; he feared that, being such as he was, he might draw others to his religion; and he was angered because he had shown no regard for the honor bestowed upon him. But since he could not believe his ears entirely—being, as he was, unable to bear the loss of such a man (for what one is unwilling to accept, one cannot easily believe)—he wished to take his eyes also as witnesses of what he had heard. He therefore orders the man to be brought before him.
7. But he, standing before him, neither shuddered at the magnitude of the imperial power, nor did he shrink from the expected torments, nor did he fear the savagery of the judge; but, considering this cruelty a material for showing more openly his love for God, he went straight into the contest like a lion, looking and speaking with nothing soft or effeminate, but by his own words rather confirming what the tyrant had previously heard of him: that he was indeed a soldier of Christ and a worshiper of Him alone. For he was so far from honoring the gods called upon and worshiped by them, that he openly mocked and loathed them; and that as for the torments they threatened, he would only suffer and grieve when he saw his torturers inflicting them more mercifully or sparingly than he desired. "But your honors, O king," he said, C "and the good things you give to those who obey you, how could they entice one who even despises and dishonors that very kingship of yours, with its riches and treasures—that object of envy—no more than a fine dust? And if you should even bring death upon me, then know that you are doing for me that which is most to be desired, as you would be sending me on to true life and to Christ, whom I desire more than life itself."
8. When Maximian heard these words, spoken with a steadfast spirit, a noble bearing, and a free tongue, he immediately deferred the torment, acting rather with cunning than with kindness. For he wished to instill repentance in him and to allow the passage of time to abate his vehement constancy; and for this reason, he ordered for the present that Demetrius be taken into custody in the vaults of the furnaces of the nearby public bath. Maximian, having entered the theater, which he was preparing at that very hour, and having gathered the athletes and assembled the people, and having arranged the contests, sat himself upon the lofty judgment seat.
