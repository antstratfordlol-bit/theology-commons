PROLOGUS.
I. Quemadmodum vernans pratum, variis distinctum floribus, dispositarum apte inter se areolarum fragrantia inspicere volentes oblectat, locumque commutantes e loco, eodem unde abierant cogit recurrere, aut saltem oculos sponte obliquatos reflectere, eumdem plane in modum Christi Ecclesia, eorum, qui a sæculo sunt sanctorum multiformis exempli compta ornatu, ad divinæ laudis confessionem audientes impellit, iique quibus unum ex alio festum indicitur, exsultabundi incitantur suum cuique dierum mutatis appellationum vicibus honorem deferendum publicare. Ad hoc enim et ii, qui cæteris magis sunt religiosi, concurrunt, satagentes suam adaugere virtutem et desides alias atque insensati conversationis suæ damnantes ignaviam, animum coguntur inducere ad pietatem. Volenti igitur, dilectissimi, sanctum prædicare virum, attentas præbete aures, atque hodiernæ diei de eo narrationem prompte sapienterque suscipite. Hic autem quis? ille, ille inter ascetas celebratus Theophanes, magnus orthodoxæ defensor religionis, veræ propugnator doctrinæ; qui sævire volentibus alacriter se tradidit, et regnantis impietatem facunda redarguit eruditione; qui a prima pueritia in vita, opere, ac sermone excultus, ea quæ vulgo videntur suavia, omnino despexit, continentiam coluit in conjugio, et temporales divitias dispartiens pauperibus, justitiam, ut ait inter prophetas Psalmista, manentem in sæculum sæculi sibi thesaurizavit.

II. Cum igitur memoria justi sit recolenda cum  laudibus 2, ne meæ quæso imputetis præcipitantie laudibus efferentis eum, cujus necdum vitam enarravi. Et vero congruum fuerat eam præmittere : sed quoniam sapientibus sanctisque viris (1) nonnullis adnitentibus distincte expositam habemus hujus beati ab ipsis usque natalibus conversionem, propensionemque ad Dei cultum : ipsam quoque ejus in monastica vita exercitationem conversationemque purissimam, necnon deficientium inperfectionum, et rectæ fidei ostentationem atque in molestiis perferendis constantiam, quibus vita ejus magis quam auro, margaritis, lapidibus pretiosis ornatur, equidem ut iis adderem aliquid, minime adduci potui : sed quæ dispersa fuerant, ex illis ipsis narrationibus in unam, ut lapidarii solent,  compagem colligenda putavi : sciens quod qui impossibilia facienda suscipit, omnem agendi rationem indecore pervertat. Compendiosa igitur narratione usus rudem duntaxat quamdam recte factorum ejus complexionem concinnavi.

III. Beatus iste, Patres Fratresque charissimi, ab Christianæ religionis signaculo nomen adeptus, nobilium et dignitatibus conspicuorum parentum filius fuit, quique Constantini Copronymi tempore vitam ex recte fidei regulis, occulte tamen, instituebant. Et vero ex tali suæ nativitatis principio debebat refulgere hæc stella, quæ a Deo in carne manifestati mysterii congruum habitura nomen  erat : neque enim purissima hæc margarita hodie proponeretur nobis, si in illa perversarum opinionum caligine genitores ejus propalam pietatem fuissent aversati. Cum autem iis unigenitus esset, a puero divinas edoctus Litteras atque ad totius Scripturæ initiatus cognitionem, ne profana quidem sapientia deficiebat : sed quidquid in ea esset pulcherrimum colligens, id infra mentis suæ thesaurum abdidit : quod vero fabulosum erat, longe illud valere jubens, prout conveniebat, despexit. Eo admodum juvene adhuc et necdum pubertatis annos ingresso, parentes ejus hinc ad Dominum emigrarunt, et solus ipse inter copiosas remansit divitias ; superna forsitan id ita disponente Providentia, ne eorum amor obex ei fieret recta ad Deum via perrecturo. V. Præsens quidem vita, conjux charissima, brevis est, ut notum omnibus, et magna plerumque futuri incertitudine tenetur; judicium autem servandum manet eos qui in hac vita bonis et voluptatibus affluunt. Ideo, quamvis usus minime rejiciatur a Deo, ipsæ tamen, quæ eum comitantur sæculi curæ, plenam divino spiritu mentem evacuant alienantque, nec sinunt puris oculis intueri futura. Novimus Lazarum in Evangeliis per eas, quas hic toleravit miserias, in Abrahæ sinum fuisse delatum; et in gehenna ardentem divitem ne hoc quidem obtinere potuisse, ut ad se dimitteretur Lazarus : cui, etiam aquæ gutta negaretur, illud funestum insonuit : « Recepisti bona in vita tua ». Neque vero ex evangelicis beatitudinibus illa continet bonorum promissionem divitibus factam; sed flentibus nunc, quia istic ridebunt, aut pauperibus spiritu, aut persecutionem sustinentibus propter justitiam, vel contumelia propter Christum affectis. Atque ut uno semel verbo absolvam : « Augusta porta et arcta est via, quæ ducit ad vitam : lata vero et spatiosa, quæ ducit ad perditionem », adeo ut impossibile omnino sit nobis tribulationem evadere. Si namque in hac vita jucunde vixerimus, in futura nos tribulatio excipiet. Quapropter, si placet, charissima, corporibus ad modicum tempus conjuncti propter patris tui insolentem ferociam, subducemus nos ipsos salebrosæ huic atque inquinatæ conversioni, vitamque monasticam amplectemur, ut æterna requie perfruamur.

Atqui vitæ finis, ut ipsemet numerabiles laquei. Quod si castitatem clam colentes tum finem perducendum a Deo.
VII. Obstupuit ad tam generosam puellæ mentem beatissimus juvenis, gratioseque respondens : Quod utile fuerit, inquit, manifestabit nobis Deus. Ergo totam egere in precibus noctem, Deum invocantes, ut sibi eam in rem consulere atque prospicere dignaretur. Circa auroram vero contigit, ut obdormiscerent ambo et unum idemque eo tempore somnium viderent. Viderunt autem quemdam eximio fulgentem decore, placida ad eos voce utens : Dominus, inquit, propositum animi vestri suscipiens, misit me, ut signarem vos ad hoc, ut intactos vos integrosque sibi offerretis. Et continuo per totum corpus eos consignans ex oculis evanuit intuentium : qui cum evigilassent, et quæ viderant sibi invicem absque ulla diversitate narrassent; date Deo gloria, qui tam facile invenitur quærentibus se, de cœtero specie quidem ut conjuges, reapse ut germani inter sese vixere. O rem miram! Novit Dominus qui sunt ejus 5, et quoniam solum Deum exquisierunt, continuo auxiliator atque adjutor pro desiderio ipsis inventus est. Quid hoc inferius eo miraculo, quod circa tres pueros in fornace gestum admiramur 6? His igitur divino nutu ad eum modum constitutis, cum magnos brevi thesauros pauperibus latenter divisent, aut potius ipsis sibi per eorum manus condidissent, latere dintius puellae patrem non potuere, severum utique, immitem, inhumanum, atque ab omni commiserationis alienum affectu. Cognoscens ergo infelix eam, quam novi nupti optimam instituerant, mercandi viam, et quod cognoverat non pietati imputans, sed infelicitati maximae, in palatium ad imperatorem progressus; ita fatur: O me calamitosum, imperator, propter generi mei inertiam! opes enim suas pessimis dilapidans indis, ne filiae quidem meae substantia abstinuit, et ejus maximam jam partem disperdidit: sed neque liberorum ex eo procreatione infelix filia mea digna invenitur; ideoque per omnia miserum eum atque ignavum censeo, quem omine plane infausto cognovit. Sed si majestas tua annuat, ut senectas mea ab ejusmodi paululum respiret et relevetur inertia, procul eum jubebis amandari, publicis alicubi negotiis vacaturum, ne demum penitus spaliatis filiola mea annumeretur. Equidem unde edoctus sit insanam illam vivendi normam, intelligere nequeo, qui instar aeneae statuae immobilis permanet ad desideria voluptatum; quodque etiam molestius accidit, velut usuvenit saepe, quidquid ab obvio quovis fuerit postulatus facillime praebens, ut mente capti facere saepe solent, suis quotidie privatur opibus, in eos maxime liberalis, qui pauperiem simulant. Profecto ne figlinorum quidem ei copia suppetet, nisi quantocius ab ea qua cingitur inopia factus prudentior, idoneus fiat rebus suis retinendis.
IX. Ergo accersitus continuo ad imperatorem beatus, ablegatur ab eo in partes Cyzicenas, publicorum quorundam negotiorum gratia: erat enim regiae dignitatis in aula, non tamen eorum, qui principem locum apud eum obtingebant, sed minister equorum regiorum elegantiorum (3*) inter eos, quos stratores (4) consuevit ordo (5) nominare. X. Recognoscens vero diligenter locum, atque ad exercitationes spiritus peridoneum utilemque invenisse gavisus, in partem sese oraturus recepit, Deum deprecans, ut, quod expediens esset id sibi revelaret. Diuturna autem multarum horarum genuflexione deficiens cum paululum resedisset, velut in exstasi factus est, oculisque vigilantibus vidit eum, qui prius in thalamo sibi apparuerat, splendidissimum virum; qui manus extentæ digito locum designans : Tuus quidem, inquit, socer, ejusdemque eo mentis imperator intra exiguum tempus ex hac vita discedent : tunc autem bonis tributis, huc adveniens acceptam Deo vitam institues. XI. Neque enim multum effluxit temporis, quin finem uterque vivendi faciens, plenam ipsis relinqueret libertatem, suis pro arbitrio rebus utendi; quas continuo inter pauperes diviserunt, famulis etiam domesticis libertate donatis : ipsi vero pannoso induti habitu sibi invicem valedixere; beato viro mulierem piam confirmante monitis salutaribus, ut divina observaret mandata, spiritualem erga proximum exerceret charitatem, orationi sinceræ atque imperturbatæ vacaret pro viribus, vitam irreprehensibilem, summanique teneret humilitatem, eique in recessu dicente : « Salvans salva animam tuam. » Ita illi convictu sunt ab invicem separati : qualem autem vitam duxerit pientissima illa mulier norunt omnes, quotquot

Sufficiebant autem ei vilis in lectum storea, cilicium in operimentum et lapis pro pulvinari. Nulla fuit in amictu ejus ostentatio, nulla mollities, nec fuit in eo fullonibus laborandum; non equum, non mulum unquam ad summ possedit usum: coquorum artificia in congrui sibi victus apparatu nescivit, nec odorato vino palatum irrigavit suum; sed cibus ac potus ei durissimus panis et aqua permodica fuit.

II. Since, therefore, the memory of the just is to be celebrated with praises, do not, I pray, impute to me rashness for praising one whose life I have not yet narrated. And in truth, it would be appropriate to set it first; but since we have a clearly expounded account of this blessed man—by some wise and holy men who labored at it—from his very birth, his conversion, and his inclination toward the worship of God, and likewise his exercise and most pure conversation in the monastic life, indeed also his lack of imperfections, his manifestation of the true faith, and his constancy in enduring hardships, by which his life is adorned more than with gold, pearls, and precious stones, I could not in the least be induced to add anything to these. But I thought it right to collect what had been scattered, from those very narrations into one structure, as stone-cutters are accustomed to do; knowing that he who undertakes to do the impossible perverts every method of acting in an unseemly way. Using therefore a concise narrative, I have put together a rough, preliminary summary of his virtuous deeds.
CAPUT I. Nativity, marriage, and life in the world
III. This blessed man, most dear Fathers and brethren, having received his name from the seal of the Christian religion, was the son of noble parents, notable for their dignities, who, during the time of Constantine Copronymus, lived their lives according to the rules of the true faith, though secretly. And indeed, from such a beginning of his birth, this star, which was to have a name congruous to the mystery of God manifested in the flesh, ought to have shone forth; for this most pure pearl would not be set before us today if his parents had openly turned away from piety in that darkness of perverse opinions. Since, however, he was an only child to them, he was instructed from childhood in the divine Letters and, being initiated into the knowledge of the entire Scripture, he did not lack even secular wisdom. Whatever was most beautiful in it he collected and stored within the treasury of his mind; but what was fabulous he ordered to be far removed, and rejected it as was fitting. When the blessed one was still very young and had not yet entered the years of puberty, his parents emigrated from here to the Lord, and he himself remained alone amidst abundant wealth—perhaps with the celestial Providence disposing it so, that their love might not become an obstacle to him as he was about to travel the straight path to God.
IV. After the sacrilegious Constantine had shamefully breathed out his foul spirit, Leo, called by his maternal surname Chazar, received the empire. During the time of his reign, there was a certain patrician
of the same name as him, quite barbarian and beastly in his manner, and of one mind with and a supporter of the nesting of heresy; who, on account of such impiety, was a daily table-companion and intimate friend to the emperor. And so, having learned of the blessed one's wealth, by means of an imperial decree he used his cooperation and tied his own daughter to Theophanes; which Theophanes, perhaps even by force and with difficulty, yielding on account of the fear of the ruler, prepared the necessities for the bridal chamber. When, therefore, the auspicious events were completed, and evening had fallen, they brought the pious young man and the glorious maiden, alone, into a delightful small room, as it might be convenient for them for the manner of pleasure. And when silence had occurred, and all had fallen asleep, and the deep night had spread itself out, the blessed one turned and said these things to the girl:
V. On the Shortness of the Present Life.
The present life, O wife, is short, as is evident to all, and it is governed largely by the uncertainty of the future; and an impartial judgment awaits those who revel in ease in this life. Therefore, even marriage is not rejected by God; but the cares of this life, altering the God-inspired mind, do not allow one to gaze with purity upon the future. We know also the Lazarus in the Gospels, who through the constraint of this world obtained the bosom of Abraham, and the rich man burning in Gehenna, who was not worthy even of the arrival of Lazarus to him, and was deprived of a drop of water, and was accused with, "You received your good things in your life" (Luc. xvi, 25). And the Beatitude does not promise good things to those who abound in wealth, but to those who weep now, because they shall laugh there; to those who are poor in spirit, to those who are persecuted for righteousness, to those who are insulted for Christ; and once and simply, the way that leads to life is narrow and afflicted, but wide and spacious is the way that leads to destruction (Matth. vii, 14). There is, then, no escaping without tribulation; for if we live joyfully in this life, tribulation will await us in the future. But if you wish, O wife, having shared for a little while in nature on account of your father's stubbornness, let us withdraw ourselves from this muddy and base way of life, and let us choose a solitary life, so that we may obtain eternal enjoyment.
VI. The Response of the Wife.
What then, O pious woman? Did she refuse at these things? Did she say things that were not proper? "Why have you dimmed the beauty of my youth? Why have you shut off my fortune? Why have you changed my joy into lamentation? If you had thus decided to consult for your own salvation by that reasoning which has been spoken, you were not the only young man among the nobles; you ought, therefore, before marriage, to have renounced the betrothal, and not to wither my beauty, which is freshly blooming, like a faded
fruit. Just so, the noble woman, strengthened by the excellent exhortation of her spouse, readily agreed, and answering, she said: "I am not uninstructed, most sweet husband, in the divine commands; for the Savior Himself clearly cries out in the Gospels: 'Whoever does not leave father, and mother, and wife, and children, and brothers, and sisters, and houses, and fields, and take up his cross, and follow Me, is not worthy of Me' (Luc. xiv, 26). Let us, therefore, if you wish, present ourselves pure and unspotted to the heavenly Bridegroom, Christ, keeping our outer man also immaculate. For what profit will come to us if, as you say, we were to enjoy for a short time an embrace? Who shall guarantee that we will not, in that short time, beget offspring that will become an impediment to us? If it were male, a portion of our substance and affection would have to be cut off; if female, then the evil is twofold, for we would need to wait for the age suitable for marriage and bestow other care upon her. And the end of life, as you said before, is uncertain, and the snares of this life are innumerable. So, if we live in secret chastity, I am certain that God will bring our desire to the desired end."
VII. The most blessed youth was astonished at the maiden’s most noble resolve, and answering graciously, he said: "The Lord will show us what is profitable." Having spent the whole night in prayer, calling upon God to be the provider and guardian for their interest in this matter, it happened that towards dawn both fell asleep and saw one and the same dream. For they saw someone shining with extraordinary beauty, who with a gentle voice said to them: "The Lord God, having accepted your resolve, has sent me to seal you, that you may present yourselves to Him whole and blameless." And having sealed them over their entire body, he vanished from the sight of the beholders. When they awoke and recounted the vision to each other word for word, giving glory to God, who is so easily found by those seeking Him, from then on they lived in appearance as spouses, but in reality as brother and sister. O wonderful deed! "The Lord knows who are His" (Num. xvi, 5), and since they sought only God, He was immediately found an ally and helper for their desire. What is this, inferior to that miracle which we admire concerning the three youths in the furnace (Dan. iii, 50)? There, indeed, the angel, changing the burning breeze of the fire into a wind blowing as dew, saved the youths unharmed; here, similarly, the angel, transforming the swift and fiery ardor of pleasure into the dew of temperance, kept the young couple unharmed, and taught fire to be joined with straw without damage.

For after dissipating his own wealth in the worst of ways, he did not even spare the possessions of my daughter; for he has already made away with the greatest part of them, and my pitiable daughter is not even considered worthy of offspring; and I think it is because he is in every way a wretched and inactive man. But if your Majesty commands," he says, "that my old age might find a little relief and respite from such inactivity, you will command that he be sent away to public duties elsewhere, so that my little daughter might not at last be counted among the utterly destitute. Like some bronze statue, he remains unmoved before the choice of pleasant things, and what is more grievous, as often happens to those who are deranged in mind, whatever he is asked for, he most easily provides from what he has, and is robbed daily of his own means, especially by those who falsely pretend to be in poverty. And he will not even hold on to his potsherds, unless by quickly being seized by poverty himself, he becomes capable of maintaining his own affairs."
IX. And so, the blessed one having been quickly summoned by the emperor, he is sent by him to the region of Cyzicus for the administration of certain public affairs; for he was indeed of royal status, not of those in the highest roles, but a minister of the richly adorned royal horses, whom it is customary for the order to call *stratores*. When this command had been given to the blessed one, and joy not of a common sort had suffused him, as if another opportunity for his departure from the world had been arranged by God, he made ready with speed the necessities for the journey; and having feigned a pretext of illness, he sent his servants on ahead by road, while he himself, boarding a swift boat, reached the mouth of the great river.
and, approaching the region which is there, he continued his journey with a calm voyage, observing the craggy shores of the mountain which is in Sigriane. By a certain divine providence, he came upon a deep, precipitous grove, wooded and full of caves; departing from the ship at that place, he went up alone, compelled by the weariness of the journey, having instructed the others to remain quietly in the boat.
X. Having carefully reconnoitered the place, and rejoicing that he had found it suitable and useful for spiritual exercises, he withdrew to a secluded spot to pray, beseeching God that He might reveal to him what was expedient. Being exhausted from the genuflection which he had maintained for many hours, when he had sat down for a little while, he was as if in an ecstasy, and he saw with waking eyes the man who had appeared to him in the chamber, a most radiant man; he, with a finger of his extended hand C pointing out the place, said to him: "Your father-in-law, and the emperor of the same mind as him, will depart from this life in a few days; then, having freely distributed your goods to the poor, with no one to oppose you, you shall come here and establish a life pleasing to God." The blessed man, therefore, understanding from this apparition the things that contributed to his benefit, and having reached the parts of Cyzicus, and having finished the task enjoined upon him as quickly as possible, he held a straight course for Constantinople. And having rendered to the emperor a satisfactory account of the matters he had been asked about, and having been honored by the emperor with a higher rank (namely, to the list of the spatharioi), he lived there for the rest of the time, awaiting the realization of the vision, which also came to pass.

### Chapter II.
The monastic life of Theophanes and his struggles for the veneration of the holy icons
XI. For not much time passed before both of them, having brought their life to a close—having already, by that time, distributed their possessions to the poor without fear, and having granted freedom to their domestic servants—clothed themselves in rags and bade each other farewell; the blessed man confirmed the pious woman with salutary admonitions, that she should keep the divine commandments, exercise spiritual charity toward her neighbor, devote herself to sincere and undisturbed prayer with all her might, and maintain an irreproachable life and supreme humility; and as he departed, he said to her: "In saving others, save your own soul." Thus they were separated from one another in their way of life. But all they who inhabit the region of the Bithynians know what kind of life that most pious woman led, how she sold herself voluntarily, and took up a life of servitude [index: entries preserved]
Having been recognized by some, she fled. But as for her struggles in Prinkipo and Calonymus, who could even record them in writing, even if he were deemed worthy of grace and divine powers? Part of her achievements are recorded for the benefit of those who chance upon it, in the writing composed concerning her and her blessed husband by the most holy Patriarch Methodius.
XII. But what of the truly blessed Theophanes? He boarded a ship, reached the shores of Sigriane, and, having disembarked, proceeded on foot to that wooded precipice. There, having subdued his body for many years and having been invested with the monastic habit and rule by Christopher, that brilliant star of his age and region, he himself also became famous thenceforth; for a city set upon a hill cannot be hid. And as those who desired the angelic life flowed to him day after day under his instruction, he converted that wilderness into a monastery, which even to this day, blooming with divine virtues, is shown and shall be shown. Contending in it spiritually, he appeared as a genuine initiate of the Trinity and became a spectator of the greatest visions. A cheap mat sufficed him for a bed, a hair-shirt for a covering, and a stone for his pillow. He did not boast of soft raiment for his attire, nor did any fuller toil over his garments, nor did any horse or mule serve his needs, nor did the artifices of cooks provide him with necessities, nor did fragrant wine lubricate his throat; rather, his food and drink were the coarsest bread and a little water.
XIII. When the emperor had ended his life, and Irene, with her son Constantine, was piously governing the kingdom, following the death of Paul, who had become a monk after having been patriarch, Tarasius, that most holy luminary of orthodoxy, was proposed as High Priest of Constantinople by the empress, the entire senate, and all those distinguished for the accuracy of their orthodox doctrines, and an ecumenical synod was convened.
confirming the holy synods that had preceded it, and anathematizing Constantine and Nicetas, the heresiarchs and fraudulent patriarchs, enrolling the most holy patriarch Germanus in the diptychs, and dissolving the vain error of the Iconoclasts as if it were a spider's web. Fathers and teachers from every part of the earth were gathered together, as well as the vicars of the apostolic sees and the leaders of the monks; I mean Plato, that most brightly shining star from those of Saccudion, Nicetas and Nicephorus, the ever-memorable Fathers from the monastery of Medicius, and the aforementioned Christophorus from the Small Field. Then, indeed then, like a transparent star, at the invitation of this spiritual festival, the blessed Theophanes also arrived at the synod, under compulsion, it is true, yet he came; for he was one who always dearly longed for and pursued stillness, and held the clamor of the multitude in aversion. XIV. All of them, then, were led, being served by horses, mules, slaves, and carriages; but the
holy Father, like a true father, was with his hair-shirt and his pastoral staff. When the venerable synod had taken its seat, although the blessed one was seen in a humble guise, he shone with piety above all others, and they did not disregard him, but invited him to deliver a public discourse on the dogma; for they knew well that he was a worthy laborer of the Spirit. He, having spoken at length in few words, and by natural, scriptural, and dogmatic proofs having refuted and silenced the arrogant and blasphemous Iconoclasts, restored to the Church her pristine beauty. O miracle! What can be said that is more magnificent than this? For the Holy Spirit breathes where He wills, and as much as He wills. These things having been thus accomplished, and the Fathers having returned to their own places, the blessed one repaired again to the heaven-mimicking monastery which he had newly built. For just as in heaven the sun shines with the stars, illuminating the whole universe with the light of its radiance, in the same way the blessed one, on earth, together with the monks and fellow-ascetics chosen by the Spirit for him, illuminating the inhabited world, brightened it with the rays of his angelic way of life.
XV. Therefore, the ray of orthodoxy shone forth in the borders. But after many years had passed, there arose, by the permission of God, Leo, of the same name as the former, who, immediately upon his proclamation as emperor, having cast down the holy icons, extinguished the beauty of the Church, and like some wild boar, ravaged the assembly of the orthodox, and it was appropriate for them to say: "O God, the nations have come into your inheritance," and what follows; for he subjected the Fathers, the champions and defenders of the orthodox faith, to many tortures and afflictions, and destroyed them miserably; among whom was also Theodore, the most holy abbot of Studiou, who, having refuted his foolish opinion, was, after being tormented for a time, sent into exile. Having acted with a deceitful mind, he sent fawning letters to the blessed Theophanes, asking him to come to him and agree with his wickedness. But the blessed one replied in these written terms:
XVI. Know, O emperor, the one who granted you the empire, through whom kings reign and tyrants rule the earth: know, I say, how God, being uncircumscribed, yet deigned to be circumscribed by assuming our nature, having become like us in all things except that he had no sin; how he raised the dead, and gave sight to the blind, and cleansed lepers, and the rest of his miracles in order; how he willingly endured the passion by the Jewish madness, and having risen on the third day, ascended into the heavens, of [the] path towards the Father
s sitting at his right hand. Since the book of the Gospels teaches us this, we embrace it, and thereby render honor to Christ; and if, in embracing it, we are not subject to accusation, what accusation is there against us for not failing to honor and kiss the iconic representation of that same explanation, through which those who are instructed from among the barbarians are easily taught the truth? How many of those who do not know letters, seeing the miracles before their very eyes as if they were present, glorify the Christ who suffered for our sake, whose salvation you have begrudged? What council has ever pronounced this shameful doctrine? Did not Christ himself send his own image, made without hands, to Abgar? Did not the Apostle Luke, having documented it, hand down to us the image of the form of the Theotokos? And what will you say about the teachings handed down by the Fathers? For indeed, Basil, the initiate of ineffable mysteries, says: "The honor paid to the image passes to the prototype." And the golden-mouthed John also says: "I have also loved the painting cast in wax." Moreover, Cyril, the lyre of the Spirit, also says this: "I have often seen an image of the passion on a painting, and I have not passed by the sight without tears," and so on. If, therefore, the preceding six holy councils did not condemn the veneration of the holy images relative to their prototypes, are you become wiser than they? It is your duty, emperor, to war against the enemy; let the doctrine of the Church be the concern of the Fathers.

XVIII. On the following day, the emperor ordered that the blessed Theophanes also be brought before him. The miserable man, now despairing of such an undertaking, sent forth a decree against the blessed man, to send him into exile on the island of Samothrace, which is situated in the parts of the sea belonging to Maroneia, a place desolate and grievous to all who wish to approach it. In it, the blessed man, pressed by hunger, thirst, and imprisonment, and having lived for some years, passed on from this life, having transferred to a better and more divine glory, having kept his clay-born body and his soul unharmed. By whose blessed prayers, O beloved listeners, may we be strengthened, sending up glory and thanksgiving to Christ our most kind God together with the Father who is without beginning, and the life-giving Spirit, now and always, and unto all the infinite and endless ages of ages.
