Ado archiepiscopus Viennensis, in libello De festivitatibus sanctorum apostolorum, hoc elogium edidit : « Natalis beatorum apostolorum Philippi et Jacobi : ex quibus Philippus, cum pene Scythiam convertisset, diaconibus, presbyteris et episcopis ibi constitutis, reversus est ad Asiam, ubi continua prædicatione aliquot annos insistens, multitudinem populorum Christo, laboribus piis semper inserviens, lucratus est : quique apud Hierapolim Asiæ civitatem dormivit cum patribus suis, beato fine sepultus. » Hæc Ado ; quibus addit Notkerus : « Exstincta hæresi Ebionitarum, qui Christum Jesum ante Incarnationis tempus fuisse negabant. » In hodierno Martyrologio Romano adduntur nonnulla de ejus martyrio his verbis : « Natalis beatorum apostolorum Philippi et Jacobi, ex quibus Philippus, postquam omnem fere Scythiam ad fidem Christi convertisset, apud Hierapolim Asiæ civitatem, cruci affixus, et lapidibus obrutus, glorioso fine quievit. » Hæc ibi, quibus similia in suo Martyrologio tradit Galesinius. At Maurolycus aliquanto plura interserens, « Natale, inquit, beatorum apostolorum Philippi et Jacobi, ex quibus Philippus, postquam Scythiam ad fidem Christi convertit, ubi dejecto Martis simulacro erexerat crucem, et venenosum draconem exterminarat; apud Hierapolim civitatem Asiæ glorioso fine quievit : sive ab Ebionitis cruci affixus et lapidatus occubuit : juxta quem duæ filiæ ejus virgines tumulatæ sunt; tertia Ephesi jacet. » Hæc Maurolycus : præter quæ Constantinus Felicius nonnulla miscet ex Actis Græcis, de quibus mox agemus. 3. Nicolai prope Cusam : neque ab his diversa sunt, quæ habuit auctor historiæ de Translatione brachii S. Philippi Florentiam. Legendaria si libuisset excutere, plura possemus indicare exempla. III. Veniens igitur in Galilæam Jesus, et magnum hunc Philippum reperiens, sequi se jubet. Hic vero ut vocantem audiit, statim venit in memoriam eorum quæ a puero perceperat collegeratque de Christo ipsumque esse cognovit, quem libri Mosaici venturum demonstrabant. Exinde vocanti se Magistro adhæsit, et paulatim proficiens in virtute, discipulorum præcipuis fuit annumeratus. Quod autem adeptus erat bonum aliis communicare voluit; illudque haud abscondens, primo cui occurrit Nathanaeli (erat hic ei amicus et familiaris a longo tempore) præsentiam venientis, non ut futuram, sed ut jam existentem annuntiavit, dicens: Non jam amplius in spe consistit Israel salus: sed quem propheticæ linguæ in Spiritu prædixerunt Salvatorem, extremo in tempore suscitandum, ipse nunc adest. Hunc ergo cum invenerimus Jesum a Nazareth, neque possimus esse increduli excellentiæ miraculorum ejus, aliis omnibus longum valere jussis conversationem illius suscipiamus.
IV. His dictis post se Nathanaelem trahens, licet ægre persuasum, et num ex Nazareth boni aliquid esse posset percontantem, a cura Israelis abducit, Christoque obsecuturum stitit, atque hoc primum sinceræ suæ mentis argumentum præbuit. Nathanael vero integra cum fide assistens, liberrime exclamavit: Rabbi, tu es Rex Israel. Quem ut sensit Christus ex animi incommutabilis sententia sic loqui, manifestat ei quod magnorum conscius esset futurus secretorum et regni Dei speculator. Quid enim ait? «Amen, inquit, dico vobis, videbitis cœlum apertum, et angelos Dei ascendentes et descendentes super Filium hominis¹. » Ex illo ergo tempore sanctissimus Philippus, sacris mysteriis aurem accommodans, toto in illis erat animio, nullique alteri curæ eum advertens, lumine divinioris cognitionis in mente purgabatur; ac primam exuens ignorantiam, renovabatur quoad hominem interiorem. Progrediente vero ætate perfectiorem erga Christum charitatem assumens, ejus vicissim æqualem erga se recipiebat amorem, ut tanquam filius reputaretur, et loco hæredis esset, atque in adventu Spiritus sancti princeps super omnem terram constitueretur.
V. Cum autem instaret tempus salutiferæ Passionis, qua passibili naturæ comparanda impassibilitas erat, aderat ipse continue Christo. Contigit vero tunc gentilium legatos aliquos mitti Hierosolymam

COMMENTARY ON THE HOLY APOSTLE PHILIP
(From the Greek Vatican MS. numbered 1798. Translated by Daniel Papebroch.)
1. God, the Word of God, always coexisting with the Father, and being timeless, chose also to become like us at a certain time and to come under time, because of the fall of our nature; hence also he is circumscribed in the flesh by a Virgin, and deifies by his communion that which he had assumed. But since His coming in the flesh was to work salvation for all, it was fitting that He should also have spectators and participants in this divine and ineffable dispensation, through whom He might copiously minister the heavenly calling to our race. After, therefore, the preaching of John had passed through the entire region bordering the Jordan, and by the working of the Spirit of grace many were being baptized by him (when even my Christ received baptism), this same incomprehensible Word of God left the urban crowds (for He was indeed seen to dwell in Jerusalem) and journeyed to Galilee.
2. Here the admirable Philip was dwelling, being from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter, but present at that time in Galilee; and he, even from his earliest age, was immediately given over by his parents to the liberal arts; being of good disposition and apt for any praiseworthy knowledge, he immediately perused the Mosaic books and imbibed whatever was predicted concerning Christ, how, namely, He was to come in the last days and be about to impart salvation to all: and
for it was not permitted to those who were then teaching to impart any other doctrine to their students, unless they had first read through the teachings of Moses.
III. Jesus, therefore, coming into Galilee and finding this great Philip, invited him to follow Him. As soon as he heard Him calling, he immediately brought to mind those things which he had perceived and gathered from his boyhood concerning Christ, and he recognized Him to be the very one whom the Mosaic books indicated would come later. From then on, he clung to the Master who had called him, and, advancing little by little in virtue, he was numbered among the chief of the disciples. And because he wished to share with others the good which he had obtained, he did not hide it, but, meeting first with Nathanael—who happened to be his friend and companion from long ago—he proclaimed the presence of Him who was coming, not as still being in the future, but as already present. He said, "The salvation of Israel no longer consists in hope alone; but He whom the prophetic tongues in the Spirit predicted as the Savior to be raised up in the last days, He is now present. Having ourselves found this Jesus of Nazareth, and being unable to be incredulous regarding the excellence of His miracles, we have bidden long farewell to all others and have chosen the way of life with Him."
IV. By these words he won over Nathanael, though he was reluctant at the matter and questioned whether any good could come from Nazareth, and he drew him away from the concerns of Israel and presented him as one who would obey Christ—and this was the first proof he gave of his incomparable goodwill. Nathanael, standing firm with perfect faith, cried out very freely, "Rabbi, you are the King of Israel." As soon as Christ perceived that he spoke from an immutable conviction of mind, He revealed to him that he would be conscious of greater mysteries and a spectator of the kingdom of God. For what does He say? "Amen, I say to you, you shall see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending upon the Son of man." From that time forward, therefore, the most holy Philip, lending his ear to the sacred mysteries, was entirely occupied with them in his mind, and, turning his attention to no other concern, he was purified in his intellect by the light of diviner knowledge; and, putting off his former ignorance, he was renewed as to his inner man. As he came to adulthood, he received a more perfect love toward Christ, and in turn received the same love from Him, so that he was accounted as a son and occupied the place of an heir, and at the arrival of the Holy Spirit he was ordained a prince over all the earth.
V. When, moreover, the time of the saving Passion was drawing near, through which impassibility was gained for the passible nature—and he himself continued to be with Christ without hesitation—it happened at that time that some envoys of the Gentiles were sent to Jerusalem
for the sight of the feast. These men, having been struck in mind by the wonderful things reported about Jesus (for great things were already being reported about Him, even to them, beyond belief—how He would raise Lazarus from the dead, and how He was ushered along by the whole crowd with favorable cries, and would perform ten thousand other miracles), became possessed of a longing to come to Him by means of an introduction. They were overcome, therefore, by this noble longing, as they heard of my Jesus; and approaching Philip, they explain the purpose of their arrival. He, having shared the matter concerning them with the first-called Andrew, reports their arrival to Jesus along with him; and He signifies to them the matters concerning the Passion and His glory by the word, saying: «Unless the grain of wheat falling into the earth dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit.» B VI. These, then, Christ set forth to them. But when, after the Passion and the Resurrection, He frequently manifested Himself to the disciples and made them spectators of things exceeding human comprehension, this great Philip himself, as a fellow-student and participant in the ineffable mysteries, was present at what was taking place, and was reckoned as one of them. Now when our Lord and Savior Jesus Christ had completed the entire order of the dispensation undertaken for us in the flesh, and had miraculously united earthly things with heavenly things, and had been taken up into heaven with glory and sat at the right hand of the greatness of the Father, and had sent the Comforter, the Spirit, to His eyewitnesses and confidants as He had promised, in the form of tongues of fire—Philip also, being filled with this, prepared himself for the course of the Gospel. C VII. And when some of them set out for the Eastern parts, others for the Western, and some also traversed the Northern and Southern climates, fulfilling the proclamation entrusted to them, this admirable apostle was allotted Asia; and going round this, and visiting every city and town in it, he brought an innumerable multitude to piety; and marking them all with the light of regeneration, he brought them to the heavenly Father. Furthermore, he cured those oppressed by certain ailments and diseases, and also those who were evilly afflicted by a demon, by a word alone and the imposition of a holy hand; invisibly driving away the adversaries themselves, but visibly saving those who were vexed by them, while through the magnificence of his preaching and the operation of stupendous miracles, as if by certain nets, he brought them to the knowledge of the truth.
He appointed and built priests and altars everywhere in those places; instead of sacrifices on the altars, he reverently prepared the holy mystery to be celebrated in them, so that the rule of the Gospel might be kept even there. Thus, therefore, he brought a very great multitude of those who believed in Christ through him. Now, when the time arrived for him to depart from there, and for him to pass on to the Lord, something of this sort befell him toward the end.
Ⅷ. There is a prominent city in Phrygia, called the Holy City. This city, having flourished in population beyond others and being named the mother of other cities, received the most holy Philip after those countless labors for the Gospel. When this great apostle arrived, so that he might preach the Gospel there as well, he saw that it offered worship—alas!—to idols, and to a certain monstrous, venomous viper, among the pestilential creatures. His soul was kindled with divine zeal, and by applying himself more intensely to prayers and invoking the name of Christ, he immediately showed that this ruinous cause of destruction for many was dead.
Ⅸ. Since his petition to the Lord against this cruel beast had succeeded so well, he began thereafter to urge and advise everyone to believe that God lives in the heavens and not to pay attention to beasts crawling upon the earth. For God, who is always existing, a God perfect and incomprehensible, after having produced this visible and invisible world, also formed man after the image of His own majesty; and by honoring him with free will, He allowed him to enjoy the good things of paradise, commanding only that he abstain from the tree of knowledge. Yet, since it was not for God to overlook him—having transgressed the commandment and slipped from friendship with Him—He first instructed him through the law and the prophets; and in the end, He willed that the Word, consubstantial to Him, be born of a pure Virgin according to our nature. Appearing in the likeness of men and, though impassible, communicating in our sufferings, and rising on the third day from the dead, He lovingly manifested Himself to us and departed to the heavens from whence He had descended. We believe that He will come again to resurrect His creature and render to each according to his works. You, too, who have been deemed worthy of the divine light of regeneration, will know this; for your mind will be purged of every base passion and you will be filled to satiety with His gifts—if, indeed, satiety of them can be—and you will inherit the promised good things.
X. The divine apostle discoursed of these and more than these, both publicly and privately. Those whom he saw, more than others, receiving the word of faith, he brought to the light of regeneration, enrolled in the order of the priesthood, and raised up as living temples for Christ, as holy ones. Seeing the truth progressing thus favorably, his enemy could not restrain himself from laying snares and throwing a stumbling block in its way. He insinuated himself among those who then held the chief offices in the city, and, kindling their indignation like fire, he first caused them to seize Philip, suddenly inciting the multitude against him, and then inflicted upon him all the harm and destruction he could. For those who had apprehended him cast him into a harsh prison, and more harshly they beat and scourged him; then, having passed ropes through his ankles, they hung him aloft.
XI. And this could indeed signify the state of his soul, holding the straight path to things above; yet the Lord did not abandon him. But because the divine apostle Bartholomew was also in Hierapolis at that time, having been with him and announcing the Gospel—just as he had shared in the preaching, so too he wished him to be a sharer in the passion—Bartholomew also is condemned to the cross, after he, as we said, had been hung by the ankles. His sister Mariamne, a virgin in body and soul—and not less joined to him by nature than by purpose—was also present, suffering with her brother Philip, and she endured and supported the torment as much as she could by her resolve. While these things were happening, a quite strange event occurred: the earth was suddenly shaken and suffered an unusual tremor, and a fear fell upon the minds of all who had gathered for the spectacle for the sake of amusement. The entire place was overturned from its foundations and yawned open into a deep, invisible abyss; at the same time, the people were being swallowed up, and they were in danger of most wretched destruction.
XII. When, therefore, all were stunned, and could devise no useful plan, at length, though late, they recognized that they were suffering retribution for the punishment and injury inflicted upon Philip. Standing around him, they called him their savior and begged him to reach out his right hand to them for their salvation, and not to disregard the souls imperiled solely because of him. It is related that as they insisted on their prayers and spoke many things suitable to move him to pity, and did not cease from their entreaties, Jesus, having pity on those who were suffering what was occurring, bowed His ear from above toward the human-loving— D
benignity, and suddenly appeared to them; and by that appearance He immediately caused the terrors to cease, and the ground, which until then had been shaking, stood firm, and to those who were there in pitiable danger of death there appeared a more divine power, acting in the place of a ladder and providing an easy ascent upwards. This became a path of faith to salvation for the unbelievers, and it proclaimed Philip to be great, but it proclaimed as greater still the Lord whom he himself preached.
XIII. Those who had been saved therefore hastened to deliver the disciples from their bonds, and made speed to take them down from the wooden stake as quickly as possible. And they loosed Bartholomew, but Philip forbade them from doing the same to him; for he knew that he was about to depart forthwith to the One whom he desired. And so, while hanging from the wood, he spoke the whole day to the citizens concerning their salvation, confirming their souls by his exhortation and pouring out prayers for them. He departed this life holily among holy words, and passed to the Lord whom he had loved, committing his own soul into His hands.
XIV. His venerable body, having been piously taken down by Bartholomew and Mariamne, and having been granted with splendor what is customary at burials, was laid to rest with sacred hymns and honors in a prominent and holy place, on the fourteenth day of November. Bartholomew and Mariamne remained for a short time in that place, and having rendered the rites of hymns and honors to this venerable relic, and having further confirmed those present in the faith more brilliantly and more securely, they departed to their own lands, preaching everywhere the Gospel of Christ: for to Him is due all glory, honor, and worship, now and always, and unto the ages of ages. Amen.
