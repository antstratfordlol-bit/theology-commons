errorem simulacrorum magnum quidem studium  conferente, valde autem et atrociter spirante adversus pios; et alii sacrificare dæmonibus, et ante omnes cogebantur milites. Litteræ enim imperatoriæ mittebantur in omnes partes, urgentes ad impietatem et Christianos cogentes vel sacrificare, vel variis subjici suppliciis. Quæ quidem postquam venerunt ad exercitum, qui erat in Oriente, illi quidem eo tempore ea comparantes, quæ ad bellum pertinebant, continuisque et gravibus se exercentes militiæ meditationibus, alioqui autem religionem amplectentes, nihil curabant litteras illas imperatorias. Qui cum magna pugna eis esset exorta, præclaram adversus inimicos obtinent victoriam (2).
II. Hoc fuit relatum ad imperatorem Trajanum.  Ille autem propterea quod sua edicta despexerant, ne communis quidem omnium boni, nempe victoriæ, ullam ducit rationem: sed eos qui imperatorias contempserant constitutiones, erant autem numero undecim millia (3), relegat in Armeniæ Melitenam (4) et loca, quæ circa eam sita sunt. Reliquus vero exercitus erat in metu propter eorum relegationem, ne dolore affecti molirentur res novas, et eis aliquid mali afferrent, ut qui nec Christianismi, neque propter Christum susceptæ relegationis fuissent eis socii. Verum illi nihil tale fecerunt aut cogitarunt: sed ut qui erant discipuli ejus qui vere est mitis, fuerunt etiam imitatores mansuetudinis et lenitatis Magistri; et placide ferebant exsilium, et minime ægre ferebant contumeliam.
III. Romulus autem aulæ imperatoriæ tunc præpositus (5) apud Trajanum imperatorem, neque ei, neque civitati omnino dicebat expedire ea quæ facta fuerant. Ille autem, cum oporteret Romulo agere gratias, ut qui benevole esset in eum affectus, stulte et ingrate se gerens, eum potius maledictis insectabatur et ei irascebatur, et tanquam crimen objiciebat pietatem. Romulus vero verba illius ne tantillum quidem curans, gloriabatur potius contumeliis propter Christum susceptis:

V. Cum multum autem intercessisset temporis (6), Diocletianus quidem sanguine intestino et tyrannide invadit imperium (7), contendit autem eos qui præcesserant tyrannos crudelitate superare et impietate, et ostendere illorum sævitiam fuisse humanitatem. Unde etiam ad eos qui illius parebant imperio, edicta veniebant frequentia, ut omnes vel cum eo communionem impietatis susciperent, vel mors esset ea pœna inobedientiæ. Christiani itaque propter sævam et immanem illius iram, alii quidem malebant cum feris habitare in solitudinibus, quam simul cum hominibus : alii autem montes et speluncas et quæcunque celare poterant subeuntes studebant, quoad ejus fieri poterat, vel ipsum solem latere.
VI. Eo tempore defertur quidam vir pius, et plane indignus qui lateret. Ei nomen erat Eudoxius, aut Marianus; erat enim utrumque ei nomen proprium. A dignitate primicerius expulsus propter amorem Christi, descenderat ad inferiorem comitum dignitatem; quam quidem etiamsi non fuisset consecutus, satis tamen habuisset eum solum sequi, quem desiderabat. Defertur autem ad eum, qui tunc erat præses Melitenæ. Qui cum de eo singula intellexisset, qui ipsum adducturi essent, emittit protinus. Cum eum ergo convenissent et nondum eum illum esse putassent, ubinam versaretur comes Eudoxius, eum rogarunt. VII. Cum ergo parasset convivium, eos excipit  liberaliter. Illis autem mensa satis exhilaratis, et promissum deinde exigentibus et rogantibus, ut eum ostenderet quem quærebant; ille continuo eum, qui erat intus, aperit Eudoxium, dicens: Ego sum, quem quæritis. Hoc gladio vehementius illorum perculit animum; et respondentes dixerunt: Non te prodemus, qui nos accepisti convivio, neque pro hospitalitate tam male referemus gratiam. Sed hoc ad nostram sufficiet excusationem, quod cum in quærendo multum laboraverimus, non potuimus tamen invenire Eudoxium. Illi quidem pro hospitio has Eudoxio retulere gratias.
VIII. Ille autem, qui et sciebat latere cum opus esset, et rursus non latere quando hujus rei esset  tempus (divina enim, quæ aderat gratia et docebat Athletam, jussit ut eos sequeretur), cum uxorem vocasset Basilissam aut, si velis, Mandanem, nam ea quoque duplici nomine vocabatur, ei mandat quæ spectant ad familiam. Et cum omnia ejus curæ commisisset, hoc quoque postremum et germanum ei dat mandatum, ne ejus defleat decessum neque propter mortem ejus lamentetur, sed lampadibus (9) et alia lætitia potius illum diem honoret. Statim ergo suam quidem cognationem despicit Eudoxius: obliviscitur autem propter Christum sui amoris in filios, validi et inevitabilis vinculi parentibus. Ut enim illum lucrifaciat, omnia damnum existimat et stercora. Et vadit ad  præsidem.
IX. Is autem cum ipsum vidisset, Salve, inquit, comes Eudoxi. Ille autem vicissim reddit salutem, dicens: Tu quoque salve, præses. ### CAPUT III.
Mirabili constantia et magnanimitate acerbissimos perfert cruciatus; capite damnatus orat Deum, mandata uxori dat, mortemque amico prædicit, Sancti et aliorum martyrium.
XIII. Excipiens autem præses: Videtur, inquit, tanta mea lenitas et mansuetudo te ad tantam adducere stultitiam et audaciam. Sed tuam quidem in nos contumeliam, quod ad nos quidem attinet, tolerabimus; ipsos autem deos et imperatorem pati a te contumelia affici, plane est stultum et non multum abest ab amentia. Deinde martyr: Imo vero, inquit, sunt hæc grata iis qui sapiunt et non sunt emotæ mentis. Hoc majori furore concitat præsidem, et statim jubet athletam extensum a quatuor hominibus (15) recentibus loris verberari crudeliter. Cum autem jam diu cæsus incorpoream (16) ostenderet philosophiam in mortali corpore, et se primum turpiter irrisum vidisset tyrannus, martyrem quidem relaxavit a plagis, conjicit vero in carcerem. I. Cum Maximianus esset præses Ciliciæ, et impietatem augere et extollere, convenienter edicto imperatorum, magno studio contenderet, venisset autem in ipsam quoque Ciliciæ Pompeiopolim, et aureum, quod illic colebatur, simulacrum splendido sacrificio et alio studio honoraret, et sic ostentaret suam falsam religionem: quidam adolescens patriam habens Lycaoniam, et ab ortu quidem Tarasius, in divino autem baptismo Sozon denominatus, cum priore vita amisso quoque nomine, cum esset pastor ovium, fit etiam nihilominus pastor hominum. Nam in quibus locis degebat in pastione, illic omnes excipiebat convivio verbi pietatis, et introducebat in Dei mandram. Hic vir cum esset moribus hilaris et mitis, in lege autem Domini haberet voluntatem, et in ea noctes et dies meditaretur ¹, ea ratione nec prophetica frustratur beatitudine.
II. Cum autem fuisset aliquando Sozon in quodam loco, qui habebat fontem, et fonte herbaque, quæ nata
care and zeal for the loathsome error of idols, and was breathing out great and savage threats against the pious, everyone, and above all the military, were forced to sacrifice to the demons. For letters were sent by the emperor everywhere, promoting impiety and ordering that Christians either sacrifice or be subjected to various punishments. Since these arrived at the army which was in the East, those men, as they were at that time occupied with preparations for war and were busy with constant and strenuous military training, and furthermore being devoted to the Christian religion, paid no heed to those imperial letters. Indeed, when a fierce battle broke out for them, they gained a most brilliant victory over their enemies. II. This was reported to the emperor Trajan. But he, because of the oversight of his orders, did not even take into account the common good of everyone—that is, the victory itself—but exiled those who had disregarded the imperial decrees (the number of them was eleven thousand) to Melitene in Armenia and the surrounding areas. The rest of the army, however, was fearful and terrified by their exile, lest they, being grieved, might look toward rebellion and then harm them as well, as they had not shared in either their Christianity or their exile on account of it. But they neither did nor even thought of any such thing; rather, as true disciples of the Meek One, they were imitators both of their Teacher’s meekness and of his compassion; they bore the exile gently, and were not in the least bothered by the abuse. III. Romulus, who was at that time the praepositus of the imperial palace, told the emperor Trajan that what had happened was to the advantage of neither him nor the city. But he, when he should have offered thanks to Romulus as one who was well-disposed toward him, acting like a fool and an ingrate, instead railed at him and grew angry, and brought forward piety as a crime. Romulus, however, caring not even a little for his words, prided himself all the more in the insults endured for the sake of Christ; and, being burned inwardly with the most ardent zeal for Him,
he openly and profusely reviles his gods with reproaches, while clearly confessing Christ and boldly professing piety. Trajan, therefore, being more intensely irritated, orders him to be stretched and beaten with rods.
IV. Romulus, however, receiving the blows with a great and brave spirit, cried out to Trajan: "Now know that I am being affected by the greatest benefit. For the body that is flowing around me with this red blood washes away the filth that I contracted from the stench of the idols, and the soul is clothed in a clean robe of incorruption." Trajan could not bear these things, but, moved to greater anger, he prepares for them a swift release and brings about a brief martyrdom; and he decrees that the head of Romulus be cut off. And he indeed gladly looked upon the sword for the sake of Christ, and most joyfully entered the stadium of martyrdom.

VIII. IX. And when he saw him, the governor said, "Hail, Count Eudoxius." And he in turn immediately returned the greeting, saying, "And hail to you, O governor." The governor said again, "We have summoned your Excellency that you may obey the emperor's decrees and offer the sacrifice due to the gods, especially to the father of all gods, the great Zeus, to Phoebus Apollo, and to the dear maiden Artemis." The martyr said, "I have one sacrifice: that of the true God, who brought all this universe into existence; and to Him I shall offer a sacrifice of praise, who is recognized as God in a trinity of persons, the dispenser of life and the provider of true salvation. To this, the other was unable to look—from where indeed would he, since he was sleeping in the deep stupor of impiety? "I," he said, "exhorted you to carry out the emperor's commands and to perform the customary sacrifices to the gods; but you, as I see, show contempt both for the gods and for the emperor, in that you have so shamelessly insulted the gods whom he worships, and are introducing some new-fangled religion contrary to this, and, as has reached my ears, are persuading many to adopt it."
X. The governor was saying these things, with many soldiers standing around. Looking fiercely and grimly at them in the meantime, he said, "Whoever of you also does not follow the imperial decrees, take off your military belts and acknowledge yourselves to be stripped of the symbols of your rank." Why did that wicked man say this? In order that, if any through the fellowship of military service felt kindly toward Eudoxius and were of the same mind as he, they might feel ashamed to be the only ones standing apart in such a great multitude; for he supposed them to be few in absolute number. And from this he intended both at the same time to put Eudoxius to shame, thinking thus that he held views alien from the whole army, and at the same time, having found him alone, to do with him whatever he wished. But He who catches the wise in their craftiness turned this maneuver against his own head. For he had not yet finished his entire speech, when the noble Eudoxius (for at that time he was still wearing the insignia of his rank) immediately removed his belt and threw it into the governor's face. This became an exhortation to the others and ignited their zeal; and the whole company standing around, numbering one thousand one hundred and four, immediately taking the good example of Eudoxius, also cast their belts into his face.
XI. Trembling therefore at the multitude, the governor reported the state of the matter to Diocletian. He ordered the authors and ringleaders of such audacity to be punished with the most severe tortures, but left the verdict regarding the others to the senate. XVII. Having thus provided for his friend, and having seen Zeno perfected by such an end, since a voice from above was calling to him even then, and he himself sought death just as one would a feast, he gladly submitted his blessed head to the sword.
The wife, moreover, beholding the man who had been slain for Christ, not only in body but also in spirit, did not look on with sloth, nor did she utter anything ignoble or womanish; but running with all speed, she receives the athlete’s blood in a fleece of wool, for this had been commanded to her by her husband. Having lifted the body, she honors it with burial just where she had been instructed, not neglecting any of his other commands; not because she had lost him, but because she had not departed with him, counting her widowhood a misery. XVIII. When she was thereafter summoned to the tyrant, she came of her own accord and mocked his gods, making them a laughingstock, not even leaving him immune from scorn for enduring the honoring of such things. "But you," said the tyrant, "do these things wishing to be killed by me, and making haste to catch up with your husband, so that you might gain some reputation among these impious Galileans. I, however, shall by no means bring death upon you, even though you are worthy of many deaths; do not think that you will obtain this from me." But she said, "My Master sees my heart and accepts my intention before my deeds. Therefore, because I have not been slain by you, I am not deprived of my crowns; but because I dedicated myself in the readiness of my spirit, I shall partake of the same rewards and enjoy equal recompense." When she had said this, he drove her from the tribunal. XIX. Not yet had seven days passed when the glorious martyr Eudoxius appearing in a dream to his wife, ordered her to send to the praetorium Macarius, his familiar friend. When she reported the vision to Macarius, he went to the praetorium as quickly as possible; and when the attendants knew who he was, they immediately seized him and led him to the governor. And he immediately asked him what was his religion and what was his race. He answered him, "I am a Christian, a slave of Christ. I have long since learned to despise the gods of the gentiles and their profound deception, this having been taught to me by the wonder-working Eudoxius. Hence it is that I strive to offer myself as a sacrifice to the true God, through which I shall be able to reach my lord Eudoxius quickly." XX. The governor, therefore, when he heard the name of Eudoxius and learned that this man was his disciple, was immediately perplexed in all things, for he knew that he would accomplish nothing more in the disciple than he had accomplished in the master. And the miserable man said, "Let this Macarius have his wicked head cut off with the sword forthwith."

THE MARTYRDOM OF THE HOLY AND GLORIOUS MARTYR OF CHRIST, SOZON, WHO SUFFERED IN CILICIA UNDER THE IMPIOUS MAXIMIAN.
I. When Maximian was governor of Cilicia, and was striving with great zeal to increase and exalt impiety, in accordance with the edict of the emperors; and having arrived at the very city of Pompeiopolis in Cilicia, was honoring the golden idol worshipped there with splendid sacrifice and other attentions, and was thus displaying his own false religion: a certain young man, having Lycaonia as his fatherland, and named Tarasius from birth, but renamed Sozon in divine baptism—having cast off his name along with his former life—being a shepherd of sheep, became no less a shepherd of men. For in whatever places he would happen to be while tending his flock, he would welcome all there to the feast of the word of piety, and would lead many into the fold of God. This man, being cheerful and gentle in character, and having his will in the law of the Lord, and meditating upon it day and night, for this reason did not fail to attain the prophetic blessedness.
