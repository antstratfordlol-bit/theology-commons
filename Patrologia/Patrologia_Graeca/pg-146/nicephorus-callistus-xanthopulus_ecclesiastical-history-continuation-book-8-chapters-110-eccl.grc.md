ECCLESIASTICAL HISTORY
VOLUME VIII
CHAPTER I
Recapitulation of what has been said, and a proposal of what is to be spoken of.
The deeds of Constantine, he who is to be compared to the Apostles himself, from the time he began warring as an enemy against the impious Licinius up to the building and improvement of Byzantium, stand thus. Yet, since it seems to me entirely absurd to leave such an illustrious administration of the empire—the very foundation and, so to speak, the pinnacle of true piety toward God—intermingled (as it necessarily had to be) with the history and register of the most impious tyrants, I cannot but separate it as far as possible from them and pursue it in its own book; for it is truly vast and excelling in all respects. Therefore, what I have previously explained in connection with the times of the tyrants, I shall touch upon as briefly as possible in my account: regarding his father, his birth, and his upbringing, and how he came to the administration of affairs; then, how he secured the empire of ancient Rome; how he beheld the sign of victory in the heavens, through which he overcame those ruinous and detestable tyrants; next, how he was deemed worthy of the divine laver in Rome; what he accomplished for the Christians and the Churches; by what laws promulgated for our religion he caused our affairs to receive great growth in a short time; how at last he crushed Licinius; and, finally, how, having subdued Byzantium, he drove out the brigands therein and magnificently raised his own namesake city, adorning it both within and without with sacred and secular buildings, porticoes, projections, statues, water-works, and other public structures.
