JOSEPHUS GENESIUS
NOTITIA
(FABRIC. Bibliotheca Græca ed. Harles, tom. VII, p. 530.)
Quem Josephum Genesium viri docti appellant, is ab uno memoratur Joanne Scylitze in proœmio Historiæ. Sed in ms. Seylitzæ distingui Josephum ac Genesium, notat Labbeus (a), ita, ut duo fuerint historiæ Cpolit. scriptores diversi, unus Josephus Byzantinus, et alter Genesius Byzantinus. At enim, ut, patria Byzantium fuisse Genesium, perquam est incertum, licet Byzantinam historiam scripserit, sic Josephum diversum a Genesio apud Scylitzen laudari, ægre mihi persuadeo. Quidquid vero sit, Lipsiæ in patria mea servatur in bibliotheca Paulina codex antiquus membranaceus Græcus Historiæ Genesianæ, jussu Constantini Porphyrogeniti compositæ, et res imper. quinque CPol. Leonis Armeni, qui a. Chr. 815 cœpit imperare, Michaelis Balbi, Theophili, filii Michaelis, et Basilii Macedonis, a. 889, Kal. Mart. defuncti, comprehendentis. Ex hoc cod. collegii Paulini bibliothecæ acad. Lipsiensis pagg. 77 in fol. constante, vir doct. Jo. Andreas Bosius jam pridem illius editionem parare se significavit in diatriba De testimonio Flav. Josephi De Christo (b). Apographum Bosianum cum brevibus Thom. Reinesii, quocum Bosius illud communicaverat, notis servatur Ienæ in bibl. publ. acad. cui libros suos ille moriens legavit, ex quo Genesii editionem deinde molitus fuit ejusdem acad. professor cel. Georgius Schubartus, ut memorant Acta erudit. Lips. a. 1685, p. 441. Aliud apographum cum iisdem Reinesii notulis servat bibliotheca senatus Lips. Novum etiam ac singulare studium Genesio in lucem proferendo impendit præclari vir ingenii et magnæ, supra ætatem, doctrinæ, Godfridus Wagnerus, Lipsiensis, pastor ad S. Joannis; sed, illo florentibus annis exstincto, etiam ista spes (c) decollavit, ita ut palma hæc integra relicta sit viro præstantiss. Godfrido Oleario, qui scriptorem illum, ex eodem codice Paulino descriptum et versione ac nota se illustratum, jam in scriniis habet, et proxime prælis commissurus est (d). Versus, quibus Genesius suos quinque Βασιλειῶν libros Constantino Porphyrogenito offert, hi operi præfiguntur :
Τὴν ἐξ ἱστορίας τελέσας βίβλον ὡς ἐκέλευσας, Αὐτοκράτορ, μελέταις καὶ καμάτοις μεγάλοις, Ἐν σοὶ δῶρον ἔθηκα, ὅπως ἐγνωσμένον εἴη, Ἐκ φιλοδεσποτίας συντεθὲν ἔκ τε πόθου.
Hos versus, a Wagnero cum Caveo communicatos, et ab eo vulgatos in parte altera Histor. litter SS. EE. ad a. 940, ita utcunque transtuli :
Historiam, jussu quam, Cæsar magne, tuople Perfeci curis atque labore gravi, Hane tibi dona fero, notum sit ut omnibus, ista Natu scripta tuo, te cupiente legi.
Leguntur et alii versus Olympii cujusdam ad voluminis calcem, qui mihi non videntur ad Genesium pertinere (e), sed ad Joannem quemdam, historicum, Scylitzæ itidem laudatum, et cujus opus fortasse olim Genesio in cod. Lips. ms. adhærebat, postea ab eo separatum periit.

JOSEPHUS GENESIUS
NOTICE
(FABRIC. Bibliotheca Græca ed. Harles, tom. VII, p. 530.)
The Josephus Genesius whom learned men mention is recorded by a single author, John Scylitzes, in the proemium to his History. But in the manuscript of Scylitzes, Labbe marks a distinction between Josephus and Genesius, implying that there were two different writers of Constantinopolitan history: one, Josephus the Byzantine, and the other, Genesius the Byzantine. But really, while it is highly uncertain that Genesius’s homeland was Byzantium—even though he wrote a Byzantine history—I am quite unable to persuade myself that Josephus is, at Scylitzes’ hands, praised as a person distinct from Genesius. However that may be, in my homeland of Leipzig, there is preserved in the Paulinum library an ancient Greek parchment codex of the *Genesian History*, composed by order of Constantine Porphyrogenitus, which includes the imperial affairs of five Constantinopolitan rulers: Leo the Armenian, who began to reign in 813 A.D.; Michael Balbus; Theophilus, son of Michael; and Basil the Macedonian, who died on the Kalends of March, 889. B Concerning this codex of the Paulinum college of the Leipzig academic library—consisting of 77 pages in folio—the learned Jo. Andreas Bosius long ago indicated that he was preparing an edition, in his dissertation *De testimonio Flav. Josephi De Christo* (b). A copy of the Bosius manuscript, containing the brief notes of Thom. Reinesius, with whom Bosius had shared it, is preserved in the public academic library of Jena, to which he bequeathed his books upon his death; from this copy, the celebrated professor of the same academy, Georgius Schubart, later undertook the edition of Genesius, as the *Acta eruditorum Lipsiensium* of the year 1685, p. 441, record. Another copy, with the same notes of Reinesius, is preserved by the Leipzig Senate library. C Furthermore, a new and singular effort toward bringing Genesius to light was expended by a man of brilliant talent and great learning—beyond his years—Godfried Wagner, a pastor at St. John's in Leipzig; but with his death in the prime of his years, even this hope (c) collapsed, leaving this palm in its entirety to the most distinguished man Godfried Olearius, who already has in his desks that author, transcribed from the same Paulinum codex and illustrated by himself with a translation and notes, and who is soon to entrust it to the presses (d). The verses in which Genesius offers his five books of the *Bασιλειῶν* [Kings] to Constantine Porphyrogenitus are set before the work:
Having finished the book from history as you commanded,
O Emperor, with great studies and labors,
I have placed the gift in you, so that it might be known,
Composed out of love for the master and out of longing.
These verses, which were communicated by Wagner to Cave and published by him in the second part of his *Historia litteraria SS. EE.* for the year 940, I have translated as follows:
The history which, by your command, great Caesar,
I completed with care and heavy toil,
I bring to you as a gift, so that it may be known to all that these
writings [were made] by your will, and that you wished them to be read.
There are also read other verses by a certain Olympius at the end of the volume, which do not seem to me to pertain to Genesius (e), but rather to a certain John, a historian, who is likewise praised by Scylitzes, and whose work perhaps once adhered to Genesius in the Leipzig manuscript, but later, after being separated from it, perished.
NOTES.
(b) Bosius, *De testimonio Flavii Josephi*, Jena, 1673, cap. 12, p. 24. "Some years ago we compared the Augustan codex of Theophanes’ *Chronographia* with the printed edition, by whose aid we shall one day publish that writer, much more corrected, and in some places fuller, by filling in certain gaps by which he is here and there marred, together with Georgius Syncellus and the continuator of both, and Genesius, hitherto unedited and seen or known by few of the most learned men," etc. He transcribed Genesius in 1652. FABR. Add Morhof, *Poly. hist. litter.*, lib. 1, cap. 7, 44, where more works are mentioned which Bosius had proposed to himself to edit. HARL.
(c) As was also the hope of an edition of Genesius promised by Ludolph Kuster.
(d) Yet that plan did not have a prosperous outcome, although Olearius was stirred to action by Oudin in a special dissertation, *De adversa fortuna Josepho Genesio, historiae Byzantinae scriptori quoad operis editionem*, inserted in his *Commentarii de S. S. E. E.*, tom. II, p. 435 sqq., and more pointedly in the journals titled *Histoire critique de la République des Lettres*, tom. XV, no. 5. Grundmann, in *Miscell. Lips.*, tom. II, p. 757 sq., reports that Olearius, who died on November 10, 1715, at age 43, would have edited many other things but did not finish them. HARL.
(e) That these verses, which Fabricius brought forward, as well as the other verses of Olympius, were added by another hand is noted in the Venetian edition. HARL.
