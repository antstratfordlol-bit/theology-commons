As a lover of the naturally beautiful, the man came to have a longing to relearn the Greek tongue; and from there, having moved to the Calydonians, he had everything refashioned there—language, beard, clothing, bearing, and character. For he no longer judged that he needed to shave his beard, nor to be clothed in Latin attire while among the Greeks with whom he was about to mingle, a foreign tribe; but rather, that he might not be disbelieved or suspected by anyone, he even went so far as to darken his robe. Having waited there for a significant time, he came into a clearer habit of the Greek language... And he, through this acquisition of the Greek language, having climbed high, thought himself to be a great man and pregnant with all wisdom; and he was looking down upon others as if from a watchtower, as they wallowed in some base and lowly life. But when he heard that Athens, the mother-city of all wisdom, was being feebly administered at that time regarding discourse, due to the misfortune involving Metrodorus and Nicagoras, and the oblivion and silence that had long ago been passed upon it, he was the more moved toward a more rash way of reasoning. He thought he finally had the opportunity, with no one opposing him, to depart for Athens and become a claimant of the popular glory. Driven by this hope as if on a raft, he hastened toward Athens, being able to perceive nothing moderate or unworthy concerning himself.
But as he came, in the dark robe as we have said, his immediate business was to walk around the markets and festivals, promising an exhibition of a certain great wisdom, and if anyone wished to become wise according to him, it was easy to make him so in a short time, receiving a small sum of money, and indeed much less than its value. (He was a man of memory, and all that he had previously read he would recite in sequence from memory as if from a book, especially the interpreters of the Aristotelians; but by his "barbarizing" in the flow of his words, it was clear that he was a Latin, for he was no longer recognizable by his beard.)
Toward these things, Nicagoras smiled slightly and said: "Come now, best Xenophanes, tell us what kind of wisdom you have come to demonstrate."
"All-encompassing and varied, Nicagoras, and sufficient for all things, as many and as varied as one might wish to ask."
"Well, let us try, so that they may know, both you with you, and you with these."
But Nicagoras, having judged the magnitude of the promises of Xenophanes, set himself to strike his brow with a certain skill, beginning from the astronomical science. And if he decided to leave aside all the major astronomical hypotheses, whose complex arguments trace out their proofs, and having passed over them, chose to refute him through smaller matters, so that he might be the more put to shame—for the difficulties in the greatest contests do not bring down unadulterated or manifest ridicule, but rather a small and, so to speak, oculary and misty one, and like the gait of Hephaestus, for the most part limping and wavering—but the proofs in the lesser and more ready matters would be a refutation of the ignorance of the bold, very clear and more manifest than the noonday sun. It is therefore easy to understand how, following incidents over which we pass here, a contest of learning arises between Barlaam and Gregoras, and Metrodorus’s friend. When everything is ready for this scholarly encounter and the audience has assembled at the house of Demaratus, one of the leading men of Athens, Nicagoras, acting as questioner, soon establishes beyond dispute that his opponent Xenophanes lays claim to encyclopedic knowledge in the fullest and most literal sense. “At this Nicagoras smiled slightly and said, ‘Come then, my excellent Xenophanes, tell us what sort of wisdom you are going to display, and of what character.’ ‘Wisdom of every sort and varied character, Nicagoras, sufficient for anything and everything one might wish to ask. But put me to the test, so that these men too may know along with you, and you in turn along with them.’” After this verse of Homer, and once Xenophanes’ own words have firmly established his claim to be ignorant of nothing—so that, if he proves unable to answer questions about the principles and ordinary problems of any particular science, he will plainly have incurred the charge of charlatanism or frivolity, if not both—Gregoras, whom we know to have possessed astronomical knowledge and to have been exceedingly vain of it, begins the examination with astronomy. He asks the Italian to determine the time of day astronomically, by means of the horoscopic instrument, intending to lead him from this starting point to determining the positions of the sun and the planets in the zodiac; and he takes care to inform us that the single question he now puts is one addressed to students of astronomy:
Nicagoras, however, disapproving of the magnitude of Xenophanes’ promises, undertook to give his arrogance a deft rebuff, beginning with the science of astronomy. Passing over all the greater astronomical questions, whose demonstrations are pursued by various methods, he decided that he should expose him through the lesser ones, so that his shame might be all the greater. For perplexity in the greatest contests brings neither unqualified nor manifest ridicule, but only a little, bleary-eyed and misty, so to speak, and even that, like the gait of Hephaestus, largely limping and uncertain. (Nous omettons à regret des détails) Ὁ δὲ Νικαγόρας ἐπιστραφεὶς καὶ σιωπὴν ἐπιτάξας τῷ δήμῳ τὸ πρὸς γέλωτα τῶν πολλῶν ἐμέμφετο ἀκρατὲς, καὶ οὐ πάνυ ἅμα πᾶσιν ἔφασκε δίδοσθαι πρὸς θεοῦ, ἀλλ’ ἄλλῳ μὲν ἄλλο, ἄλλῳ δ’ ἄλλο. . . Τί τοίνυν καινὸν ἢ γέλωτος ἄξιον, εἰ καὶ ὁ σοφὸς οὗτος Ξενοφάνης ὁ Θρασύμαχος, ἐν Λατίνοις καὶ φὺς καὶ τραφεὶς, παρ’ οἷς οὔτ’ ἀστρονομία οὔθ’ ὅσα πλεῖστα παρ’ Ἕλλησιν ἤνθησαν εἴδη σοφίας οὐδὲν ἐκεῖ πολιτεύεται, τῶν τοιούτων ἄμοιρος ἔμεινε καὶ αὐτός · φανήσεται γὰρ ἐν ἄλλοις εἴδεσι δόκιμος ἴσως εἶναι. Ὁ δὲ Ξενοφάνης ὑπὸ τῶν τοιούτων λόγων ἀναθαρρήσας, Εὖ λέγεις, ὦ Νικαγόρα, φησὶ · πλὴν γὰρ ἀστρονομίας καὶ ὅσα ταύτῃ τῶν μαθημάτων ἑπόμενα, τἆλλα πάντα σοφίας εἴδη αὐτός τε ἐξεμάθον καὶ ἄλλους πάντως ἱκανός εἰμι ἐκδιδάσκειν.
