A, since those who knew him had judged him well, was received kindly and enrolled—in no mean position, as the law provides—but in a certain perfect rank of a man of that name who had already departed this life, whose name was Augustus. Thus, his name was written with the appointment: Marcian, who is also Augustus. From this it was sufficiently clear that, along with the appellation, the purple of Augustus had also reached him; and it was as if this surname would not remain in him without the dignity itself, and that the dignity, in turn, required no other name for its magnificence. Thus, the same word, being both proper and appellative, became one, clearly indicating by a single title both the dignity and the name. This, then, was the first omen and sign in Marcian that portended he was to ascend to the imperial dignity.
But another event also occurred from which it was possible to conjecture that he would be emperor. Marcian was serving under Aspar against the Vandals; and after they had gained the victory, he, too, was led away as a captive with others from the battlefield across the plain. Gaiseric, the leader of the Vandals, wished to see the captives along with the other slaves. And when they were gathered in a certain place, Gaiseric, sitting on a high hill, feasted his eyes on the multitude of the captives. As time was passing, he permitted them to do whatever each wished; for by Gaiseric’s order they had been freed from their bonds. While others were doing various things, Marcian, overcome by the intense heat of the sun on the plain, fell asleep. An eagle, flying thence from somewhere, lowering its wings against the sun and hovering directly above him, created an aerial cloud and a sort of shadow, by which, by divine will, it refreshed him not insignificantly. Seeing this, the barbarian immediately conjectured openly what would become of him; and summoning Marcian to him, he granted him his liberty and bound him by the sanctity of an oath that, should he be raised to imperial power, he would be faithful to him and the Vandals and never wage war against them. This Marcian did by means of an oath, as he [Gaiseric] wished, and kept his faith in deed, as Procopius writes.
Before this, while marching against the Persians, he came into Lycia to a city named Sidema. Having fallen ill, he formed a deep friendship with two brothers, men of distinction there, Julian and Tatian, who received him into the hospitality of their home, cared for him as was fitting, and later went out hunting with him. At midday, all were overcome by sleep. When Tatian first awoke from his sleep, he saw a wondrous sight: an eagle of vast size spreading its wings over Marcian and casting a shadow, as it were, upon him. Having awakened his brother, he made him see the same thing. ### CHAPTER I. Concerning the reign of Marcian.
What signs preceded it, portending his imperial rule, and what his character was.
Marcian, having firmly established the foundations of his empire, proved himself to be even more excellent than the hopes that had been entertained of him. But if you wish, let us recount who he was, whence he came, and how he attained the imperial throne; and then, in due course, let us go through the events that occurred during his reign. This celebrated Marcian, as Priscus the rhetorician also says, was Thracian by birth, born of a soldier father. When his father died, and he had determined to follow in his father’s footsteps and engage in military service, he set out for the city of Philippopolis, where he judged he would properly enlist. While on his journey there, he happened upon the fresh corpse of a man who had been slain, which lay thrown carelessly upon the ground. He halted for a short time at this sight, for in addition to the other excellent qualities with which he abounded, he was also outstandingly humane and merciful; and, taking pity on the man’s fate, he performed the customary burial rites for the deceased. But when some passers-by saw him occupied with burying the corpse, they reported the matter to the officials in Philippopolis. They subsequently apprehended Marcian as he arrived, and set themselves up as judges of his murder. Since the circumstances made the case seem probable, and the conjectures formed at the outset, along with the truth itself, carried more weight than the speech of Marcian denying the crime laid against him, he would have paid the penalty for homicide, had not a certain person, by divine providence, been brought into the court as the perpetrator of the slaughter, who, by paying the penalty for the crime with his own head, preserved the head of Marcian.
