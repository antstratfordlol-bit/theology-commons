1. Si vis veritatem discere, citharœdi exemplum imitare. Inferius enim ille caput inclinat, et cantui aurem præstat, plectrum manu movet et, chordis perite sibi invicem respondentibus, cithara melodiam reddit, et exsultat citharœdus, tanquam mollis dulcedine captus.
2. Sit tibi, o studiosissime vitis cultor, evidens exemplum, et ne diffidas. Si enim vigiles inde, ut citharœdus, ad cordis nempe profundum, facile quod inquiris, poteris habere. Anima enim divino amore capta, ad ea quæ retro sunt, converti non potest. «Conglutinata est, inquit divinus David, anima mea post te.»
3. Cor tuum citharam esse cogita, dilectissime: chordas autem sensus, plectrum vero cogitationem quæ per rationem plectrum continuo movet; quod quidem est Dei memoria, ex qua ineffabilis lætitia animæ supervenit et puræmenti divinos splendores tanquam in speculo ostendit.
4. Nisi corporis sensus debilitemus, non saliet in nobis viva illa aqua, quam Dominus mulieri illi Samaritanæ largitus est. Quærens enim aquam hanc sensibilem, aquam vitæ invenit in seipsam intus salientem. Quemadmodum enim terra naturaliter aquam habet et simul diffundit, ita et cordis nostri terra aquam salientem, illud nempe paternum lumen, quod Adam ille per suam inobedientiam amisit.
5. Quemadmodum ex perenni fonte, aqua illa viva et saliens ex anima scaturit; quæ in divini viri Ignatii anima habitans, has voces ipsi præbebat: Non est in me ignis materiæ amans, sed aqua efficax et loquens.

Anima enim quæ divino Christi ardore vulnerata est, cum ut cognatum sequitur.

1. If you wish to learn the truth, imitate the lyre-player as an example. For he lowers his head, and applies his ear to the song, and plucks the plectrum with his hand, and at the same time, as the strings respond skillfully to one another, the lyre gives forth the melody, and the lyre-player exults, as if captivated by the sweetness of the honey.
2. Let the example be clear to you, O most diligent worker of the vineyard, and do not doubt; for if you watch from there as the lyre-player does, towards the depth, that is, of the heart, you will easily be able to possess what you are seeking. For a soul captured by divine love to its very depths cannot turn back to what is behind. “For my soul,” says the divine David, “cleaves to thee.”
3. Imagine for me, beloved, that the heart is the lyre, the senses are the strings, and the intellect is the plectrum, which by means of reason moves the plectrum continuously; this is the memory of God, from which an ineffable joy comes to the soul, and it portrays the divine rays to the pure mind as in a mirror.
4. If we do not close the senses of the body, the living water which the Lord granted to that Samaritan woman would not spring up in us. For while seeking this sensible water, she found within herself the water of life springing up. For just as the earth naturally possesses water and at the same time brings it forth, so too does the earth of the heart, by nature, possess this springing and bubbling water, as it were the paternal light, which that Adam lost through his disobedience.
5. Just as water from an ever-flowing spring, so does the living and leaping water spring forth from the soul; which, indeed, having dwelt in the soul of the God-bearing man Ignatius, prepared him to say: “There is no fire in me that loves matter, but water that is living and speaking.”
6. This blessed, and indeed thrice-blessed, intellectual watchfulness of the soul is like water leaping and springing up from the depth of the heart; and the water given from the source fills the source, and that which springs from the heart, and
so to speak, is moved unceasingly by the Spirit, makes the whole inner man full of divine dew and spirit, and renders the outer man fiery.
7. The mind that has been purified from external things, and has wholly subjected the senses through ancestral virtue, remains motionless like the axis of the heavens, always looking toward the depth of the heart as a center, and governing the head; it looks thither, having, as it were, the rays of the understanding as arrows drawing divine thoughts from thence, and subjecting all the senses of the body.
8. Let no one, hearing [of these things], among those who are uninitiated or in need of milk, lay hold of such forbidden things out of season. For the holy Fathers have judged those who seek after the things of the season before the season, and who hasten to enter, as it were, the harbor of stillness without possessing the things they ought to possess, to be suffering from a kind of mental error; and nothing more than that. For it is impossible for one who does not know his letters to read from a tablet.
9. That which is moved by the divine Spirit in the soul through struggle, renders the mind tranquil, crying, "Abba, Father"; but that [divine movement] is without form or figure; yet it transforms us with the brightness of the divine light, and refashions us as is fitting, together with the burning of the divine Spirit; but it also changes and alters us, as He alone knows how, by divine authority.
10. The mind that has been purified through watchfulness is easily darkened if it does not depart entirely from external things through the continuous memory of Jesus. But he who has harmonized action with contemplation—that is, the guard of the mind—does not reject tumult; that is, he does not cast out [the presence of] confusing or even non-confusing noise. For the soul that has been wounded by the divine ardor of Christ follows Him as its beloved.
11. Those who live in the world must restrain the passions of the flesh and their stirrings, or else be at peace with reason, according to the verse: "Be still, and know," and recognize that this is possible. But it is impossible to destroy or dissipate them [entirely]. The eremitic life, however, knows how to clearly uproot them.
12. Of leaping water, one part has a sharper motion, while the other has a tranquil and slower one. Therefore, the former cannot easily be muddied because of the speed of its motion; and if it is slightly muddied, it is more easily purified again, since it has such motion. But when the flow of water has diminished so that it becomes very small, it is not only muddied, but it remains almost motionless; for it needs a certain new cleansing, so to speak, and movement.
13. To those who are beginners, and those who are moral and practical, the demon enters through noise that is confused or even articulated. But to those who are engaged in contemplation, he presents certain fantasies, so that it seems to them that he is coloring the air like light; at other times he brings them forth as fire, so that by the contrary part he may lead away
the athlete of Christ into error.
14. If you wish to learn how one ought to pray, contemplate the end of attention, or even of prayer, and be not deceived. For the end of this, dearly beloved, is unceasing compunction, contrition of heart, and love for one’s neighbor. The contrary, however, is manifest: the thought of desire, the murmuring of detraction, hatred toward one’s neighbor, and all such things.
OF THE LORD CALLISTUS TELICOUDES ON THE PRACTICE OF QUIETUDE
It is not possible to repent without quietude; nor is it possible to attain purity without withdrawal; nor, amid the association and sight of men, is it possible to be accounted worthy of the association and contemplation of God. Therefore, those who make it their care to repent of the errors they have committed, to be purified from the passions, and to attain and enjoy the association and contemplation of God—the end and goal of those who live according to God, and the earnest, if I may so speak, of the eternal inheritance of God—pursue quietude with all their might. They make it their concern to withdraw and to flee from men, with all that is in the soul correctly disposed; and hence, for these, there is a beginning: in quietude, sorrow, self-reproach, and the condemnation of oneself, so that one may become more purified; vigils, standing in prayer, temperance, and bodily labor, the end of which is altogether the shedding of tears falling from eyes that feel humbly, in the compunction of the heart. And thus, they apply themselves to purification and attain it through practice. In these, again, the end is the peace of the thoughts, just as in those, as we have said, the shedding of tears. And thence the mind begins, in a natural way, to examine the natures of things, to contemplate the art of God, to conceive a divine notion, and to inquire into the power, the wisdom, the glory, the goodness, and all other things that can be examined concerning God; and it enters into the mysteries of the Scriptures, and tastes of supernatural goods, and enjoys the supercelestial beauty, and is formed—

14. If you wish to learn how one ought to pray, contemplate the end of attention, or even of prayer, and be not deceived. For the end of this, dearly beloved, is unceasing compunction, contrition of heart, and love for one’s neighbor. The contrary, however, is manifest: the thought of desire, the murmuring of detraction, hatred toward one’s neighbor, and all such things.
OF THE LORD CALLISTUS TELICOUDES ON THE PRACTICE OF QUIETUDE
It is not possible to repent without quietude; nor is it possible to attain purity without withdrawal; nor, amid the association and sight of men, is it possible to be accounted worthy of the association and contemplation of God. Therefore, those who make it their care to repent of the errors they have committed, to be purified from the passions, and to attain and enjoy the association and contemplation of God—the end and goal of those who live according to God, and the earnest, if I may so speak, of the eternal inheritance of God—pursue quietude with all their might. They make it their concern to withdraw and to flee from men, with all that is in the soul correctly disposed; and hence, for these, there is a beginning: in quietude, sorrow,   self-reproach, and the condemnation of oneself, so that one may become more purified; vigils, standing in prayer, temperance, and bodily labor, the end of which is altogether the shedding of tears falling from eyes that feel humbly, in the compunction of the heart. And thus, they apply themselves to purification and attain it through practice. In these, again, the end is the peace of the thoughts, just as in those, as we have said, the shedding of tears. And thence the mind begins, in a natural way, to examine the natures of things, to contemplate the art of God, to conceive a divine notion, and to inquire into the power, the wisdom, the glory, the goodness, and all other things that can be examined concerning God; and it enters into the mysteries of the Scriptures, and tastes of supernatural goods, and enjoys the supercelestial beauty, and is formed—

14. Si vis discere quomodo orare oporteat, intuere attentionis finem aut etiam orationis, et noli errare. Hujus enim finis, dilectissime, est indesinens compunctio, cordis contritio, in proximum charitas. Contrarium autem manifestum est, cupiditatis cogitatio, calumniae murmur, odium in proximum, et alia istiusmodi.
Non licet poenitere absque tranquillitate; non licet puritatem assequi sine solitudine; neque cum frequentatione ac visu hominum possumus digni haberi qui cum Deo conversemur cumque contemplemur. Ideo qui poenitere curant de culpis quas admisere, et a cupiditatibus mundari, et assequi et gustare Dei conversationem contemplationemque (qui terminus est eorum qui secundum Deum vivunt, et pignus, ut ita dicam, aeternae Dei haereditatis), totis viribus tranquillitatem persequuntur; et recedere ac fugere homines multi faciunt, omnibus quae in anima sunt recte dispositis; inde sane iis principium est, in tranquillitate dolor, proprium sui vituperium et accusatio, ita ut magis mundentur; vigiliae, stabilitas, temperantia, et corporalis labor, quorum finis omnino est lacrymarum effusio ex oculis humilia sentientibus decidens, nempe in cordis compunctione.
