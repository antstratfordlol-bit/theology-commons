MAXIMUS PLANUDES CPOLITANUS MONACHUS — NOTITIA (Fabrie. Bibliotheca Græca ed. Harles., t. XI, p. 682.) — Maximus Planudes, monachus Constantinopolitanus, inter eruditissimos suæ ætatis, apud Andronicum Palæologum seniorem gratiosus, ab eodem ad Venetos a. 1327 ablegatus est cum Leone Orphanotropho, ut ex Pachymeris libro IX Ristor. Allatius, De consensu, pag. 841, adnotavit. Vixit adhuc a. 1353 (a). Scripta ejus hæc mihi innotuere : Versa in Græcam linguam. 1. Ciceronis Somnium Scipionis. Vide Lambeeium V, pag. 155, necnon Macrobii in idem somnium Commentaria. Ms. in bibl. regis Christianissimi, in Seguieriana sive Coisliniana. 25. *Anthology of Greek Epigrams* revised by Planudes. For no one can persuade Jo. Harduin, who on page 495 of his *Selected Works* writes that he was not merely an editor, as is commonly held, but more truly the author of the anthology. Nevertheless, he is considered the author of the lemmata which are prefixed to the epigrams, as noted by Huetius, p. 34, and Fabricius, who even cites concerning the title *Anthology* the dissertation of Benedictus Averanius, Vol. I, Part I of his *Works*, and in his praise, dissertation 21. He imported a new form into the anthology of Cephalas, such that he distributed it, following the example of Agathias, into seven books, and yet he did not include the same things that Agathias had in each volume. For he omitted many poems, and he either cut out or interpolated very many obscene ones. And in the Greek scholia prefixed to Book VI: "In this seventh section are contained certain*hetaeric* [courtesan] apophthegms. Some as encomia, some as letters, some just as each happened to occur, those which do not incline toward the more unseemly and shameful. For Planudes says that he left out many such things that were in his copy." However, Fulvius Ursinus noted in his own codex: "But even these exist in the old copy by Angelus Colleotius" (a). But as for Planudes cutting out the obscene things, he did that well and prudently, nor was it fitting to repeat them and, as it were, endorse the character of a monk. Indeed, since he was neither a historian nor a good poet, he ought not to have interpolated, much less, while proscribing numerous obscene passages of Meleager and Philippus, to have received from the more recent age of Silentiarius, Macedonius, and others, things no less chaste than those. He did this, therefore, so that he might leave many things and cut others out, led not so much by considerations of modesty as by the need for brevity, and by the intention of reducing the volume of Cephalas to a smaller bulk. Thirteen books were published from those which he had written to Dionysius—I know not who he was (Diophantus calls him "most honorable")—and which John Regiomontanus, Pref. to *Albuguanum*, long ago testified were preserved in the Vatican manuscripts, along with six books of *Arithmetical Problems* and one book *On Polygonal or Multangular Numbers* (a). With marvelous subtlety, he resolves questions designed even more subtly by a new analytical method, which he himself acknowledges was unknown before his time; modern writers call it Algebra, borrowed from the Arab Gebir, and falsely attribute it to the Arabs as inventors, even though they certainly received it from the Greeks, as they did most other things (b). In the sixth book, he even begins to apply his arithmetical speculations to geometry, following the example of Euclid, whom learned men have criticized in this regard for no good reason (c).
