MALCHUS, Philadelphiensis, ex illa Philadelphia quae in Palaestina est, ut recte et nomine viri Syriaco, cum plura ita vocarentur oppida, infert Fabricius, sophistam Constantinopoli egit (46). Ejus historiam, qua Priscum continuavit, ab a. 474, decimo septimo Leonis imp., usque ad mortem Nepotis (47), qui a. 480 occisus est, septem annorum res totidem libris, quos Byzantiaka inscripsit, persecutam Photius legit: atque intra hoc temporis spatium eclogae de legationibus, quasque ex aliis titulis Suidas servavit, comprehenduntur: ut certum sit litteratas Constantini Porphyrogeniti operas nihil amplius habuisse. Monet tamen Photius, his libris opus ad terminum quem auctor sibi proposuisset perductum non esse; ipsum innuere plura se additurum si vita suppetat. Cum igitur Suidas Malchi historiam usque ad Anastasium deductam esse scribat, non contradicat, si quis sibi persuaserit ultima parte truncatam ad Photium Constantinumque pervenisse; integram ei innotuisse qui paulo post Justinianum imp. lexicon de scriptoribus composuit: quod Suidas compilavit. Edidit certe Malchus mortuo demum Zenone, cujus ignaviam probraque mansurae infamiae tradidit: ac tanta quidem cum libertate ut etiam sub miti Anastasii imperio mirandum sit majestatis poenam evasisse qui talia contra decessorem principis, praesertim qui Augustae maritus fuerat, auderet. Verum non unicum hoc est sub imperatoribus Byzantinis mirandae in scribendo libertatis exemplum; fortasse a contemptu litterarum ortum: nam Tiberius, valde in iis versatus, quaecunque edebantur anxie scrutabatur, crudeliterque vindicabat. Magis etiam miramur Eunapium non morte poenas dedisse pro his quae quemvis Christianum non minus quam principes laederent. Malchum non maledicentiae, sed veritati studuisse cernimus. Difficilior ad expediendum inde oritur quaestio quod Malchus historiam a Constantino M. inchoasse a Suida dicitur: in quod tam disertum testimonium suspicio erroris eo minus cadit quod Photius e primi libri initio constare docet, alios ejusdem auctoris atque argumenti praecessisse. Casus autem efficere non potuit ut pars superstes operis initio ac fine truncati inciperet ubi Priscus substiterat: itaque necesse est aut partem quae praecederet ab aliquo qui catenam historiae conficiebat, cujusmodi plures exstitisse constat, recisam; aut res a Constantino M. usque ad Prisci finem diverso opere comprehensas fuisse. It is necessary, however, for ambassadors, when they have come to those to whom they are sent, to show themselves humane and kind, magnanimous and liberal, as much as their faculties can bear, to praise equally their own things and those of the enemy, and not to hold the affairs of those among whom the ambassadors are in low esteem. It is also necessary for ambassadors to administer affairs with the greatest moderation and to serve the times, and not to accomplish commanded things by force, unless by chance they have been ordered to reach the end by any means whatsoever. Namely, if someone is sent to bring gifts to neighbors, as friends, and discovers them doing hostile things: he has retained the gifts with the letters, and brought forth benevolent speeches instead of gifts. Someone will say, and not undeservedly, that it would have been more appropriate for the ambassador to hand over the gifts, by which he might appease and soften the hostile spirit of the enemies: or indeed to retain the more precious ones, but to give others, but that he should not have retained everything in his own power with himself, with this temperament, that he might indeed wish to suppress and cut back the malevolence of the enemies, but not to render them increased and stronger in resources. The ambassador is indeed tested before he is sent, if the heads of the embassy are proposed to him and he is questioned, how he is going to conduct each one, whether in this way, or otherwise, as things may happen to him.

This was around Lake Moeris: perhaps the same as that which Palladius in the *Life of Chrysostom*, p. 195, calls *Ὄασιν τὴν γείτονα τῶν Μαζίκων*, and John, deacon of the Roman Church, in the *Lives of the Fathers*, ch. 12, calls *Oussa*, where there is a race of Mazyces: for the Mazyces inhabited Marmarica, which is adjacent to this, often hostile to the Pentapolitans, as we have said elsewhere. Josephus in *Against Apion* 11, 3, affirms that Apion was born *ἐν Ὀάσει τῆς Αἰγύπτου*, who, when in ch. 4 he professes him to have been born *ἐν τῷ βαθυτάτῳ τῆς Αἰγύπτου*, left it uncertain whether he meant this or the greater Oasis. It is strange, however, that the Mareotic nome is passed over by Hierocles, which other *Notitiæ* and the Acts of the Councils often mention. Incidentally, we will note the ridiculous error of Corderius, who translates these words of John Philoponus, *Book 5 on the Creation of the World*, ch. v: "The rising of the Nile happened to be very slight, and the lake called Maria, not having much depth due to the lack of water," [translating] *Ἐλλειπούσης τοῦ Νείλου γέγονεν ἡ ἐπίβασις, καὶ τῆς λίμνης τῆς καλουμένης Μαρίας βάθος οὐκ ἐχούσης πολὺ διὰ τὴν τοῦ ὕδατος ἔνδειαν*.
