Ibid. Selinus. We have it as most certain from Ulpian and Xiphilinus that it was called Trajanopolis because Trajan had reached the end of his life there. The city later returned to its original name, the site of which Basil of Seleucia describes excellently in Miracles of St. Thecla, 11, 11: "Selinus is a city, small indeed and maritime, once very great and formerly prosperous from peace, but an object of envy both then and now to its enemies." He says it is a small city in his time and maritime, once, when there was peace, great and happy and therefore exposed to the envy of enemies. "This city," he says, "is therefore girded by the sea as if by a natural trench not made by hands, and a precipitous cliff, like a helmet on a head, guards it and does not make it accessible to enemies." Thus, since it was surrounded by the sea, as if by a trench made by nature, and a steep rock defended it like a helmet on a head, it appears to have been in a most fortified location. See Corn. van Binkershoek, Observ. Jur. v, 21.
