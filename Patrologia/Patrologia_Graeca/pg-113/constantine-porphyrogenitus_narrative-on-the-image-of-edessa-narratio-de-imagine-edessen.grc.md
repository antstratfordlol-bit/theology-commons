For the ship in which they were to cross the Euphrates, while it still stood on the shore toward the parts of Syria, as soon as the bishops carrying the divine image and the letter had stepped into it while the storm of sedition still gripped the agitators, it immediately, without a rower, without a helmsman, and without being towed, set off and reached the opposite bank, steered solely by the divine will. This event filled all who were present and saw it with astonishment and wonder, and persuaded them willingly to allow the departure of the sacred pledges.
XXVI. From there, those who were the ministers of the holy burden arrived at Samosata. There were the bishop of Samosata and of Edessa, the first of his presbyters, and other pious Christians, and with them also came the emir’s minister, who was called "the Roman." After they had spent a few days there, having performed many miracles at the site of the sacred pledges, they continued their journey. During the course of the journey, again, countless miracles were wrought by the holy image and the letter of Christ: the blind were suddenly gifted with sight, the lame were shown to be sound, those long bedridden leaped up, and those whose hands were withered went forth with vigorous strength. To sum it up, every disease and infirmity was put to flight; and those who were healed glorified God and praised His wonders.
XXVII. Now, having nearly completed the journey, they arrived at the monastery of the Holy Theotokos, which is also called the house of Eusebius, situated in the theme called that of the Optimates. In the temple of this monastery, the chest containing the wonder-working image was piously and religiously deposited. Many who approached with sincere hearts were healed of their diseases. There also came one possessed by a demon, which the wicked spirit used as an instrument, uttering many things in praise of the image and the letter; just as spirits of the same kind once said to the Lord: "We know who you are, the holy one of Israel." Finally, he delivered this prophecy: "Receive, city of Constantine, brightness and joy; and you, Constantine Porphyrogennetos, your empire." When he had said this, the man was healed and immediately released from the vexation of the demon. Many, however, of these words...


[449] {#pg.113.449}
For since the emperor had sent the first men of the council of state to honor and meet the object of their desire, and many of the imperial guard had gone out with them, it came to pass that the magistri and patricians, along with those of lower rank, heard these things and were witnesses to them. And since the things that were spoken received their fulfillment so quickly, it is worth asking whence the demon obtained this foreknowledge. For they are not believed to possess this by themselves, having slipped away from the divine glory and become darkness instead of light. Or is it plain that, just as the divine power made use of Balaam as a minister for the prophecy of that time—and often used others on other occasions who were not worthy of the matter—for some wise and ingenious dispensation, so too [now] the power in the divine image made use of the demon? And for this reason it revealed what was to come to pass shortly thereafter. But perhaps it is not untimely to have mentioned this, since it happened in the meanwhile. Let us now move on to the next parts of the narrative.
XXVIII. On the fifteenth day of the month of August, when the emperors were, as usual, celebrating the feast of the Translation of the Ever-Virgin and Mother of God, Mary, in her most venerable temple which is in the Blachernae, the ministers of these holy relics arrived there at high dusk, and they placed the chest containing the image and the letter in the upper oratory of that same holy temple. And the emperors, approaching, reverently venerated it, fixing their gaze upon it from without. Then, with honor and imperial guard and many lamps, they carried it to the imperial trireme, and with it they reached the palace. And in the holy temple that is there, which is called the Pharos (perhaps because it is adorned in an extraordinary way, like a shining garment), they deposited it.
On the following day, which was the sixteenth of the month, having again performed their greeting and veneration with awe and piety, the priests and the younger emperors (for the elder, because his strength was failing, had been left at home) took it from there. With psalms and hymns, and a profusion of light, they made their way down to the sea, and, having again placed it on the imperial trireme, they navigated along the very edge of the city, so that by this sea journey it might in some way protect the city. They anchored outside the city wall that lies to the west, where, having disembarked from the ship, the emperors themselves, all the senate, and the chief of the sacred rites, together with the whole assembly of the Church, proceeded on foot. With the appropriate imperial escort, they conveyed that vessel containing the most holy and venerable relics, as if it were another ark—or rather, something even greater than an ark. Having traveled outside the walls as far as the Golden Gate, and then entering into the city from there, with the loud sound of psalms and canticles and spiritual songs, and with dancing, and the infinite light of lamps, and the gathering of the whole populace, [they performed] the procession.
