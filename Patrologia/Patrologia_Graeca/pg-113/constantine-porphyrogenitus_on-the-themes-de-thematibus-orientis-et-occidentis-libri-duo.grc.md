Thema I, Anatolicum appellatum
The appellation of the themes, as it seems to me, by no means has the origin that many suppose; for it is neither ancient, nor did any of those who wrote histories make mention of such an appellation as is now in use. But formerly and at the beginning, there were certain troops and legions inscribed by nation, as the legion of the Forty Martyrs was called the Lightning-Bearing A (4), and another of the Marmaritae (5), and another the Pisidic, and another the Thessalic, and another otherwise named, being under a duke and a leader (6), and often also a praepositus (7). And these [existed] once, when the emperors campaigned along with the people, and imposed the yoke of Roman servitude upon those who rose against them; and they besieged and brought to order, or silenced, almost the whole world—like Caesar Julius, like the venerable Augustus (8), like that renowned Trajan, like the great among emperors Constantine and Theodosius, and those after them who embraced Christianity and piety. For it was not fitting, with the emperor present in the army, for a general to be in command. Under leaders and taxiarchs they served, and all those things relating to the enemy depended upon the emperor’s counsel; and the whole people looked to one and the only emperor. But when the emperors ceased to campaign, then they also appointed generals and themes. And here the Roman empire ended, down to the present day. Now, however, the Roman empire having been narrowed (12), that is, the Roman rule, and mutilated in both the east and the west from the time of Heraclius the Libyan, those who held power after him did not [achieve…].

The old province (37) under a consiliarius, five cities. NOTES OF ANSELM BANDURI (28) What Porphyrogenitus here draws from Hesychius regarding the indictions are trifles and old wives' tales. See what we have written about the Indictions in the first Commentary, in *Antiquitates CP.*, p. 473, no. 21. Concerning Hesychius of Miletus, however, and his writings, Joannes Meursius treats this more extensively in the beginning of his *Notes to the book on Famous Men*, by the same author, Hesychius. (29) This passage from Nazianzen, cited here by Porphyrogenitus, is found in his panegyric: not far from the beginning, where it has: "At the Holy Lights of the Epiphany... the terrifying and dark apparitions of Hecate's specters, and the underground pastimes and divinations of Trophonius? Or the ravings of the Dodonean oak? Or the sophistries of the tripod? Or the divinatory draught of Castalia? This alone they did not divine: their own silence." (30) The Greek scholiast of the aforementioned panegyric of Gregory Nazianzen states: "There is a Castalian fountain near Antioch, over which Apollo also presides. Therefore, a certain oracle was issued according to such a fountain, of the water, not according to a voice; for it was not some voice that was explained, but simply a sound and spirit being given off, and an outflow; standing before which, and understanding these symbols, some would say these things." (31) Hierocles has: "The province of New Epirus, under a consiliarius, nine cities: Dyrrhachium, formerly Epidamnus." (32) In the editions: *Scampta*. In the manuscripts of Hierocles: *Scampa* and *Scampta*. (33) In Hierocles, *Sceupton* is read. (34) But only four cities are enumerated under Constantine, the fifth perhaps being omitted through the negligence of the scribe, but it can be supplied from Hierocles; from whom indeed it is placed in the first position at the roots of Mount Parnassus, sacred to the Muses... from the virgin Castalia, who, fleeing Apollo, was precipitated and turned into a fountain. (35) *Naisus*, *Naissos* and *Naissos* is how this name is written; but it ought to be written with a simple 'o' according to Holstenius, because it is so written in Hierocles, and the gentiles are called *Naisitani* on a coin or lead seal, which the same Holstenius had seen in Rome with Baron Gualdus. But as for what Porphyrogenitus says, that Naisus is the fatherland of Constantine the Great, he seems to have drawn this from Stephanus of Byzantium, who writes the same; although there is controversy among writers concerning the fatherland of Constantine the Great: for some say he was born in the town of Naisus in Dacia, others in Trasus, others in Drepanum, a city of Bithynia on the Nicomedian gulf; others, indeed, in the Gauls; others finally in Britain. (36) And in the manuscript of Constantine is read *Remesiana*; for besides Hierocles, *Nemisiana* is found in the *Antonine Itinerary*. (37) A corrupt passage, if I am not mistaken, both here and in Hierocles, where it is read: "The province which is... under a consiliarius, five cities." Which Holstenius interprets and emends thus: "The province which is at... (namely, the bank of the Danube, and is Dacia Ripensis)."
