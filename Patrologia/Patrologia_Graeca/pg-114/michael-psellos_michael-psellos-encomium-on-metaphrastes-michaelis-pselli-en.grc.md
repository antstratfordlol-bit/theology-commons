A nee iis, qui linguae ornamento utuntur nimis rhetorice, nec iis qui suum studium posuerunt in disciplinis, sed nec iis, qui studuerunt naturae contemplationi, quod nec iis dicendi genus um mistio eluceret, nec figurae dicendi sint aspersae, neque ulla naturae dubitatio ac solutio, neque linearum demonstrationes cum importunis nominibus, neque Philosophorum sententiae quae subjectum verbis in altum attollant. Volunt enim qui sunt sapientia supra modum dediti, omnia scribi ad ostentationem, non ad morum correctionem. Ego autem dico hujus viri scripta nullo horum esse privata; sed quoniam non ad ostentationem comparabatur ejus scriptio, ideo non ponuntur frequenter, neque statim a principio, neque ubique, B neque apud omnes [118], sed quando res dat occasionem, usus iis, quae postulat oratio, redit statim ad scopum propositum. Ejus enim oratio neque ad sophisticum intuetur jactantium ac forensem apparatum, neque ad artificiosam naturalium rerum excogitationem, neque ad linearum contemplationem, aut curiosiorem numerorum intelligentiam. Sed neque est ei studio orbis motus, aut quemadmodum inerrans mundus procedat circa stellas errantes : sed neque sunt ei curae consonantes aut semivocales, neque qui per eas fit concentus. Propterea haec quidem amandat tanquam supervacanea; nonnunquam tamen eis utitur, ac in tempore. Veritati autem adhaeret, et a falso alienae narrationi. Dictionis C autem sciebat multos modos: usus est autem eo qui sufficiebat ad auditionem simul et bonorum et malorum, et utrisque partibus simul satisfaciens : nam dictionis quidem numero et pulchritudine eruditum sic attraxit auditorem, et satis delinit gratiarum illecebris : eo autem, quod esset clara et luculenta oratio, effecit, ut aures imperitorum ab ejus voce penderent. Ambos autem induxit breviter et probabiliter. The writers who before this blessed man recorded the deeds of those others did not attain to their magnificence; but partly they lied about them, and partly, being unable to provide suitable discourse for the facts, they wrote them up in a way that was   good but unworthy of ambition, neither grounding them in a beautiful conception nor adorning them with suitable words, nor accurately describing either the savagery and cruelty of those who punished them or the quickness of the martyrs in giving their answers. Hence it came about that some could not bear even to read the things that had been committed to writings, while to others what was said provided material for laughter; and the unloveliness of the composition, the absurdity of the thought, and the lack of connection, along with the worthlessness and baseness of the diction, were disagreeable to those who heard them, and they were loathed rather than accepted; and because of those who had written them, the wonderful struggles   and trophies of the servants of Christ were held up to ridicule for us. And to speak of this aspect and allow myself a brief digression, I am well aware that this man’s works are not highly prized either by those who employ the embellishments of language with excessive rhetorical art, or by those who have devoted their efforts to the mathematical sciences, or again by those who have pursued the contemplation of nature, because no brilliant blending of rhetorical styles appears in them, nor are they sprinkled with figures of speech; nor do they contain any question concerning nature and its solution, geometrical demonstrations with their troublesome terminology, or pronouncements of philosophers that elevate the subject through lofty language. For those who are excessively devoted to learning want everything to be written for display, not for the correction of character. I maintain, however, that this man’s writings lack none of these things; but because his writing was not designed for display, they do not occur frequently, or immediately at the beginning, or everywhere, or before every audience [118]. Rather, when the subject offers an opportunity, he employs what his discourse requires and at once returns to his intended purpose. For his discourse looks neither to the sophistical and forensic apparatus of those who parade their accomplishments, nor to ingenious speculation about natural phenomena, nor to the study of geometrical figures or an overcurious understanding of numbers. Nor does he devote himself to the movement of the heavens, or to the way the sphere of the fixed stars moves around the wandering stars; nor does he concern himself with consonants or semivowels, or with the harmony produced through them. He therefore sets these things aside as superfluous, though he sometimes employs them at the appropriate moment. He adheres instead to truth and to narrative free from falsehood. He knew many styles of expression, but employed the one that would suffice for the hearing of good and bad alike, satisfying both groups at once. Through the rhythm and beauty of his language he attracted the educated listener and amply charmed him with the allurements of grace; through the clarity and lucidity of his discourse he made the ears of the uneducated hang upon his voice. He won over both groups with brevity and persuasiveness. For my part, lest I say nothing by concealing what falls short of the truth, I know that there are many supremely beautiful styles of speaking: those, I mean, of the more accomplished orators, whose example is not set before the ordinary public for imitation because of the sonority and rhythm of their discourse. Yet I do not know whether anyone has ever adapted a finer manner of expression to the writings he had undertaken to compose. Even if one of those outstanding in both expression and thought had taken up subjects of this kind, he would indeed have produced a more meticulous and elaborate discourse, but not, I maintain, one equally suited and appropriate to its hearers. For the opening of his discourses immediately touches upon the subject, and as he proceeds through the material he makes clear the purpose of his writing; in some discourses, after summing up the entire subject, he immediately divides it into its parts. He adapts himself to persons and circumstances: all his discourses have the same coloring and a single quality of expression, but his portrayal of character varies in many ways.

Consider, O my miserable and most wretched soul, the most dreadful judgment seat of the great trial. For you are about to stand naked and exposed, giving an account for what we have done in life, whether good things or bad, unjust or just. If you have been merciful while living, you shall receive mercy; or if you have given out of necessity, you shall likewise be saved. God is no respecter of persons; for he assigns equal rewards. If you have covered someone naked in this life, and have pitied the needy, and visited the sick, and filled the hungry, and satisfied the thirsty, and prepared a dwelling for the homeless and wanderers, the Judge will repay you with interest and grace. Likewise, he will make you a stranger to all kinds of goodness if you have not at all pitied any of the needy. If you have not filled the poor man, the hungry and the thirsty. But you, O soul, being attentive and reflecting on these things, open your bowels of compassion to those in need. Distribute things that perish, so that you may gain things that remain. Lend pure love and faith to God, O soul, and love your neighbor as yourself, so that we may be deemed worthy of the kingdom above.
