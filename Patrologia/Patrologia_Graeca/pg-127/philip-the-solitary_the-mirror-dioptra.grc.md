DIOPTRAM JAC. PONTANI PRÆFATIO.
Qui, quoniam Latinis auribus parum noti sunt, et eos multis locis rerum subtilitas sermionisque Romani egestas non admitteret, soluta et perpetua oratione, non sine exemplo interpretandos censuimus. De paucis singulorum librorum capitibus multo plura fecimus : cuique lemma, sive summam suam nostris verbis, uberiusque et accuratius aliquanto, quam scriptor ipse fecerat, præfiximus, quo omnia luculentiora fierent planiusque perciperentur : tametsi nihilominus ad quædam promptius intelligenda graviores et severiores illas scientias libasse opus erit. Œconomia non immerito cuipiam admirationem movebit, cur nimirum, cum hosce libros in dialogo conscriberet, ANIMÆ interrogandi ac discendi, CARNI respondendi ac discendique partes dederit : et illam discipulam, hane magistram esse voluerit in hujuscemodi rebus tam sanctis ac spiritualibus, addo etiam, tam eruditis, cum contra potius faciendum fuisse videatur. Caro quippe est anima inferior, et appetitus sentiens, Augustino anima irrationalis; anima vero hic est superior anima, anima rationalis, ut idem Augustinus nominat, et quæ a Paulo spiritus dicitur. Accedit quod animam caro semper κυρίαν et δέσποιναν, dominam, heram; anima carnem semper δούλην, θεράπαιναν, παιδίσκην, πρόςπολον, ὑπηρέτιδα, servam, famulam, ancillulam, ministram vocat. Ex quo utri potiores partes convenissent, nullo negotio quis rationem concludet. Ut respondeamus aliquid, et scriptorem quoque modo tueamur, primum, illud certum est, cum toto quantum est volumine de anima et corpore agatur, non indecore hanc προςωποποιΐαν introductam : et multa non melius, neque commodius magistræ et discipulæ, aliisve personis adhibitis explicari potuisse. Deinde disputantur ab Anima quæ non apte et recte a Carne, et vicissim a Carne, quæ non tam idonee ab Anima disputarentur, etiamsi illa magistræ, hæc discipulæ officio fungeretur. Quamobrem et nos, cum principio œconomiam istam pro absurda haberemus, commutare personas instituimus; sed in progressu, mutata sententia, eas, ut invenimus, ita reliquimus. Sunt et alia per hos libros, in quibus τὸ πρέπον negligentius servatum animadvertas, nec Philippum satis scivisse, quid esset dialogum scribere. Verum hæc et similia peccata homini ascetico, quique de una sanctimonia nobis persuadenda laboraret, ignoscenda sunt, et sæpe illud cogitan tum, quod mihi libros istius potissimum generis tractanti et grammaticis, quibus puer sum imbutus, frequenter solet occurrere,

PHILIPPUS SOLITARIUS
DIOPTRA
THAT IS, THE RULE OR CARPENTER’S LINE OF THE CHRISTIAN LIFE
TO CALLINICUS THE MONK,
In dialogue form, namely of the SOUL as disciple, and the FLESH as teacher, explained in four books (1).
With the notes of Jakob Gretser.
JAKOB PONTANUS’S PREFACE.
This volume, inscribed *DIOPTRA*, a term for a geometer's instrument with which they measure altitudes from a distance, such as what is called the geometric quadrant and the staff of St. James, we have, I think, quite appropriately called the *Rule*, or *Carpenter's Line*, of the Christian life, in accordance with the subject matter discussed therein. Having found this volume in the Augsburg library, I judged it deserving—all the more so—of being rendered in the Latin tongue, that it might reach those who are studious of virtue and the Christian life. Its author and begetter is Philippus Solitarius, who testifies in the fourth book that he finished it in the year of our Lord 1105, at which time Alexius Comnenus was in the sixteenth year of his reign. The rest may be learned from the prefaces of Psellos, of Phialites the corrector, and of the author himself. I could have greatly wished that this Phialites had shown greater accuracy in that emendation. Philippus breathes piety everywhere, and he burns with the zeal to make us more grateful to God. He made use of political verses, regarding which we discoursed in the preface to the *Orations of Simeon*, translated by us last year and brought to light.
Jakob Gretser’s notes.
It is cited in *Turco-Graecia*, fol. 198, a book inscribed *Dioptra*, consisting of political verses, and containing a dispute between soul and body as to why they do not prepare themselves for dying. Its beginning is reported as this:
Πῶς κάθῃ, πῶς ἀμεριμνεῖς, πῶς ἀμελεῖς, ψυχή μου.
Ὁ χρόνος σου πεπλήρωται, ἔξελθε τοῦ σαρκίου.
Ποῦ τραπεζῶν ἁβρότητες, μαγείρων μαγγανείαι;
Ποῦ τῶν αὐτῶν τὰ θέλγητρα, τύμπανα καὶ λύρα;
Ποῦ ἡ χαρὰ ἡ πρόσκαιρος τοῦ βίου ἡ ματαία;
Ὡς ὄναρ παρελήλυθεν, ὡς καπνὸς διελύθη.
*How do you sit, how are you free of care, how are you neglectful, my soul? / Your time is fulfilled, depart from the flesh. / Where are the delicacies of tables, the magic of cooks? / Where are the delights of the same, the drums and the lyre? / Where is the ephemeral joy of the vain life? / Like a dream it has vanished, like smoke it has dissolved.*
But since our *Dioptra* has a far different beginning, we do not rashly suspect that it is different from that which *Turco-Graecia* mentions. The first chapter contains an illustrious passage against ancient and modern sectarians concerning the necessity of works for salvation; the commendation of which, when the heretical author of *Turco-Graecia* found it in his own *Dioptra* as well, he could not restrain himself from smearing this stain upon the margin of his book: Μοναστικαὶ ἀσκήσεις σωματικαὶ καὶ ψυχαὶ (Monastic exercises are corporal and animal); "Ἔργοις μὴ πίστευε (Do not believe in works). Θεοῦ σώζει χάρις εἴη, ἣν πίστις δέχεται (May it be the grace of God that saves, which faith receives). Πίστιν δείκνυσι τὰ ἔργα (Works manifest faith). This appendix these men added, after they had perceived what an immensity of absurdity that phrase about "faith alone," with all works excluded, would drag along with it.
PREFACE OF JACOBUS PONTANUS TO THE DIOPTRA
Because these matters are little known to Latin ears, and because the subtlety of the subject matter and the poverty of the Latin language would not in many places admit of a literal translation, we have determined to interpret them in free and continuous discourse, and not without example. Of the few chapters of the individual books, we have made many more; to each, we have prefixed a lemma, or summary in our own words, somewhat more abundantly and accurately than the writer himself had done, so that everything might be rendered clearer and more plainly perceived, although nonetheless, to understand certain things more promptly, there will be the need to have tasted those graver and more severe sciences. The arrangement will not undeservedly move some to wonder: why, namely, when he was writing these books in dialogue form, he gave the Soul the task of questioning and learning, and allowed the Flesh to respond and teach, and wished the former to be the disciple and the latter the mistress in such holy and spiritual matters—I might even add, such learned ones—when it would seem that the opposite should have been done. For the Flesh is indeed the inferior of the Soul, and its sentient appetite, or, in Augustine’s terms, the irrational soul; while the Soul here is the superior soul, the rational soul, as that same Augustine names it, and what is called "spirit" by Paul. It is added that the Flesh always calls the Soul A *kyrian* and *despoinan*, mistress and lady, while the Soul always calls the Flesh *doulen*, *therapainan*, *paidisken*, *prospolon*, *hyperetida*, servant, maid, handmaid, and minister. From this, one will conclude without effort for which of the two the more noble parts would have been appropriate.
To answer in some way, and to defend the writer in some manner, first, it is certain that, since the entire volume is concerned with the soul and body, this *prosopopoiia* (personification) was introduced not unsuitably; and many things could not have been explained better or more conveniently by employing a mistress and disciple, or other persons. Furthermore, things are disputed by the Soul that could not have been aptly and rightly disputed by the Flesh, and conversely, things by the Flesh that could not have been so fittingly disputed by the Soul, even if the latter performed the office of mistress and the former that of disciple. Wherefore we, too, although at the beginning we considered this arrangement absurd, set out to change the persons, but in the progress of the work, having changed our opinion, we left them as we found them. There are other things throughout these books in which one may observe that the *prepon* (propriety) was kept somewhat negligently, and that Philip did not know well enough what it was to write a dialogue. But these sins and similar ones are to be forgiven to an ascetic man who laboured to persuade us of one holiness, and it is often necessary to meditate upon that which is wont frequently to occur to me, who handle books of this type especially, from those grammarians with whom I was imbued as a boy: "In living follow the good, in writing the experts."
I have joined the fifth book, which is of few pages and not distinguished by chapters, with the fourth. The notes in the margin—either by Psellus or by Phialites—bearing the sentences of the Holy Fathers, I have received into the text, yet having made a selection and expressed them in a different typeface. For they seemed to bring not a little light and grace to the work itself. Those political verses, lest I make a monster of it, and for other reasons, I have not added. What is adduced more fully from Damascene in Book II, chapter 12, and in chapters 16 and 17 of the same book, we have decided should be attributed to their interpreters. Now, so that you may know those Fathers of whom I was speaking, I shall append a catalogue.
