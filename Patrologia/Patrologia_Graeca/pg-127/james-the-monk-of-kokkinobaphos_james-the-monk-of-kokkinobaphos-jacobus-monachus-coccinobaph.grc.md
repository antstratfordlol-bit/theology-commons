Id quidem et præfata historia indicat: Dona enim Domino duplicia offerebat, dicens intra se: Quod ex mea substantia superabundat, sit omni populo: quod vero est pro debiti mei solutione, sit Domino Deo meo.

ORATION I
On the Conception of the most holy Mother of God and regarding the histories of the twelve tribes of Israel, selected from the sacred Scriptures.
I. Today the earth is illustrated by the raying forth of grace, [it is]
it shines more brightly than the heavens with spiritual brilliancy; it is adorned more than the celestial heaven. For it receives the heaven that is born on high, truly the heaven that is beyond light and more spacious, not the one that undergoes the setting of the sun, but the one that has brought forth for the world the Sun of Justice that knows no setting; the one adorned not with visible stars, but with mental torches. For this is what we celebrate today, this is what has set forth this most splendid feast, this is what has gathered this present dance; this is what has stirred up the word, calling all into its own participation, and gladdens the multitude with the flashing forth of graces. Not receiving the intensity of the light from any participation, but by the distribution of its own [light] it unfolds it to those who gather and demonstrates its brightness, stretching out along with the manifestation of the radiance. And just as this sun which is visible to us has within itself, even when it is hidden from our eyes, the most brilliant light; yet rising and appearing from the steep mountain peaks, it spreads its rays over the most beautiful things of creation, and by their shining around them, it extends the greatness of the wonder: so also this feast today, being over-brightened by the intellectual light of grace, possesses in itself indeed the never-fading brilliance, yet it extends this further, in proportion to the gathering of those who run together and are illuminated. Since, therefore, it shines with these mystical rays of the Spirit, and all who gather together in it are illuminated, it would be better than everything desirable to run to it eagerly and be illuminated in the mind, and to be surrounded by the light that is not covered by a cloud of corruptible things. For such is the brightness that spreads itself out in the present joy; and in this way it sends forth an even more lasting, or rather, a more transcendent radiance to those who appear.
For today, when the expansion of the spiritual cloud is proclaimed, the rays of the light that never sets rise up for the world, and the darkness that dominated in atheism begins to diminish and be driven away. Today, when the construction of the divine tabernacle is announced, the foundations of piety are laid, while the fortresses of impiety are shaken from their very roots. Today, when the royal purple is being woven, the arrival of the King of all is announced. For the earnest-money is already proposed, and the unwed bride receives it.
And the palace has been both brought forth and prepared today. Therefore, the present brightness leads the way for the joy of the ineffable dispensation; and for this reason, we who celebrate beforehand this pre-feast—the cause of the end-result—must, while bright in outward appearance, be yet brighter in the adornment of our souls. We must deck this present festival with praises, leaping more joyfully, rejoicing more spiritually than bodily, and striving to emulate the exultation of the intellectual and divine orders, as we urge all of creation to rise up for these events.
III. But the angelic choral assembly anticipates the word that summons us, and today they celebrate the end-result through which they beheld the mystery; the heavens also anticipate, and they honor the height of this new and most sublime firmament; the luminaries run ahead, and they are enlightened more brightly by the rays of the spiritual sun's cloud; the earth arrives unbidden, and glories and rises up along with its own sprout; nature, leaping at the new wellsprings of waters, honors the one who brings about its sanctification.
IV. Since all things have today offered their own signs of exultation with such confidence, I, with better right, shall call upon the most noble creature, crying out with a resonant voice: "Come, every age and every rank, as much of this worldly order as that which is above it; priests and kings, and you who demonstrate the life and citizenship of heaven while yet in the lower realm, rulers and subjects, elders and youths, virgins, mothers, and the barren, and you who are as infants in your innocence: let us receive and celebrate this saving day with more divine joy." For on this day, the one fitting for all was brought forth from a barren womb—the Theotokos Mary, whom all generations should bless as is our duty.
glorification; on this day from barren wombs sprouted the ornament of the priests, the royal power, the perfection of those who exercise themselves in virtue, the unshakeable order of those who rule and those who are ruled, the understanding of children, the adornment of virgins, the crown of mothers, the fertility of the unfruitful, the solution to sterility—from the sterile blossomed the abundance of good things; the most just fruit from the just abounded.
But what display of praises could be seen? What wondrous honor among the most wondrous could be discovered? Consider, I beseech you, the election of all the just and the prophets, the intimacy with God who chose them, and you will see the incomparable superiority of Joachim and Anna; you will see their dignity, honored and glorified beyond all measure. Look, I say, how their circumstances exceed all comparison; since from their blood, because they surpassed others in virtues or royal eminence, He Himself (the Lord) was clothed in the royal purple of our nature.
V. But it would be useful for the present celebration, and for the illumination of their prerogatives, to set forth a few things from the history which takes its beginning from them; for thus the truth of what is said will shine forth more clearly for us. And it is, so to speak from their very beginning, as follows: "In the histories of the twelve tribes of Israel," it says, "there was a certain Joachim who was very rich." What then does "in the histories" signify? In the genealogies, namely, of the twelve tribes of Israel: and by this it strives indeed to intimate that he derived his royal lineage from his ancestors, unmixed, and that he was noble through both ancestral and personal observance of the laws, and therefore stood out as a certain distinguished and preeminent man among the tribes of Israel. Then, having set forth his wealth as a certain argument for his excellence, the writer also proclaims his singular eminence in justice above all his fellow tribesmen. For, he says, he offered his gifts to the Lord in duplicate. For the offering of double gifts diminished the present and fleeting goods, but it prepared, that they might be accumulated as treasures remaining forever. But, as is manifest, he did not permit fleeting riches to be gathered, storing up only those that were sufficient for daily use. This, indeed, the narrative also demonstrates; for he offered his gifts to the Lord in duplicate, saying within himself: "Let that which is from my substance in abundance be for all the people; and let that which is for the payment of my debt be to the Lord my God."
See how far it extends beyond legal justice! Observe the evangelical manner of life, proper to the apostles, which had been prefigured, or rather, I should say, surpassed, in these just persons. "Let the abundance of my wealth be for all the people," he says; "let nothing surplus to what is necessary remain; let what is beyond need be set before all the people; let the fruits of my prosperity be common to all." Oh, most just counsel and judgment! Oh, riches stored in no-thieftreasure-houses! Thus, from the very beginning, their virtue stood supreme. For he did not look to others, nor did he base these actions on any examples set before him, but by his own self he discovered the good and fulfilled it. And he is not led to such an achievement by ancient precepts and legislation; rather, he himself initiates the performance of noble deeds, becoming a legislator of practice for those who would follow him. See how remarkably these men are preferred before all other just men! And I say "remarkably," not by the gift of the mystery (for grace exceeds every reward of works), but by a comparison with the institutions of their own contemporaries.
VI. But it is pleasing to go on with the rest of the history. For it relates that when he had approached in the customary way to offer his gifts on a certain significant feast day, he was met with the most bitter reproaches from one of his own tribe because of his childlessness. "For it is not lawful for you," he said, "to offer your gifts, since you have not raised up seed in Israel."
Some copies, however, contain: "It is not lawful for you to offer first." It is likely, then, that he, being a just man and conspicuous for his twofold nobility, was accustomed to be given the first place in all things, and even in the offering of gifts. But this was intolerable to the envious, even though they had nothing in his life that they could criticize; for he was known to be not only free from any fault, but also, because of the excellence of his virtue, beyond the reach of any accusation. Therefore, in order to contend against him, they argue that he was not acting according to the prescriptions of the Law, and they object to his being childless. But that these just ones had remained unfruitful up to that time, and then were stirred to obtain offspring through prayer, was of divine providence. For the one fact manifested their confidence in God, when for those who petitioned, the effect of their petition was not delayed; while the other showed the mystery of grace being worked out at a defined time.
VII. Being thus insulted, or rather repulsed, the just man is seized with most grievous sorrow—not because he had been attacked with curses or had suffered a loss of reputation, but because he feared lest some neglect of the divine commandments had brought this rejection upon him before God, and that it was being made manifest through men. To such a degree...
did he consider all things that concerned him as belonging to divine providence! For this reason he did not contend, nor did he quarrel, nor did he object; but he accepted the rebuke through insult, and fled to God, the examiner of hidden things. And he chose the wilderness as a place most suitable for carrying on a conversation with God, not showing himself even to his wife, who was of one mind with him, nor sharing his counsel with her, nor committing the care of his external affairs to those in his household. Who, even in our own times, would have shown such firm hope in God and such contempt for temporal things? Much less in that age, when it seemed arduous to practice even moderate and prescribed virtues.
VIII. And let the things concerning the patriarch Abraham be passed over in silence for now. For in terms of time, as well as the eminence of his virtue, he will perhaps seem to some to have surpassed not only the men of old, but this man as well. Although if one were willing to compare the deeds which we are now setting forth with those, he would find that the present ones in no way fall short of them. For to him, both the migration and the land in which he was to dwell were announced in advance; furthermore, by Him who cannot lie, both the cessation of his childlessness and the ending of Sarah’s barrenness were predicted. And because he already held these matters as if attained on account of the promise, he nourished his hope in the meantime and confirmed his mind to secure a happy outcome through his prayers. For what could seem more effective for lifting up the soul than the promise of God?
But this man, having received no promise as yet, believed that even things impossible could be brought to pass by the Almighty, and that nature could be changed; he fixed his firm hope upon His hidden providence, and therefore, withdrawing himself from domestic cares, he persevered in persisting with prayers to God.
He, indeed, that he might not doubt concerning the future possession of the land and the wondrous increase of his posterity, required some sign; he also inquired by what manner those things that were being promised would be fulfilled. But this man, believing with certainty that such an outcome as one’s confidence in prayer warrants is what will follow, was therefore taught by an angel about the receiving of his offspring, and yet he did not inquire of the angel by what means this would come to pass. For he regarded the message as being the same as the fulfillment of the promise.
But furthermore, it would not be out of place to bring into the middle and contemplate that marvelous sacrifice: how indeed that man acted upon a command, while this man sacrificed of his own free will; and that man, in truth, offered his only-begotten, while this one did so by the reason of the promise. And whereas that man, having been commanded, accepts back the victim, this man in truth...
having consecrated it, offered it as a whole burnt offering, and having given it back, presented it: and finally, the former offered a patriarch of tribes and of just men; but the latter offered the Mother of God, the most worthy Queen of patriarchs. B Truly, such a deed as this exceeds not only this, but any comparison whatever. Do you see, therefore, how greatly the prerogatives of this just man superabound? Consider, moreover, his life spent in solitude, and that abstinence of his, exceeding the powers of nature (which it is more fitting to call an angelic way of life); how strange it was, how unusual, whether you compare his deeds or his words! For history relates that he spent forty days and as many nights constantly without food, nourished only by prayers and hope in God. For he fasted forty days and nights, saying: "I will not descend either for food or for drink."
IX. Such were the deeds of that just man. But those things which Anna accomplished—being joined to him not less by conformity of character than by the bond of marriage—how great and wonderful they are, and how they not only do not fall short of the grace of her spouse, but even advance further! For she had to undergo a double struggle of patience. For both the absence of her husband and the ignorance of the place to which he had withdrawn, as well as the taunts inflicted because of her childlessness, pressed upon her from every side. Contending against these with the aid of hope and faith, she emerged superior. Furthermore, these reproaches because of her sterility were brought against her from two directions: first, indeed, by her fellow-tribesmen, and then also by her own handmaid, which indeed caused a most grievous insult; for reproaches sent by strangers are not wont to affect one in the same way as those which come from domestics, especially if these should be offered by someone of those set under one's hand.
How, then, did that most noble woman conduct herself? Nothing of these things breaks her long-suffering; but casting off the ornaments importunately offered to her (14), and at the same time laying aside her mourning garment, and putting on a pure, nuptial, spiritual robe, adorned with this twofold garment, she persisted in prayers and supplications before Him who is most pure. Nor did she, seeking the interior of the temple, pour out her prayers to the Lord of all, but choosing a quiet place, remote from all noise, in the garden, there covertly, as in an unsearchable inner chamber, she brought forth her prayer, in which she strove to persevere for a long time, groaning from her heart, and, needing more, she practiced it over time. Joachim, however, had obtained no offspring in reality, except for the Holy Mother of God, Mary.
Nevertheless, no one has suspected that she had taken no food. Furthermore, since the scriptural writer of that history is considered a Jew by race and contemporary with the apostles, someone will more rightly judge that his words are to be referred to a certain common manner of speaking, by which he intended to affirm nothing else than what we understand in the cited words of Luke: that her handmaid had offered her mistress certain vain ornaments; which, when Anna refused, she, stirred by anger, broke out into insults, and among other things reproached the barrenness of her mistress.
But why did she not select a chamber in her own home, remote from the bustle, in which she might offer her secrets and her prayers with groans to God? Because by this method she would not have fully satisfied her desire; for she revolved in her mind many things that needed to be poured out through prayer, and it was not permitted her to open these purely to God because of the domestic noise and the annoyances of kinsmen arriving. For this reason, she did not betake herself to the temple, either, to offer there the words of supplication to the Lord, although that place had been designated for all her fellow-tribesmen for prayer. But not even this offered her the opportune quiet she sought, since there were many things in it by which she feared she might be hindered. For, as she reckoned with herself, if the Jews, who brought offerings at the same time, had—after having previously seen her and her husband bringing gifts to God and performing kindnesses for the whole people—conceived envy and assailed them with insults and cast them out of the temple: what taunts would they not have leveled against her now, had they seen her alone and in untimely hours constantly persisting in long prayer? B Nothing, in truth, would have restrained the Jewish madness from accusing her, if found in such a state, of either insanity or drunkenness. For thus, in times past, that blessed woman, namesake of this one—Anna, I say—was severely harassed by the insults of those who seemed more modest than the rest, inasmuch as they exercised the priesthood, as they termed her supplication to God, the highest disgrace, an intoxication. She, indeed, with ardent affection and a sober mind, poured forth those fervent vows; but they took her sobriety for drunkenness, and her divine speech for empty babbling. But this new and most prudent of all, Anna, fearing lest she be compelled to endure anything of this kind while pouring forth her lengthy prayer, abstains from the temple just as she did from the walls of her own house, and, that she might find quiet, selects the most solitary place in her garden, in which she wisely foresaw that she would have the facility to pray without impediment. Perhaps, too, not only for this reason, but also because of the truth now clearly perceived by her regarding what was to befall her, she prefigures it by her very actions. Now, therefore, Anna, thanks to her namesake, through that supplication offered to God in this manner and through that reasonable sacrifice, shows in advance that the time had now begun when men would no longer imagine that the divine ears were encompassed by the ambit of any place, but that prayers were to be poured forth before God, who is everywhere present and fills all things, and that requests were not to be defrauded of their result. This, in truth, the just Joachim also prefigured, while he abstained from the temple and offered that acceptable and admirable prayer upon the mountain; by this making it plain that, as the new grace arrived, the temple would be left desolate of typical shadows, but that the rays of that very light would be spread to all, whosoever they might be.

For from a contrite heart a strange and wonderful voice was heard echoing; for the righteous one, having begun to offer her supplication in an orderly manner, introduces into the discourse, in the order of lamentation, that blessed humility. Wherefore, not comparing herself to those of the same race and of the same tribe, she says that she is deprived of their blessing and grace through unworthiness; but she also signifies that she is utterly alienated from the very brute animals and from inanimate creation in regard to the similarity of that blessing. For this reason, in her solitary prayer, she introduces a mention of birds and quadrupeds, of wild beasts and of the earth, and of the things that grow upon the earth, and of waters and of the creatures that generate in them, in order that she might demonstrate her own insignificance; for towards these, both by nature and by word, the most compassionate providence of the Creator is accustomed to incline in its care. For this reason, the wise one says: "I am more worthless than all born things; for these, by your command, O Master, both enjoy your blessing and grace." But I, as one unworthy, deprived of this splendor of the race, become a stranger to the procreation of the manifold animals; and yet even they have shared in the same glory. The earth is prolific, perpetually pouring forth abundance according to your command; the meadows bring forth fruit, and the fair blossoms of plants put forth flowers, and they yield fruit seasonable and most sweet, and through the pleasing affection of those who enjoy them, they render a tribute of glory to you; and I am deprived of the grace of these, since I consider myself not worthy to be numbered even with these works of your goodness.
XI. Such were the words of Anna, that she might move to pity God, who alone is most merciful. But He anticipates her words; and before the offered supplication is completed, a message of the success of the things that were sought, favorable beyond all hope, is brought to the one asking. For "Behold," it is said, "while she was speaking," angels appearing foretold the birth beyond hope. To Anna, I mean, and to the righteous Joachim on the mountain, angels brought tidings of the conception of her who would surpass the angels themselves in dignity: angels announced the conception to her who was laboring under barrenness. For, he says, "while she was speaking, an angel of the Lord came, saying to her: God has heard your prayer, and you shall conceive and bear; and your seed shall be spoken of in all the world." He heard, who, before the words of the prayer were conceived in the mind, knew these things and weighed them ineffably. He heard the prayer, who prompted the prayer and disposed it to obtain its effect. He heard the contrite heart, He who for this same reason made his abode in that heart. He heard your prayers, He who through the voices of the prophets had already foretold the outcome of these same prayers.

### XII. Oration on the Conception of the Most Holy Mother of God
For, He says, your seed will be spoken of throughout the whole world. It will be spoken of to the world, that much-fabled boast of hers, her uniquely renowned fruit-bearing, that most famous adornment of creatures. The mouth of human nature, that eloquent reasoner, will speak of it—the articulate defense of those who had been silent; she of whom glorious things will be spoken in every rational nature, whose majesty the intellectual powers will hymn and whose glory they will proclaim. Your seed will be spoken of throughout the whole world. For you, He says, O Anna, will bear fruit to God in the abundance of virtues, and you will obtain a fame that will be heard of throughout the lineage of child-bearing. Having been delivered from the reproaches of sterility, you will be numbered and counted among the mothers, nor will you be excluded from the blessing that is considered dear to them. But the fame of your name will not halt even at these limits; nor will the fruits of your prayers and the fruitfulness of nature be spoken of and magnified in Israel alone or among your kinsmen, but it will be heralded throughout the whole world, among kings and potentates, among priests and the righteous, among nations of many tongues, among the wise and the unwise; among so many will your fruit be spoken of, and having been echoed in the ears of so many, it will be magnified in a manner befitting its own miracles.
XIII.
And these things, indeed, the angel said. But what of the blessed and magnanimous Anna? What sort of countersign does she give to the One offering such a blessing? What sort of gifts does she return to the Benefactor? What sort of holocausts of gratitude does she promise to offer? It is not the opulence of votive offerings, not sacrifices of irrational animals, not the firstfruits of crops, nor any other of the customary offerings presented in return, but the very fruit of nature given to her—that very sprout, desired and longed for by her, for whom heroic endurement, for whom persistent prayers and abundant tears were poured forth, and for whom all other things that prepare for a pleasurable life were disregarded.
For she says, "The Lord my God lives; if I give birth to either a male or a female, I will offer it as a gift to the Lord my God, and it shall be ministering unto Him all its days." See the fervent affection of Anna toward God; see her surpassing magnanimity; see how, through this offering, she also demonstrated that she was offering the abundance of all external things to God liberally. For if the fruit of nature given to her was to be offered in such a way, it is obvious that she was offering all things else as well.
(15-16) Petrus Argivus introduces human nature rejoicing, because, whereas until that day when she brought forth Mary she had been barren and cursed—in that she had yielded no good fruit, but only thorns and thistles, that is to say, children corrupted by vice—now at last she had germinated an excellent sprout, Mary, who is exempt from that common contagion. You have here a sentiment not unlike this, where the Deipara is exhibited as the unique wealth of the whole world, a theme often recurring in the writings of the holy Fathers, whereby the condition of the Virgin Deipara is separated from the condition of all other mortals. For Mary is shown as the mouth and patroness of those who, because of shame, were forbidden from lifting up their countenance and speaking. Moreover, it is clearly evident that the guilt meant here is that which affects everyone, both because human nature—which indeed embraces all mankind—is called the mouth of the Deipara, and because the circumstances of the matter itself demand this; for otherwise there would be others besides Mary from whom the shame of guilt was absent. But, first, there is no common guilt of all, except the crime propagated from the first parent. Second, if the Deipara had been subject to the same guilt, then she herself would necessarily be found among those to whom shame imposed silence. Therefore, through these things also, the Virgin Deipara is declared immune from the common guilt of all.

XIV. Such are the advantages and the accounts of those righteous ones. These are the bright characteristics of their virtues, which caused the noble and, more than those who had appeared before, the brilliant beauty of the soul to shine forth. For it was necessary that the incomparable gift among those born of women should proceed from an overriding election; it was necessary that from abundant virtues the most holy wealth should overflow. It was necessary that from such labors such fruit should be gathered, that from a noble root a most noble shoot should spring up, and from a good loins a most goodly progeny should be brought forth—the evergreen adornment of the race, the most beautiful sprout of nature, the high-reaching trunk of the mystery, from which the flower of immortality sprang up and breathed forth eternal fragrance, whose fruit becomes life and incorruption and perpetual stability for all who partake of it. But how glorious is the memory of them that extends through the ages! How constant and indelible remains their glory! How blessed is the election, and how blessed is their approval in virtues, through which it came to pass that they were chosen! For this reason, they were deemed worthy to bring forth the Queen of all, as the fruit of piety and power. It was necessary that from royal nurseries the royal rod should be offered you; it was necessary that from the abundance of virtues your inexhaustible wealth of goods should flow over; it was necessary that you should be the daughter of such parents, and they should be declared the parents of such a daughter. For indeed (You appeared, O Pure One, higher than the heavens, and ever holier than the Seraphim, as you have in a supernatural manner given birth to the Lord and King of all).
Just as you were chosen as Mother of God for all creation, so they were deemed worthy to be preferred before all parents. How super-glorified, then, is the magnificence of this providence! How much more desirable than all desirable things are the good things that have come to be through you!
For the fame of your great works will be spoken of to all; it will be spoken of and proclaimed, not on earth alone, but also in heaven; not to human ears, but it will be made known to celestial intelligences. For David foretold these things, and as though the outcome of future events were already accomplished, he proclaimed it beforehand, expressing the certain truth of those things that were to come through the time that had passed, saying, "Glorious things have been spoken of you, O city of God" [Ps. LXXXVI, 3]. Glorious above all glory that can be narrated; glorious before the celestial powers; glorified and desired by men of the same nature; desired by the patriarchs; revered by the progenitors; recorded and sought after by the prophets. For these reasons all creation rejoices; for these reasons the angelic hosts rejoice with it; for these reasons the entire world is adorned with splendor. O how blessed are those who have attained to enjoying these in a manner worthy of them! For this alone would be justly called enjoyment; this is pleasure that does not waste away—the enjoyment, that is, of your marvels.
XV. But O you who are the cause of present and future joy, O you who are the remarkable proclamation of those who approach you with confidence and honor you, may you, in return for the gladness with which your festivals are celebrated, bestow a heavenly banquet; in return for the eagerness with which we celebrate these things, may you cause your gifts to increase. Fill the present assembly with joy that is both sensible and spiritual; guide the choir that has been gathered together in a more divine manner; suggest to it a harmonious song, such as is the song of those who rejoice and keep the festival in the very heavens. Look upon the mind of each one; examine the fervor of their yearning, and show this to the Lord, who searches the secrets of the conscience. Present our affections to Him who sees the hidden things manifest. He knows indeed everything, as the knower of things invisible, and He looks upon the affection and desires of those who praise you on His account; yet it is His will, and indeed most lovingly so, that these things should both be offered and fulfilled through the intercession of the Mother. This, indeed, is your glorification for His sake, and ours for yours; this is the sign of the grace which the human race has found with Him through you. Therefore, being lifted up by hope, we are not terrified by our humble state, and we forget our base and earthly manner of life. Hence we desire to depart from these depths and hasten toward heaven; hence we have the most certain expectation of eternal things. Therefore, with a most propense mind, we celebrate this illustrious and divine festival, offering up thanksgiving to Him who has provided all things for His own glory, for the honor of His Mother, and for the saving adornment of His subjects.
