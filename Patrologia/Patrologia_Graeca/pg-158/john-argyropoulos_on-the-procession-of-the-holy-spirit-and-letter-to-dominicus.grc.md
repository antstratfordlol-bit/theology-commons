ARGYROPULUS. While the Father begets the Son, by the generative power, as we were saying, the generation is joined to its own power, just as the Son is, and so forth.
AGALLIANUS. To these things we also make answer, saying thus: The Word knows how to call one generation in the Father and the Son, though it is not viewed in the same way in both. For that which is taken from "to beget" is active and corresponds to the identifying mark conjoined with it, the name "Father"; but that which is taken from "to be begotten" and corresponds to the name "Son," which signifies a begotten hypostasis, is not generative. Therefore, in the same way, the power for begetting differs from the power for being begotten, even if the one is in the Father and the other is in the Son. And just as the being of the Father and the being of the Son is one and the same, but it does not befit the Son to be the Father, nor the converse; so, even if we posit that the generative power and the generation are in the Father and the Son with respect to all other things, yet they differ in their definitions, being distinguished relationally, just as all other things are one in the Father and the Son—the one according to the relation of the Giver, the other according to that of the Receiver. And thus, neither must the generation which is in the Father be in the Son, lest the Son be the Father, nor must the generation which is in the Son be in the Father, lest the Father become the Son.
ARGYROPULUS. As also the generative power.
AGALLIANUS. If, therefore, the Father were giving to the Son the generation by which he begets, and the power towards it, the Son would also have to beget. But if the Son does not beget, He does not give Him such power and operation, but rather, one might say, something of the opposites—namely, the power of being begotten, and the passive generation. Therefore, while this man says that the generation is connected to its own power by which the Son is begotten from the Father, one must ask: which generation and which power are we to understand—that which is conceived actively, or that which is conceived passively? For if this is not defined, the argument is utterly hopeless. But according to the foundations of the faith, and as the truth dictates, it would be pious to say that when the Word looks towards the Father, one must receive the power and the generation actively; for it is the property of the Father to beget. But when it looks towards the Son, one must understand both the power and the generation passively; for it is His property to be begotten, according to the theologians. For by being begotten, He receives existence through generation, and for this reason, He is Son. It would not, however, be pious to say that He receives the act of begetting through generation, for He is not the Father.
D ... generative power is given to the Son, not as a power, but as a perfection. It is perhaps sufficient that he himself confessed that the generative power is not given to the Son as a power, but as a perfection. Yet he said afterward that the Son has the generative power—that is, the power to beget—contradicting himself. And not only that, but he also says that the generative and [the Spirit’s] productive [power] is shared with the Spirit. I do not know what happened to him or what moved him to fall into such an absurdity, and to dishonor the divine hypostases so, judging them worthy to be called participants in ineffective powers, in the same way that we speak of an infant as "potentially a grammarian." Thus, the Spirit too [is said] to have come forth from a power, which power, when the Son was being begotten, did not yet have the operation combined with it, but received it when the Son was begotten, and other such things he fabricated.
One might perhaps think that he arranged his argument this way for fear that the unity of the essence might be broken, and that, considering these powers as perfections of nature, he distributed them equally to the divine Trinity of persons; but [he does not do so] equally, if he gives them to the Father alone with the operation, but to the others he gives them as inactive and common. Wherefore he has fallen heavily into the very thing he was trying extremely hard to avoid: for how could things that do not operate equally with the same perfection be of the same essence? But if the Son’s having the generative [power] without operation is sufficient for the unity of the essence, because it is joined to the generation, and if the Holy Spirit has both [generative and productive] without the operations themselves—what is the necessity for the Son to have the productive [power] with the operation, given that many absurdities grow from this? For the unity of the essence, for which he seems to be contending, would be preserved even in this way, in addition to the fact that the absurdities would be kept silent.
But rather, if the generation is with its own power, and the generation according to operation is not given to the Son nor to the Spirit, then neither is the power of it [given] to the Son at the same time as the procession, as the Word was delivered through the saints. Then, as if overturning himself, he says, "The Father shares the powers with those who are from Him as perfections only, not as powers." And what perfection could there be in God that is not also perfected in act, or to which the operation—according to which He never ceases to act—does not belong? Which, then, would have been better: to isolate the productive powers of the divine persons in one person, the Father, so that He alone is producing, while the rest are, as he said at the beginning—rightly saying this—the ones being produced, and to assign the responding passive [powers] to these, which are no less capable of the logic of equality (for there is nothing to the honor of God, in being the begetter, [that is lacking] in being begotten from God), and likewise also concerning the Spirit? Or [is it better] to make the Son a producer only to establish the productive powers—which, as hypostatic properties, are constitutive of the Father’s person and are reckoned to the Father alone—[and] to give them to those who proceed from Him, and after having deprived the Father of His own? But you, unfolding their condensed theology according to your own opinion, have confirmed a dogma—bareheaded, as it were, and separating it from the whole of the Godhead—that is unknown to them and contrary to the words of Orthodoxy. For how could those most divine men have held this, when they had received it neither from the divine tradition, nor from the apostolic teaching, nor from the Church herself; but, on the contrary, had received a definition confirmed by countless bonds and penalties, that the holy Spirit proceeds from the Father, and is co-worshipped and co-glorified with the Father and the Son, and thus to believe and preach? And the implication that you brought in seems to me very bold, namely, when you said: "From the Son, but not in this way." For from this, the duality of the cause is manifest. For if the Father is the cause of the holy Spirit in one way, and the Son in another, and the "in another and another" introduces a difference, and you believe it this way, you have surely fallen into ditheism. For how could there be one and the same cause, given that the persons are distinct, while they proceed—not as they are in the essence—and the modes of the progression are different? And the notion of "by the same power and by procession" seems to me to be the same as saying that two carpenters, coming together by the one power of the same craft residing in them, produce one bed. How much blasphemy is in that, it is not difficult for the wise to understand. ARGYROPULUS. Because if anywhere he [the author] says the Son is a cause and origin of the Spirit’s subsistence, just as it is written in the definition of the sacred eighth ecumenical council at Florence, he does not wish for Him to be an origin or cause simply, but an "origin from an origin" and "cause as a co-cause." AGALLIANUS. How graceful this council would have been—if this is what it must be called—if one were to heed what this man added, instead of the exact opposite, as if he brought forth this blasphemy along with the others. ARGYROPULUS. By dogmatizing the Son as "origin and caused"; for in this way he would have surpassed every heresy in impiety! AGALLIANUS. If he makes the "cause" and "co-cause" two causes, and through the mediation of the divine substance the holy Spirit subsists [as decreed] from the Father, then by understanding it as "proceeding through the Son," he ranks the Spirit with the things of which the Son is an "origin from an origin." But perhaps these things result in the absurdity of those people. And yet, if two blasphemies are poured forth, one greater and one lesser, the lesser is not to be chosen simply because the other is vehemently forbidden, if indeed in sacred theology there must be degrees of such things. Since by power, all of it equally destroys the whole, according to the Theologian Gregory, who states in the first of his *Peace* [orations]: "To cut off anything from the Three, or to alienate it, is for us the same as to destroy the whole." For it would fall into contradiction if it were thus in things, while proclaiming the Spirit from the Son. AGALLIANUS. And indeed, it falls into the very same contradiction now, by confessing the Spirit to be God and yet making it a creature—because it deprives it of essential and natural power, and for that reason, of the power of procession common to the Father and the Son; and furthermore, by handing down a difference of origins, calling the Father an "unoriginate origin" and the Son an "origin from an origin," which difference clearly introduces ditheism. ARGYROPULUS. He says that one origin of the holy Spirit is venerated. AGALLIANUS. But the Theologian Gregory does not hand it down this way, nor does he call the Father an "unoriginate origin" so that this new theology might have room, as if the Son were an origin from an "unoriginate" within the Godhead; but rather he understands the "unoriginate" and the "origin" as the property of the Father’s person, saying: "The properties of the Father are that He is conceived as unoriginate and as an origin, while the Son is called Son; but the Son is nowhere unoriginate, though He is the origin of all." Then how does he not make two origins, by referring the Spirit—who is one person—to two persons, hypostatically and personally? For the relations inside are of persons, not of essence and power. ARGYROPULUS. But if, these things having been said by us, someone should ask, firstly, why the Son does not beget, just as He proceeds, partaking of the same generating power as the Father? Or how the holy Spirit neither proceeds nor begets, if the powers are different in Him as they are in the Father and the Son? AGALLIANUS. Now, with bared head, he has let out the absurdity: that the Son has the generating power of the Father along with the processing one, and likewise the Spirit. And look at what forced him to this. Realizing that if the Son has the processing power, the Spirit must also have the generating one—and indeed the processing one as well, as is always said by us to follow as absurdities. If the Son were a producer and he had no way to escape, he does not grant these as not being absurd; and in order to show that these things are not absurd, he assigns to the divine persons impotence rather than powers, and deprivations, and such like.
