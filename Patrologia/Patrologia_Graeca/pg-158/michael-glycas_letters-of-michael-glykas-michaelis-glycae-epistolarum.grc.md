DIES III. De die tertia creationis mundi. De plantis, arboribus et herbis, quæ simul e terra prodierunt. De differentiis herbarum, et quorsum quædam Deus noxia creavit, quædam utilia. De differentiis arborum. De grossis ficus, et qua virtute sint præditi. Quomodo ficus calidum, acrem ac incidentem halitum emittat. De succino. De plantis semper virentibus. De Atlante. De congregatione aquarum. Qua ratione in unum congregatæ sint aquæ. Quomodo salsum mare. De Nilo. De calidis aquis. De fontibus calidis. De pluviis, aquis, et quomodo corrumpantur. DIES IV. De quarta die creationis mundi. De duobus luminaribus sole et luna. De primigenia luce luminarium. De nocte. Unde dies vocetur. Inanes veterum opiniones de sole et luna. De sole et astris, de lumine et magnitudine. De magnis luminaribus. Quomodo luna conferat ad animalia et plantas, et quædam alia. De defectu solis et lunæ. De luna, nec non potestate et accidentibus ejus, quodque solis non dicatur defectus, sed obscuratio, seu obtenebratio. De anno. Cur nubibus obducatur, seu tristetur cœlum. De signis lunæ. De fato. De signis stellarum. De cometis, et unde fiant. De trajectione seu de astris cadentibus. Observationes quædam de nubibus, de fulgure, tonitru, ac prestere, et quomodo fiant. De iride. De fulguribus. De prestere. De fulmine. Quod sol sub terram feratur. De primigenia luce. DIES V. De quinta die creationis mundi. Quod a Propontide pisces veniant in Pontum Euxinum. De echino. De sepia. De polypo. De cancro. De cetis. De ceto qui prion dicitur. De remora. De magnete, seu Herculeo lapide. De palma. De polypo. De loliginibus et sepiis. De cicada. De testa molli præditis. De sepiis, loliginibus et polypis. Qui pisces branchias habeant, qui iis careant. De piscibus quod non inter se consuescant. De serpentis et murænæ coitu. De volucribus seu volatilibus. De ape.

Thus, while the sun rises and sets, light and darkness necessarily follow, being contrary to one another, yet both necessary for mankind; for the one contributes to the work of human beings and reveals things done, while the other gives them rest and dissolves the weariness originating from work. Night, moreover, is found to be useful to mankind in this respect also: for, situated as it is between the days, it causes time to be measured. For if light had remained without succession, we would not have learned the cycles of years, and one day would have seemed by all means to be the measure of the present age. This we believe the expected age to come will possess; for to those who are becoming immortal and without succession, such an age is appropriate. In the present time, however, it was necessary to know the measures of time, so that, seeing it passing by, we might take care of ourselves and prepare for our departure; wherefore God most wisely ordained that night should intervene. In this way, then, the sun both rising and setting, it is necessary that either light or darkness should follow; which two things indeed are opposed to each other, yet both are useful to men. For light provides no small service to man for performing work, and points out to him things that fall under his gaze: but darkness, in turn, cherishes mortals through rest, and dissipates all the trouble conceived from labor. We also discern night to be useful to mankind in another respect. For since it is interposed between the days, it effects a measurement of time. For if light were continuous without any interruption, we would not have noticed the orbits and revolutions of the years, but the duration of the whole age would have seemed to be one day entirely, which we believe will be the case in the age about to follow. For since we shall be immortal in it and exempt from changes, such an age will also be suitable for us. But in this age it was necessary that the measures of time be known to us, so that, seeing it glide by swiftly, we might be solicitous about ourselves and prepare for our migration. And for this reason we see that God most wisely interposed nights between the days. And thus the prophets of the Spirit think and speak concerning the original production and constitution of the stars; but those who are always busying themselves with empty things—who say they are wise, but, to speak with Paul, have become foolish—have given themselves over to myriads of opinions on these matters. For immediately Anaxagoras conjectured these things concerning the nature of the stars: he said that stones were snatched up from the rotation of the universe, and these, being ignited and fixed on high, were named stars. The same view also appears to Democritus regarding this. Diogenes said they were like pumice, having certain pores. But he also argues that some of them fall into the earth, and being extinguished, it is proven that they possess the nature of stones. And he uses as evidence the one that once fell at Aegos-Potami in a fiery manner. Anaximander [held] that they were certain systems of air full of fire, compressed in the manner of a wheel, emitting flames from certain vents. Xenophanes said the sun and moon were ignited clouds, while others said they were a fiery mass or stones. Philolaus philosophized that the sun was glass-like, receiving the reflection of the fire existing in the world, and filtering it to us. Such were the vain things these wise men thought regarding the nature of the stars; for, ignorant of God their Maker, they did not allow a rational cause to preside over the genesis of all things. Hence the great Basil, lamenting them, says that they will be condemned and [suffer] more than others on the day of judgment, because they turned themselves so towards vain things.
