CENTURY 15: GEORGIUS CODINUS: NOTICE
(Fabric., Bibl. Gr. ed. Harles tom. VII.)
Georgius Codinus, having formerly discharged the highest office in the Constantinopolitan court as Curopalates, flourished in the latest times of the Palæologi, and survived the empire itself, inasmuch as he concludes his booklet on the emperors of Constantinople with a commemoration of the city’s capture by the Agarenes, or Turks, in the year 6961, that is, 1453 of Christ. Of his writings there remain: *De officiis et officialibus aulæ imperatorum et magnæ ecclesiæ CPolitanæ* (On the offices and officials of the palace of the emperors and of the Great Church of Constantinople). Codinus also treats of the majority of festivals, and of the emperor’s coronation, the inauguration of the patriarch, and other matters; whence in another Bavarian manuscript codex B the title of the first part is conceived as follows: *Τὰ ὀφφίκια τοῦ παλλατίου* (The offices of the palace), etc.

To return to the book of Codinus: Allatius, in *De Georgiis* (p. 362), mentions his manuscript copies, beyond those which I have already praised, including the Vatican, the Escorial, and the Sforza codices, and notes that he wrote in an unpolished and barbaric style, such as was in use among the common people in that age. For, he says, besides the difficult understanding of the subjects and names, often the very manner of speaking, being rustic and harsh, obscures and blinds the meaning, and this is made even more obscure by the errors of the scribes and by foreign words introduced in place of proper ones through the carelessness of the learned. He copied most of his work almost verbatim from Cantacuzenus and other later historians, as Pontanus and Gretser noted among others.

A Moreover, what Codinus narrates here about the tokens or images of the pagans, he took from Cornutus and John Lydus the Philadelphian, in his book *On Months*, from which Suidas also, as Lambecius observed, drew the same things. The rest concerning the statues and things worthy of note in the city, he described almost word for word from the anonymous collection of antiquities of Constantinople, which bears the title *Brief Historical Collections*, which Lambecius first published in Greek from a code of the King of France with his own notes at the end of Codinus, pp. 85-105, and observes that they were excerpted from Theodore the Reader, Papias, Eusebius, Socrates, Marcellus the Reader, and many other writers. The same Anonynymous Collections, far more corrected, were given by Francis Combefis from that time, in Greek and Latin with his own version and notes, in his *Fasciculus of Constantinopolitan Affairs*, at the end of Allatius' *On Simeon*, Paris, 1664, 4to. And since he frequently chides Lambecius in those notes, Lambecius wished to reply to him in a new edition of Codinus which he had promised, which he wanted to insert by equipping it primarily from the Imperial library as a supplement to the *Corpus of Byzantine History*. See Book III of his *Commentaries on the Library of Vienna*, p. 426. [Book VIII, p. 970 Koll.] But that Supplement never saw the light of day. There follows in that first and last Lambecian edition of Codinus the work of the same writer *On the Buildings of Constantinople*, *On the monasteries and other buildings of Constantinople*, pp. 37-64; or *On the structure of the church of St. Sophia and the Holy Apostles*, pp. 64-74. Which part is also read in the edition of Georgius Dousa, and finally *On the years from the creation of the world down to the empire of the great Constantine, and on those who reigned in the same queen of cities, down to its very capture by the Hagarenes*, pp. 75-83. [Concerning the codices, we have already spoken above.]   There is subjoined at the end of the volume *An explanation of the offices of the Holy and Great Church, and they were interpreted according to their order*. An anonymous explanation of the offices of the Holy and Great Church of Constantinople according to their order: which Bernard Medonius of Toulouse first edited from a manuscript code belonging to Carolus Monchallus, Archbishop of Toulouse, and added his own Latin version and Glossary, and brief notes in the margin.
There exists also, but which Lambecius rightly omitted, the Mass of Gregory I the Pope, translated into Greek by the same Codinus, which, with that Greek interpretation (in both languages, not in Greek only, as Wharton writes), was edited by Fed. Morellus at Paris, 1595, 8vo, with whom John a S. Andrea, a canon of the Parisian church, had shared it. Furthermore, an edition from Paris, 1583, 8vo, is mentioned in the Barberini library, which I have not seen. It exists also in Greek and Latin in Fronton du Duc's *Auctuarium of the Library of the Fathers*, Paris, 1624, vol. XII. Be it known, reader, says Allatius, *On the Georgii*, p. 362, that this same Mass is read much more fully and perfectly in the Barberini library, cod. 75, which is nevertheless said to have been translated into the Greek language by Manuel Chrysoloras: *Liturgy translated from the Latin dialect into the Greek, by Lord Manuel Chrysoloras*. The beginning is the same as that which is attributed to Codinus: "The priest who is about to celebrate, etc." There appeared also with the Lambecian Codinus the epistle by the same Manuel Chrysoloras to John Palæologus.
