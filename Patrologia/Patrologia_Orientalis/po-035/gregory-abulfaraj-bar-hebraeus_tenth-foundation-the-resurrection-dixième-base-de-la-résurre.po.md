### TENTH FOUNDATION
ܕܫܬܐܣܬܐ ܥܣܝܪ̈ܝܬܐ
OF THE TREATISE ON THE (RE)LIFE OF THE DEAD, THAT IS TO SAY, ON THE RESURRECTION OF BODIES
ܡܛܠ ܡܠܬܐ ܕܥܠ ܚܰܝܬ݁ ܡܝ̈ܬܐ ܐܘܟܝܬ ܩܝܡܬܐ ܕܦܓܪ̈ܐ. ܘܒܗ ܐܝܬ ܩ̈ܦܐܠܐܐ ܬܠ̈ܬܐ ܀

### CHAPTER ONE
ܩܦܠܐܘܢ ܩܕܡܝܐ. ܡܛܠ ܡܬܬܗ̈ܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܕ݁ܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ.
ON THE (RETURN TO) EXISTENCE OF THAT WHICH HAS CEASED TO EXIST. There are two parts.

### FIRST PART
ܦܣܘܩܐ ܩܕܡܝܐ. ܡܛܠ ܗܺܝ ܕܡܬܡܨܝܢܝܬܐ ܗܝ ܥܛܝܦܘܬ
THAT THE RETURN TO THE RE-EXISTENCE OF THAT WHICH HAS CEASED TO EXIST IS POSSIBLE. There is one section.

### On the proofs that establish this question
ܡܬܬܗܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܕܗܿܝ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ. ܡܛܠ ܡܚ̈ܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܡܫܪܪ̈ܢܝܬܗ̇ ܕܒܥܬܐ ܗܕܐ ܀ ܡܚܿܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܩܕܡܝܬܐ.ܐܡ̇ܪ̈ܝܢܢ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܐܝܬܘܗܝ. ܐܠܘ ܠܐ ܡܬܡܿܨܝܢܝܬܐ ܗܘܬ ܠܗ ܐܝܬܘܬܐ. ܠܐ ܣܟ ܗܼܘܐ. ܒܕܓܘܢ ܗܼܝ ܗܺܝ ܡܬܡܨܝܢܘܬܐ. ܐܢ ܐܘܣܝܝܬܐ ܗܝ ܠܟܝܢܗ. ܠܐ ܙܿܕܩ ܕܬܿܫܢܐ ܡܢܗ. ܘܟܕ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܘܟܕ ܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ. ܗܐܓܝܪ ܒܪ̈ܢܫܐ ܘܟܕ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܘܟܕ ܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ. ܟܝܢܗ ܚܳܝܐ ܡܠܝܼܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ.
First proof: We say that what exists - unless its existence is impossible - has (a possibility) to exist without end. Consequently, this possibility, if it is essential to its nature, must not separate from it, whether it exists or does not exist. Here is, in effect, man: whether he exists or does not exist, his nature is to be a rational animal. And if (this possibility) is accidental to his nature, his nature has received the power of this accidental possibility. If it is essential to him, it must not separate from him, whether he exists or does not exist, as we have said. And if (this possibility) is accidental, necessarily it holds a power in its likeness. And thus the first division returns, and the reasoning continues without

Second proof: We say that everything that has "become" necessarily was capable of "becoming" before existing. But if this (power) was without end, it existed also even after it has "become" and has ceased to exist. This very power, it must hold it, because the prior deprivation is distinguished in nothing from the subsequent deprivation: all deprivations are identical.

### SECOND PART
ܟܠܗܝܢ ܫ̈ܘܝܢ ܐܢܝܢ ܀ ܦܣܘܩܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܢܐ ܕܩܦܠܐܘܢ ܩܕܡܝܐ ܕܫܬܐܣܬܐ ܥܣܝܪ̈ܝܬܐ. ܡܛܠ ܬܪ̈ܥܝܬܐ ܕܗܿܢܘܢ ܕܠܐ ܡܬܡܨܝܢܝܬܐ ܐܕܝܿܢܝܢ ܠܥܛܝܦܘܬ ܡܬܬܿܗܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܕܗ̇ܘ ܕܐܬܠ̈ܝܬܝ. ܘܒܗ ܐܝܬ ܢܝ̈ܫܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܢ ܀ ܣܥܝܐ ܩܕܡܝܐ. ܐܡܪܝܢ ܕܡܬܡܨ̈ܝܢܘܬܐ ܚܿܝܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܬܢܝܐ. ܘܥܠ ܗܿܘ ܕܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ. ܟܐܡܬ ܕܐܬܠ̈ܝܬܝ. ܐܝܟܢܐ ܡܿܨܝܐ ܕܬܬܘ̇ܛܪܲܓ. ܐܝܬܘܬܐ ܓܝܪ ܒܗ̇ܘ ܕܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܘܠܘ ܒܗܿܘ ܕܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܩ̇ܝܡܐ. ܠܐ ܡܕܝܢ ܫܪ̈ܝܪ̈ܪܐ ܕܢܚܪܘܩ ܕ݁ܗܽܘ ܕܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܡܟܢ ܠܡܬܬܿܗܘܝܘ ܡܢܕܪ̈ܝܫ ܀
of the first chapter of the tenth foundation: ON THE OPINION OF THOSE WHO JUDGE IMPOSSIBLE THE RETURN TO THE RE-EXISTENCE OF THAT WHICH HAS CEASED TO EXIST. There are two sections. First section: On their rational objections. First objection: They say, regarding the possibility of the faculty to exist: how can it be attributed to that which does not exist, namely to that which has ceased to exist? Existence resides, in effect, in that which exists, and not in that which does not exist. Therefore, it is not true to decree that that which does not exist can return again to existence.
ܣܥܝܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܢܐ. ܐܿܡܪ̈ܝܢ ܐܠܘ ܡܬܡܿܨܝܢܝܬܐ ܗܝ ܥܛܝܦܘܬ ܡܬܬܿܗܘܝܢܘܬܗ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܿܝܬܝ. ܡܬܡܿܨܝܢܝܬܐ ܗܘܬ ܐܦ ܗܝ ܕܠܗ ܠܙܒܢܐ ܗ̇ܘ ܩܕܡܝܐ ܕܒܗ ܗܘܐ ܡܢܩܕܝܡ. ܝܗܘܒܗ ܐܠܗܐ ܬܢܝܿܢܘܬ. ܒܕܕܓܘܢ ܟܕ ܢܿܗܘܝܘܗܝ ܐܠܗܐ ܠܙܒܢܐ ܗܿܘ ܩܕܡܝܐ. ܘܢܿܗܘܝܘܗܝ ܒܗ ܠܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ. ܗܝ ܗܿܝ ܡܬܬܗܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܒܙܒܢܐ ܗܿܘ ܩܕܡܝܐ ܘܠܘ ܒܗܿܘ ܬܪܝܝܐ
Second objection: They say: If the return to re-existence were possible for that which has ceased to exist, it would also be possible that, for the first time in which it existed at first, God would grant it to it again. Consequently, when God would give existence to this first time and would make exist in it that which had ceased to exist, it would be a return to existence in this first time, and not existence in this second one. And one concludes that what would be a return would be a non-return, which is absurd.
ܗܿܘܝܐ. ܘܡܬܟܿܢܫܐ ܕ݁ܗܽܘ ܬܢܝܢܘܬ ܗܼܘܐ. ܠܘ ܬܢܝܢܘܬ ܗܼܘܐ. ܗܺܝ ܕܫܟܝܪ̈ܐ ܀ ܣܥܝܐ ܬܠܝܬܝܐ. ܐܿܡܪ̈ܝܢ ܕܟܕ ܡܿܗܘܐ ܐܠܗܐ ܠܗ̇ܘ ܕܐܬܠܺܝܬܝ. ܡܼܨܐ ܐܦ ܕܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ ܐܟܘܬܗ. ܕܒܟܠܡܕܡ ܠܗ ܕܡܳܐ ܢܼܗܘܐ. ܘܡܢ ܗܪ̈ܟܐ ܐܠܨܐ. ܕܚܕ ܦܚܡܐ ܫܘܝܐ ܢܗܹܐ ܠܬܪ̈ܝܗܘܢ. ܠܘܬ ܗ݇ܘ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ. ܐܘ ܬܪ̈ܝܗܘܢ ܢܗܘܘܢ ܗܘ ܕܐܬܠ̈ܝܬܝ. ܗܺܝ ܕܫܟܝܪ̈ܐ. ܒܗ̇ܝ ܕܠܚܕ ܬܪ̈ܝܢ ܥܿܒܕܐ. ܐܘ ܠܐ ܚܕ ܡܢܝܗܘܢ. ܢܗܐ ܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ. ܗܺܝ ܕܫܪܝܪܐ ܘܡܫܪܪ ܠܠܐ ܡܬܡܿܨܝܢܘܬ ܡܬܬܗ̇ܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܿܝܬܝ ܀
Third objection: They say that when God (re)gives existence to that which has ceased to exist, He can also similarly (give existence) to another that in everything would be similar to it. And by that, a likeness must be equal to two, in relation to that which has ceased to exist. Therefore, either there would be two (things) in that which has ceased to exist, which is absurd, because one would make two; or that which has ceased to exist would be neither of the two (things), which is true and which confirms the impossibility of the return to existence of that which has ceased to exist.

ܐܦܠܐ ܬܘܒ ܡܿܨܝܐ ܕܚܕ ܡܢܗܘܢ ܕܝܠܟܝܐܝܬ ܢܗܐ ܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ ܣܛܪ ܡܢ ܗ̇ܘ ܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ. ܒܗ̇ܝ ܕܒܟܠܡܕܡ ܕ݁ܡܰܝܺ̈ܝ ܠܚ̈ܕܕܐ ܣܼܡܢ ܕܐܝܬܝܗܘܢ. ܘܐܢܗܘ ܕܬܐܿܡܪ̈ܘܢ ܕܚܕ ܡܢܗܘܢ ܕܝܠܢܝܐܝܬ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܗܽܘ ܕܐܬܠܿܝܬܝ. ܒܗ̇ܝ ܕܗܘ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܗܽܘ ܕܐܬܠ̇ܝܬܝ. ܘܗܘ ܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ ܕܡܐ ܠܗ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܘܠܘ ܗܼܘ. ܐܿܡܪ̈ܝܢܢ ܕܗܕܐ ܠܘ ܡܚܿ̇ܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܗܝ ܐܠܐ ܥܘܦܦܗ̇ ܒܠܚܘܕ
And it is not impossible either that one of the two, separately, becomes that which has ceased to exist, to the exclusion of the other, because we have posited that they are in everything identical to one another. And if you say that one of them, separately, (re)becomes that which has ceased to exist, because that which has ceased to exist becomes (existent), and that the other resembles it, but does not become it, we say that this is not a proof, but only the repetition of the proposition that by that you want to prove, which is absurd.
ܕܡܠܬܐ. ܐܝܕܐ ܕܠܗܿ ܒܗ̇ܿ ܨܒܝܢ ܐܢܬܘܢ ܕܬܫܪ̈ܪ̈ܘܢܗܿ. ܗܺܝ ܕܫܟܝܪܐ ܀ ܢܝܫܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܢܐ. ܡܛܠ ܫܖ̈ܝܐ ܕܣ̈ܥܝܐ ܗܠܝܢ.
Second section: On the refutations of these objections.
ܫܪ̈ܝܐ ܕܣܥܝܐ ܩܕܡܝܐ. ܐܿܡܪ̈ܝܢܢ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܗܘܐ ܘܐܬܠܝܬܝ. ܟܐܡܬ ܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ ܗܘܐ. ܠܘ ܒܟܠܡܕܡ ܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ. ܒܗ̇ܝ ܕܒܗܘܢܐ ܠܝܘ ܠܐ ܐܝܬܘܗܝ. ܘܗܟܢܐ ܒܐܝܬܘܬܐ ܗܕܐ ܗܿܘܢܢܝܬܐ. ܐܝܬܝܘܬܐ
Refutation of the first objection: We say that that which was existing and which has ceased to exist, that is to say that which no longer exists, it is not in every way that it does not exist, for, in spirit, it is not non-existent. And likewise, in this existence in spirit, subsists the existence of the possibility (of being), which, itself also, subsists in spirit.
ܕܡܬܡܨܿܝܢܘܬܐ ܗ̇ܘܝܐ ܕܐܦ ܗܝ ܡܬܗܘܢܢܝܬܐ ܗܿܝ ܡܬܩ̇ܝܡܐ ܐ ܀ ܫܪ̈ܝܐ ܕܣܥܝܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܢܐ. ܐܡܪ̈ܝܢܢ ܕܠܘ ܟܠܡܕܡ ܕܗ̈ܘܐ ܥܠ ܙܒܢܐ ܣܢܝܼܩ ܕܢܗܘܐ. ܐܠܐ ܐܦ ܙܒܢܐ ܒܗܘܝܗ ܥܠ ܙܒܢܐ ܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ ܣܢܝܼܩ ܗܘܐ. ܘܗ̇ܘ ܐܚܪܢܐ ܥܠ ܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ ܘܗܟܢܐ ܠܠܐ ܣܘܦ ܗܺܝ ܕܫܟܝܪ̈ܐ. ܡܛܠ ܗܕܐ ܠܐ ܣܢܝܼܩ ܐܠܗܐ ܫܘܒܚܐ ܠܛܝܒܘܬܗ ܠܘܬ ܡܗ̈ܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܝܬܝ. ܥܠ ܡܗ̇ܘܝܢܘܬܐ ܕܙܒܢܝܐ ܗܘ ܩܕܡܝܐ ܕܒܗ ܐܗܘܝܗ ܡܢ
Refutation of the second objection: We say that it is not everything that exists that needs a time to exist, but time also, for its own existence, needs another time; and this other, another, and so on without end, which is absurd. That is why God - glory to His goodness - does not need, for the re-existence of that which has ceased to exist, the creation of this first time, in which He created it formerly.
ܠܩܘܕܡܝܢ ܀ ܦܪ̈ܝܫܼܝܢ ܕܝܢ ܡܢ ܚ̈ܕܕܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܗܘܢ ܗ̈ܘܝܐ. ܒܗ̇ܝ ܕܗܿܘܝܐ ܗܽܘ ܩܕܡܝܐ. ܗ̇ܘܝܐ ܗܘ ܕܠܐ ܩ̇ܕܝܡ ܠܗ ܗܿܘܝܐ ܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ. ܘܗܿܘܝܐ ܗܽܘ ܬܢܝ̈ܢܐ ܗܿܘܝܐ ܗܘ ܒܬܪ ܗ̈ܘܝܐ ܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ. ܘܡܛܠ ܗܕܐ ܟܐܢܐܝܬ ܗ̈ܘܝܐ ܗܿܘ ܩܕܡܝܐ ܒܪ̈ܝܬܐ ܐܘ ܡܿܘܠܕܐ ܡܬ ܩܪܐ. ܘܗ݇ܘܝܐ ܗܽܘ ܬܢܝ̇ܢܐ ܩܝܡܬܐ ܕܗ̇ܘ ܕܢܦܼܠ.
In effect, the two becomings are distinguished from one another in this: that this first becoming is a becoming before which there is no other becoming; and the second becoming is a becoming after another becoming. And that is why, rightly, the first becoming is called creation or birth; and the second becoming, straightening of that which had fallen, or restoration of that which was corrupted, or even resurrection of that which was dead.
ܐܘ ܚܘܕܬܐ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܒܠ̣ܝ. ܐܘ ܢܘܚܡܐ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܡܝܬ ܡܬܟ̇ܢܐ ܀ ܫܪ̈ܝܐ ܕܣܥܝܐ ܬܠܝܬܝܐ. ܐܿܡܪ̈ܝܢܢ ܕܠܘ ܚܕ ܦܚܡܐ ܫ̈ܘܝܐ ܩܢܝܢ ܬܪ̈ܝܗܘܢ ܗܠܝܢ ܕܗܼܘܘ ܠܘܬ ܗܽܘ ܕܐܬܿܠܝܬܝ. ܘܐܦܢ ܕ݁ܡ̣ܺܝܝ ܠܚ̈ܕܕܐ ܐܝܬܝܗܘܢ ܒܐܣܟܝܡܐ ܘܕܡܼܘܬܐ. ܒܗ̇ܝ ܕܚܕ ܡܢܗܘܢ ܡܢ ܡܢܘܬܗ ܒ̈ܝܬܝܬܐ ܕܗܿܘ ܕܐܬܠܰܝܬܝ ܪ̈ܟܒܗ ܘܳܐ ܗܼܘܝܗ ܐܠܗܐ. ܘܗ݇ܘܐܚܪ̈ܢܐ ܡܢ ܡ̈ܢܘܬܐ ܢܘܟܪ̈ܝܬܐ.

First proof: We say that it has been established above that God - glory to His goodness - lets nothing escape His knowledge, whether it be something universal or something particular. Consequently, for the bodily parts, even when they will be dissolved into their elements and dispersed, the creator will know how to recognize, for each of them, of whom it is a part. Third proof: We say that God—glory to His goodness—created men either so that they would be tormented and not happy; or created men so that they would be neither tormented nor happy; or so that they would be happy and not tormented. Now the first (case) is not true, because God is good and merciful and just; and one who is such cannot be the cause of torment. And the second (case) is also not true, because even if they were not the work of the creator, (men) would be neither happy nor tormented. And if, even after having been created, their existence were of this kind, their creation would turn out to be useless, which is absurd. It remains, therefore, that the third (case) is true, namely that it is so that they might be happy that God created men. And if, finally, they are tormented, it is not because of their creator that they suffer, but because, as they were stained by sins through their own will, the torment is attached to them.

Consequently, we say that this happiness, for which they were created, they will have, either in this world or in the future world. That this world is deprived of happiness and that its satisfactions are not true satisfactions, but only a life of pain, we have shown above. It follows, therefore, that there exists another world, the future one, where those who deserve it receive their happiness: and where those who deserve torment will be tormented. And it is evident that the resurrection means nothing else.

### ON THE SADDUCEAN OPINION WHICH DENIES THE RESURRECTION OF BODIES

Second objection: They speak of a woman who, at the time of a famine, cooked her child and ate him. Now, necessarily, parts of her child's body became parts of her own body, because all food, whatever it may be, must assimilate to the one who feeds on it. Consequently, at the resurrection, these parts, if they return to the body of her child, her own body perishes; and if they return to her own body, it is the body of her child that perishes. And it is not possible for a single part to be a part of two bodies, since on the one hand it is not possible to find one body at the same time in two places, so, necessarily, one of these two bodies will not rise. And if one does not rise, none rises, because all are identical by nature. Thus is refuted the theory of the resurrection.

Fifth objection: They say that God—glory to His goodness—raises the bodies and unites them again to their souls, either so that they may be tormented in them, or so that they may be happy, or so that they may be neither tormented nor happy. Now the first (case) is not true, because it is incompatible with the goodness of a good God to torment such a soul already freed from the body which weighed upon its lightness and tarnished its splendor. In the same way, the second (case) is not true either, because, when (the soul) resides among its spiritual consubstantials, which are separate intelligences, its satisfaction will be more intellectual than the satisfaction it had while with the body. And the third (case) is not true either, because for the soul, this second union would be in vain. Now nothing that has been accomplished by God is in vain. From there one knows that a second union of the soul with the body is impossible.

### ON THE REFUTATION OF THE OPINION THAT DENIES THE RESURRECTION
ܡܛܠ ܣܬܘܪ̈ܝܗ̇ ܕܬܪ̈ܥܝܬܐ ܟ̇ܦܪ̈ܬ݁ ܒܩܝܡܬܐ. ܘܒܗ ܐܝܬ ܢܝ̈ܫܐ ܬܪ̈ܝܢ ܀

And it is by this that one knows that for each member, and a fortiori for each body, the true parts are those which it does not acquire through food. And when a member or another body is nourished by (these parts), it is not the true parts of the members

Consequently, at the resurrection, every non-elemental body

We have shown previously that, at the resurrection, the body rises in the fullness of all its parts. Consequently, the organs of speech also rise; and we have shown likewise that its thoughts do not disappear from the soul. Consequently, it is not far (from there to say) that, through sensible speech, men express to one another the thoughts that arise in their souls. Saint Evagrius also confirms this by saying: "If sensible words also serve in the world to come, then the wise of this world will also inherit the kingdom of heaven." Nevertheless, others interpret this word in reverse, saying: "The Master's intent is this: just as the wise of this world will not inherit the kingdom of heaven, because God has made their wisdom foolish, as the apostle says, likewise sensible words will not serve in the world to come." Likewise what was said: "If a camel enters through the eye of a needle, the rich man will also enter the kingdom of heaven." But we say that it is known that this interpretation is not true, because the Master also said: "But if it is the purity of the nous that obtains the vision of the world to come, it is evident that the wise of this world will not be kept at a distance from the science of God." And by that it is confirmed that the wise of this world too, when their mind is purified and they have acquired virtuous acts, will not be deprived of the kingdom of heaven, nor of the knowledge of God, for this is properly the kingdom of heaven. And, as we have not occupied ourselves with this state, but with the use of sensible words and that this goal has been reached, we must not extend ourselves (any longer).

Response. We say that the premise which affirms that the resurrection corresponds again to what existed in the beginning is not defined or precise; that is why it is appropriate to draw from it a partial and not a general conclusion, namely that, in certain aspects only, the state after the resurrection resembles the first (state). I say: for the glory, for the radiance of the bodies, and for the purification from passions, and not for everything. And Our Lord ate and drank after the resurrection, not because he had need of food and drink, but to confirm his resurrection and to remove from his disciples the idea of a phantom. And this is evident from what he said to them: "Touch me and know that a spirit has neither flesh nor bones, as you see that I have." But, as for this (verse): "You will eat and drink at the table of the kingdom," we say that in the end, with (the help of) God, we will show that the kingdom of heaven is a certain knowledge. Therefore, its food and drink must also be (of an) intellectual and not a bodily (order).
