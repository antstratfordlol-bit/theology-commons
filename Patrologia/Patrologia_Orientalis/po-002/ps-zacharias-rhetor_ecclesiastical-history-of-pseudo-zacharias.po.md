Xenaeas was an eloquent and learned Syrian, and zealous in his faith. Bishop of Mabboug, in the time of Zeno, he wrote and sent a [profession of] faith, questioned Zeno regarding his faith, and received a reply. It was he who exposed Calandius of Antioch and drove him from his see. Suspecting also that Flavian was a heretic, he wrote and sent pressing messengers to the Emperor Anastasius to request that a council be held in Sidon. [The emperor] gave the order and [the council] was assembled in 560 according to the era of Antioch. Xenaeas urged the faithful and zealous monks of the East, as well as Cosmas, an eloquent man from the convent of Mar Agiba of Kennesrin (= Chalcis), who lived in Antioch, to make a petition (δέησις) and to present it to Flavian and to the assembly

of the bishops who were with him in Sidon. In a wise and consistent manner, they wrote 77 chapters of blame and numerous testimonies (χρήσεις) from the holy doctors, which confirmed the criticisms against the Council of Chalcedon and the Tome (τόμος) of Leo, and gave them to the council, praying and adjuring the priests to make reforms, to remove the stumbling blocks from the path of the Church, and to purify it by openly anathematizing the council. But the patriarch Flavian and some of the priests who were with him prevented the matter by saying: "For us, it is enough to anathematize the writings of Diodore and the accusations that some have brought against the XII Chapters (κεφάλαια) of Cyril, as well as Nestorius, for fear that we might awaken the sleeping dragon and that he might cause many to perish by his venom." It was in this way that the council was dissolved.

Severus succeeded him: he was an eloquent and distinguished monk from the monastery of Theodore; a former lawyer (ἀπὸ δικανικῶν) from Gaza who was an apocrisiarius in the imperial city, and who was known and loved by Probus and his relatives. Subsequently, he held a council in Tyre with Xenaeas and the priests of his diocese, as well as with those of Lebanon Phoenicia, Arabia, Euphratensis, and Mesopotamia (Μεσοποταμία), and he explained the Henotikon of Zeno, showing that it had been written to annul the assembly of Chalcedon.
