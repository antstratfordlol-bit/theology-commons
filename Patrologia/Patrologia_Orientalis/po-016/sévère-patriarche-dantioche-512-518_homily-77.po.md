### LXXVIIth HOMILY OF SEVERUS ARCHBISHOP OF ANTIOCH
ܡܐܡܪܐ ܩܥܙ ܕܩܕܝܫܐ ܣܘܝܪܐ ܪܝܫ ܐܦܣܩܘܦܐ ܕܐܢܛܝܘܟܝܐ ܕܠܐ ܐܬܚܠܦܘ ܚܕ ܥܡ ܚܕ ܐܘܢܓܠܝܣܛܐ ܒܡܕܡ. ܟܕ ܡܫܚܠܦܐܝܬ ܐܫܬܥܝܘ ܥܠ ܦܘܪ̈ܫܐ ܕܩܝܡܬܐ ܕܡܫܝܚܐ ܐܠܗܢ ܘܦܪܘܩܢ.
That the evangelists did not contradict each other in any way when they recounted differently the facts relating to the resurrection of Christ, our God and our Savior, facts that we read on the night of every Sunday.
ܐܝܠܝܢ ܕܩܪܝܢܢ ܒܠܠܝܐ ܕܟܠܚܕ ܫܒܬܐ. ܟܠܗܘܢ ܓܝܪ ܐܝܟ ܕܢܐܡܪ ܡܘܕܝܢ ܠܩܪܝܢܐ ܕܐܘܢܓܠܝ̈ܐ ܩܕܝ̈ܫܐ ܕܐܩܝܡܢܢ ܫܦܝܪ ܒܠܠܝܐ ܕܫܒܬܐ ܥܠ ܩܝܡܬܗ ܕܐܠܗܢ ܪܒܐ ܘܦܪܘܩܢ ܝܫܘܥ ܡܫܝܚܐ. ܐܝܬ ܕܡܘܕܝܢ ܕܡܬܬܘܣܦܝܢ ܡܢܗ. ܐܠܐ ܡܛܠ ܕܣܒܪܝܢ ܕܐܝܟ ܕܡܬܚܙܐ ܙܒܢܝܢ ܣܓܝ̈ܐܢ ܫܡܥܝܢ ܠܗ ܡܬܬܙܝܥܝܢ ܐܡܪܝܢ ܕܠܐ ܚܕ ܚܕܐ ܐܡܪܝܢ ܐܘܢܓܠܝܣܛܐ ܥܠ ܦܘܪ̈ܫܐ ܚܕ ܚܕܐ. ܐܠܐ ܡܕܡ ܕܡܬܚܠܦ ܘܡܕܡ ܕܝܬܝܪܐܝܬ ܠܥܕܡܐ ܠܠܐ ܗܝܡܢܘܬܐ ܡܘܒܠ ܠܫܡܘܥܐ.
All, so to speak, approve the reading of the sacred Gospels that we have instituted appropriately, on the night of Sunday, concerning the resurrection of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ. Some acknowledge, it is true, that they derive profit from it, but, estimating that they hear it too frequently in a way, they declare that they are troubled by it, the evangelists not saying the same things about the same facts, but things that contradict each other and that lead the listener rather toward disbelief.

For who must one believe? Matthew who writes that the resurrection took place ὀψὲ τῶν σαδθάτων, John who recounted that the same fact occurred in the morning when darkness still reigned, or Luke and Mark who called the same moment, one first dawn, the other sunrise. To solve the problem submitted to us and the other questions that arise from the examination of the text of Scripture, we must, despite our weakness, be encouraged by the God who is risen and present an explanation that is clear. For he who has spread the seed of the reading and who has made it grow in the ears of all, it is just that he also give an account of the questions it raises. I will try to do so, and to deduce the solution to the questions from the very words of those who raised them. The sacred writers of the Gospels did not say that the Lord is risen either ὀψὲ σαδθάτων, or when the greater part of the night had passed, or again at dawn, or when the sun had already cast its rays.

But they wrote that the women went to the tomb, sometimes at one moment, sometimes at another, but not at the same moment—how would that be possible since they came there on various occasions—and that all heard the angels say similarly concerning the Savior: He is risen, he is not here, without adding when (that took place). It follows that if the resurrection took place that divine night, by the admission and agreement (of all the evangelists), none indicated the hour, which is unknown to everyone, except to the God who is risen and to the Father—who alone knows the Son as he himself is known by the Son—and to the Spirit who searches everything, even the depths of God.

using the past tense: He is not here, but he is risen. And this also clearly emerges from the fact that the apostles, in preaching the Gospel, said that Christ had been raised by the Father, thus making the news easy to admit. But the angel who first opened his mouth to announce the resurrection, unveiling the power, entirely worthy of God, of Him who was risen, cried out: He is not here, but he is risen. Declaring that he had been raised by the Father, as I said, spared the weakness of the listeners, but has the same meaning and not another. For how does the Father act? By his own power, obviously. Now, who is the power of the Father? No one other than Christ. Christ is, indeed, the power of God and the wisdom of God. Christ therefore raised himself, even if he is said to have been raised by the Father. One could be embarrassed, with good reason, by the following question: How does the Savior, after having promised his disciples, sometimes through the intermediary of angels, sometimes in person, to show himself to them upon their arrival in Galilee, get ahead of his promise, in Jerusalem itself? According to Luke, he makes his appearance to the Eleven assembled on the very day of the resurrection; according to John, he makes it both on that day and on the eighth day, placing himself in their midst, saying to them: Peace be with you, and letting himself be touched by Thomas. But this establishes the richness of his liberality and his love for men, and cannot be taxed as a lie. He did not say, in effect: "They will see me only in Galilee", and, after having appeared in Galilee, he did not refrain from showing himself in Galilee as he had promised—that would have been lying to his own words—but if he appeared to them, on the one hand, in Jerusalem, when they were locked in a house for fear of the Jews and they needed his encouragement; if, on the other hand, he kept his promise, by appearing to them in Galilee, both appearances, which took place really and out of love for man, escape all reproach.
